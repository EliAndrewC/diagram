## 1. Sources: the tomato

**China**
- **Chinese Repository vol. III** (archive `b1/b10e3dc5fb9b`), Canton, Feb. 1835: "Carrots, asparagus, gourds, squashes, melons of many kinds, cucumbers, pumpkins, tomatoes, winter cherry, egg plauts, and okers, are to be found in most parts of the country." English original.
- **Doolittle, *Social Life of the Chinese*** (archive `ce/ce3bd5278564`), 1865, Fuzhou. The OCR breaks off mid-sentence: "no musk-melon, nor beets, nor tomatoes of a large species. A very small kind of tomato, about the size of a small cherry,"
- **King, *Farmers of Forty Centuries*** (archive `84/842e8bba9073`), Boone Road market, Shanghai, 6 April 1900, in US cents: "Tomatoes, per lb. 5.16".
- **Li Xinsheng, 《读书》 2020.1** ([The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_5453847)), Ming to PRC: 「番茄虽然万历年间始有记载（王象晋：《群芳谱》），但是一直是作为观赏植物，民国时期，番茄的栽培范围不断扩大，但主要集中在大城市（如京沪一带）郊区，且栽培不多。」「新中国成立后，番茄才迎来了栽培和食用的全盛时代」
  - (translation) Recorded from the Wanli reign but always ornamental. In the Republic its range grew, but mainly around big cities such as Beijing and Shanghai, and little was grown. Only after 1949 came its heyday in cultivation and eating.
- **Liu Yanchun, Shanghai Botanical Garden, 2022** ([page](https://m.thepaper.cn/baijiahao_18019776)): 「长期作为观赏植物，只是被零星栽培在城郊，直至1930年代，番茄的食用性受到人们重视，我国东北、华北、华中地区才开始种植番茄。而番茄作为全国性的主要蔬菜，被大面积栽培则始于20世纪50年代。」
  - (translation) Long ornamental, grown only sporadically near cities. Only in the 1930s did its food value gain notice and planting begin in the northeast, north and centre. Large-area cultivation as a national vegetable began in the 1950s.
  - The same page gives 《群芳谱》「蕃柿」 and 《猗氏县志》「西蕃柿」.
- **Ai Lisi, 《北京日报》** ([page](https://m.thepaper.cn/baijiahao_7335825)) quotes 《群芳谱》: 「蕃柿，一名六月柿。茎如蒿，高四五尺；叶如艾，花似榴，一枝结五实或三四实，一树二三十实。」
  - (translation) Fanshi, also called sixth-month persimmon; stem like mugwort, four or five feet high; leaf like artemisia, flower like pomegranate; five, or three or four, fruits to a branch; twenty or thirty to a plant.
  - Also: 「即使到了清末民初时，番茄也只在北京、青岛、上海、广州这样的大城市周边郊区种植，供应到城市的西餐厅里。」 (translation) Even in the late Qing and early Republic it was grown only around Beijing, Qingdao, Shanghai and Guangzhou, for Western restaurants.
- **《植品》 1617, secondhand only** ([简书 blog](https://www.jianshu.com/p/88d5bd770608)): 「明朝的赵崡写的《植品》（1617年），提到，番茄是西洋传教士在稍早的万历年间和向日葵一起带到中国来的。」 (translation) Zhao Han's *Zhipin* (1617) says Western missionaries brought it, with the sunflower, earlier in the Wanli reign.

**Japan**
- **Isono Naohide 2007, 明治前園芸植物渡来年表** ([Keio PDF](https://koara.lib.keio.ac.jp/xoonips/modules/xoonips/download.php?file_id=13125)): 「寛文 8（1668）トマト（唐なすび）7月12日，狩野探幽が写生（草木花写生図巻）」 and, for 1703, 「トマト（おらんだなすび）8月15日，狩野常信が枝に成った実を写生（常信写生図巻16）。現在の通称ミニトマト的な大きさ。」
  - (translation) 1668: tomato ("Chinese eggplant"), sketched by Kanō Tan'yū. 1703: tomato ("Dutch eggplant"), fruit on the branch sketched by Kanō Tsunenobu, about the size of today's mini tomato.
- **ja.wikipedia, トマト** ([page](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%83%88%E3%83%9E%E3%83%88)): 「貝原益軒の『大和本草』（1709年）にはトマトについての記述があり」; 「日本で食用として利用されるようになったのは明治以降で、1868年（明治元年）に欧米から9品種が導入され、「赤茄子」（あかなす）とよばれた」
  - (translation) The *Yamato honzō* (1709) describes it. Food use came after Meiji began; in 1868 nine varieties were brought from the West and called "red eggplant".
- **NARO, 2009** ([page](https://www.naro.go.jp/publicity_report/season/006534.html)): 「明治時代になり、食用のトマトが輸入されましたが、当時のトマトは酸味が強すぎて日本人の口に合わず、定着しませんでした。」「昭和に入ってアメリカから導入された「ポンテローザ」という臭みの少ない桃色系の品種によって、ようやく日本人の食生活にトマトが定着し始めたとされています。」
  - (translation) Food tomatoes were imported in Meiji but were too sour for Japanese taste and did not take. Only with the low-odour pink "Ponderosa", introduced from America in the Shōwa era, did the tomato begin to settle into the Japanese diet.
- **The arrival date conflicts:** [ALIC 2004](https://vegetable.alic.go.jp/yasaijoho/yasai/0407_yasai1.html) has 「18世紀の初頭」 (early 18th c.); [ALIC 2014](https://vegetable.alic.go.jp/yasaijoho/yasai/1409_yasai1.html) has 「江戸時代初期にオランダ人により」 (early Edo, by the Dutch).

**Korea**
- **《지봉유설》 1614, via [중부매일](https://www.jbnews.com/news/articleView.html?idxno=1437372)**, garble as on the page: 「南蠻枾者。草?也。春生秋實。其味似枾。本出南蠻。近有一使臣得種於中朝以來」
  - (translation) The southern-barbarian persimmon is a herb(?), sprouting in spring and fruiting in autumn, tasting like a persimmon. It came originally from the southern barbarians; recently an envoy obtained seed in China and brought it back.
  - Reading this plant as the tomato is the modern writers' identification.
- **고영, 경향신문 2020** ([page](https://www.khan.co.kr/article/202007240300035)): 「조선 사람이 17세기 즈음에 접한 토마토는 감 맛이 나는 신기한 과채였다. 하지만 본격적으로 재배되지는 않았다.」「20세기에 '프린세스 오브 웨일스' 등 품종이 들어오고, 토마토 농사도 궤도에 오른다.」 It also quotes 동아일보, 13 Sept. 1935: 「근래에 와서 우리 조선 사람도 토마토를 많이 먹게 되었습니다.」
  - (translation) To Koreans of about the 17th century it was a curious persimmon-tasting fruit, but it was not seriously cultivated. In the 20th century varieties such as Prince of Wales came in and tomato farming got under way. 1935: "Recently we Koreans too have come to eat many tomatoes."
  - He also reports Yi Gyu-gyeong (b. 1788) treating it as a weed easily confused with the chili.

**India** (no primary text read)
- **Roxburgh and Watt, via [Foodologics blog](https://foodologics.wordpress.com/2020/08/22/eggplants-from-portugal/):** "In 1832, it was recorded as being commonly used throughout the subcontinent by William Roxburgh in the book titled Flora Indica." Also, Watt 1889: "natives are beginning to appreciate the fruit, but the plant is still chiefly cultivated for the European population."
- **[Ergodicity blog 2013](https://ergodicity.net/2013/03/09/the-history-of-new-foods-in-india/), summarizing Mazumdar:** "these ingredients were only integrated around 150 years ago!" The blog's quoted passage puts tomatoes in the "first wave, in the sixteenth and seventeeth centuries".

## 2. Sources: what made crops spread fast or slow

No source here is from outside the four countries, so nothing is marked COMPARATIVE.

- **Li Xinsheng, 《中国经济史研究》 2020.3** ([text](https://news.qq.com/rain/a/20210125A054N800?id=20210125A054N800&path=a&app=news&suid=&redirect_pc=1)), maize and sweet potato, China:
  - Limits and tax: 「即使略有优势也难以打破原有的种植制度，在"钱粮二色"的赋税体系下到底只是糊口作物」 (translation) Even with some advantage they could hardly break the existing cropping system; under a tax system of coin and grain they remained subsistence crops.
  - State promotion failed in the north: 「对番薯大加劝种，但番薯未融入北方的种植制度，多是昙花一现」 (translation) Sweet potato was urged strongly but never entered the northern cropping system, and mostly vanished quickly.
  - Prohibition failed too: 「多见官方禁种玉米……因玉米暗合棚民开山的需求，屡禁不止」 (translation) Officials often banned maize, but it suited the shed people's clearing of hills, and bans failed.
  - Conclusion: 「种什么都是农民自发选择，不是国家权力所能管控的」 (translation) What to plant was the farmers' own choice, beyond state control.
  - Timing: complete in the southern mountains only 「在道光年间」 (in the Daoguang reign).
- **Li Xinsheng, 《读书》 2020** (link above), crop by crop:
  - Cuisine and fit: 「决定新作物传播的因素很多，包括它们是否有助于农业生产和适合某种农业体制，是否易于做成菜肴和被饮食体系接纳，以及能否引起文化上的共鸣」 (translation) Many factors decide spread: help to production and fit with a farming system, ease of cooking and acceptance by the food system, and cultural resonance.
  - Tobacco, fast: 「烟草在美洲作物中的传播速度是数一数二的」「可见经济作物之天然优势」 (translation) Among the fastest of the American crops, showing the natural advantage of a cash crop.
  - Pumpkin, fast: it combined food and staple functions.
  - Peanut, slow: 「产量低，木榨榨油效率低，需求量很低」 (translation) Low yield, inefficient wooden oil press, low demand. It changed with the American large peanut from 1862 and machine pressing.
  - Potato, slow: late arrival, poor heat tolerance, degeneration and late blight.
  - Chili: taken up in the southwest (Guizhou, Sichuan, Yunnan), partly as a substitute for scarce salt.
- **Crosby, *The Columbian Exchange*, ch. 5, drawing on Ho** ([Warwick PDF](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/arts/history/research/centres/foodhistory/the_columbian_exchange_biological_and_cultural_con..._----_5_new_world_foods_and_old_world_demography_.pdf)):
  - China: "adopted rapidly because it did not compete with rice and other traditional crops, but prospered in previously unutilized soils"; "its cultivation urged by official edicts".
  - Japan: "its people have never acquired a liking for maize"; the sweet potato "has proved to be nearly unfailing famine insurance".
  - His "quick" directly conflicts with Li's "late".
- **Li Hua, Shandong under Qianlong** ([text](http://agri-history.ihns.ac.cn/scholars/lihua1.htm)): 「频繁的灾荒，是山东劳动人民迅速接受番薯普遍推广的又一因素。」 (translation) Frequent disasters were a further factor in the rapid acceptance of the sweet potato. He also sets out population pressure, with land per head falling from 7.6 mu to 3.41 between 1753 and 1812.
- **Mazumdar, via Ergodicity:** "The paper draws a distinction between how land ownership practices in India and China made a difference in how fast new foods were incorporated into the common diet."
- **Tax exemption, popular claim only** ([The Paper](https://m.thepaper.cn/baijiahao_9569713)): 「清朝赋税，可以要钱，也可以要粮，却不会要番薯、玉米和土豆。」 (translation) Qing tax took coin or grain, but not sweet potato, maize or potatoes. No statute is given.
- **Japan, climate and soil:** [MAFF Kyushu](https://www.maff.go.jp/kyusyu/seibibu/kokuei/13/pdf/satumaimonodennrai.pdf): 「土壌・水利条件に恵まれない鹿児島で広く栽培されるようになりました」 (translation) It came to be widely grown in Kagoshima, poor in soil and water.

## Verdict

**ATTESTED** (part 1), with long lags between record and common food. China first recorded it in 1617/1621, grew it as an ornamental, and took it up regionally in the 1930s and nationally in the 1950s, a lag of about 310-330 years. Japan's record starts in 1668 and the tomato began settling into the diet in early Shōwa (from 1926), about 260 years later. Korea's starts in 1614 and common eating is reported in 1935, about 320 years later. India's first date is 1832 by secondhand report; in 1889 it was still chiefly grown for Europeans, and no source read fixes when it became common. Both claims under test hold. The closest counter-evidence for China is the 1835 Canton statement, plus the 1865 cherry-sized fruit and the 1900 Shanghai market price, which show presence but not commonness. **ATTESTED ONLY FOR** China (part 2): cuisine, cropping-system fit, the tax system (Li), non-competition with rice and official edicts (Crosby, on Ho), and famine and land pressure (Li Hua). Li and Crosby disagree on speed, and state promotion is disputed. For Japan I found only Crosby's paragraph and agency pages, and no historian's comparative explanation. Queries covered 年貢 and 田畑勝手作, Totman, and the Dutch at Nagasaki. **NOT ATTESTED** (part 3): no source read says the edicts stopped arrivals, and dated arrivals continue after them through Nagasaki (Chinese and Dutch ships), Tsushima and Ryukyu-Satsuma.
