## Sources that bear

**1. Kitakyushu city, 八坂神社石鳥居 (prefectural cultural property).** https://www.city.kitakyushu.lg.jp/contents/02100290.html
- **Date of evidence:** the page gives none. The beam tablet is uninscribed and the arch was moved in 1992, so this cannot count as premodern evidence.
- 「この鳥居は花崗岩製の明神型に近い鳥居で、額束は無銘である。 高さ4.51m、笠木の長さ5.87m、柱間3.61m、柱径0.47m。」
  - (translation) "A granite torii close to the Myōjin type; its beam tablet is uninscribed. Height 4.51 m, top beam (kasagi) 5.87 m long, 3.61 m between the posts, posts 0.47 m thick."
- So the top beam is about 1.63 times the post interval.
- This is a shrine at Kokura castle, not a village shrine.

**2. Kato city, 河高住吉神社 石造明神鳥居.** Archived `03/0304f5f3ba07`, https://www.city.kato.lg.jp/.../1510384600965.html
- **Date of evidence:** late Muromachi to early Edo.
- The record already cites this page (`kato-kawataka-torii`, on 0220), but only for its date. Its post interval has never been compared with the 16 ft span.
- 「柱間が2.5m、高さが2.8mを測る」
  - (translation) "It measures 2.5 m between the posts and 2.8 m high."
- 「高室石の特性から長い柱を製作出来なかったことによって、これらのずんぐりとした形状の石鳥居が生み出された」
  - (translation) "Because long posts could not be made from Takamuro stone, these stubby stone torii came about."
- So its proportions are atypical.

**3. Kokugakuin, Encyclopedia of Shinto, "Torii".** Archived `1e/1e4316f6bb18`, https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9635
- **Date of evidence:** a general description, undated. No page in the record cites it.
- "In addition to functioning as independently standing architectural elements, torii can act as integral parts of sacred fences (tamagaki) and are incorporated into the entrances of arcades encircling some shrine structures."
- The archived en.wikipedia "Torii" page (`10/10950bec3e03`) agrees: "there usually are no doors or board fences, but exceptions exist, as for example in the case of Ōmiwa Shrine's triple-arched torii".

**4. Mie prefecture, registration summary for the 中森家住宅 (Iga Ueno, a retainer's house).** https://www.pref.mie.lg.jp/common/content/000370740.pdf
- **Date of evidence:** late Edo.
- 「井戸屋形及び板塀：江戸末期、【建築面積】…井戸屋形及び板塀：１２㎡、延長７ｍ」
  - (translation) "Well house and board fence: late Edo. Building area … well house and board fence: 12 m², 7 m long."
- The 12 m² covers the well house and a 7 m fence together, so it is only an upper bound on the roof.

**5. 宮内貴久「屋敷地内に植える樹木の吉凶」, 比較民俗研究 16 (1999).** https://tsukuba.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/4566/files/4.pdf
- **Date of evidence:** 20th-century oral folklore from Oitama (one informant born 1912). This is not premodern evidence.
- 「柿は，風よけになり，実も食べられるから植える。一31一松は，たいていの家が植えている。しかし，松の葉は枯れると，茅屋根に刺さってしまって腐り，屋根が長持ちしない。そのため，屋根葺き職人は嫌い，松は植えるなと言う。」
  - (translation) "Persimmon is planted because it breaks the wind and its fruit can be eaten. Pine, most houses plant; but when its needles die they stick into a thatched roof and rot it, so the roof does not last. So the thatchers dislike it and say not to plant pine."
- Against that, from the 南陽市史 as the paper quotes it: 「○柿の木は鎮守さまが嫌う。」 (translation) "The persimmon tree is disliked by the village tutelary god."
- The paper cites 村田 on rules in early-modern 家相書 (house-siting manuals). I did not read that.

**6. ja.wikipedia 鎮守の森.** Archived `da/da963d7a7682`. It describes modern ecology, undated.
- 「近世の段階で、境内における林野資源の利用が進んでいたという報告もある…道路の拡張などによって森林の周囲が刈られたりすることがままある。その場合、マント群落やソデ群落を失った森林は林床の乾燥などを引き起こして荒廃しやすい。」
  - (translation) "There are also reports that by the early modern period the precincts' woodland resources were being used … The surroundings of a wood are often cut back, as by road widening; a wood that has lost its mantle and fringe communities is then prone to degrade, with its floor drying out."

**From the record**, these already bear:
- **0247 (eaves-drip strip):** 2 to 3 chi of drip ground in Jiaxiang (survey of custom made before 1949), a 3 chi drip in a Baoding deed of 1884, and in Ono district, Mino (custom edition of 1892), "a house in a village had to stand back about 3 ft from the boundary of a road or of cultivated land".
- **0196 (wells):** a Meiji (~1900) well house at Tokugawa-en on about 1.6 m².
- **0048 (byres):** "every arrangement found ties the beast to one keeper". Large owners placed cattle with borrowers; Sanuki hired cattle from Awa.
- **0046 (persimmon):** the persimmon "casts shade over the house in summer".
- **0077 (fuel woods):** a worked wood's shrubs were cut, and its edge reads as a grass fringe.
- **0058 (wet ground):** settlements stood on dry levees, with paddy on the back marsh.
- **0035 (stream banks):** "Nothing we read places a household's own garden, privy, yard or storehouse across a channel from its house."
- **0068 (channel widths):** field ditch about 0.3 m, lateral about 1 m, brook about 2 m (a GUESS), main canal up to 11 m, Takase canal 8 m.
- **0220 (torii):** Nagao's approach is "a little over 7 meters (4 ken)", a Kamakura mounted-archery course.

## Reading

**NOT ATTESTED — five figures or rules the evidence contradicts or points against:**
1. **Well roof, 24.75 ft across (about 57 m²).** At true size it is 5 to 35 times the roofs found: at most 12 m² late Edo, 1.6 m² Meiji. The drawing page does say the marker is drawn larger than life, but it gives no roof size.
2. **A torii is "never in a barrier".** Sources 3 and en.wikipedia say torii can be part of a sacred fence, as at Ōmiwa.
3. **Borrowers walk over to a shared shed.** In the record a borrowed beast was placed with, and kept by, the borrower.
4. **"No roof under a yard persimmon".** 0046 has the persimmon shading the house. Source 5's roof objection is about pine on thatch, not persimmon.
5. **A fringe of young trees outside the wood.** For a worked wood, 0077 points the other way. Even a shrine grove's edge was a shrub-and-herb mantle (source 6), and shrine woods were themselves worked by the early modern period.

**ATTESTED ONLY FOR the 16 ft top-beam span's order of size.**
- A castle-town arch of unknown date has a 19.3 ft beam.
- A village arch of about 1600 stands 8.2 ft between its posts. By the Kitakyushu ratio its beam would be about 13 ft, but that is this pass's arithmetic, and the Kato arch is stubby by its own page's account.

**ATTESTED for the 3 ft drip-line gap** (0247).

- a well house's size at a commoner's well
- a premodern conifer share in a wood
- trees and the byre roof
