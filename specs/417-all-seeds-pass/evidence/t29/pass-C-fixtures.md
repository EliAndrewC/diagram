- **Chita peninsula, late 16th c. onward (premodern):** 「知多半島で田畑の隅に埋められた肥甕の最も古い事例は，16 世紀末に編年される甕である」

  - (translation) "Usually it was first put into a tub called a sanjaku-dame, buried at the corner of the dry field … a sanjaku-dame is a tub about three shaku [~3 ft] in both diameter and height."
- **Dazaifu, folklore record (undated):** 「たいていの農家が，持田の隅の道つきで便利のよい場所に大きなドツボ…を掘って」

- **Yonago, folklore record (undated):** 「一時田畑の畦に作った肥溜めに入れて腐らせておき」

- **Arao:** 「田畑の脇に大きな穴を掘って」
  - (translation) "a large hole dug beside the field."

## Reading

**ATTESTED ONLY FOR the pit's size and its place beside the way. NOT ATTESTED for the margin it is held back from the field.**
- **Size:** a field pit was round and about 1 m to 3 shaku across (~3-3.5 ft). That is excavated in Kyoto in the 16th-17th c. and recorded in Gunma folklore. The jar mouth runs 1-1.5 m. The 3.5 ft size is right; the square shape is not, since every source says round.
- **Place:** pits stood along roads (excavated, Kyoto, 16th-19th c.), at the field's corner by the road (Chita, late 16th c.; Dazaifu; Gunma), or on the bund itself (Yonago). The "beside the way, not on it" rule is attested.
- **Distance:** the 8 ft set-back from the paddy edge goes against the sources, which put the pit at the field's corner or on its bund. The 3 ft clearance off a lane has no figure in any source.

**ATTESTED for "not by wealth".** Kakimochi, 1885 read back to the late Edo period: even small households mostly kept a retirement house, the cause was custom, and the largest house kept none. The rolled order of owners is the engine's own device.

**SEARCHED-SILENT for wells.**
- Spacing between wells: I searched in Japanese and Chinese, and the record had already noted it found none.
- A well's distance from the house: no source gives one.
