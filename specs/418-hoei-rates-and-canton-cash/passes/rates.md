I found a year-by-year market series for 1705-1719 that names the silver each rate is quoted in. On Canton I found only one cash rate placed there, and it is per dollar, not per tael.

The series is Kusama Naokata's 『三貨図彙』 (compiled about 1815; 1932 printing, NDL pid 1279564, internet-public). I read it as page images in the NDL viewer, because NDL's machine text for this book is refused (403). This is also coin-walk's source for 1706-1718. Its rice-price columns come from Kusama: 1714 rice is 100.9 / 126.1 / 155.2 / 201.8 in both, to the digit, and 1707 rice is 75-93 in Keicho silver and 120-150 in Hoei silver in both. Its silver-rate column comes from somewhere else: in 1711 coin-walk has 64.90-65.82 where Kusama has 70-73; in 1714 it has 76.00-89.00 where Kusama has 75 at mid-year and then 92-93 falling to 85-86. The chain is coin-walk, then 『日本史資料総覧』 (1986), then Kusama for the rice.

## Q1. Japan 1706-1718: 『三貨図彙』 物價之部 卷五-六, printed pp. 八〇三-八二三 (viewer frames 409-419)

**Sources and where each rate comes from**

- **Kusama** is the yearly series. He wrote 1705-1719 as a nearly contemporary merchant's compilation, not a daily diary.
- **"代X匁"** in his lines means the ryo of gold in monme of silver, or the kanmon of copper in monme.
- **City is my reading, not the page's.** Kusama was an Osaka money-changer of the Kōnoike house. His rice is Higo and Chikuzen rice, which is how the Osaka market quoted. In 1715 he switches explicitly to 「今年江戸乾金一兩ニ米…」 ("this year in Edo, for one ryo of Kenji gold, rice…"), which implies his other figures are not Edo's.

**Which silver chiefly passed: two passages answer it.**

- **p.804, end of 1706:**
  「右ハ元ノ字銀ニテノ相場ナリ、尤慶長銀ニテモ同様ナレドモ、慶長銀ハ次第ニ拂底ニナリ、多分元ノ字銀ニテノ價ナリ、…今年又銀增吹コレ有、是ヲ寳ノ字銀ト云、茲ニ因テ諸相場此寳永銀ヲ以テ交易ス、此頃慶長・元祿ノ金一兩代、寳永銀ニテ凡八十目前後、錢一貫文代廿五匁前後ナリ」
  (translation) "The above are rates in Genroku silver. Keicho silver gives the same, but it has gradually run short, so these are mostly prices in Genroku silver. … This year silver was again minted, called Hō-ji [Hoei] silver, and from then all rates were traded in this Hoei silver. At this time a ryo of Keicho or Genroku gold was about 80 monme in Hoei silver, and a kanmon of copper about 25 monme."
- **p.820, entered under 享保二 (1717):**
  「此時通用銀ト云ハ、永中銀・三ツ寳・四ツ寳銀、此三品ニテ賣買ノ直段ナリ、尤慶長銀・元祿銀、倶ニ五品ナリ、…私ニ其品ヲ分チ、自ラ交易穩ナラズ、尤正德六年迄ハ、諸相場皆通用銀ノ相庭ナリ、…今年十一月ヨリ、諸物ハ新金銀相場ニ相成」
  (translation) "'Current silver' (tsūyō-gin) here means Eiji, Mitsuho and Yotsuho silver; prices were made in these three. With Keicho and Genroku silver that makes five kinds. … People privately sorted them by kind, so trade was unsettled. Up to Shōtoku 6 [1716] all rates were rates in current silver. … From the eleventh month of this year goods went onto new-gold-and-silver rates."
- **p.816 (1714)** adds 「但シ永中、又ハ三ツ寳・四ツ寳銀ヲバ通用銀ト云フ」 (translation) "Eiji, Mitsuho or Yotsuho silver is called current silver."

- **The gold named changes, too.** It is Genroku gold in 1705-07, Keicho-or-Genroku gold in 1708-13, Kenji gold alongside it from 1710, and Keicho-or-new gold from 1718. By the 1714 decree a ryo of Kenji gold passed as half a ryo of Keicho or new gold, so a rate table has to say which gold it uses.
- **Split by coinage, from Kusama:** only for Keicho, Genroku, Hoei (Futatsuho), "current silver" and new silver. Eiji, Mitsuho and Yotsuho are never quoted separately on the market. They are lumped together as current silver.
- **The only three-way split is the law's.**
  - The 1714 decree, p.816-817, in silver per 1 kanme of Keicho or new silver: 「永中・三ツ寳・四ツ寳銀ハ 二貫目」 (2 kanme), Genroku 一貫六百目 (1,600 monme), Hoei 一貫三百目 (1,300 monme).
  - The 1718 decree, p.821-822, per 10 kanme of new silver: Genroku 12.5 kanme, Hoei 16, Eiji 20, Mitsuho 25, Yotsuho 40.
  - On p.822, rice at 33 monme in new silver is restated as 41.25 in Genroku, 52.8 in Hoei, 66 in Eiji, 82.5 in Mitsuho and 133 in Yotsuho silver.
- **Copper is new to the record.** Page 0287 says no copper market rate was found for 1706-1721.

**Edo rates: from Japanese Wikipedia only, each with the printed source it footnotes. I did not read those sources.**

- https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝永四ツ宝丁銀 : 「正徳4年5月（1714年6月頃）に至り江戸の銀相場は金1両=銀89匁と下落し」 (translation) "by the fifth month of Shōtoku 4 (about June 1714) Edo's silver rate had fallen to 1 ryo = 89 monme." Footnote: 『両替年代記』 (1933), pp. 285-302.
- https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/享保丁銀 : 「江戸においてそれまで乾字金1両＝銀82-3匁で推移していた相場が、享保2年（1717年）春夏には平均69匁7分5厘、翌3年5月初頭（1718年5月30日-）には新金1両＝新銀58匁3分5厘であったが同年9月に新金1両＝新銀43匁8-9分と銀が高騰する」 (translation) "in Edo the rate, which had run at 1 ryo of Kenji gold = 82-83 monme, averaged 69.75 in spring-summer 1717; at the start of the fifth month of 1718, 1 ryo of new gold = 58.35 monme of new silver, and in the ninth month 43.8-43.9, as silver rose." Footnote: 田谷 (1963), pp. 213-218 (Tanaya Hiroshi, I believe; the article gives the surname only).

**Verdict: ATTESTED ONLY FOR** a single merchant's yearly rates, most likely Osaka's (my reading), for 1705-1714, 1717, 1718 and 1719. Kusama gives no gold or copper rate for 1715 or 1716. The rates are quoted in:
- Genroku silver until 1706;
- Hoei Futatsuho silver from 1706-07 to 1713;
- current silver (the three low Hoei silvers together) for 1714-1717;
- new silver from the eleventh month of 1718.

Kusama answers "which coin chiefly passed" directly: Genroku silver, then Hoei silver, then current silver until 1716, then new coin. Separate market rates for Eiji, Mitsuho and Yotsuho are **NOT ATTESTED**; the market quoted them together.

## Q2. Canton, 1830s

**Sources**

- **Chinese Repository vol. 6, May 1837, p. 44, a letter dated "Canton 1st May, 1837."** I checked it against the page image at https://archive.org/details/chineserepositor6118unse (scan page n57).
  - Verbatim: "Lintin authorities state, that a devoted opium-smoker can easily use one *tael weight* per day, the price of which is $ 1¼, ready for use. So a person *using* two mace incurs a daily expense of $ ¼, which is a large sum for a Chinese, equal to 250 cash, the only coin in China."
  - This is a letter-writer's conversion, not a quoted exchange. It makes about 1,000 cash to the dollar at Canton in 1837.
  - At 0289's own Canton 1835 figure of about 0.71-0.72 tael a dollar, that is roughly 1,390-1,410 cash a tael. That figure is this project's arithmetic, not the source's.
- **Chinese Repository vol. 4 (1835-36), review of Thompson's *Considerations respecting the Trade with China*** (https://archive.org/details/thechineserepository18351836v4.112):
  - Verbatim: "He puts, in his table of money and bullion at Canton, '1,000 cash=1 tael or six shillings,' he might as well say as many pence, for, as a matter of course, the value of a tael weight of sycee silver must vary with the market price of that article".
  - This tells us the 1,000-a-tael figure was nominal at Canton, but it gives no market number.
- **Chinese Repository vol. 5, July 1836, p. 140: translation of Xu Naiji's memorial on legalizing opium** (in https://archive.org/details/chinesereposito10willgoog). This is empire-wide, not Canton.
  - Verbatim: "Always in times past, a tael of pure silver exchanged for nearly about 1000 coined cash, but of late years the same sum has borne the value only of 1200 or 1300 cash ; thus the price of silver rises but does not fall." The OCR has "past^"; I took it as a comma but did not check the page image.
  - "the same sum has borne the value only of 1200 or 1300 cash" is the OCR's wording as printed. It means a tael now fetched 1,200-1,300 cash.

**Verdict: ATTESTED ONLY FOR** cash per dollar at Canton: about 1,000 in May 1837, one letter-writer's conversion. No source I could read gives Canton's market rate in cash per tael.

Other places, all outside Canton:
- **Tianjin, 1832** (vol. I): "The value of the tael, here, varies from 1300 to 1400 cash."
- **Nan'ao, eastern Guangdong, 1832** (vol. II, Lindsay's voyage): "1000 cash, which is equivalent to a dollar". This is Guangdong but not Canton.
- **Fujian tea district, 1835** (vol. IV): "300 cash (720 to a dollar,)".
- **Taiwan, 1838** (vol. VI): "600 cash (about two thirds of a dollar)".
- **Shanghai** (vol. XVIII, 1849, outside the period): "from 1520 down to 1460 cash for a 'Shanghai dollar.'"
- **Vol. I (1832):** "about 800 of which are equal to a Spanish dollar" and "eight hundred or a thousand of which are given in exchange for a dollar". Neither is placed or dated.
