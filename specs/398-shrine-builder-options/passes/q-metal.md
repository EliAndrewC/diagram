# Pass - metal and furnishings (#7 Q3) (research-pass, 2026-10-19; its reply verbatim)

I found no price for any of the six objects, in Edo Japan or in Qing China. The one hard figure on the Japanese side is a price per kin of copper (1 kin ≈ 600 g, about 1.3 lb). It works only as a floor for the cost of the metal. The Chinese fallback added a per-jin price for coarse, heavy copper ware sold as goods (1 jin ≈ 597 g, the Qing equivalent of the kin), which is the nearest thing to an object price.

## Sources that bear on the question

**1. kotobank, 銅座, from the 改訂新版世界大百科事典 (author 佐々木潤之介)**: https://kotobank.jp/word/%E9%8A%85%E5%BA%A7-103544. The evidence is from 1774, at the Osaka copper office.

> 長崎輸出銅値段は74年（安永3）の記録によると定高の銅について1ピクル（100斤）当り，中国向けには銀115匁，オランダ向けには銀60匁25と定められていた。…これにたいし地売銅値段は，変動しつつも中国向け輸出銅値段の2倍ほどの水準を保っていた。

(translation) According to a record of 1774 (An'ei 3), the Nagasaki export copper price per picul (100 kin) of quota copper was fixed at 115 monme of silver for China and 60.25 monme for the Dutch. … The price of copper sold within Japan, though it fluctuated, stayed at about twice the China export price.

> なお1774年の記録によると，前掲の輸出銅値段にたいし，銅座の銅買上げ値段は100斤当りで秋田銅銀156匁52と150匁64，南部銅銀139匁48，別子立川（たつかわ）銅銀139匁48，吉岡銅銀144匁であった。

(translation) According to the 1774 record, against those export prices, the copper office paid per 100 kin: Akita copper 156.52 and 150.64 monme of silver, Nanbu copper 139.48, Besshi-Tatsukawa copper 139.48, and Yoshioka copper 144.

**2. 高岡銅像物語, 山元醸造**: https://www.yamagen-jouzou.com/murocho/aji/douzou/douzou1.html. The evidence is from 1833 (Tenpō 4), at Takaoka 関野神社. The page gives the mirrors' size and weight, but no price.

> 鏡は直径約７３センチ、重さは３４キロ、その大きさに圧倒されます。

(translation) The mirrors are about 73 cm across and weigh 34 kg; their size is overwhelming.

> 三枚の鏡にはどれも「天保四歳癸(みずのと)三月吉辰(たつ)」と刻まれていて、天保４（1833）年に作られたと知られます。

(translation) All three mirrors are inscribed "an auspicious day of the third month, Tenpō 4, year of mizunoto", which shows they were made in 1833.

> そして、神鏡一枚は御役人からの寄付によってなんとか代金をまかなうことができたが、あとの二枚分は高岡の裕福商家からの援助金をあてたいと願っているとし

(translation) The letter says the price of one sacred mirror was just barely covered by a donation from officials, and that he hoped to meet the cost of the other two with aid from Takaoka's wealthy merchant houses.

The maker was 甚右衛門, a Buddhist-altar-fittings maker from Takaoka who also had a workshop in Kyoto (as 鏡屋弥助). The alloy is copper, tin and lead. The ledger already holds a `nothing-found` line for this page from this pass. I did not change it; whether to cite the page is your call.

**3. 杨煜达, 《滇铜、汉铜与清代中期的汉口铜市场》, 清史研究 2013 no. 2**: http://iqh.ruc.edu.cn/old/qdjjsyj/jjs_ztyj/syjt/41c78389d9b24041a36cfcee20e0321b.htm. This is the China fallback. It quotes memorials to the throne from the early Qianlong reign and from 1747.

> 即今银一两换钱七百五六十文，计重五斤十一两，每钱文一斤，仅值银一钱七分，熔铜制器，即粗重者每斤可卖银二钱七八分，银一两换钱熔铜制器，便可获利银六钱

(translation) Now one tael of silver exchanges for 750-760 cash, weighing 5 jin 11 liang, so a jin of cash is worth only 0.17 tael; melted down and made into copper ware, even the coarse heavy kind sells for 0.27-0.28 tael a jin, so changing one tael into cash and casting it into ware makes a profit of 0.6 tael.

This is a memorial by 李世倬, 右通政, from the early Qianlong reign (note 62: 录副奏折03-0768-016). The article dates the passage only to 乾隆初年 (the early Qianlong years, from 1736). The neighbouring note (61) is a memorial of 乾隆八年 (1743), but I can't confirm that 李世倬's memorial is from the same year.

> 今已与商人议定，每年供鼓铸四十万斤，每百斤价银十九两，较前已减价二两

(translation) It has now been agreed with the merchants that they supply 400,000 jin a year for coin casting, at 19 taels of silver per 100 jin, 2 taels less than before.

This is the 1747 memorial (乾隆十二年) by 塞楞额, Governor-General of Huguang, on the market for traders' copper at Hankou.

## Verdict for each object

- **Sacred mirror (神鏡): SILENT.** No price was found. The only figure is my own estimate of the metal, not a sourced price, worked out as follows:
  - The Takaoka mirror weighs 34 kg, which is about 57 kin at 600 g.
  - At the 1774 copper-office purchase prices of 139.48-156.52 monme per 100 kin, its metal comes to about 79-89 monme.
  - At the domestic price, which the dictionary gives only as "about twice" the China export price of 115 (so about 230 monme per 100 kin, my arithmetic), it comes to about 130 monme.
  - Set against the record's Kyoto laborer wage of 0.92 monme a day (1802-04, from 0250), that is about 86-141 laborer-days of metal.
  - Against a carpenter at 4.7 times that wage (Osaka, 1802-04), about 4.3 monme a day, it is about 18-30 carpenter-days.
  - There are gaps: about 30 years between the copper price and the wage, and a further 30 between the wage and the 1833 mirror; Osaka and Kyoto versus Takaoka; and the metal is a copper-tin-lead alloy, not pure copper.
  - Casting and polishing labor would be added on top. The letter's need for an official's gift for one mirror and merchant subscriptions for the other two suggests the cost was far above this metal floor.
- **Hanging gong (鰐口): SILENT.** This agrees with 0258's "no price for bell and rope". The Chinese search for temple-bell casting records that state the silver spent (铸钟碑记 用银) also found nothing.
- **Hanging lanterns (釣灯籠): SILENT.** The modern Kasuga Taisha donation figure (about 2 million yen for a hanging lantern) is current practice, not premodern, so I have not used it.
- **Small gilt-bronze reliquary (舎利容器): SILENT.**
- **Offering chest with a lock (賽銭箱 / 錠前): SILENT** for both the box and the lock.
- **Lacquered chest for remains: SILENT.** The 1765 Harimaya Nakai funeral account totals 23 ryō 2 bu and lists a 棺掛け (coffin cover) line, but the web excerpt leaves out every line amount, and no coffin or box price is given.
- **Copper as a materials floor: ATTESTED** for raw copper, Osaka 1774 (source 1). Paired with the Kyoto laborer's 0.91 monme (1745-54), the copper office's purchase price comes to about 1.5-1.7 laborer-days per kin; the gap is about 25 years, and Osaka against Kyoto.
- **China: ATTESTED ONLY FOR** coarse, heavy copper ware sold by weight, at 0.27-0.28 tael per jin in the early Qianlong years (source 3, place not given), and raw copper at Hankou in 1747, 19 taels per 100 jin. None of these is an object like those asked about. Against the record's Qing wages in 0250, one jin of coarse copper ware was about 6 days at the 0.0456-tael Yangzi baseline (1700), or about 3 days at Canton's 0.08-0.1 tael (18th century).
