## 0267 Did English traveling players gather their money at the door or pass a hat? (comparative-money)

- Gathering at the door - ATTESTED ONLY FOR the door/gate: a touring company (the Queen's Men) in the Red Lion inn yard,
  Norwich, 15 June 1583: a playgoer refused to pay until inside "while the players were in playeng" and spilt the money
  out of the gatekeeper's hand (Brown's and Knee's depositions, REED Norwich 72-73) (`lostplays-norwich-affray-1583`).
  That the gatekeeper was still taking money after the play began is labeled the page's reading of the two depositions.
- London's standing theaters, 1599 (Platter): 1 penny to stand, a 2nd penny at a further door to sit, a 3rd at another
  door for a cushion (`enwiki-groundling`; Wikipedia's uncredited English translation). Gatherers with sealed
  earthenware money boxes at the entrances (hedged, "would have been stationed"), and Jonson 1614 "drop but sixpence at
  the door" (`sbt-tudor-money-pot`). What went round mid-play was food and drink, at the buyer's cost (Platter).
- Passing a hat before the end - NOT ATTESTED: absence note `english-players-hat-absence` (q-plays pass 2026-10-18 plus
  two web searches 2026-10-19 for gatherers mid-performance and "pass the hat"). Lawrence 1912's "secondary 'gathering'
  in the galleries" is labeled a historian's reading, footnote 7, not a document of the period, silent on when
  (`lawrence-1912-inn-yards`).
- English day wage beside it - FOUND, with limits (the brief allowed for this): Internet Shakespeare Editions (UVic)
  gives a skilled city journeyman up to 1 shilling a day, more usually 6d, with food and drink; thatchers and hedgers
  about 6d; threshers and reapers about 3d (farm workers, not Londoners); Henslowe's actors 1s; the pit 1d, galleries
  2d; 12d to the shilling (`ise-elizabethan-cost-of-living`). The page names no source and no date beyond
  "Shakespeare's money". The penny as a sixth of a journeyman's usual day and a third of a thresher's is labeled this
  page's arithmetic.

## 0190 Notice boards (kosatsuba) - extended (research page only; the drawing page unchanged)

- What an official notice was made of - ATTESTED ONLY FOR Edo: plaques of cypress, plum or similar wood, the law written
  in ink, hung high (`kotobank-kosatsu`, Heibonsha); title, text, date and issuer in ink on wood (`kosatsu-jawiki`, an
  existing note); no one could re-ink a board without permission, and the shogunate and domains kept board keepers,
  kosatsu-ban (`kosatsu-jawiki`).
- Who could post - ATTESTED ONLY FOR Edo: the shogunate's kogi kosatsu beside the jibun kosatsu of daimyo and hatamoto
  (`kotobank-kosatsu`, Heibonsha and Nipponica); "some were for private matters", with no word on by whom (Nihon
  Kokugo Daijiten). Pasted paper: ATTESTED ONLY FOR the one case, the Ichikokubashi lost-child stone of 1857, which
  Nishigashi-cho's house managers (ienushi) petitioned the South Magistrate to set up and were permitted; parents and
  the ward keeping a child pasted papers in recesses about 30.5 x 22.5 cm (`chuo-ichikokubashi-maigo-sekihyo`). The
  brief's "only on structures the magistrate permitted" is written as that one case; whether paper could go on an
  official board or anywhere without leave is SILENT (`pasted-paper-absence`).
- The village case - SILENT: `village-notice-absence` (what a village notice was made of, who besides the lord could post).
- Fixes the source-readers asked for, applied to existing write-ups: `kotobank-kosatsu` "guarded" -> "strictly kept";
  `kosatsu-jawiki` "price schedules" -> carriage-fee schedules, the siting list widened to the page's own, Nihonbashi
  "first of the six" not "principal", court AND prefectural-office gates, the hedge on the six only.
