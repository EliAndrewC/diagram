## R1. Does the country monk live at the shrine? (the GM's first question)

**Finding: yes, and it is the historically ACCURATE form, not a liberty.** Three bodies of evidence
agree once the setting's fusion of the Fortunes and Shinsei is read as what it is - the pre-1868
Japanese fusion of the kami and the Buddhas.

- **Japan, the village Buddhist temple (檀那寺, the parish temple every household was registered
  with).** The priest LIVED there: the kuri (庫裏) is "the living place of temple priests"
  (ja.wikipedia 庫裏), and the one designated kuri whose form its record describes "has a form resembling the
  honmune-zukuri farmhouse characteristic of this region" (bunka.go.jp, Tokuun-ji). (A first reader
  attributed a sentence about raised floors and vernacular houses to en.wikipedia's Kuri page; a
  second reading found it is not on the page, which says only that the kuri was in origin the
  abbot's kitchen - the form claim rests on Tokuun-ji.) A late-Edo kuri (Chōnen-ji, Kawasaki)
  held the priest's living quarters, a kitchen with hearth, a ten-mat reception room, a tea room and
  a study. The limit: some rural temples were 無住, unstaffed, with a distant priest commuting
  (ja.wikipedia 住職).
- **Japan, the village Shinto shrine (鎮守 / 氏神).** Nobody lived at a pure Shinto village shrine:
  "small shrines often had no dedicated priest, so parishioners took turns managing them" (ja.wikipedia
  神社); "Most village shrines during this period were too poor to have a dedicated priest, and so
  rituals were conducted by laypeople" (en.wikipedia Kannushi); the miyaza's members served as priest
  in annual rotation (ja.wikipedia 宮座); a hereditary shake family "often built their houses near the
  shrine" - near, in houses of their own (ja.wikipedia 社家). Small shrines had no office building at
  all; business was done at the administrator's private home (JAANUS, shamusho).
- **Japan, the FUSED form, which is the setting's.** Under shinbutsu shūgō a village shrine commonly
  had a jingūji or bettō-ji attached, "the temple where the bettō resided" (ja.wikipedia 別当寺), whose
  head "was tellingly called shasō or 'shrine Buddhist monk', and was both a shrine priest and a
  Buddhist monk" (en.wikipedia Jingū-ji) and who "performed the shrine's rites in Buddhist form - such
  as sutra-reading before the kami". The 1868 separation edict "ordered the defrocking of shasō and
  bettō (shrine monks performing Buddhist rites at Shinto shrines)" and told them to "become 'shrine
  priests' (kannushi) and return to their shrines" (en.wikipedia Shinbutsu bunri); an estimated 30,000
  Buddhist structures were demolished 1868-1874. So before 1868 the ordinary arrangement at a village's
  sacred site WAS a monk living in a temple building on or beside the shrine's precinct, serving the
  villagers' rites - which is the GM's country monk exactly.
- **China (the setting's first model).** Mixed. Qing village temples in Mianning "engaged monks and
  Daoist priests to reside and guard [the temple], responsible for watching over property, cleaning,
  and managing the temple's incense affairs" under elected lay society heads (Liu, 清代冕宁的村庙组织);
  the earth-god shrine proper is a few square meters and nobody lives in it (zh.wikipedia 土地庙);
  popular temples "are established and administered by local managers, village communities, lineage
  congregations and worship associations" (en.wikipedia Temple (Chinese)).
- **The RPG's own source material.** "There was no village without some manner of shrine"; "Shrine
  Keepers ... were also charged with a shrine's protection" and "Priests were the shrine's
  administrators and primary caretakers" (l5r.fandom Seidō, summarizing Emerald Empire p. 143); of
  temples, "most had at least one resident monk", the smallest relying on visiting clergy (l5r.fandom
  Shinden, summarizing Emerald Empire pp. 176-178). The wiki paraphrases the books; it is cited as
  what it is, a public tertiary summary.

**What it decides.** The country shrine is drawn as the fused site: a sanctuary, and a hall that is the
villagers' rite-place and the monk's home in one building by default (the GM's words: "the shrine is
both their home and the place where the villagers come"; the form is attested at Kaie-ji, R3, and called
rare there - the ordinary parish temple kept a separate kuri, which is the knob's other value). The GM's preference and history coincide, so
the residence is labeled ACCURATE (Japan, jingūji/bettō-ji; the parish temple's kuri) with the
disclosure that a pure Shinto village shrine had no resident and that some rural temples stood
unstaffed. No knob is needed for residence: the setting's canon fixes it and the record supports it.

## R4. Where the record is silent

- No readable page gives a village shrine's or temple's precinct area in Japan; the one compound
  figure is Chinese (2.5 mu). The precinct size on the sheet is therefore a GUESS, bounded by what it
  must contain.
- No page gives a headcount for an ordinary village temple. The GM's canon (one monk, a few
  part-time acolytes on loan from village families) stands as canon.
- Whether an Edo village priest farmed his own tax-free land or had it worked by tenants: not found.
- The "3 by 3 ken village hall" figure that search results repeat could not be found on a page and is
  NOT used.
- Baidu Baike (庙祝, 村庙, 里社) refuses fetches; the Sinica and Palace Museum PDFs on Ming temple-land
  exemption would not read. The Chinese tax-exemption claim is therefore not cited.
