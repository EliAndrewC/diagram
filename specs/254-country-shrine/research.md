# Feature 254 - the research pass on the country shrine

*What was searched, what was found, and what it decides. The passages are quoted in the readers'
reports (five background readers, all Sonnet, 2026-09-19; the verbatim passages with their URLs are
transcribed in the session's scratchpad and will be footnoted on the record's citations page when the
entry lands, task T-record). Nothing below is a rule yet: the rules are in `spec.md`, each pointing
back to a finding here.*

## R1. Does the country monk live at the shrine? (the GM's first question)

**Finding: yes, and it is the historically ACCURATE form, not a liberty.** Three bodies of evidence
agree once the setting's fusion of the Fortunes and Shinsei is read as what it is - the pre-1868
Japanese fusion of the kami and the Buddhas.

- **Japan, the village Buddhist temple (檀那寺, the parish temple every household was registered
  with).** The priest LIVED there: the kuri (庫裏) is "the living place of temple priests"
  (ja.wikipedia 庫裏), and the surviving kuri are "a suite of raised-floor living rooms" with "an
  earthen floored entry and service area" that "have many features in common with traditional
  vernacular houses of the same period" (en.wikipedia Kuri). A late-Edo kuri (Chōnen-ji, Kawasaki)
  held the priest's living quarters, a kitchen with hearth, a ten-mat reception room, a tea room and
  a study. The limit: some rural temples were 無住, unstaffed, with a distant priest commuting
  (ja.wikipedia 住職).
- **Japan, the village Shinto shrine (鎮守 / 氏神).** Nobody lived at a pure Shinto village shrine:
  "small shrines often had no dedicated priest, so parishioners took turns managing them" (ja.wikipedia
  神社); "Most village shrines during this period were too poor to have a dedicated priest, and so
  rituals were conducted by laypeople" (en.wikipedia Kannushi); the miyaza's members served as priest
  in annual rotation (ja.wikipedia 宮座); a hereditary shake family "often built their houses near the
  shrine" - near, in houses of their own (ja.wikipedia 社家). Small shrines had no office building at
  all; business was done at the administrator's private home (JAANUS, shamusho).
- **Japan, the FUSED form, which is the setting's.** Under shinbutsu shūgō a village shrine commonly
  had a jingūji or bettō-ji attached, "the temple where the bettō resided" (ja.wikipedia 別当寺), whose
  head "was tellingly called shasō or 'shrine Buddhist monk', and was both a shrine priest and a
  Buddhist monk" (en.wikipedia Jingū-ji) and who "performed the shrine's rites in Buddhist form - such
  as sutra-reading before the kami". The 1868 separation edict "ordered the defrocking of shasō and
  bettō (shrine monks performing Buddhist rites at Shinto shrines)" and told them to "become 'shrine
  priests' (kannushi) and return to their shrines" (en.wikipedia Shinbutsu bunri); an estimated 30,000
  Buddhist structures were demolished 1868-1874. So before 1868 the ordinary arrangement at a village's
  sacred site WAS a monk living in a temple building on or beside the shrine's precinct, serving the
  villagers' rites - which is the GM's country monk exactly.
- **China (the setting's first model).** Mixed. Qing village temples in Mianning "engaged monks and
  Daoist priests to reside and guard [the temple], responsible for watching over property, cleaning,
  and managing the temple's incense affairs" under elected lay society heads (Liu, 清代冕宁的村庙组织);
  the earth-god shrine proper is a few square meters and nobody lives in it (zh.wikipedia 土地庙);
  popular temples "are established and administered by local managers, village communities, lineage
  congregations and worship associations" (en.wikipedia Temple (Chinese)).
- **The RPG's own source material.** "There was no village without some manner of shrine"; "Shrine
  Keepers ... were also charged with a shrine's protection" and "Priests were the shrine's
  administrators and primary caretakers" (l5r.fandom Seidō, summarizing Emerald Empire p. 143); of
  temples, "most had at least one resident monk", the smallest relying on visiting clergy (l5r.fandom
  Shinden, summarizing Emerald Empire pp. 176-178). The wiki paraphrases the books; it is cited as
  what it is, a public tertiary summary.

**What it decides.** The country shrine is drawn as the fused site: a sanctuary and a hall for the
villagers, and the monk's dwelling on the same precinct. The GM's preference and history coincide, so
the residence is labeled ACCURATE (Japan, jingūji/bettō-ji; the parish temple's kuri) with the
disclosure that a pure Shinto village shrine had no resident and that some rural temples stood
unstaffed. No knob is needed for residence: the setting's canon fixes it and the record supports it.

## R2. What is in one? (the program)

From the sources, the parts of a small rural sacred site, Japan-first:

| part | evidence | keep? |
|---|---|---|
| a sanctuary (honden) where the deity's object is kept | the ikkensha nagare-zukuri is "the most common honden style nationwide" and its front is ONE bay (ja.wikipedia 流造); a measured Kansei-era village honden is 1.98 by 1.82 m (Ehime pref., 1789) | yes - tiny, at the back, on the axis |
| a hall where the villagers gather (haiden / hondō) | the same site's haiden is 9.78 by 10.62 m (1790); the minimal temple "generally has only a main hall and a bell tower" (homemate, 七堂伽藍) | yes - the compound's largest building |
| the monk's dwelling (kuri) | farmhouse-like in plan (en.wikipedia Kuri); Chōnen-ji's is 9 by 5 ken; Kaie-ji (Sakai) has hall and kuri in ONE building, 25.2 m long, called rare | yes - a farmhouse-class house, attached to or beside the hall |
| an arch on the approach (torii) and the approach (sandō) | "The area within the torii is regarded as the sacred precinct" (ja.wikipedia 神社); the map convention already fronts every shrine with an arch | yes |
| a purification basin / well (temizuya) | "near the sandō stand the temizuya, for purifying the body" (ja.wikipedia 神社) | yes - the well the village maps already give a shrine |
| a bell tower (shōrō) | the minimal temple's second building (homemate) | knob - present or absent |
| a graveyard | the village maps already put the burial ground in the shrine's yard (religion-and-death) | yes, beside the precinct |
| a grove (chinju no mori) | recorded already (chinju-no-mori-jawiki) | yes - the precinct sits in its wood |
| a fence, not a wall (tamagaki), lanterns, guardian figures | listed among shrine features (ja.wikipedia 神社) | fence yes; figures optional |
| an office building (shamusho) | NOT at small shrines (JAANUS) | no |
| the tax-free plot | 除地 covers "shrine/temple precincts and fields or residences holding a document of tax exemption" (ja.wikipedia 除地); 朱印地 ran "as little as 1 koku" (ja.wikipedia 朱印地); Chinese temple land came by gift, "some donated fields and land outright, some partly purchased and partly donated" (Liu) | the fields lie OUTSIDE the sheet; the sheet shows the kitchen garden and the yard |
| the registers | temples "prepared registers equivalent to today's household registers" and issued the travel certificate (ja.wikipedia 寺請制度), while the ledger itself was compiled "town/village by town/village, by village headmen" (ja.wikipedia 宗門人別改帳); China: the baojia, never the temple | a writing room / record chest in the dwelling; nothing separate |

## R3. How big is it, and is it bigger than a farmhouse? (the GM's second question)

The village maps draw a farmhouse at 46 by 28 ft (about 120 sq m) and the village shrine as one hall
of 60 by 48 ft (about 275 sq m), a band the record labels "this project's own calibration".

What the sources give, in feet (3.28 ft per m; a ken is 6 shaku, about 5.97 ft):

| building | measured example | ft | sq m |
|---|---|---|---|
| honden, village, 1789 (Ehime) | 1.98 by 1.82 m | 6.5 by 6 | 3.6 |
| haiden, same site, 1790 | 9.78 by 10.62 m | 32 by 35 | 104 |
| kuri, late-Edo parish temple (Chōnen-ji, Kawasaki) | 9 by 5 ken | 54 by 30 | 149 |
| hondō, same temple, 1824 | 57.3 by 55.5 shaku | 57 by 55 | 292 |
| hall + kuri in one building, early Edo (Kaie-ji, Sakai) | 25.2 by 8.0-13.0 m | 83 by 26-43 | 200-330 |
| hondō, a typical early-modern Sōtō temple (Daiō-ji) | 25.5 by 16.3 m | 84 by 53 | 416 |
| earth-god hall, a large one (Xietang) | 10 by 7 m, three bays | 33 by 23 | 70 |
| earth-god shrine, ordinary (zh.wikipedia) | "a few square meters ... to over ten" | - | 3-15 |
| village temple compound, Zhejiang (Gantang) | 2.5 mu | - | ~1,670 |

Two things follow. (1) **The precinct is larger than a farmhouse, because it contains one**: the
dwelling alone is farmhouse-class (Chōnen-ji's kuri is a rich temple's; a village kuri is a farmhouse),
and the hall stands beside it. (2) **The hall the village maps draw is not wrong in area** - 275 sq m
sits inside the band of a village hall-and-dwelling under one roof (Kaie-ji, 200-330 sq m) - but it
is drawn as one building where a precinct has two or three, and the maps draw no dwelling for the
monk. That is a Mode B question this feature records and does not fix (the GM's request is a Mode A
type; the village tier is frozen hand-authored work).

## R4. Where the record is silent

- No readable page gives a village shrine's or temple's precinct area in Japan; the one compound
  figure is Chinese (2.5 mu). The precinct size on the sheet is therefore a GUESS, bounded by what it
  must contain.
- No page gives a headcount for an ordinary village temple. The GM's canon (one monk, a few
  part-time acolytes on loan from village families) stands as canon.
- Whether an Edo village priest farmed his own tax-free land or had it worked by tenants: not found.
- The "3 by 3 ken village hall" figure that search results repeat could not be found on a page and is
  NOT used.
- Baidu Baike (庙祝, 村庙, 里社) refuses fetches; the Sinica and Palace Museum PDFs on Ming temple-land
  exemption would not read. The Chinese tax-exemption claim is therefore not cited.
