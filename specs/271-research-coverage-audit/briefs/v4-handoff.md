## Items

A37 KNOB - water/400: a ditch was split between villages mostly by rule and rotation (bansui: channel widths and gate hours fixed, strictest in drought), and four works are attested at a split: a mouth cut or sandbagged to an agreed width, a graduated stake (bunboku), a timber proportional flume (Kuji, mid-Edo), and in China counted openings between iron pillars; the round divider is 1914 and after - a fork inside one settlement's fields draws nothing but the fork (as now); where a map shows a ditch splitting between two settlements, roll an open fork (likelier) or a timber division flume about ditch-wide and ~6 ft long (the flume's size and the proportion are guesses); never draw a round divider.

A46 ACCURATE (count SILENT) - water/410: Japanese paddies relied little on lifting - the chain pump (two treaders, Kinki, early Edo) gave way from the 1660s to the one-person treadle wheel, a CARRIED tool (4.5-5.5 shaku, lift about a yard, 2-3 in series) that spread only in reclaimed lowlands and new-field districts, and the two-person swing bucket where there was no wheel; China lifted far more (chain pump 1-2 m, 5 mu a person-day, twice by ox; the river noria) - no lifting device on gravity-fed comb fields (unchanged); where fields stand ABOVE their water (a polder, a low terrace) roll: nothing (the carried wheel) or one fixed chain pump (about 10-21 ft) per feeding ditch at its lip, or a noria beside a river with current (proportion and count are guesses). How many a village owned is silent (absence note).

A47 B62 KNOB - water/420: power water mills in Japan date from the Edo period and grew from the mid-Edo on brewing and castle-town demand, mainly for rice pounding (10 mortars and a millstone in one 1903 mill), owned mostly by wealthy farmers and village officials, 33 on the whole Musashino plateau in 1788 under a permit regime, 60,000 nationwide by 1897; in China a household of eight or more pounded with a foot pestle, and the water pestle (2-3 mortars on a small stream, up to 10 on a large) belonged to hill people by rivers, flour milled by ox, donkey, hand or water mill; a town's rice was polished in its retailers' shops (Edo 1744: some 5,500 mortars) - a village or hamlet whose stream has fall rolls whether it has one water mill (none is the other form: household pounding, nothing drawn); if it does, one mill-house (about 15 x 20 ft, a guess) on the bank below the intake with its wheel in a short race, near the headman's house (seat, race and size guesses); a town draws no mill of its own.

A48 B117 KNOB - water/430: washing was done at running water - a shared footing or steps on the stream or ditch bank (kawado on a river, araiba on a channel; Gujo, Hirosaki, a Saga village's channels with washing rules), a board slotted into a channel to pond water for washing, or a place at each house on a channel (Harie's kabata; a landing at every Jiangnan riverside door); a castle town could run river-fed channels through its streets (Gujo 1652; such systems doubled as irrigation and fed moats), or pipe water to take-wells (Omihachiman) - where a stream or ditch passes the houses, roll a shared washing footing (about 4 ft wide, one per few households: guesses) or, where a channel runs past the houses, a step at each house; a town rolls wells alone or street channels from its river with washing places along them (piped water draws as wells). Counts and widths silent (absence note).

A49 KNOB - water/440: a river-plain village sat on its natural levee (first answer, not a knob); the ring dike (waju) is late (open-downstream levees first, complete rings from the 17th century; continuous river levees only from Meiji) and regional to the lowest plains, and a ringed village drew water from wells since an intake through the dike invited a breach; the better-off farmstead raised its plot and built a mizuya (storehouse, sometimes habitable) on a stone-faced mound some tens of cm to 3 m above the house, northwest of it, with a horse stone and a boat under the eaves; everyone else used a communal refuge mound or a shrine on raised ground; China's Taihu plain was polder country (channels every 5-10 li, linked polders in the Song) - seat a flood-plain village's houses on the natural levee; on the lowest plains roll a ring dike round village and fields, and a ringed village gets wells, no intake through the dike; the headman's and one or two more farmsteads (share a guess) carry a mizuya on a stone-faced mound 1-10 ft high to the northwest of the house; a village short of them has one communal refuge mound or its shrine on raised ground.
