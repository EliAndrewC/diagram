## Items

- A82 KNOB - ox against horse is regional: the 1886 army inventory (and the 1877 survey) put plow cattle in western Japan west of Shiga and Mie, plow horses in the east from Niigata, Nagano and Yamanashi, and both in Kyushu and Shikoku; Buck's 1920s survey puts oxen and (more) water buffaloes in China's rice country, horses and mules in the north; the GM's canon adds daimyo surplus horses handed to farms through the headman - the byre glyph is unchanged (homesteads/070 now cites this and points at 460); the kinds' modals should name the beast by the settlement's roll (horse-leaning for an eastern-model village, ox for a western one, mixed for the south, water buffalo or ox for a south-China hamlet), and which part of the setting stands for which region of Japan is recorded as this project's decision. The share of households keeping a beast is 269 B16's (homesteads 300), not restated.
- A83 ACCURATE (share) / SILENT (placement) - Buck: hogs on nearly two-thirds of east-central (rice-country) farms against a third in the north; ducks fewer, commoner in the rice country, fed on the reaped fields and herded, not penned; the only readable placement is the Hakka weilongwu's pigsties in its rear ring - the generator should draw a pig sty on plain paddy hamlets too (about two households in three, a band rolled per hamlet), small, on the household's plot against its buildings (placement a labeled GUESS), and no yard duck pen on a paddy hamlet.
- A95 KNOB - a farm plot was usually enclosed (Britannica: a hedge or an earthen wall, some a ditch); the grove on the north and west was also the boundary (kune = the line between holdings; igune divides inside from outside); westward the plot was more often closed in by its own outbuildings than by a grove (Nipponica); the Izumo pines stood on a flood bank that once ringed the house; Okinawan houses were walled in coral and limestone - the generator should roll per settlement among a hedge line along the plot (thin green, broken for the way in), the north-and-west grove (vegetation 270 / homesteads 046), outbuildings set around the yard (the western form), and an earth bank or stone wall where the map stands for a flood plain or a storm coast; line widths are a map drawing convention; an open plot is a GUESS kept rare.
- A148 KNOB - Tiangong kaiwu (1637): half of rice threshed on a floor with an ox dragging a stone roller, southern households with large plantings mostly so, and in a wet harvest in a tub in the field; a shared floor is attested in two regional forms (the Hakka weilongwu's drying floor between pond and house, serving a dozen to a hundred households; the Cantonese comb village's front lane widened into a drying ground), and a household's own floor in a Hunan memoir - a south-China paddy hamlet should roll between each household's own floor before its house and one shared floor at the village front by its pond or hall (little or no yard at each house); a shared floor's size is SILENT (only the Hakka proportion: as wide as the main house is tall) and stays a labeled GUESS scaled to the households served.
- A17 KNOB - Nipponica sets four drying methods by region (on the paddy floor, a little everywhere; heaps, Inland Sea to northern Kyushu, Chubu and parts of Tohoku; stakes along the bunds 1-3 m tall, Kinki eastward and above all Tohoku; racks on poles in rainy regions); racks mostly on the reaped fields, multi-tier racks sometimes built around the dwelling; Niigata planted alders on the bunds as posts; a single-tier rack ran up to 100 m; roadside rows of drying trees date from 1940s consolidation - no summer map changes; a seasonal map rolls the method per settlement and draws racks as lines on reaped paddies and bunds up to ~330 ft, some in yards where tall racks are rolled, never a roadside tree row; racks per household is SILENT (a labeled GUESS).
