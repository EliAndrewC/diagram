## Items

- B78 ACCURATE - A post town's serving women (meshimori) were unlicensed prostitutes. The shogunate half-tolerated them as servants of the inns and capped their number (1772: 150-500 at four Edo post towns); they lived and worked in the inns, not in a house of their own (580). - For towns: nothing new is drawn, and the inns already on the map are the feature.
- C138 D151 DEVIATION (from canon) plus a recorded KNOB - Only the three great cities and twenty-odd other places had a licensed, walled quarter (yukaku), and most pleasure districts grew up unenclosed. The three great quarters took three forms: inside the town with a fence on one side (Osaka), at the edge with an earthen wall (Kyoto), or cut off beyond the town by a wall and ditch with one gate (Edo). Each stood on new ground at the edge and was pushed outward as the city grew. The Yoshiwara measured 720 ft square, then 840 ft by the smaller of its page's two figures. Song cities had open entertainment precincts (wazi), not brothel quarters. The setting's notes put prostitution in geisha houses and call it illegal, so the record decides that no map draws a walled quarter (590). - For capitals and provincial cities: no walled pleasure quarter. The capital's entertainment district stays teahouses, geisha houses and playhouses among the townspeople's streets. The three-form knob (720-840 ft square, on new ground at the edge) is recorded for use only if the GM rules that a capital licenses a quarter.
- C108 ACCURATE - Castle towns, provincial ones included, settled their merchants and artisans by trade into named wards: smiths, carpenters, dyers, drapers, oil sellers. Song Hangzhou had trade rows and markets named for where they stood, beside a main street of mixed shops (600). - For provincial cities and capitals: lay out part of the townspeople's ground as single-trade wards, each a block of that trade's shop-houses, and keep the main street mixed. The number of wards and which trades beyond the five named are a guess. Towns below a city draw none.
- C134 KNOB - The Song ordered a poorhouse (juyangyuan) and an infirmary (anjifang) in every county and every town of 1,000+ households, and its capital fed 300 a day in four almshouses. Japan had dispensaries and almshouses only at the ancient capital, and in the Edo period one infirmary in the shogun's city: a shingled longhouse of 40 beds inside the herb garden. Its pharmacies were shops (610). - For provincial cities, capitals and county seats: roll per city between the Song form (a walled yard with two longhouses, a county seat included) and the Japanese form (a single longhouse in the ruler's herb garden, capital only, none below). The footprint of about 25 x 110 ft per longhouse is a GUESS reckoned from 40 beds, and no floor area was found. The spec paragraph is on 610.
- C137 ACCURATE (size, count) plus a KNOB (form) - The Kanamaruza (1835, modeled on an Osaka theater by one account) is about 80 x 145 ft. Edo licensed three or four playhouses and Osaka five, and they stood together on one theater street; Edo's were moved in 1842 to one site of over 10,000 tsubo, far outside the moat. A Song precinct could hold 50+ halls. Roofed playhouses stood only in the three great cities before Meiji (620). - For capitals: 3-5 roofed playhouses, the count rolled, each about 80 x 145 ft. The form is rolled per capital between side by side on one theater street (the Japanese form) and halls inside the entertainment precincts (the Song form). Provincial cities and towns keep no roofed playhouse, only a temporary hut or a shrine stage.
