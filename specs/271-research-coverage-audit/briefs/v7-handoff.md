## Items

- A74 D95 KNOB - homesteads/520: a headman could build a gate and a formal entrance step and the nagayamon was reserved to village officials (a right, not a duty); the house was the village office; the surviving headmen's farms hold one or two storehouses, a nagayamon and in one case an earthen wall on 2,500-2,800 m2 plots, five to nine times a Nanbu farmer's 3-5 se; the canon gives the headman's household only "slightly larger" - the maps' no-gate, no-wall headman (GM ruling 2026-07-21, homesteads/110) is now recorded as a deliberate deviation from this finding until the GM rules on the gate/wall knob; one storehouse stays right (a second is attested); the plot size carried from the survivors is a labeled guess.
- A75 SILENT - homesteads/530: the office was usually held by an old or founding family, and in the setting the headsman lives in the district's main village, never a hamlet; where in the village the house stood no page says (absence note) - no generator change; siting stays a guess, the oldest cluster the page's reading.
- A76 D97 KNOB - homesteads/540: the landless (mizunomi) and dependents (nago, kadoya) often lived in huts and gate-huts inside a larger farm's enclosure (Sukumo: 120 plot-holders of 360 cultivators), while Nanbu sent new branch houses to the field edge and hill foot; the canon makes most farmers tenants, so a tenant's house is an ordinary house; sizes point to homesteads/400 (many under 10 tsubo) - the generator could draw some households as a lived-in hut inside another farm's plot and some as a small house at the settlement's edge; the split is a guess.
- D96 ACCURATE - homesteads/550: the gōnō were village landlords from the mid-18th century, often the headman's own family; their homesteads are gated, walled compounds with an entrance wing, a kitchen wing and two or more storehouses (Yamada 840 tsubo, 8 structures; Kashiwakura kin houses ringed by ~1,000 m of black walls); the canon makes most landlords merchants - draw a rich farm as the headman's homestead at the top of 520's range rather than as a second compound; how often a village has one is a guess.
- A146 KNOB - homesteads/560: the village met most often at the headman's house, else a temple, shrine or nenbutsu hall or the festival host's house, and in the Kinki region at a kaisho inside the tutelary shrine's precinct; household heads, usually twice a year; in south China the ancestral hall - only the kaisho form adds a building (in the shrine precinct); its size is an absence note and a guess.
- A147 KNOB - homesteads/570 (pointer added to 180): in Cantonese comb-plan villages the ancestral halls stand in the front row next to the pond, facing it, with the drying floor between; the weilongwu puts pond, drying floor and gate on one axis; some villages have no hall (three layouts: one central hall, a front row of halls, none); the common hall is three bays wide and one line three halls deep, a rear hall 8.5-9 m deep; the whole footprint is an absence note - the generator should draw a south-China village's hall (when rolled) in the front row facing the half-moon pond, door to the water, drying floor left open between, footprint a labeled guess.
