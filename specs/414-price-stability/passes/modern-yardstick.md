**Search pass, feature 414 (diagram#15), the modern yardstick: report**

The GM expected premodern prices to be steadier than today's. That holds for the long-run trend: prices drifted slowly, a few tenths of a percent a year against roughly 2-4% a year since 1971. It does not hold year to year: premodern prices swung several times more than a modern price level does. Every European figure below is **COMPARATIVE**: it gives a yardstick, never a Rokugani price.

I could not reach the BLS, FRED or Statistics Bureau download files from this host (busy page, timeouts, 404s). I read the same series from the DBnomics mirror instead. Wherever a number below is my own calculation from a series, it is marked **(our arithmetic)**. My volatility measure throughout is the standard deviation of annual log changes, the same measure Jacks, O'Rourke and Williamson use. Changes above about 10% are also given as plain percentages.

**Munro, "Money, Prices, Wages, and 'Profit Inflation' in Spain, the Southern Netherlands, and England during the Price Revolution era, c. 1520 - c. 1650" (Toronto Working Paper 320, 2008)**
- https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/workingPapers/tecipa-320.pdf. Evidence dates 1501-1650. **COMPARATIVE.**
- Verbatim: "Thus the Price Revolution, as an era of generally sustained, continuous inflation, lasted about 130 years; and there is no other period in European economic history that demonstrates an inflation enduring so long." And: "Southern England experienced an intermediate rise in its quinquennial mean CPI: from 101.43 to 697.54 - a 6.88 fold increase."
- **(our arithmetic)** That is about 1.35% a year from 1501-10 to 1646-50, which matches the 1.2%/yr the Bank of England series gives for 1500-1650.

**Rolnick and Weber, "Inflation, Money, and Output Under Alternative Monetary Standards" (Minneapolis Fed Staff Report 175, revised Oct 1995)**
- https://www.minneapolisfed.org/research/sr/sr175.pdf, page 7. The PDF is a scan; I ran OCR and checked these figures against the page image.
- The 1997 JPE version, reprinted as Quarterly Review 22(2), returned 403. Its figures may differ from this 1995 version.
- Evidence dates from the 1600s to the 1990s, 15 countries. **COMPARATIVE.**
- Verbatim: "the average rate of inflation during commodity standard episodes (weighted by episode length) is 1.0 percent; the average during fiat standard episodes—excluding the episode which includes the German hyperinflation—is 13 percent."
- And: "A 3.4 times increase in the price level over 100 years, as occurred in Spain, amounts to an average annual inflation rate of only 1.2 percent ... A 3.5 times increase in the price level over 133 years (taking the shortest interval for England) amounts to an even lower average annual inflation rate of only 0.94 percent, lower than any of the inflation rates for fiat standard episodes in Figure 1."
- The abstract finds that under commodity standards "inflation, money growth, and output growth are all lower".

**Jacks, O'Rourke and Williamson, "Commodity Price Volatility and World Market Integration since 1700" (NBER w14748)**
- https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w14748/w14748.pdf. Evidence dates 1700-2008. **COMPARATIVE.** These are wholesale traded-commodity prices.
- Verbatim: "Volatility is calculated as the standard deviation of the price ratio, ln(Pt/Pt-1)". And: "Has commodity price volatility increased over time? The answer is no: there is little evidence of trend since 1700."
- Also verbatim: "Three centuries of history shows unambiguously that economic isolation caused by war or autarkic policy has been associated with much greater commodity price volatility".
- Table 3 gives English annual prices (Clark) at 0.137 for 1700-1819 and 0.131 for 1820-1869.
- The paper's monthly modern figures (0.06-0.08, from UNCTAD and the IMF for 1960-2005) are a different frequency and should not be set beside the annual ones. Use the paper's conclusion, not that cross-frequency comparison.

**Albers, Pfister and Uebele, "The Great Moderation of Grain Price Volatility: Market Integration vs. Climate Change, Germany, 1650–1790" (EHES Working Paper 135, 2018)**
- https://ehes.org/wp/EHES_135.pdf. Evidence dates 1651-1790. **COMPARATIVE.**
- Verbatim: "Temporal volatility declined from 26% (1651–75) to 14% (1766–90)."
- This uses a different measure: the coefficient of variation of the real rye price over time, within five-year windows. Do not set it beside the standard deviations above.
- It shows premodern grain volatility itself falling as markets integrated.

**Gregory Clark, "Riding the Wave" (Reason, Aug/Sep 1997; a review of Fischer's *The Great Wave*)**
- https://reason.com/1997/08/01/riding-the-wave/

### Reading

**ATTESTED ONLY FOR the long-run drift.** Under commodity money, the English price level moved about 0.2-0.4% a year over 1210-1913, and that movement mostly came back. The 16th-century Price Revolution, the one long inflation, ran about 0.9-1.4% a year (Rolnick-Weber, Munro, and the Bank of England series). Commodity-standard episodes averaged 1.0% a year against 13% for fiat ones. Since 1971 the US has run 3.9% a year and Japan 2.3%.

**NOT ATTESTED for short-run stability; the evidence points the other way.** England's whole price level had an annual SD of 7-12% before 1750 and 4.3% in 1821-1913, against 1.1-1.6% for the US, Japan and the UK in calm modern decades. English wheat swung 17-27% a year, against about 9% for US rice and flour today. Jacks et al. find no trend in commodity volatility since 1700.

**What this means for the closeness rule (question 4):** a borrowed premodern price is more exposed than a modern one to a harvest swing a year or two away. It is less exposed to the trend decades away, so it would carry a disclaimer for a few years' gap for grain, but could be carried decades for wages, cloth and fuel. The exceptions are known trend breaks such as the Price Revolution and recoinages. N and M must still come from the Edo and Qing series.
