**Verdict for part (a):** none of the works I could read takes a weighted mean of each good's single-good conversion, or any single basket index, as "the" modern value. They do three other things instead:
- They report the answers side by side (MeasuringWorth; the Bank of Japan's 貨幣博物館).
- They compute a Laspeyres and a Paasche index and resolve them with a Fisher, which is the geometric mean of the two. Then they check that the conclusion survives the choice of formula (Allen et al. 2011; Deaton & Heston).
- They avoid any index across time or place altogether: a fixed physical requirement is priced in each place's own prices (Allen 2017; Moatsos).

## Reading

**ATTESTED ONLY FOR** a narrower case than the GM's question.

- **What the baskets compute (c).** In Allen 2001, Allen et al. 2011, Bassino & Ma, Allen 2017 and Moatsos, the basket is a denominator. Its cost in local money is divided into a wage (the welfare ratio) or set as a poverty line priced in each place's own prices. The result is a ratio or a headcount, never "what a coin is worth today." Only Allen 2017 puts a basket into dollars, and he does it to show the problem: US prices and rupee-at-PPP prices differ about 2:1 and reorder the baskets.
- **Index choice (a).** When two consumption patterns are compared, the practice is a Laspeyres/Paasche pair and their Fisher mean. The Laspeyres/Paasche spread is read as the uncertainty itself (Deaton & Heston), and the authors check that conclusions are robust (Allen et al.). I found no source that takes a Laspeyres headline alone where relative prices moved by orders of magnitude. Deaton & Heston say a spread of 5–10× makes one doubt "whether the data support any such calculation." Nordhaus adds that over two centuries, conventional indices miss most change in heating, lighting and housing, though much less in food and plain cloth.
- **One figure or a range (d).** Where a modern-money equivalent is published, several answers are the norm: MeasuringWorth's indicators, and the BoJ's ¥63,000 by rice against ¥345,000 by wage. Each is labelled by what it measures.
- **Rice as numeraire (e).** It is accepted only as a convenience: the NDL calls it 便宜的 ("a convenient method"), and the BoJ and silver/grain-wage authors use it alongside other measures. Its weaknesses are named:
  - harvest swings (the BoJ sheet), which are why Allen's wage tables use 50-year averages;
  - rice's own relative price drifting against other goods, across a recoinage (Kobayashi on Kyōhō);
  - grain wages giving wrong rankings that a full basket corrects (Broadberry & Gupta).

  The Japanese academic series deflate by Shinbo's multi-good CPI, not by rice.
