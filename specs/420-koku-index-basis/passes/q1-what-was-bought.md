**Feature 420 Q1: what form of each basket good was priced, and at which market level, Kyoto/Osaka c. 1745**

The main finding is that only four goods have a price observed in 1745-54, and the one price labeled "retail" is an editor's label on the Mitsui house's own purchases. The soybean price comes from an Osaka wholesale table. Fish, the grains and beans are worked out from 1880s ratios. Cloth is an Osaka price from the 1840s. No retail markup for Kyoto or Osaka c. 1745 was found for any good.

Bassino & Ma 2005 is in the archive in two copies:
- `/diagram/.specify/source-archive/7c/7c5d60ee3cc3/20261018T232430Z/text.txt` (https://www.econhistdbm.com/uploads/8/1/9/7/81977286/reh_japanese_wages_bassino_and_ma.pdf)
- `/diagram/.specify/source-archive/b0/b0f4cdc78b07/20261018T232636Z/text.txt` (https://gpih.ucdavis.edu/files/Bassino_Ma..pdf)

Its note to Table 3 answers most of the form questions directly.

**Sources**

1. **Bassino & Ma 2005, note to Table 3** (it describes prices of 1745-54 and 1882-86). Original is English.
   - On 1745-54: 「Data for prices of rice and lamp oil in 1745–1754 are based on Mitsui Bunko retail prices in Kyoto (Table 6). Prices of soybeans are available for Osaka in Mitsui Bunko from 1757 (Table 3). Unit-price is slightly higher than the price of white rice in Kyoto (Table 6); considering that the soybeans traded in Osaka were of above average quality, we just assume that the soybeans price in 1745–1754 to be equal to rice prices. … There are no retail prices for ﬁsh, barley and wheat, buckwheat and other grains, and linen for 1745–1754. We derived them using their price ratio to rice in early Meiji (beans 0.63, barley and wheat 0.51, buckwheat and other grains 0.41). For price of cotton linen, we used price in Osaka in 1840–1849 (Miyamoto, 1963, pp. 208–210) … As lamp oil is rapeseed oil, we used the price of lamp oil as a proxy for edible oil. Rapeseed oil in Japan is comparable to olive oil in Southern Europe used both for lighting and cooking. It is neither a cheap nor a low-quality item, as few alternatives existed (sesame oil was more a condiment than an edible oil).」
   - How the Meiji ratios were taken: 「for barley, wheat, buckwheat, and other grains, for which retail price data are not available, we ﬁrst calculate the ratio of the farm gate prices of barley, naked barley, and wheat relative to paddy for available years in 1874–1901; then we multiplied that ratio to the actual average retail rice price in 1882–1886; we adopted the same procedure for buckwheat and others (buckwheat, foxtail millet, proso millet, barnyard millet, sorghum); … we calculated the ratio of wholesale price of ﬁsh (salmon and cod) to wholesale price of rice in 1882–1886 and multiplied this ratio to the retail price of rice」
   - Note to Table 1: 「Soybeans are used as a proxy for various processed products: soybean paste (miso), soy sauce (shoyu), tofu, and fermented soybeans (natto).」

   - (translation) "Table 1, 'Osaka market-price table of main goods, 1802-1873'; Table 2, '… Oct 1725 - Apr 1726'; Table 3, '… 1st month 1757 - 1773' … It prints the price of Kawachi cotton in silver monme per tan." **Reading, good by good**

- **Rice.** Form: **ATTESTED** as white (polished) rice. Market level: **ATTESTED ONLY FOR** an editor's "retail" label on the Mitsui Kyoto account books. These were a large merchant house's purchases, not a laborer buying by the sho, so "retail" cannot be confirmed as a shop price.
- **Soybeans.** Form: **ATTESTED** as whole dry beans of "above average quality", standing in for miso, shoyu, tofu and natto. Market level: the benchmark is **wholesale** (an Osaka 相場 table of 1757) applied to Kyoto 1745-54 at the rice price. Observed Kyoto miso and shoyu prices for 1745-54 exist in the same Mitsui source (source 4) and are unused.
- **Beans other than soybeans.** Species: **SILENT**. The price is derived from a Meiji ratio, and the paper does not say on what basis that ratio was taken.
- **Barley and wheat; buckwheat and millets.** Form: **ATTESTED** as unprocessed grain, priced by farm-gate ratios to unhusked rice, 1874-1901. Not flour or noodles. Market level: a farm-gate ratio applied to the retail rice price. It is a construct, not an observed price at any market level.
- **Fish.** Form: **NOT ATTESTED** as sardine or niboshi. The paper prices salmon and cod, by a wholesale fish-to-rice ratio of 1882-86 applied to retail rice. The resulting market level is mixed. What urban commoners ate is attested only weakly, for Edo in 1860: sardines and salmon (source 7). For Kyoto or Osaka c. 1745 the evidence is **SILENT**.
- **Edible oil and lamp oil.** Form: **ATTESTED** as rapeseed oil for both. Sesame is called "more a condiment". Market level: the Kyoto Mitsui "retail" lamp oil, with the same caveat as rice.
- **Cloth.** Form: **ATTESTED** as plain cotton ("cotton linen"). The tan is about 34-36 cm by 10.2-10.6 m (record 0261 and source 5). Market level: an Osaka price of the 1840s from Miyamoto 1963, pp. 208-210, which I could not read. Its level is unknown, and at about 19.9 monme a tan it is about twice the Osaka quotations.
- **Salt and firewood.** Market level: Mitsui Kyoto "retail", same caveat. Form: salt grade **SILENT**; firewood is 薪 by the 10 kan.
- **Markup.** For Kyoto or Osaka c. 1745: **SILENT** for every good. **ATTESTED ONLY FOR** two cases:
  - Mitsui's Edo cotton store, 1790s-1840s: 2-12% over its purchase price (source 5);
  - a village side-trader selling salt in Owari in 1842: about 5-10%, my arithmetic across cities' coin rates (source 6).
- **A label to correct in the record.** Kumon's GPIH sheet says 「Prices are retail prices」, but its first soybean and cloth figures (1725-26) fall exactly in Table 2's dates, and its first edible oil (1802) at Table 1's start. Its non-rice Osaka goods are therefore probably 相場 (wholesale) quotations; that dating is my reading.
