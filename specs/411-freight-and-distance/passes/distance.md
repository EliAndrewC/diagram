I found no source that names a distance at which carriage equalled or doubled a good's price. The nearest are official freight rates and surcharges that give carriage as a share of the grain, and a ratio between land and water costs. The figures below for where carriage equals the grain's price are my own arithmetic, not attested. The Shiji saying is real, but it is Han and the historian who quotes it says Ming–Qing trade no longer followed it. Skinner's market radius is in the archive already. For tea, cloth, silk and von Thünen I found nothing.

## Sources that bear on it

**1. Shiji 129, 貨殖列傳 (the Shiji's chapter on money-makers).** https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/史記/卷129. Evidence is Han, about 90 BC, a proverb Sima Qian quotes.
> 諺曰：「百里不販樵，千里不販糴。」居之一歲，種之以穀；十歲，樹之以木；百歲，來之以德。

(translation) "The proverb says: 'Do not trade firewood over a hundred li; do not trade grain over a thousand li.' To stay one year, grow grain; ten years, plant trees; a hundred years, draw men by virtue."

**2. Xu Tan 许檀, 明清时期城乡市场网络体系的形成及其历史意义 (on how the Ming–Qing town and village market network formed).** Archived copy of https://www.aisixiang.com/data/21367.html. A modern historian describing Ming–Qing trade.
> 中国历史上长距离贩运由来已久。不过，其贩运的商品主要是奢侈品，即所谓"百里不贩樵，千里不贩籴"……明清时期这种贸易格局发生了根本性的变化：民生日用品取代奢侈品成为大规模、长距离贸易的主体

(translation) "Long-distance trade in China is old. But what it carried was mainly luxuries, as the saying 'do not trade firewood over a hundred li, nor grain over a thousand' has it... In the Ming–Qing this pattern changed fundamentally: everyday goods replaced luxuries as the main body of large-scale, long-distance trade."

> 水路运输是最便捷、低廉的运输方式，故流通枢纽城市多分布在主要水道沿线

(translation) "Water transport was the most convenient and cheapest mode, so the main trading-hub cities lay mostly along the great waterways."

> 经由淮安关流通的商品以粮食为最大宗，每年连樯而下贩往江南"不下数百万石"

(translation) "The largest item passing the Huai'an customs house was grain; every year fleets carried 'no less than several million shi' down to Jiangnan."

> 苏、杭……为全国的丝织生产、销售中心，其产品不仅销往华北、华中、西南、西北各省，且远销东南亚、欧美和俄国

(translation) "Suzhou and Hangzhou were the empire's centres for weaving and selling silk; their goods went to the provinces of the north, centre, southwest and northwest, and as far as Southeast Asia, Europe, America and Russia."

She also lists Beijing's imports as 粮食、绸缎、布匹、纸张、茶叶 (grain, silks, cotton cloth, paper, tea).

**3. Qu Dajun 屈大均, 廣東新語 (New Words on Guangdong) 卷25.** Archived copy of https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/廣東新語/卷25. Qing, about 1700.
> 西寧在萬山中，樹木叢翳，數百里不見峰岫，廣人皆薪蒸其中，以小車輸載，自山巔盤回而下，編簰乘漲，出於羅旁水口，是曰羅旁柴。其古木數百年不見斤斧，買田者必連柴山，山近水者價倍之。

(translation) "Xining lies among ten thousand hills, so thickly wooded that for several hundred li no peak shows. The Cantonese cut firewood there, carry it on small carts winding down from the summits, bind it into rafts and ride the flood out through the Luopang river mouth: it is called Luopang firewood... Whoever buys fields must take a firewood hill with them, and a hill near water is worth double."

**4. Qiu Jun 丘濬, 大學衍義補 (a statecraft treatise), 卷34, 漕輓之宜下.** https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/大學衍義補_(四庫全書本)/卷034. Ming, 1487.
> 自古漕運所從之道有三曰陸曰河曰海陸運以車水運以舟而皆資乎人力所運有多寡所費有繁省河漕視陸運之費省什三四海運視陸運之費省什七八葢河漕雖免陸行而人輓如故

(translation) "Since antiquity grain has gone three ways: by land, by river, by sea. Land carriage uses carts, water carriage boats, and all rest on men's strength... River carriage costs three or four tenths less than land; sea carriage seven or eight tenths less. For river carriage, though it escapes the road, still has men hauling the boats."

**5. Qiu Jun, same work, 卷33.** https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/大學衍義補_(四庫全書本)/卷033. He quotes Han and Tang evidence in 1487.

(translation) Qin army supply, Han source: "on average thirty zhong [each 6 hu 4 dou] were spent to deliver one shi... so 190 hu got one shi there." This was a military haul from the coast to the Ordos.

> 武帝時通西南夷……負擔饋糧率十餘鍾致一石

(translation) Han Wudi, the southwest: "carrying grain on the back, on average more than ten zhong to deliver one shi."

> 繇潤州陸運至揚子斗米費錢十九……繇揚州距河隂斗米費錢百二十

(translation) Tang, under Liu Yan: "overland from Runzhou to Yangzi, each dou of rice cost 19 cash; from Yangzhou to Heyin, each dou cost 120 cash."

**6. 明史 (the official Ming history) 卷79, 食貨三 (economy, part 3), 漕運 (tribute-grain transport).** https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/明史/卷79. It records a 1431 rule (the history was compiled in 1739).

(translation) "Jian Yi and others submitted the scale of surcharges paid to the army for carrying the people's tax grain, graded by distance. ## Reading

**ATTESTED ONLY FOR** the following. Read the derived figures as my own arithmetic.

- **Rules (c).** The saying in the Shiji is attested verbatim, but it is Han. Xu Tan says plainly that Ming–Qing everyday goods broke it. The working rule for the Ming–Qing is water against land:
  - Cheng: water costs about 1/10 of mule-pack land carriage (1690s Yangzi rivers and the Qing statutes).
  - Qiu Jun in 1487: the Grand Canal saves only 30–40% on land, the sea 70–80%, because canal boats were still hauled by men.

  These are two different waters, so report both. Skinner gives the market radius: 3.4–6.1 km, modal about 4.5 km to the town. Qu Dajun's "a hill near water is worth double" is the only Qing firewood statement and it is a value-against-access rule.
- **Distance at which carriage equals the price (b).** No source states one. What is attested is carriage as a share of the grain:
  - The Ming 1431 canal surcharge was 0.5–0.8 shi per shi delivered to Beijing, by province (later 0.25–0.6). On the longest canal hauls that is near parity.
  - Hagi's sea freight to Osaka was 3.4% of the rice in 1652, about 4% up to 1763, and at most 6.4–6.9%.
  - The Han and Tang extremes, quoted by Qiu Jun, are 10 to 190 units spent per unit delivered on military overland hauls.

  Derivation, not attested: with Cheng's 1680 price, 32.22 g silver per 100 L, one shi of about 103 L is worth about 0.89 tael. At the official land rate of 0.001 tael/shi/li, carriage equals the grain's price at about 890 li (about 510 km). For porters (0.0015) it is about 590 li, for hill roads (0.002–0.003) about 300–450 li, and on a secondary river (0.01 per 100 li) about 8,900 li. As a check, the Shandong, Shanxi and Shaanxi rate of 0.1 tael/shi/100 li reaches one shi's price at about 1,000 li, which happens to match the saying's 千里. These are official payment rates, and at mid-18th-century Jiangnan prices (higher) the distances would be longer.
- **Typical and maximum hauls (a).**
  - Firewood: head-load about 15 km one way into Kyoto (Ohara). In Guangdong it went by cart and then by raft over hundreds of li. Chūma pack-horses carried it from Ina toward Nagoya and Edo (no distance stated).
  - Charcoal: from Izu to Edo by sea.
  - Grain: millions of shi a year down the canal at Huai'an. Hagi rice went to Osaka by sea.
  - Silk, cloth and tea: Xu Tan attests their destinations, not distances or costs. Silk went empire-wide and abroad; cloth and tea went to Beijing.
