## Feature 411, pass 3: tea caravans

### Sources that bear on the question

**1. Rockhill, *The Land of the Lamas* (1891), Cornell scan.** https://archive.org/stream/cu31924098819877/cu31924098819877_djvu.txt (saved at /tmp/l7r-pass/411-tea-rockhill/). Rockhill's own observations date from his 1889 journey. The price list of brick-tea grades he prints is quoted from Father Desgodins' 1883 pamphlet, so its evidence is 1883. The scan's OCR errors are kept as printed and marked.

- **Porters and loads, 1889, pp. 298–301:** "Most of them were carrying tea from Ya-chou, or the neighboring towns, to Ta-chien-lu, a distance of about one hundred and fifty miles, which they make in seven- teen or eighteen days. … The average load is nine packages (pao) of tea, or 190 to 200 pounds, but I saw several men who had seventeen packages (over 300 pounds)" … "Women frequently carry seven or eight packages of tea, and I have seen children of six or seven with a package, or a package and a half" … "The regular pay for tea coolies is two mace (about twenty-five cents) a package, irrespective of weight, from Ya-chou to Ta-chien-lu. They live on maize cakes and vegetables, with now and then a little pork." He also counted traffic: "between Wa-ssii- k'ou and Lu-ting ch'iao (about fifteen miles) I counted 481 carrying tea".
- **Pao weight, duties and repacking, 1889, p. 277:** "This tea is brought from Ta-chou [sic, = Ya-chou] and neighboring localities by porters, who are paid two-tenths of an ounce of silver for each pao weighing from eighteen to twenty-three pounds. … a duty of Tl. 0. 8 is charged for every five pao (or one Pao [OCR, = picul])". Then: "each pao is divided into four separate packages or bricks, called in Tibetan parka … and made up into cases of nine bricks and securely sewn in rawhide ; then it is ready for exportation to Tibet, two cases or yam making an ordinary yak-load." His footnote: "The average weight of a gam is forty-five lbs."
- **Packing at Ya-chou, 1889, pp. 310–311:** "they are packed in long bamboo baskets, sprin- kled with rice-water, and put inside a wooden mold ; they are next pressed into the baskets with a heavy iron-shod rammer … The establishment I visited sold between 30,000 and 40,000 pack- ages (each nominally of eighteen catties), a year".
- **Price at origin, 1889, p. 311 note:** "The best brick tea is sold for Tls. 2 a pao at Ya-chou, or about Rs. 6. 8."
- **Price at Ta-chien-lu, 1889, p. 280 note:** "When I was at Ta-chien-lu the price was T1.0. 2.4 (1 rupee =T1.0. 3.2)." This note appears to belong to the third-grade brick (the OCR column order makes the attachment uncertain), and "I paid Ee. 1 for it at Ta-chien-lu" goes with the first grade.
- **Price ladder from Ta-chien-lu to Lhasa, Desgodins 1883, pp. 279–280.** First grade: "Each brick weighs generally five and a half English pounds, and is sold at R. 1.4 at Ta-chien-lu, Rs. 2 at Bat'ang … Prom Rs. 3 to Rs. 4 at Lh'asa". Third grade: "Each brick weighs two and a half English pounds, and is sold for ten annas at Ta-chien-lu, Re. 1 at Bat'ang, Rs. 1J [OCR, = 1½] to Rs. 2 out of the highroad … Rs. 2 to Rs. 2£ [= 2½] at Lh'asa. … the current money of traders. … 'this horse is worth twenty packets'". Fifth grade: "nearly five English pounds, is sold at twelve annas six pies at Ta-chien-lu … Rs. 2 to Rs. 2i at Lh'asa."
- **Annual volume, 1889, p. 277:** "from ten to thirteen millions of pounds being brought to this town annually".
- **Kansu route, 1889, p. 113 note:** "Ch'iung-chou tea is made into pack- ages weighing fifty catties, and sells in the Ts'aidam for about Tls. 10 of silver each package."

**2. 明史 (*Mingshi*, History of the Ming) 卷80, 食貨四, 茶法 (tea law).** https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/明史/卷80 (saved at /tmp/l7r-pass/411-tea-mingshi/). This is a Qing compilation completed in 1739, describing Ming practice.

- **Route length and the tea-horse bureaus, Hongwu reign:** 「設茶馬司於秦、洮、河、雅渚州，自碉門、黎、雅抵朵甘、烏思藏，行茶之地五千餘里。」 (translation) "Tea-horse bureaus were set up at Qin, Tao, He and Ya prefectures; from Diaomen, Li and Ya to Duogan and Ü-Tsang, the tea-trading land runs over 5,000 li."
- **Exchange rate, Hongwu, before 1397:** 「茶馬司定價，馬一匹，茶千八百斤，於碉門茶課司給之。番商往復迂遠，而給茶太多。……詔茶馬司仍舊，而定上馬一匹，給茶百二十斤，中七十斤，駒五十斤。」 (translation) "The tea-horse bureau fixed the price at 1,800 jin of tea per horse, paid out at the Diaomen tea-tax office. The foreign merchants' round trip was long and roundabout, and too much tea was being given. … It was decreed that the bureau stay as it was, and the rate was set at 120 jin of tea for a top horse, 70 for a middling one, and 50 for a colt." The passage comes just before 「三十年改設秦州茶馬司於西寧」 ("in the thirtieth year [of Hongwu, 1397] the Qinzhou bureau was moved to Xining").
- **Licence unit, early Ming:** 「引茶百斤，輸錢二百」 (translation) "A licence covers 100 jin of tea, for a fee of 200 cash."
- **Hongzhi 3 (1490):** 「每引不過百斤……可得茶四十萬斤，易馬四千匹」 (translation) "No more than 100 jin per licence … 400,000 jin of tea can be had, to trade for 4,000 horses." That is an implied 100 jin per horse.
- **Packing by the 篦 (bi, a basket package), 正德十年 (1515).** The year is read from context: the line follows 正德元年. 「番人之市馬也，不能辯權衡，止訂篦中馬。……十年巡茶御史王汝舟酌為中制，每千斤為三百三十篦。」 (translation) "When the Tibetans traded horses they could not judge weights, and bargained only by the bi … In the tenth year the tea-inspecting censor Wang Ruzhou fixed a middle standard: 1,000 jin makes 330 bi." That is about 3 jin per bi.
- **Price conversion, Chenghua era (1465–1487):** 「令茶百斤折銀五錢」 (translation) "100 jin of tea was commuted at 0.5 tael of silver."

**3. Parkes, "Report on the Russian Caravan Trade with China," *JRGS* 24 (1854).** https://archive.org/stream/jstor-3698123/3698123_djvu.txt (saved at /tmp/l7r-pass/411-tea-parkes/). The evidence dates from about 1849–1853. OCR errors are kept and marked.

- **Camel load, days and freight:** "The former animals, when laden with burdens not exceeding 300 catties (400 lbs.) in weight, require from 40 to 50 days to per- form the journey between Kalgan and Kiakhta, estimated at 804 miles. … [rates] range between IJ and 3^ taels [OCR, = 1½ and 3½] for 100 catties … the average or most usual rate appears to be 2^ taels [= 2½], or about 3J dollars [= 3½] per 100 catties. The oxen are owned chiefly by Isakhar, and the camels by Mongol tribes".
- **Chests:** "One statement says that the chests average 50 catties in size, and 18 dollars in cost, which would nearly be equal to 25 taels per pecul ; other informants speak of their weighing 40, 60, and 90 catties ; whilst a Russian account makes mention of 2^ poods, or 90 pounds English."
- **Bricks:** "Those sent to Russia appear to average an oka (2f lbs.) in weight … in cost from 16 to 22 pence each."
- **Distance from Fujian:** "the distance from Kaigan to the N. of Fuhkeen … is about 1200 miles … the expense of transport on the Chinese side may fairly be computed at 3Jrf. per lb."
- **Packing for the Russian leg:** "the chests of tea being packed in raw hides".
- **Barter rule at Kiakhta:** "the use of money is strictly interdicted. The value of all the commodities is fixed by Commis- sioners".

**4. Archived: en.wikipedia "Kyakhta trade"** (archive text 96/9675ad12416d). This is a modern summary describing the 18th and 19th centuries: "2. 625 miles southeast to Kalgan by camel caravan across the Gobi Desert. This took about 30 days. … 1. 170 miles south to Urga. Here carts could be used. … 3. 100 miles southeast from Kalgan to Beijing … about four days and used mules".

**5. Archived: zh.wikipedia 张家口堡** (archive text 8c/8c1fe6c8c65b). This is a modern statement about the late Qing and early Republic: 「張庫大道通車後，通過該道路每年交易30萬箱砖茶」 (translation) "after the Zhangjiakou–Kulun road opened, 300,000 cases of brick tea a year passed over it." It gives no weight per case, and part of the period it covers falls after 1912.

**6. Archived: Michie, *The Siberian Overland Route* (1864), gutenberg 45167** (archive text 0b/0bd056c336e0). Evidence from 1863: "Silver is not much in use among the Mongols, their only real currency being very coarse brick tea." The mule load he gives ("reputed to carry 300 catties, or 400 lbs.") was for his own baggage between Peking and Kalgan, not tea.

**7. ja.wikipedia 宇治採茶使 (redirect from 御茶壺道中).** https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/御茶壺道中 (saved at /tmp/l7r-pass/411-tea-ochatsubo/). Modern summary of the period 1633–1866: 「旧暦の4月頃、将軍家伝来の100個以上の空の茶壷が江戸を出て東海道を運ばれた。宇治に着くと初昔、後昔など最高級の碾茶を詰められて復路は中山道・甲州街道を江戸に向けて運ばれた」 (translation) "Around the fourth lunar month, more than 100 of the shogunal house's empty tea jars left Edo along the Tōkaidō. At Uji they were filled with the finest tencha, such as Hatsumukashi and Atomukashi, and on the return were carried toward Edo by the Nakasendō and Kōshū-kaidō." Also: 「多い時には1000人を超える大行列」 (translation) "at its largest, a procession of over 1,000 people", and 「採茶使は旧暦の4月頃江戸を出て土用の頃江戸に戻った」 (translation) "the envoys left Edo around the fourth month and returned around doyō (midsummer)." In 1867 the jars instead went 「荷物として宿継人足に委ねられて」 (translation) "as plain baggage entrusted to relay porters."

**8. Archived: akiyama 街道豆知識 PDF** (archive text 71/71845d7a9990). Modern summary of Edo practice: 「毎年4月下旬頃に将軍家の茶道頭らが、茶壷を宇治へ運んで、新茶を詰めて江戸に持ち帰る……茶壷が湿気を嫌うことから、七里の渡しや今切りの渡しを避けて美濃路や本坂峠を通ることもあった」 (translation) "Each year around late April the shogun's tea masters carried jars to Uji, filled them with new tea and brought them back to Edo … because the jars disliked damp, they sometimes avoided the Shichiri and Imagiri ferries by the Minoji or Honzaka pass." Also: 「宿泊には本陣が充てられ」 (translation) "honjin inns were assigned for their lodging."

### Reading

**Tea Horse Road, Ya-chou to Ta-chien-lu: ATTESTED.** Rockhill gives the whole picture from 1889:
- **Route:** about 150 miles, walked in 17–18 days.
- **Packing:** bamboo-basket pao of 18–23 lb, nominally 18 catties. At Ta-chien-lu each pao is split into four bricks, nine bricks go into a rawhide case of about 45 lb, and two cases make a yak-load.
- **Porter load:** 9 pao (190–200 lb) on average, up to 17 pao (over 300 lb).
- **Wage:** 2 mace (0.2 tael) per pao.
- **Day wage (my arithmetic, not a stated wage):** 9 pao × 0.2 tael = 1.8 tael per trip; over 17–18 days that is about 0.1 tael a day, and the porter fed himself.
- **Price, origin to destination:** the only same-year pair is 1889. Best brick tea sold at Tls 2 a pao at Ya-chou, and a brick (probably third grade) at Tl 0.24 at Ta-chien-lu. The two figures are for different grades and units, so they are not clean.
- **Price ladder:** the Ta-chien-lu → Bat'ang → Lhasa ladder is Desgodins, 1883. For the third grade it runs 10 annas → Re 1 → Rs 2–2½, roughly a fourfold rise to Lhasa.

**Ming tea-horse exchange: ATTESTED ONLY FOR official rates and the license weight**, as the *Mingshi* (compiled 1739) records them:
- 1,800 jin per horse at first, then 120/70/50 jin by grade of horse, under Hongwu before 1397.
- About 100 jin per horse implied in 1490.
- A licence covered 100 jin, and the 1515 standard was 1,000 jin to 330 bi.
- The tea-trading land ran "5,000-odd li".

It gives no porter loads, days on the road or wages.

**Kyakhta: ATTESTED** for camel load, route and freight, about 1850 (Parkes):
- **Camel load:** up to 300 catties.
- **Route and days:** Kalgan to Kiakhta 804 miles in 40–50 days; the modern Wikipedia summary gives about 30 days for the 625-mile Gobi stage.
- **Freight:** usually 2½ taels per 100 catties.
- **Chests and bricks:** chests of 40–90 catties, about 50 on average and costing about 18 dollars; bricks of about 2¾ lb.

A same-grade price pair at origin and at Kiakhta is **NOT given**: his Canton and Russian retail prices are for different grades, and money was banned at Kiakhta itself. No day wage is given.

**Uji to Edo: ATTESTED ONLY FOR the procession** — its route, season, 100+ jars and up to 1,000+ people, from modern summaries. Jar weight, freight cost, prices and wages are **SILENT**.
