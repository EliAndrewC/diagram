## 1. `urban-features.html` fn-74 - villages capping their own well numbers

**Source.** Endo, Takahiro (2022). "The Kabu-ido system and factors affecting local groundwater
extraction control: case study of a customary groundwater management in Japan." *Water History*
14: 169-191. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12685-022-00302-1

**Verdict: CITED.** Every one of the five elements the record wants - the cap, the permit, the fee,
the residents' own patrolmen, and the government declining to interfere - is in the paper, quotably.
The reservation is not about whether the paper supports the sentence; it is about how far that
sentence reaches, and it reaches a good deal less far than a reader of the record would assume.

### The system in one sentence, from the abstract (p. 169)

> The Kabu-ido system was a set of rules, including regulation of the number of wells per village,
> introduced to de-escalate the conflict. Under the system, groundwater uses were controlled not by
> external authority, but by the community residents themselves.

And, at greater length, p. 170:

> Under the Kabu-ido system, groundwater uses were controlled – not by an external authority, but by
> the community residents themselves. The number of wells was strictly controlled and only those who
> had special permission could build a well. It is worth noting that kabu (株) means "the privilege
> to do business" (Miyamoto 1977) and ido (井戸) means "well" in Japanese.

### The cap, the permit and the fee: the 1812 agreement (p. 176)

The agreement is printed as a numbered list. Note that the printed list jumps from Article 4 to
Article 7 - Articles 5 and 6 are not given in this paper, and I am not inferring what they said.

> Article 1: The number of artesian wells is confined to 45. No additional wells are permitted. If
> any upper village breaches this article, all existing wells in that village must be eliminated;

> Article 2: Four people (two from the upper and two from the lower villages) act as well operators.
> A well must be opened in the presence of these operators;

> Article 3: The annual salary of a well manager is 1 ryo (unit of money at the time). The upper
> villages pay this salary;

> Article 4: Each well must be locked, and the key deposited with well managers of the upper
> villages in a key box. The key to the key box is deposited with well managers of the lower
> villages;

> Article 8: A well inside a house must be filled in;

> Article 9: If an irrigation well does not function adequately, the owner can dig another well in
> the presence of a well manager from the lower villages;

> Article 10: When the drainage gates in the lower villages are closed, wells must not be opened,
> even in cases of drought;

> Article 11: If water flows out from a buried [filled in] well, the well must be completely buried
> in the presence of well managers of the lower villages. If residents of the lower villages find
> that upper village residents use the well without reporting its use, the residents of the lower
> villages should consult the local magistrate's office.

And the paper's own gloss on that list (p. 176):

> This agreement was significant in that it set out the institutional foundation for groundwater
> utilization by introducing a variety of rules, including an upper limit on the number of wells

**On the fee.** Article 3 is the only fee in the agreement and it runs the opposite way from a
license fee: the upper villages PAY the well managers 1 ryo a year. There is no charge for holding a
well permit anywhere in the paper. Separately, the village expenditure notes show what a well cost
to make - "Silver 9 hun 2 rin To Ubei (person's name) as land tenure for construction of Kajiya
well", "Silver 3 hun 9 rin To Tomisaburo (person's name) as ditch tenure for Kanatsubo well",
"Silver 112 monme 3 hun 4 rin Worker fee for provisional well construction" (p. 178) - but those are
construction and land-tenure costs, not a permit fee. **If the record's "fee" means a fee paid for
permission to hold a well, that half is unsupported; if it means the salary the well users paid
their own inspectors, it is exactly supported.**

**The cap in numbers** (Table 1, p. 179). The 45 wells are allocated by village: Fukuzuka 9, Hondo
1, Kami-Ogure 6, Nakago 2, Namba 4, Niremata 5, Niremata-shiden 4, Nishijo 4, Oyabu 4, Sato 6,
"Total 45". That is ten villages. The ring levee held sixteen; only the ten UPPER villages had wells
to allocate.

### The permit in operation (p. 180)

> Under the Kabu-ido system, to avoid the conflicts, groundwater use by the upper villages was
> subject to a permit from the lower villages.

> A description on July 25, 1844 says "It's fine and cool today. Four well managers from the upper
> and lower villages were present to see the opening of a well in Imao."

### The residents' own patrolmen (pp. 179-180)

From the Nishimatsu Diary, April 16, 1835 (p. 179):

> Today, a message came from the lower villages that patrolmen would be sent to Nishijo tomorrow.
> Toubei and I were appointed as the Ido-soudai this year.

The paper's summary of the patrol (p. 180):

> Descriptions of wells often began in April in the Nishimatsu Diary, when a few well managers from
> the lower villages were documented as visiting the upper villages on well patrols. The oldest
> description dates back to April 1, 1821. Well patrols also appear in records for the following
> dates: April 29, 1836; April 14, 1841; and April 17, 1852. Moreover, a description from April 1,
> 1865 used the phrase "annual well patrol as usual", which showed that the lower villages carried
> out well patrols over a long period of time. Furthermore, the lower villages sometimes announced
> well patrols in advance and the upper villages treated them to food and sake

> The lower villages carried out surprise inspections as well as inspections with advance
> notification.

The officers are the villagers themselves: the Ido-soudai (井戸惣代, "well representative") rotate
annually and are drawn from the villages on both sides of the quarrel (p. 179).

### The government declining to interfere (p. 183)

> Local residents limited groundwater use through such dual internal surveillance with little
> recourse to local government at the time.

> Generally, an internal settlement called Naisai (内済) was the principal resolution for water
> conflicts in the Edo Period (1603–1867). During this period, even if parties could not resolve a
> water dispute and filed a lawsuit, the government rarely made a decision (at that time,
> administrative and judicial powers were not clearly separated). Instead, it promoted an internal
> settlement by further negotiations between the parties involved. A civil mediator was appointed by
> the government, if necessary. As a water dispute is affected by changing factors such as rainfall
> and actual water use, local water users have much more information on local conditions than
> government officials. The government at the time was afraid of losing its authority by making a
> decision that did not fit with local circumstances.

> The Kabu-ido system was no exception to this rule. In this case, the local government appointed
> Shouya from adjacent ring levees as mediators to promote an internal settlement.

This is a sharper and better-sourced version of the record's "the government declining to interfere"
than the record currently has: the shogunate's policy was not indifference but a **standing
preference for internal settlement, with a mediator appointed if needed**, and the stated reason is
that the authority feared being shown wrong by people who knew the ground better. Note too that the
magistrate's office is not absent from the agreement: Article 11 sends the lower villages to it when
an unreported well is found. The forum of last resort exists; it is simply not the forum of first
resort.

### What the paper does NOT support - read this before the number goes near a map

This is the one piece of evidence cutting against the project's deliberate liberty of drawing two to
three times the attested well count, so it is worth being exact about what it weighs.

**a) It is one district, not a country.** The Fukuzuka Ring Levee is a single low-lying enclosure of
sixteen villages on the Noubi Plain, "almost flat" (p. 175), where "There were more than 80 ring
levees in the late nineteenth century on the Noubi Plain, (Bekki 1932)" (p. 173). The paper says
plainly that the institution is a local one, and obscure even in Japan (p. 170):

> The Kabu-ido system has been studied, but mainly by local historians and geographers (Bekki 1932;
> Sakurai 1934; Katano 1941; Shimizu 2014) and, thus, it is largely unknown outside the Noubi Plain,
> not to mention outside Japan.

On how widely it spread, the honest answer is "wider than was thought, within this one plain, and
nobody has counted" (pp. 170-171):

> no previous studies have been done on how widely such institutions for groundwater management
> spread across the Noubi Plain. Endo (2018) surveyed the official histories recorded by the
> municipalities in the plain and showed that ring levees, which introduced the regulation of
> groundwater development, spread more widely than previously known.

**b) It is unusual, and the paper says so in those words.** Its interest to the literature is that
it is a first (p. 170):

> Although the Kabu-ido system disappeared in the 1930s, Takahashi (1973) regarded it as a
> pioneering example of an institution for groundwater management in Japan.

**c) The period is short and late.** Created by an inter-village agreement in 1812, after a riot in
1801 and a broken promise in 1811; "major revision" in 1877; gone by the 1930s. The wells themselves
are barely older than the rule: "In 1782, a technique for deep well construction with iron bars and
bamboo was invented in an adjacent ring levee" (p. 174). So the whole episode sits in the last
half-century of the Edo period and the first sixty years after it - not in a long-settled custom.

**d) The wells being capped are not the wells this project draws.** Every capped well is an
**artesian well**, and the grievance is not scarcity of groundwater but **drainage nuisance
downhill**: the upper villages' free-flowing wells waterlogged the lower villages' paddy. The paper
makes the contrast explicit (p. 182):

> commons studies have focused on the lowering of groundwater tables caused by excessive groundwater
> development (Blomquist 1992; Ross and Martinez-Santos 2010; Boone and Fragaszy 2018). The
> background problem of the Kabu-ido system was, however, excessive drainage water from artesian
> wells.

A domestic draw-well in a house yard, which is what our hamlet glyph is, causes none of that. Indeed
the agreement's Article 8 bans the indoor well - and the paper's reason is monitoring convenience,
not water (p. 185): "the monitoring cost was reduced by the prohibition of an indoor well (Article
8). It is certainly much more difficult to check an indoor well compared to an outdoor one." So the
paper is not evidence that a village anywhere limited how many buckets-on-a-rope its households
could sink; it is evidence that ten villages on one flood plain limited how many free-flowing bores
they could open into a shared drainage basin.

**e) The cap did not hold, and was deliberately relaxed.** This is the part most likely to be lost
if the record only carries "villages capped their own well numbers". The paper's whole second half
is a measurement of failure (p. 182):

> The surviving data show that, while regulation worked in 16 out of 26 years (62%), excess wells
> were drilled in 10 out of 26 years (38%). Thus, it is possible to say that, in Niremata Village,
> quantitative restrictions worked in some years and did not work in others.

and of sanctioned exceeding (p. 187):

> The flexibility indicates that there were three kinds of wells: legal, temporary, and illegal.

> This implies that the upper limit of the 1812 agreement was relaxed to adjust to climatic
> conditions.

**Where that leaves the record's sentence.** "Villages capping their own well numbers to limit
conflict" is true as written and now quotable. But the tension it is recorded as creating with our
2-3x liberty is milder than the record says, and for a reason worth writing down: the attested cap
is a cap on a nuisance-causing bore in one waterlogged district for one human lifetime, in a
regime that the residents themselves broke in four years out of ten and suspended by agreement in
drought. It is not a general finding that East Asian villages were parsimonious with wells.

### The source itself

*Water History* is Springer's peer-reviewed journal for the history of water use and water
institutions; this article is Open Access under **Creative Commons Attribution 4.0**, stated in the
PDF's own Declarations, so quoting and linking are unencumbered. Takahiro Endo is a professor of
environmental policy at Osaka Metropolitan University whose field is groundwater governance, and the
article is a documentary case study rather than a survey: it reconstructs the daily working of one
customary institution from two farm families' diaries (the Nishimatsu and the Tanahashi), a
newly-used run of village expenditure notes covering 24 of the 66 years from 1812 to 1877, a well
survey of 1813, and letters of apology exchanged between villages, all held in the Gifu Prefecture
Historical Document Center, the Ohgaki City Library and Rikkyo University Library. The reading is
disciplined - the author counts place-names in the expenditure notes to get a floor on the number of
wells actually sunk, and reports that his own institution's rule failed 38% of the years he can see.

For a setting modeled on imperial China and pre-Meiji Japan, its value and its limits are both
sharp. It is genuine Edo-period evidence, from primary manuscripts, about how a Japanese village
community governed a shared resource without the lord: rotating officers chosen from the users,
locks with the key split between the two parties, annual patrols and surprise inspections, joint
liability, apology letters between village headmen, and a magistrate's court that everyone preferred
not to trouble. That texture is directly usable, and it is the kind of thing our maps' explanations
are for. What it is not is a source about well DENSITY, about domestic water supply, about China, or
about any place that is not a flat, wet, embanked delta with artesian pressure under it. Its
specifics travel badly: no artesian aquifer, no drainage quarrel, and no reason for a cap. Cite it
for how a village policed itself; do not cite it for how many wells a village had.

## 2. `homesteads.html` fn-32 - rear-access ground behind the housing lots

**Source.** Ushijima, Akira; Yang, Dan; Nakazono, Mahito; and Mishima, Sachiko (2020). "Spatial
composition and premise arrangement of traditional Manchu village in Northeast China: Case Study on
Shengli Village in Xinbin Manchu autonomous county of Liaoning province." *Japan Architectural
Review* 3(3): 346-358. https://doi.org/10.1002/2475-8876.12146

**Verdict: PARTIAL, and the supported half is not the half the record leans on.** The paper does
document lanes other than the main street in the traditional Manchu village, and it does document
that the ground behind the housing lots was opened up and given streets and paths. But those are two
different facts from two different periods, and the record's sentence silently welds them: the
supplemental lanes are traditional, and the development of the ground BEHIND the lots is a Chinese
state program of the late 1960s.

A note on the text before the quotations: this PDF's embedded font maps the multiplication sign to
the digit 9, so an extractor returns "8 m 9 30 m". I rendered the pages and read the printed glyph;
it is "×" throughout, and I quote it as printed.

### The traditional form, as the paper reports it from the literature (p. 349)

> A housing lot is basically rectangular with a longer depth regardless of its size, surrounded by
> fences and mud or brick walls. The main building (正房) facing the south is placed in the middle of
> the north side.

And the street pattern, which the authors quote from Guo Mo et al., *Historical Landscape
Environment of Northern Manchu Residences* (2015) - this is the paper's own published English of a
Chinese source (p. 349):

> the village streets in the northern plain region are extended to the east and west, and houses are
> closely lined up in the south and north sides of the street. The shape of a rural Manchu village
> is almost a rectangle. There are two or three main streets in a village, which are extended to the
> east and west, and there are shops on most such streets. Roads extended to the north and south are
> supplemental. There are also small paths leading to farms, which are not often used [Note 19].

The authors then say this is the pre-1960s Shengli Village: "This description points out more
concrete characteristics that correspond to the spatial composition in Shengli Village before
compactification in the late 1960s" (p. 349).

Note 19 (pp. 357-358) prints a second English rendering of the same passage together with the
Chinese original. **Translated from the Chinese by the paper's own authors** (their English, not
mine):

> streets in villages in northern plains are generally extended to the east and west like a belt and
> houses are lined up in the north and south side of the street. Manchu villages in rural areas are
> rectangular and streets are extended to the east and west. There are generally two or three
> streets in one village and shops are located along the main street. Paths to the north and south
> are for supplemental use. Paths to farms are used not so often.

Original, as printed in Note 19 (the doubled 的 in 村屯的的街路 is in the source; I checked it
against a rendering of the page):

> 北方平原村落的街路大多沿东西向带状延展 住宅在街路南北院院相连。农村满族村落呈矩形 长长的街路都呈东西的方向 一个
> 村屯的的街路一般两条或三条 主要街路内都有商店。南北向的巷弄多起辅助作用。通往耕地的小道使用率较低

Two things in the Chinese are worth having in the record, because the paper's English softens them.
南北向的**巷弄** is specifically *alleys* or *lanes* running north-south, which is a stronger word
for our purposes than "roads" or "paths". And 住宅在街路南北**院院相连** says the dwellings along
both sides of the street are *courtyard joined to courtyard* - that is, the traditional row is
continuous, which argues against a lane running behind it.

### The lane widths the paper measures (pp. 349-350)

> The Old Road is classified as "Street I (6-7 m wide)"; the streets running from the Old Road to
> the north and south that existed before compactification in the late 1960s are classified as
> "Street II (5-6 m wide)"; the streets that were put in order after the compactification (including
> blind alleys in the 1960s) are "Street III (5-6 m wide)"; the small paths (blind alleys) derive
> from Streets I to III for accessing residences are "Street IV (2-4 m wide)"; and the prefectural
> road is "Street V (two traffic lanes)."

Note 20 restates the last of these (pp. 357-358): "small paths (blind alleys) used to access housing
lots are Street IV".

Street IV existed before compactification as well as after - the paper measures gate directions for
"Street IV in the old area" (p. 351). So **a 2-4 m blind alley giving access to lots off the main
street is attested in the pre-1960s village**, and so is a 5-6 m north-south cross street. Neither is
described as running behind a row and serving its rear.

### The one passage that is literally about ground behind the lots - and its date

Conclusion, finding 1 (p. 355):

> Until the early 1960s, the main housing lots were located along the Old Road, with some near the
> mountains. This is similar to the village forms indicated in the literature. After the late 1960s,
> farmers who lived in wide areas were gathered according to political measures, and the land behind
> the housing lots along the Old Road was developed so that the residential areas were expanded.

Discussion (p. 356):

> according to the political compactification of residential areas executed in the late 1960s,
> farmland behind the housing lots along the Old Road was developed, and new streets and paths
> accessible to residences were constructed.

That is the sentence a reader would take for "rear-access ground behind the housing lots", and its
agent is the People's Commune, twenty years after the founding of the PRC. Table 1 of the paper
dates it: "Approximately 1968 - Compactification of residential area in Shengli Village" (p. 349).

### Measurements the record can use

Lot shape and size, old area against new (p. 350):

> the ratio of width to depth of a housing lot in the old area is close to 1:2, but in the new area
> the ratio is closer to 1:1.

> The width of all housing lots in the old area is less than 50 m and is mostly 30 m or less. The
> depth is generally 50 m or less. Many housing lots have sizes of 300-500 m2 while there are only a
> few housing lots of more than 1000 m2.

Individual lots measured on the ground, all in the old area (p. 352, and the last on p. 353):

> each lot is 8 m × 30 m

> Case 3 is a housing lot of approximately 11 m × 40 m located at the crossing of Streets I and III.

> Case 4 is a laterally long lot of approximately 27 m × 19 m facing Street I.

> it is now a longitudinally long housing lot of approximately 15 m × 46 m, and the south yard has
> the longest depth of more than 30 m.

The depth of the working yard in front of the house (p. 355):

> The depth of the south yard is rarely over 20 m regardless of the size of the lot, except for Case
> 6, which is a traditional case.

Where the gate stands relative to the road (p. 350) - this is the measurement that bears hardest on
a back lane, and it says access is from the front:

> Each housing lot on the north side of Street I in the old area has both a gate and an entrance in
> the south that are connected to the street, while each housing lot on the south side of the street
> has a gate located in the north.

and, aggregated (p. 356): "approximately 50% of the housing lots have gates in the south... around
90% of the housing lots have the entrance on the south side."

What the rear of the lot is FOR, when it is not building (p. 354):

> Table 2 lists the housing lot utilities of 20 cases. In all, 13 cases use part of the lots as
> farms. Many cases use the north sides of the main buildings as farms of various sizes

The main building sits in the middle of the north side, so "the north sides of the main buildings"
is the strip behind the house. In this village that strip is a kitchen plot, a corn store, a piggery
or a barn - not a way.

### What this supports and what it does not

**Supported:** that a traditional village of this type has more ways than its main street - two or
three east-west main streets, supplemental north-south lanes (巷弄), blind alleys of 2-4 m reaching
lots off the street, and little-used paths out to the fields; that lots are deep rectangles about
1:2, mostly 30 m or less wide and 50 m or less deep, 300-500 m2; and that the ground behind a row of
lots is real, useful ground that can be built over and served with new ways when a village needs to
grow.

**Not supported:** that a lane ran behind the plots and served their rear in the traditional form.
The gates face the street; the rear strip is garden and barn; the Chinese the paper quotes says the
courtyards along a street join one another. The one passage about ground behind the lots being
opened up and given streets describes a 1968 state resettlement. **If the record's sentence means
"back lanes are attested in traditional Manchu villages", that is not what this paper shows and the
footnote should be re-aimed.** If it means "ways other than the main street, including narrow
lane-like access to lots, are attested in traditional Manchu villages, so a lane web is not a purely
European shape", the paper carries that cleanly and the quotations above will hold it.

### The source itself

*Japan Architectural Review* is the Architectural Institute of Japan's English journal; this is the
authorized English secondary publication of a paper that first appeared in Japanese in the
*Journal of Architecture and Planning (Transactions of AIJ)* 84(762): 1715-1725. It is open access
under **Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-NoDerivs** - free to read and quote, not to
adapt. The method is a field survey rather than a documentary study: the team worked in Xinbin
Manchu autonomous county from 2013 to 2017, spent 16 days on measurement surveys and interviews with
residents and officials in September 2014 and October 2017, drew 19 buildings and 20 houses, and
classified all 296 housing lots in the village by street type, gate position, lot dimensions and the
arrangement of outbuildings. Lot boundaries were taken from the walls and fences standing at the
time of survey, or from what residents said where a boundary was unclear.

Its limits for our setting are the ones that follow from that method, and they are serious enough to
state at the point of use. The thing measured is **one village in 2013-2017**, in Manchuria, and the
paper's own finding is that its plan was **remade by the Chinese state in about 1968**; everything
about the pre-modern form comes from two mid-twentieth-century secondary works, *Customs of
Manchukuo* (1933) and Guo Mo et al. (2015), quoted at second hand. The building culture is Manchu
and Han northeastern, shaped by the kang and by a cold-climate demand for south-facing rooms, and
the authors say the Manchu form had by 1933 become hard to tell from the Han. So it is honest
evidence that a nucleated farm village in East Asia lays out deep walled lots in rows along an
east-west street with subordinate lanes crossing it, and honest evidence of what such lots measure;
it is not evidence about Japan, not about the pre-modern period directly, and its numbers are a
modern survey of a resettled village. Cite it for the shape of a lot and the hierarchy of ways; do
not cite it for anything that depends on the village not having been rebuilt.

## 3. `urban-features.html` fn-64 - a clan border drawn as a LINE

**Source.** Mukoyama, Naosuke (2022). "The Eastern cousins of European sovereign states? The
development of linear borders in early modern Japan." *European Journal of International Relations*
29(2): 255-282. https://doi.org/10.1177/13540661221133206

**Two corrections for the registry, both read off the file.** The author is **MUKOYAMA, Naosuke**
(向山直佑), not Koyama: the name appears as "Naosuke Mukoyama" on the title page with the ORCID
0000-0003-3069-0121, as the running head "Mukoyama" on every odd page, and in the author biography on
p. 282. And the PDF carries **no Creative Commons statement anywhere** - its title page says
"Article reuse guidelines: sagepub.com/journals-permissions" and "© The Author(s) 2022". I cannot
confirm CC-BY from the file; if the registry is going to assert a license, it needs a separate
check. Note also the volume: the article was published online in 2022 and printed in the **2023**
issue, 29(2): 255-282.

**Verdict: CITED, and more than the record currently claims.** The record's sentence is a fair
summary of the paper's own abstract, and the paper supports a **drawn linear border**, not something
weaker. It also repairs two neighboring footnotes.

### The claim, in the paper's own abstract (p. 255)

> Through an investigation of boundary disputes, boundary markers, and map-making during the Edo
> period (1603–1868), it demonstrates that linear borders were not foreign to early modern Japan.
> Domains in Edo Japan were already well into the process of building a territorial order with
> demarcated borders and mutual exclusion.

The record's sentence is close enough to that to be almost a paraphrase of it. It is now quotable.

### Is a domain border a border at all? (p. 262)

> Most of the rest was under the rule of more than 200 feudal lords called the daimyō (大名) (Figure
> 1). Officially, the daimyō were vassals of the shogun, but in practice, they had a high level of
> autonomy and political power within their domains; they "maintained independent standing armies,
> wrote their own legal codes, set and collected their own taxes, controlled and policed their own
> borders" (Ravina, 1995: 1000).

### LINE, not zone - the paper's central evidence (p. 266)

Mukoyama's own translation of Iwata (1953: 10), marked as a translation in the source ("the author's
translation"):

> If you examine borders between domains during the Edo period through disputes, you would notice
> that they were mostly linear boundaries. It was especially true for the borders of tozama yūhan.
> Frontiers were more or less reorganized soon after the beginning of the Edo period, which was
> often accompanied by boundary disputes. There were cases of boundary disputes between domains in
> the mid-Edo period as well, but they are mostly geographically limited; full-scale disputes had
> been completed before the Genroku era [1688-1704] at the latest. This means domain borders had
> already become frontiers of convergence by then and finished evolving into linear boundaries,
> which was particularly the case for tozama yūhan, which did not experience transfer or include
> enclaves.

Mukoyama's own sentence after it (p. 266):

> Frontiers, which were zonal and fuzzy, became linear and clearly demarcated boundaries in the
> early modern period, much like in early modern Europe.

And (p. 267): "As Iwata (1953: 72) noted, solving boundary disputes meant the establishment of
linear borders."

**So the answer to "a drawn linear border or something weaker" is: a line.** The paper's whole
argument is that the zonal frontier became a line, and it dates the transition - mostly complete
before 1704.

### What the line looked like on the ground (pp. 270-271)

> What did these demarcated borders look like on the ground? First, it was common for the daimyō to
> construct boundary markers to clarify where their realms began and ended. Boundary markers took
> diverse forms, including stones, mounds, and trees, sometimes mixed with each other. In some cases,
> it was just one stone showing the edge of the territory, but when the borders were contested,
> setting only one boundary stone was insufficient. Disputed boundaries required more precision. In
> these cases, domains usually built multiple markers along the borderline.

This next passage **repairs fn-65 directly** - the record's Nanbu-Date mounds, currently resting on
an English Wikipedia article (p. 270):

> One of the most notable examples is its boundaries with the Sendai domain. After more than 50
> years of repeated boundary disputes, Morioka and Sendai finally succeeded in demarcating borders
> and agreed in 1642 to construct more than 100 mounds along 130 km of borderline to prevent any
> future confusion (Ikeda, 1975: 259). These mounds were large and sparse at the beginning, but they
> became smaller and more densely spaced as time passed, which illustrates the process of transition
> from zonal frontiers to linear boundaries (Iwata, 1953: 54).

Every element of the record's fn-65 sentence is there - earth mounds, ~130 km, Morioka (Nanbu) and
Sendai (Date), 1642, markers common under the Tokugawa - from a peer-reviewed article with its own
citation to Ikeda 1975, and with the bonus that the mounds got **smaller and denser over time**,
which is itself a lovely map-explanation fact. One difference to note: the record says the boundary
was "re-confirmed by the shogunate in 1642"; Mukoyama says the two domains "agreed in 1642", with no
shogunal role mentioned.

The border was also policed (p. 271):

> Domanial governments not only demarcated borders with these markers but also policed them. They
> built guardhouses on both sides of the borders and stationed soldiers to control the entry of
> people and goods. The Aisari village of the Sendai domain, which was located near the border with
> the Morioka domain, provides an example. In this village in 1656, the government introduced a
> guardhouse with 102 soldiers (Sugiura, 1991: 419).

### Mutual exclusion (pp. 271-272)

Domain currencies "could be used only inside the domain; they were worthless outside the territory
of the domain that issued them" (p. 271). Jurisdiction stopped at the line (p. 272):

> If a criminal escaped from one domain into another, the former could not enter the latter's
> territory to make an arrest. Similarly, if peasants abandoned their lands and fled to another
> territory to avoid heavy taxes, domains had to negotiate with each other to arrange their return
> rather than entering other domains to bring them back (Kimura, 1997: 255).

> Third, domains often prohibited intermarriages with people in other domains. In an extreme case,
> intermarriage was prohibited among residents of two villages that were just 300 m apart on
> different sides of the border (Ikeda, 1975: 272).

### Map-making - and this repairs the "unsourced" parenthesis after fn-66

The record currently says of the kuniezu that "the page lists mountains, rivers, roads, landmarks,
village names, rice yields and castle towns, not boundaries" and marks the boundary claim unsourced.
Mukoyama supplies it. On the projects themselves (p. 273): "the shogunate ordered the creation of
kuniezu five times—1605, circa 1633, 1644, 1697, and 1835 (Yonemoto, 2003: 9)". And on what the
map had to show (p. 274):

> As a response to the rapid increase in boundary disputes, the shogunate issued a guideline for the
> demarcation of borders when it ordered the making of provincial maps in the Genroku era. There
> were five points regarding the process of demarcation (Kawamura, 2009: 108-109):
>
>    1.   Confirm the borders by matching maps of them created by each party.
>    2.   Add a description of borders on the final map in text.
>    3.   Depict borders unambiguously on the final map.
>    4.   If the mountain that constitutes a border is called differently between the two parties,
>         add both names on the final map.
>    5.   Exchange a deed of agreement on borders.

Point 3 is, in the shogunate's own instruction, the record's "putting the line on a map is itself the
authentic act." And the causation runs the way the record wants (p. 273): "the making of provincial
maps also had a by-product; it encouraged boundary disputes between domains and led to the
consolidation of linear borders."

For the settled dispute, the map and the marker go together (p. 266): "When they finally resolved the
issue, they exchanged a map and built boundary markers to perpetuate the borderline." And of one
such map, Figure 2 (p. 267): "It depicts the Hachinohe domain's territory, showing the location of
linear borders, which are demarcated with dozens of mounds. Each mound is numbered and drawn as a
black dot with an explanation of its exact location in the text."

### What the paper does NOT support

**It is not uniform across Japan, and the author says so** (p. 276):

> All of these developments were not universal across Japan and some areas remained much less
> territorial than others, but there is no doubt that linear borders were not foreign to early
> modern Japan.

The stated exception is the center (p. 266): "areas around Kyoto and Nara, known as Kinai, were under
the control of dozens of different lords, including daimyō, the shogun, the imperial family, and
religious organizations, which complicated the territorial arrangements and made it hard to demarcate
borders clearly." And (p. 274): "the degree of the linearization of borders differed among provinces,
higher in the periphery than in the center." **The showcase cases are all peripheral tozama domains
in the far north and on the Inland Sea.** A map of a border near a capital has weaker warrant than a
map of a border in the marches.

**It says nothing about China.** China appears only in the closing questions (p. 277): "Did a similar
development of linear borders occur in the Ottoman Empire, China, India, or pre-colonial Africa?"
For a setting with an imperial-Chinese half, this paper licenses the Japanese half only.

**And the author disclaims being a historian** (p. 264):

> I do not, however, intend to conduct a full-scale historical study of early modern Japan. This is
> essentially an IR study aiming at giving feedback to IR theories on a specific aspect of territory,
> rather than a history article intending to find new historical evidence.

### The source itself

The *European Journal of International Relations* is the flagship journal of the European
International Studies Association, published by SAGE, and among the most selective venues in
international relations. Naosuke Mukoyama holds a DPhil from Oxford and is Associate Professor of
Security Studies at the University of Tokyo's Institute for Future Initiatives; his field is the
emergence of the sovereign state, mostly in East and Southeast Asia and the Middle East. The article
is a theory-driven single-case study, not archival history: it assembles the evidence for linear
borders in Edo Japan - boundary disputes, boundary markers, map-making projects - out of the Japanese
historiography, above all Iwata's 1953 study of domain borders, plus Honda, Ikeda, Kawamura, Omori
and Sugiura, and it reproduces two period boundary maps and two photographs of surviving markers. Its
purpose is to argue against International Relations' "Westphalian myth" by showing that Japan
developed linear borders in parallel with Europe rather than importing them.

For our purposes that shape is both its strength and its limit. The strength is that no other single
readable source assembles the whole case for the drawn clan border - the agreed line, the numbered
mounds along 130 km, the guardhouse of 102 soldiers, the currency that stops at the boundary, the
shogunal instruction to depict borders unambiguously on the map - and it does so with citations a
reader can follow further. The limits are three, and each is the author's own admission rather than
my inference: he is reading the Japanese historians rather than the documents, so on any specific
number we are at one remove; he states that the phenomenon was uneven, strongest in the peripheral
outside-lord domains and weakest around the capitals; and the paper is about Japan alone, explicitly
listing China as an open question. It is excellent warrant for drawing a clan border as a line on a
Rokugani map, and for saying in a modal that the marked, mapped, agreed border was real; it is not
warrant for saying every border everywhere was one.

## 4. `archetypes.html` fn-81 - the Pearl delta dike-pond mosaic

**Source.** Tian, M. (2019). "Seeing from Above: Observation of Contemporary Dike-Pond Landscape."
*Landscape Architecture Frontiers* 7(4): 130-138. https://doi.org/10.15302/J-LAF-1-050004

**Verdict: CITED - and this closes the open question in the record.** The "mosaic-like ... boundary
blurred" description IS in this article, in its English abstract, and the phrase the record carries
is a compression of it. The record's current note says the description "has no publicly readable
source" and records that it was once attributed to *Sustainability* 10(11):3860, where it is not.
That attribution was simply to the wrong paper.

### What the article actually says (p. 130, English abstract)

> A dike-pond landscape is characterized by a symbiotic and interacted relationship between water and
> land and considered an integration of human settlements with an aquaculture-agriculture system. The
> Pearl River Delta has historically enjoyed a rich river network and been shaped by the mosaic-like
> constructed ponds with the meandering natural river systems, where the boundary of the constructed
> and the natural blurred and a resilient dike-pond landscape prevailed.

The article is bilingual and this English is the journal's own published translation (the masthead
credits "TRANSLATED BY TIAN Mengxiao, Tina TIAN" - the author is one of the two translators). The
Chinese original on the same page, as the checker's anchor:

> 基塘景观由渔农复合耕作系统和人类聚落共同组成，承载着活跃的水土共生与互动关系。在河网丰富的珠江三角洲，此类景观中拼贴状的人工鱼塘和弯曲的自然河道相结合，展现了人工环境与自然环境的交融，也为景观赋予了更强的韧性。

Two notes on the Chinese for whoever writes the entry. 拼贴状 is literally *collage-shaped* rather
than *mosaic-like*; the journal's English says "mosaic-like" in the abstract and the body uses
"collage" for the same idea, so both words are the article's own. And 展现了人工环境与自然环境的交融
is "shows the intermingling of the artificial and the natural environments" - the English "the
boundary of the constructed and the natural blurred" is the translator's rendering of 交融
(intermingling, fusion), not a separate claim about a boundary.

### How the record's phrase differs from the source

The record carries: **"mosaic-like ponds with meandering natural rivers, boundary blurred"**.

The article says: **"shaped by the mosaic-like constructed ponds with the meandering natural river
systems, where the boundary of the constructed and the natural blurred"**.

The differences, exactly:

- "ponds" for "constructed ponds". The article's adjective is load-bearing: its whole subject is the
  contrast between the CONSTRUCTED pond and the NATURAL river, and dropping "constructed" dissolves
  the contrast the sentence exists to state.
- "meandering natural rivers" for "the meandering natural river systems".
- "boundary blurred" for "the boundary of the constructed and the natural blurred". The record's
  short form reads as though pond edges are indistinct on the ground. The article means the
  distinction between what people made and what the river made is hard to draw. Those are different
  claims, and only the second is the article's.
- The record's version is not a quotation and should not be presented as one; it is a summary. With
  the source in hand it can simply be quoted instead.

### Corroborating passages in the body

The same idea, in the article's own words rather than the abstract's (p. 134):

> In addition to the agricultural and ecological efficiency brought by the natural-artificial
> symbiosis, the unique and organic collage pattern of such a landscape also reflects a great scenic
> value (Fig. 1).

And its opposite, which is the article's actual argument - that the mosaic is going (p. 137):

> Besides, more and more ponds of irregular shapes are being standardized as geometrically
> rectangular ones, leading to the disappearance of the typical mosaic-like pattern. In the late
> 1950s, many dike-pond landscapes faced a high fragmentation of land and pond and increasingly
> curving waterways, which led to a lower flood storage capacity. Therefore, from the 1960s to the
> 1970s, a dike-pond system improvement initiative was launched to facilitate the irrigation and
> drainage in the poor-efficiency dike-pond areas by "standardizing fish ponds and fields,
> systemizing rivers and canals, and controlling pond water level." For example, in 1975, the
> dike-pond landscape in Xibianlang, Shunde County was reshaped into rows of uniform rectangles
> (Fig. 9).

The figure caption for Figure 10-1 (p. 137) is the cleanest single sentence for a map modal:

> A satellite image in the 1960s showing the meandering river and the irregular mosaic-like dike-pond
> landscape.

On settlements inside the mosaic (p. 135):

> The vast dike-pond systems reclaimed from the water-logged low lands had surrounded the human
> settlement patches, with only a few buildings constructed with mud and plant materials (i.e.,
> branches of mulberry trees and bamboos) dotting along the dikes.

> Resulting from the population growth and other causes, today's human settlement patches have
> gradually grown into the continuous "edges" around the ponds (Fig. 5).

On the dikes narrowing - the record's separate guess about dikes eroding "from ~20 m to under 4 m" is
NOT in this article, but the direction is (p. 136):

> Since the 1970s and the 1980s, for higher profits of pond aquaculture, the locals have sharply
> reduced the dike-pond ratio to 2:8 or even 1:9. As a result, the wide dikes around the mosaic-like
> fishponds shown in satellite photos of the 1960s have now shrunk into narrow borders and no long
> been used for any agricultural uses (Fig. 8).

Note "no long been used", printed as such - an uncorrected typo in the source, quoted as printed.
This gives a **ratio** (2:8, 1:9) and a direction, not the 20 m and 4 m widths; those two numbers
remain unsourced and the record should keep calling them a guess.

### What the source establishes and what it does not

**Establishes:** that the historical Pearl River Delta dike-pond landscape was a mosaic or collage of
irregular constructed ponds interleaved with meandering natural watercourses, in which the made and
the natural were not visually separable; that settlement sat as patches within it, with scattered
buildings of mud and plant material along the dikes; and that this irregular form was deliberately
replaced by rectangular grids from the 1960s onward.

**Does not establish:** anything about the pre-modern period from primary evidence. The article's own
historical account (p. 134) is three short paragraphs citing Ruddle and Zhong (1988), Zhong (1980),
Guo (2007) and Howard (1925); its evidence is aerial. The "mosaic-like" characterization of the
historic landscape rests on **satellite images from the 1960s**, not on any earlier record. It gives
no dimensions - no pond size, no dike width in meters, no plot count, no ratio before the 1970s. And
it is a landscape-architecture field report rather than a research paper: the author calls it "an
ongoing work", and the journal's own classification code on the title page is 文献标识码 / B, the
category for a practice report rather than a research article.

### The source itself

*Landscape Architecture Frontiers* is a bilingual Chinese-English journal published by Higher
Education Press with Peking University's College of Architecture and Landscape Architecture; this
piece ran in the "Experiments & Processes" section of 7(4). Tian Mengxiao was, at the time of
writing, a doctoral candidate in the Faculty of Architecture at the University of Hong Kong. The
method is stated in the title: **seeing from above.** She combines declassified 1960s satellite
imagery (the figures credit USGS), present-day Google Map imagery and her own drone photography,
flown during fieldwork in the delta, and reads the change between them - the argument being that a
water-and-land landscape cannot be understood from inside a village or beside a single pond, only
from a regional aerial view. The three changes she documents are settlement sprawl thickening into
continuous built edges around the ponds, the dike surfaces degrading as the dike-pond ratio is pushed
to 2:8 or 1:9, and the irregular pond mosaic being rebuilt as geometric grids, sometimes with
concrete banks. The PDF carries no Creative Commons statement; the figures are credited individually
to the author, USGS, Google and Baidu.

For a setting modeled on imperial China and pre-Meiji Japan, its applicability is narrow but real,
and the limits are easy to state. The dike-pond system is genuinely old - the article dates the
fruit-dike form to reclamation "for centuries" before the sixteenth century and the mulberry-dike
form to the silk boom of the early 1500s, prevailing in the delta until the 1910s - and it is a
southern Chinese landscape of exactly the kind a Rokugani wet-rice delta is modeled on. But this
article looks at the **contemporary** landscape and measures its **decay**, from imagery no earlier
than the 1960s. It is therefore good evidence for the FORM of a traditional dike-pond mosaic as it
survived into the twentieth century, and for the vocabulary to describe that form to a reader; it is
not evidence for any measurement, and it must never be cited as though it reported the premodern
landscape directly. Its right use in our record is the one it is already being put to: a description
of what the landscape looked like, quoted rather than paraphrased, with the aerial method and the
1960s floor stated.
