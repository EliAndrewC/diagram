**The paragraph:** Scale - the real numbers. [HERE] How large - the back grove: patch area is highly variable. The one large measured sample readable is Hong Kong's territory-wide survey of 115 village woods with GIS-drawn boundaries - the wood 「seen today behind many villages, typically in a crescent shape」: [HERE] median about 1 ha, mean 1.1 ha, half of them under a hectare, four in ten between one and two, one in ten at two or more, the range 0.06-5.3 ha [HERE] (the Herbarium's summary: 「The average area of a fung shui wood is 1 hectare」, 600 m² to 6 ha). [HERE] Across the delta in Zhongshan, Guangdong, a 2016 survey of the city's fengshui groves counts 66 of them in 66 natural villages totaling 269.85 ha - about 4.1 ha each on average, the record's own arithmetic over a total that the tail of large groves pulls up. [HERE] The great lineage villages of Fujian are the tail: fengshui forests listed as 「A forest over three hectares」 and 「A forest over six hectares」 for two Youxi County villages, and Lingtou's over 20 ha in total, kept for over 800 years, [HERE] a 22-hectare houlongshan grove in Nanjing County saved in 1958-59, [HERE] and a 133 ha lineage forest at Letu. [HERE] A TYPICAL village grove is nevertheless a small forest patch, [HERE] and the ~1-2 ha back grove drawn here sits in the upper half of the measured band - a deliberate choice, recorded: the belt must read as a forest at two feet to the pixel. How large - the water-mouth cluster: no readable Chinese source measures one [HERE] - the nearest analogue is the Korean village-entrance grove (동구숲), the analogue of the water-mouth grove (the Chinese grove type at the village's entrance, 「where the stream flows out of the village」 - Fujian's forests 「are mainly located near a villages’ entrances, ancestral halls, and temples」 [HERE] ): the most numerous type in the field survey (42 of the 100 groves surveyed), averaging 7,149 m² and standing ~730 m from the village center, [HERE] while a 24-grove sample around Mt. Jiri averages 0.22 ha (range 0.025-0.63 ha). [HERE] A Huangshan Daily piece on Jiekou (Shexian County, Anhui) describes that village's water-mouth grove as over a hundred mu - about 7 ha - with nearly a thousand ancient trees, the largest in East China: the one Chinese water-mouth figure found, an extreme rather than a type. [HERE] So the ~0.1-0.5 ha water-mouth cluster is a GUESS that sits inside the analogues' spread, positioned by Coggins' topographic rule. ### `water.html` fn-73 (searched 2026-09-12)

*Under the heading:* Why does every ditch turn on a curve?

**The paragraph:** Because water turns on a SWEPT BEND and never on a mitered corner. A hand-dug earthen ditch that changes direction does it on a sweep: a sharp corner scours on the outside and silts on the inside until the water has rounded it anyway, [HERE] and the crew that maintains the ditch re-digs the bend rather than the corner (this rests on general reading; no source is cited) [HERE] . Sharp corners belong to MASONRY - a stone-lined aqueduct, a sluice box, a revetted canal - and nothing at these scales is lined, so nothing on these maps shows one. Revisit only if a literal aqueduct is ever drawn.

### `cities/river-cities.html` fn-18 (searched 2026-09-12)

*Under the heading:* Which way does an offtake leave a river, and why?

**The paragraph:** The two ends of a river-fed moat answer differently, and the asymmetry is itself the signature of a worked waterway. At the outlet, where the moat returns its water, the junction angle is a primary control on how one channel merges into another: a numerical study of a confluence set in a river BEND found that widening the angle at which the side channel enters enlarges the zone of separated flow inside the junction, and raises the greatest bed shear stress there by 59% between 30 and 115 degrees. [HERE] So a small, downstream-pointing angle merges smoothly, while a large one drives the entering jet across the receiving channel. That the water behind such a mouth then goes slack, and that the far bank is attacked, is this record's own extension of that result rather than a finding in it - the study is of a bend, and reports neither - and the moat's two feet are drawn from the extension all the same. Natural tributaries curve to join pointing downstream, and engineered drainage returns are cut to do the same (this rests on general reading; no source is cited). [HERE] At the inlet, the same reasoning points the other way. A flow-aligned intake is the smooth-entry answer, but an offtake swallows a share of the river's bedload in proportion to how well it is aligned - the more smoothly it faces the current, the more of the river's sediment load it drinks - and a silted moat is a maintenance failure rather than a hydraulic one (this rests on general reading; no source is cited). [HERE] Classical headworks therefore kept the offtake itself near square, controlling the flow with a gate instead, siting the intake on the outer bank of a bend and skimming the cleaner upper water (this rests on general reading; no source is cited). [HERE] And a moat wants STAGE - the height of the water surface - rather than throughput: its job is to stay full, not to pass water, so it never needs the momentum-capturing mouth an irrigation canal would want (this rests on general reading; no source is cited). [HERE] Two cautions go with all of this: what the engineering literature appears to hold, in summaries of pages that could not themselves be read, is that a diversion draws its LEAST sediment at an intermediate angle rather than at the square, and that the often-quoted 90 to 120 degree rule for a headworks is measured from the WEIR's axis rather than from the river's own line. Neither could be read, so neither is cited, and neither has moved what the map draws.
