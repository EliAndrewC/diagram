*Under the heading:* Does the city wall close a full ring - and why so few gates?

**The paragraph:** A provincial seat's rampart is a closed defensive loop rather than the cost-minimized skirt a town throws around whatever ground it happens to have built on. ### `cities/fabric.html` fn-17 (searched 2026-09-12)

*Under the heading:* Why are there only one or two walled merchant compounds in a city of three thousand?

**The paragraph:** A city of about 3,000 holds a dozen or so very rich merchant families, and most of them could pay for a wall and a gate. One to three of them have one. The GM ruled on 2026-07-23 that a gated compound is not something wealth can buy: it is a right the daimyo explicitly grants to a particular family, of a piece with the other granted merchant privileges - an audience with the daimyo at the New Year's celebration, permission to carry a surname down the generations without being samurai. [HERE] So the wall marks legal standing rather than money, and a very rich merchant without the grant keeps a large unwalled house like anyone else's, only bigger.

### `urban-features.html` fn-62 (searched 2026-09-06)

*Under the heading:* Refining forges: smelting goes to the fuel, refining comes to the town

**The paragraph:** China first. Ming ironworks converted blast-furnace pig iron to wrought iron by fining, Chinese chao 炒, literally [HERE] "stir-frying": an open fire under a forced blast, fuelled with charcoal, into which wood, charcoal and broken cast iron were charged and then stirred with an iron rod once semi-molten. Song Yingxing describes a rectangular hearth (tang) set a few chi from the blast-furnace outlet and a few cun lower, with workers standing on a protective wall above it, stirring with willow poles (which burned down 2-3 cun per cycle) while spreading a mineral additive (wuchaoni [HERE] ). The practice runs back to the Eastern Han - the excavated smelting-and-fining site at Xuxiebian in Sichuan. [HERE]

### `urban-features.html` fn-85 (searched 2026-09-12)

*Under the heading:* Caste geography and status zoning

**The paragraph:** The rule the map follows: at town scale a burakumin quarter stands off every other dwelling by 60 ft of open ground, wall to wall, and that collar is reserved before anything else is allowed to grow into it. The figure has to distinguish a separate quarter from a merely dense one: dwellings inside a quarter pack at 10 to 30 ft of each other on these maps, so the seam must be several times that to read as a gap rather than as a wide lane. It is deliberately well BELOW the 120 ft pollution standoff, because this is a zoning statement about who lives beside whom rather than a buffer against kegare. CITIES are exempt by design: a ward system zones quarters wall to wall, so a city's segregation is the ward rather than the gap, and the seams between adjacent quarters on the drawn cities run about 30 ft. The 60 ft is a calibration against the drawn maps, not a finding - the historical outcast hamlet sat at the village edge or across its stream rather than at any fixed distance out (this rests on general reading; no source is cited).
