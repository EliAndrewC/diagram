### `archetypes.html` fn-82 (searched 2026-09-06)

*Under the heading:* Grid vs mosaic - the arrangement differed by system

**The paragraph:** What the research found: the answer is BOTH, and it splits by system. The individual ponds genuinely WERE rectangular oblongs [HERE] - hand-dug from marsh (挖塘培基: dig the pond, pile the spoil into the dike), [HERE] the recurring descriptor a "chessboard" of "rectangular-shaped dikes... with water in between" - because you dig a pond in whatever packs the space and straight dikes are easiest to pile, walk, and plant mulberry on. The Pearl-delta 桑基魚塘 (dike-pond) instead accreted household-by-household into a MOSAIC; the landscape-ecology literature describes the historical delta not as a uniform chessboard but as "mosaic-like constructed ponds with meandering natural river systems, [with] the boundary between constructed and natural blurred" - rectangles, yes, but of varied sizes at varied local orientations fitted around winding interior creeks (the perfectly-uniform grid is closer to the MODERN consolidated look, which the same sources note eroded the dikes considerably - ponds grew while dikes shrank 1967-2016 [HERE] (the specific ~20 m to under 4 m magnitude is unsourced)).

### `fields.html` fn-84 (searched 2026-09-12)

*Under the heading:* Why does the drain run across the slope instead of down it?

**The paragraph:** It looks wrong at a glance and it is right. The drain (akusui) crosses the field's low margin roughly PERPENDICULAR to the fall, because it is a collector: it has to gather runoff from every cascade column, and a drain that ran straight downhill would follow one column and collect nothing. A contour drain meeting a valley stream at about a right angle is ordinary side drainage (this rests on general reading; no source is cited) [HERE] . On a map whose land falls diagonally the effect is stronger still: on a sheet high to the northwest the outfall sits toward the east, which is downhill, and also up the page, where an eye expects the high ground to be. The water is not running uphill; the page is.

*Under the heading:* How far past the bank does a bridge land?

**The paragraph:** A bridge does not stop at the water's edge. Its girder bears on an abutment sill set back from the channel edge, for two reasons at once: scour, the current undercutting the bank, must never reach the bearing, and the seat itself needs a length of timber to bear on. So a modest timber bridge lands roughly 5 to 15 real feet of deck past the water on each side - a GUESS, since no page a reader can open gives either figure for a bridge of this size.
