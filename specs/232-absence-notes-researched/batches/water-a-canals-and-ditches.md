*Under the heading:* What lies between two parcels, and how wide is it?

**The paragraph:** A polder's parcels are drawn as separate shapes, so the space between two of them IS the bund - and the two directions are not the same thing. Between parcels stacked one below another down the fall there is nothing but the walking bund: a ridge of piled earth with the footpath along its top, about three feet all told (a ridge of some 20-50 cm plus the path; this rests on general reading, no source is cited). [HERE] Along the module lines the other way a delivery ditch runs with its spoil banks either side, and that corridor is about eight feet. The distinction matters because a wide gap is a PROMISE: a reader who sees eight feet of ground between two parcels is being told that water runs there. When a bay was split lengthwise into strips of a single holding, the cut between two strips of the same farm was taking the full ditch corridor - wider than any plain walking bund, and a promise of water that is not there. The decision (2026-07-24): a gap is only as wide as what runs in it. A split within a bay gets the same plain walking bund a row edge gets, and only the module lines, where a ditch is actually drawn, keep the corridor width. The dike-pond block is unaffected: its gaps are the same in both directions already.

### `archetypes.html` fn-93 (searched 2026-09-12)

*Under the heading:* Why is a hand-piled bund never straight - and never square at the corners?

**The paragraph:** How the earth moves depends on whether the bund is SHARED, and the two cases are opposite (the GM's ruling, 2026-07-25). Where the parcels are separate shapes with a real gap between them - a polder, a pond block - the rounding is SUBTRACTIVE: each parcel gives up its corners, and the bund, being the space between, simply widens there. Where a field is carved instead of laid out, the bund IS the shared line and the basins must tile without a gap, so shrinking each cell would tear holes in the field. There the correct operation is the opposite one: leave the carve untouched and ADD material, piling earth into the crossings, so that a junction stops being two hairlines meeting at a point and becomes a lumpy node of bund - and the four basin corners read rounded because the earth has taken them. That is the truest part of the whole rule, because a bund junction really is the most worked point in a field: four basins push water at it, it carries the crossing foot traffic, it is where someone stands to open and close the water, and it is the first thing to slump and be re-piled (this rests on general reading; no source is cited). [HERE] The node is drawn at true size - an ordinary walking bund is about a foot and a half across and a junction node widens to four to six feet - floored at the pen width so it never vanishes and never inflated past the node itself, since a legibility-sized dot here would be a fake fifteen-foot earthwork at every crossing. And because a junction is found from the drawn geometry, wherever three or more basin corners coincide, the pass selects itself: nothing is piled on the one fabric that must not have it, the polder, whose parcels are held apart and share no corner at all.

### `cities/river-cities.html` fn-14 (searched 2026-09-12)

*Under the heading:* Which way does an offtake leave a river, and why?

**The paragraph:** The two ends of a river-fed moat answer differently, and the asymmetry is itself the signature of a worked waterway. At the outlet, where the moat returns its water, the junction angle is a primary control on how one channel merges into another: a numerical study of a confluence set in a river BEND found that widening the angle at which the side channel enters enlarges the zone of separated flow inside the junction, and raises the greatest bed shear stress there by 59% between 30 and 115 degrees. [HERE] So a small, downstream-pointing angle merges smoothly, while a large one drives the entering jet across the receiving channel. That the water behind such a mouth then goes slack, and that the far bank is attacked, is this record's own extension of that result rather than a finding in it - the study is of a bend, and reports neither - and the moat's two feet are drawn from the extension all the same. Natural tributaries curve to join pointing downstream, and engineered drainage returns are cut to do the same (this rests on general reading; no source is cited). [HERE] At the inlet, the same reasoning points the other way. A flow-aligned intake is the smooth-entry answer, but an offtake swallows a share of the river's bedload in proportion to how well it is aligned - the more smoothly it faces the current, the more of the river's sediment load it drinks - and a silted moat is a maintenance failure rather than a hydraulic one (this rests on general reading; no source is cited). [HERE] Classical headworks therefore kept the offtake itself near square, controlling the flow with a gate instead, siting the intake on the outer bank of a bend and skimming the cleaner upper water (this rests on general reading; no source is cited). [HERE] And a moat wants STAGE - the height of the water surface - rather than throughput: its job is to stay full, not to pass water, so it never needs the momentum-capturing mouth an irrigation canal would want (this rests on general reading; no source is cited). [HERE] Two cautions go with all of this: what the engineering literature appears to hold, in summaries of pages that could not themselves be read, is that a diversion draws its LEAST sediment at an intermediate angle rather than at the square, and that the often-quoted 90 to 120 degree rule for a headworks is measured from the WEIR's axis rather than from the river's own line. Neither could be read, so neither is cited, and neither has moved what the map draws.

### `homesteads.html` fn-99 (searched 2026-09-12)

*Under the heading:* Why is there a crescent pond in front of some villages, and why is it labeled?

**The paragraph:** What it is. The half-moon pond (半月塘, banyuetang) dug at the front of a village is GEOMANTIC and not religious: no deity, no rites, nobody worships at it. It is cosmological engineering of the same kind as facing a house south. A well-sited village wants the hill behind it and water in front of it - 背山面水, beishan mianshui - and where the back half is the high ground with its grove, the front half is water; where no river obliges, the village DIGS it.
