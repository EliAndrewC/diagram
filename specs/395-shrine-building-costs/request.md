## Reading

**ATTESTED ONLY FOR** the fee to Fushimi Inari's priestly houses and its temple (愛染寺) for installing a branch Inari spirit in a small shrine. The fee was graded, and most buyers took the cheap grade, 金三百匹 (3 *bu* after 1864). It is not attested as a local priest's or monk's thank-you payment for a 遷宮, an eye-opening rite (開眼), or a ground purification (地祭). For those, and for everything else asked, the answer is **SILENT**.

The 1864 list matches the earlier one grade for grade: 500 *hiki* = 1 *ryō* 1 *bu*, 300 = 3 *bu*, 200 = 2 *bu*. So 100 *hiki* = 1 *bu* (a quarter *ryō*). That equivalence is my inference from the schedule, not something the paper states.

Setting the abbreviated rite against wages (my arithmetic, using the rates given):
- 3 *bu* is 0.75 *ryō*, about 48–51 *monme* at the Kaga official rate of 64–68 *monme* per *ryō*.
- That is about 53 days of Kyoto unskilled pay (0.92 *monme*, 1802–4).
- It is about 12 Kyoto carpenter-days (4.7 × 0.92 ≈ 4.3 *monme*), or about 17 carpenter-days by the Bank of Japan's 23-per-*ryō* rule.
- On the same basis, the small rite (500 *hiki*) is about 90 unskilled days and "outside the rites" (200 *hiki*) about 35.
- I found no matched wage for 1864, so the revised prices have no wage to set against.

**Wooden torii: SILENT. Stone torii: ATTESTED ONLY FOR one large one.** The only cost found is 3,200 ryō for Hatogamine's great stone torii, 1708–1709, as the shrine itself tells it. **Tamagaki posts, stepping stones and mason-days: SILENT.** 玉垣 寄進 一本 匁, 玉垣 刻銘 金壱両, 飛石 踏石 代 and 庭石 値段 匁 gave nothing dated. 飛田範夫, 「江戸時代の大坂の石屋」, 2001 (https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jila1994/64/5/64_5_427/_pdf) gives Osaka stonecutters' working hours (1794) and a 1700 order forbidding them to demand high wages, but no figure; I recorded it as rejected. The 1620s Osaka papers name stonecutters (石切) and wall-builders (穴太) but give them no wage.

**Clearing and mound-raising: ATTESTED ONLY FOR castle-scale earthwork, Osaka 1628.**
- Contracted earth moving cost 7–10 匁 per 坪 of earth. Levelling ground under townhouse lots (屋敷下地形, the closest case to 地均し) was 10 匁 per 坪.
- In the same ledger, unskilled labour cost 0.8–1.2 匁 per man-day.
- The paper does not define its 坪 of earth. Reading it as a 立坪 (a 6-shaku cube) is my gloss, not attested.
- The one direct count of men is for rubble: 23 men per 坪 at 16 loads each, 1620, over an unstated distance.
- I have not converted 匁 per 坪 into man-days; you have both numbers. These are moat and castle rates and may not scale down to a village plot.
- Nothing found for felling trees (伐採) or for a burial mound (塚, 土饅頭) as such.

**Stone carrying per ri: SILENT for a stone-specific rate per ri.** Two things bear on it:
1. Stone-hauling labour paid more than ordinary work in 1620: 1.3 匁 per man to bring stone down from the hills, 1.5 匁 for sledge-hauling, against 0.8–1 匁 for ordinary quarry labour.
2. In Ōya (Shimotsuke), stone travelled by pack horse as a standard load (一駄) of two blocks of about 5 sun × 1 shaku × 3 shaku. This suggests the record's existing per-ri pack-horse rates (駄賃) would apply to stone by the load, but no source says so outright.

Both tuad.ac.jp papers are 2026 secondary works citing 1620s account books, so date their figures to 1620, 1621 and 1628, not to "Edo" in general.

  (translation) "50 [carpenter-days]: carpenter's wages at silver 2 monme per person; and an allowance at silver 1 monme per person." The text also gives the total as silver 40 monme, which does not match 50 × 2.
  As printed, 銀二匁ツゝ is the wage per carpenter-day. 夫持 is most likely the per-person food allowance (扶持) paid on top of the wage, not an unskilled worker's wage. In that reading it puts the carpenter's full day at 3 monme, which the derivation below uses. If it were instead a labourer's wage, the 600 floor below would not hold.

## 2. Reading

**ATTESTED ONLY FOR** three narrower things:
- **Repair labour and cost shares.** The 1749 Kasuga sanctuary repair took 187 carpenter and sawyer days plus 24 Edo roofer days, about 211 in all. That is inside your 190–250 band for a minor repair, so the ledger's repair figure looks normal. Materials were about half the cost when Edo copper was involved (53%), and 16–29% otherwise.
- **The contract price, but not the day count, for a new one-bay sanctuary.** Shibasaki's new sanctuary (1827–41) is bigger than yours: a 6-shaku bay, a cusped gable and carved panels. The winning carpenter bid was "over 30 ryō", against rival bids of 50–80. Timber, the panel carvings (3 ryō each) and village labour were paid for outside that bid.
- **Day wages.** Tama 1749: carpenter and sawyer up to silver 1.67 monme a day, Edo roofer 3 monme. Kumehara 1818: carpenter 200 mon, sawyer and carter 180 mon. Kumehara 1830: carpenter silver 2 monme plus 1 monme food allowance.

**My own derivation, not attested:** at about 60 monme to the ryō, 30 ryō is about 1,800 monme. At 1.67–3 monme a day, with or without food, that would buy about 600–1,100 craftsman-days. This assumes the contract was almost all wages, which fits the materials being bought separately. If so, a plain 4-shaku sanctuary at 480–640 craftsman-days is plausible, not inflated, as long as it is about half or less the work of a 6-shaku carved one.
