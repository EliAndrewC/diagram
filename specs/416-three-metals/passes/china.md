## Q1. Legal rates against market rates

**S1. 清史稿 卷124 食貨五 錢法** (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/清史稿/卷124, fetched this pass). Evidence 1645–1911.

- **Shunzhi, two legal rates.**
  - 順治二年 (1645): 「二年，增重二分，定錢七枚准銀一分，舊錢倍之。民間頗病錢貴，已更定十枚准一分。」 (translation) In the second year the coin's weight was raised by 2 fen, and seven coins were fixed as 0.01 tael, old coins at double that. The people suffered from dear cash, so it was changed to ten coins per 0.01 tael. That is 700 a tael, then 1,000 (this pass).
  - The text then reaches 「十年，復開密雲…鑄局」 and, under 「是年」, says: 「是年廷議疏通錢法，以八年增重一錢二分五釐為定式…錢千准銀一兩，定為畫一通行之制。禁私局，犯者以枉法贓論。…錢糧舊制徵銀七錢三，皆著為令。」 (translation) That year the court conference took the 1.25-qian weight set in year 8 as the standard. A thousand cash equal one tael, fixed as the single rule for circulation. Private mints were banned, offenders punished as for corrupt bribe-taking. Taxes, under the old rule, were collected seven parts silver, three parts cash, and all of this became law.
  - By the text's order this is 順治十年, 1653. The rate was set by the court conference (廷議) and the Board of Revenue (戶部).
- **Kangxi, enforcement fails.** 二十三年 (1684) and after: 「久之，錢貴如故，乃申定錢直禁，銀一兩易錢毋得不足一千，然錢直終不能平。季年銀一兩易錢八百八十至七百七十。乃發五城平糶錢易銀以平其價。」 (translation) Cash stayed dear, so a ban on cash prices was decreed: a tael must not buy fewer than 1,000 cash. Still the price could never be levelled. In the late Kangxi years a tael bought 880 to 770 cash. Cash from the five wards' price-stabilising sales was released, in exchange for silver, to bring the price down.
- **1702, a legal discount by kind of coin.** 四十一年: 「新舊錢暫兼行，新錢千准銀一兩，舊錢准七錢。」 (translation) New and old coin to circulate together for now: 1,000 new cash equal one tael, 1,000 old (light) cash equal 0.7 tael.
- **1706, a recall measure, not a general rate.** 「四十五年，山東請鑄大錢。會獲得常山私鑄，上以私鑄不盡大錢，必多私銷，宜先收後禁，乃令錢糧銀一兩折收二千文，錢盡，折收銅器。」 (translation) Shandong asked to cast big cash. A private mint was caught at Changshan. The emperor judged that if the privately cast coin were not taken in, it would be melted down, so it should be bought in first and banned after. Tax was ordered taken at 2,000 cash per tael; when the coin was used up, copper vessels were taken instead.
- **1737, a market broker.** 乾隆二年: 「以錢價久不平，飭大興、宛平置錢行官牙以平錢價。」 (translation) As the price of cash had long been unsteady, Daxing and Wanping were ordered to appoint official brokers for the cash trade to steady it.
- **c. 1740s.** The passage follows the 乾隆十三年 entry, behind a 「先是」: 「復以京師錢價昂，銀一兩僅易八百文，詔發工部節慎庫錢平價。」 (translation) Cash in the capital was dear, a tael buying only 800, and cash from the Board of Works' Jieshen treasury was released to bring the price down. This matches 412 A3's Beijing 800 of 1737.
- **1837.** 道光十七年: 「而海內銀卒耗竭，每兩易錢常至二千。廷臣謀所以重錢以殺銀之勢，而議格不行。」 (translation) Silver within the realm was finally exhausted, and a tael often bought 2,000 cash. Officials planned to raise cash's value to break silver's, but the plan was blocked.
- **Xianfeng notes and big cash, 1853 onward.**
  - The notes: 「票鈔制以皮紙，額題「戶部官票」…中標二兩平足色銀若干兩」; 「鈔額題「大清寶鈔」…中標準足制錢若干文…此鈔即代制錢行用，並准按成交納地丁錢糧一切稅課捐項」. (translation) The silver notes were in so many taels of full-fine silver on the two-tael scale. The cash notes were in full standard cash, to stand for standard cash and be accepted in set shares for land tax and all levies.
  - The big cash: 「大錢當千至當十，凡五等，重自二兩遞減至四錢四分。」 (translation) Big cash from 1,000 down to 10, five grades, weighing from 2 taels down to 0.44 tael.
  - The rates: 「定議票銀一兩抵制錢二千，鈔二千抵銀一兩，票鈔亦準是互相抵，民間完納丁糧稅課及一切官款，亦准五成…大錢上下通行如票鈔，抵銀如制錢之數，輸官以三成」. (translation) A one-tael note was set equal to 2,000 standard cash, and 2,000 in cash notes to one tael; taxes could be paid up to 50% in notes. Big cash passed like the notes at their face in cash, up to 30% of payments to the state. Note the official note rate is 2,000 a tael, not 1,000.
  - Penalties: 「阻撓罪以違制，偽造鈔票斬監候」. (translation) Obstruction was punished as defying an edict; forging notes by beheading after the autumn assizes.
  - Zhili taxes, 1857: 「七年，令順天直隸各屬錢糧…以實銀四成、寶鈔三成、當十銅鐵大錢三成搭交…尋…直隸照銀七票三徵收」. (translation) Taxes in Shuntian and Zhili were to be paid 40% in silver, 30% in cash notes and 30% in ten-cash copper or iron big cash; soon after, 70% silver and 30% notes.
  - The notes fail: 「其後京師以官號七折錢發鈔，直益低落，至減發亦窮應付，鈔遂不能行矣。」 (translation) Later the official shops in the capital redeemed notes in cash at 70%, their value fell further, even reduced redemption could not be kept up, and the notes stopped circulating.
  - The big cash fail: 「大錢當千、當五百，以折當過重最先廢，當百、當五十繼廢…乃專行當十錢。…當十錢行獨久，然一錢當制錢二，出國門即不通行。」 (translation) The 1,000 and 500 pieces, overvalued the most, were abolished first, then the 100 and 50, leaving only the ten-cash. It lasted longest, but one passed for two standard cash and was not accepted outside the capital's gates.

**S2. 明史 卷81 食貨五 錢鈔** (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/明史/卷81, the page cache from 413's read today). Evidence 1375–1525.

- **The legal rate, including gold.** 1375: 「每鈔一貫，準錢千文，銀一兩；四貫準黃金一兩。禁民間不得以金銀物貨交易，違者罪之；以金銀易鈔者聽。」 (translation) One guan of paper equals 1,000 cash or one tael of silver; four guan equal one tael of gold. Trading with gold, silver or goods is forbidden; exchanging gold or silver for paper is allowed. Gold to silver is 1:4 by this rule (this pass). Also 「鑄小錢與鈔兼行，百文以下止用錢。商稅兼收錢鈔，錢三鈔七。」 (translation) Small cash circulated with paper; sums of 100 or less in cash only. Commercial tax was taken 30% in cash, 70% in paper.
- **The market strays.**
  - Late Hongwu (1390s): 「時兩浙、江西、閩、廣民重錢輕鈔，有以錢百六十文折鈔一貫者，由是物價翔貴，而鈔法益壞不行。」 (translation) In Zhejiang, Jiangxi, Fujian and Guangdong people valued cash over paper, some reckoning one guan at 160 cash; prices soared and the paper broke down. That is 16% of face (this pass).
  - Yongle: 「稅糧課程贓罰俱折收鈔，其直視洪武初減十之九。」 (translation) Taxes and fines were taken in paper, its value nine-tenths below early Hongwu.
  - Xuande: 「至宣德初，米一石用鈔五十貫」. (translation) In early Xuande one shi of rice cost 50 guan of paper.
  - Chenghua: 「是時鈔一貫不能直錢一文，而計鈔徵之民，則每貫徵銀二分五釐，民以大困。」 (translation) One guan was not worth one cash, yet tax reckoned in paper was collected at 0.025 tael per guan, and the people were greatly distressed.
  - 1525: 「鈔一貫折銀三釐，錢七文折銀一分。是時鈔久不行，錢亦大壅，益專用銀矣。」 (translation) One guan was commuted at 0.003 tael, and 7 cash at 0.01 tael. Paper had long been dead, cash was glutted, and silver alone came to be used.
- **Enforcement, in order.**
  - 1397: 「三十年乃更申交易用金銀之禁」. (translation) The ban on trading in gold and silver was decreed again.
  - Yongle 2 (1404): 「詔犯者免死，徙家戍興州」. (translation) Offenders spared death; their households sent as garrison to Xingzhou.
  - Xuande: 「交易用銀一錢者，罰鈔千貫」. (translation) Using 0.1 tael of silver in a trade fined 1,000 guan of paper.
  - Zhengtong 1 (1436): 「而以米銀錢當鈔，馳用銀之禁。朝野率皆用銀，其小者乃用錢」. (translation) Rice, silver and cash were accepted in place of paper, and the silver ban was relaxed. Court and people all used silver, cash only for small sums.
  - Zhengtong 13 (1448): 「阻鈔者追一萬貫，全家戍邊」. (translation) Obstructing paper: a 10,000-guan penalty and the whole household sent to the frontier.
  - Tianshun: 「始馳其禁」. (translation) The ban was finally lifted.
- **Ming cash per tael by era:** 412 A8 and A9 (700 in c.1500–1525; 800 Longqing; 800 Beijing against 1,200 Lower Yangzi in the 1580s–90s).

**S3. Morse, *The Trade and Administration of the Chinese Empire* (1908)** (https://archive.org/stream/tradeadministra00mors/tradeadministra00mors_djvu.txt). A modern source describing Qing practice.

- "In 1853, the year in which the issue of token coins began, the government resumed, after an interval of four and a half centuries, the issue of paper money, nominally redeemable but in practice never redeemed. … The issue of both was forced, but they rapidly depreciated in value until, in 1861, they circulated at only 3 per cent, of their face value".
- On the iron cash: "The iron coins had a temporary success, but within four years, in February 1857, there was a popular rising against them, and in a day they lost their currency."
- Evidence 1905: "the token currency of the capital is rated closely to the value of the metal contained in it."

**S4. 陈忠海, 纸币在中国古代为何难以通行 (2016)** (https://www.aisixiang.com/data/99634.html). A popular article citing others at second hand.

- 「洪武二十二年（1389），江西、福建一带面值2000文的宝钞在民间只能换铜钱500文；据彭信威《中国货币史》，宣德七年（1432）面值1000文的宝钞只值铜钱5文」 (translation) In 1389 a 2,000-wen note bought only 500 cash in Jiangxi and Fujian. Per Peng Xinwei, in 1432 a 1,000-wen note was worth 5 cash.
- 「据魏建猷《中国近代货币史》，到咸丰十一年（1861）6月，官票"似已绝迹不行"，而钱钞"则跌落到2.6％至5.2％"」 (translation) Per Wei Jianyou, by the 6th month of 1861 the silver notes had "all but vanished", and the cash notes had "fallen to 2.6%–5.2%".

**S5. Chen, Li, Pang and Zi, "A Thirst for Silver" (June 2026 working paper)** (https://wiiw.ac.at/files/events/paper-yuan-zi-n-757.pdf). The ledger said NOT-CHECKED; read through curl and pdftotext. On its Figure 3, from Lin (2020): "The horizontal dashed line indicates the official exchange rate of 1,000 wen per tael." Also: "Over the first half of the nineteenth century, the silver–copper exchange rate more than doubled."

**Reading: ATTESTED.**

- **Qing:** the legal 1,000 a tael was set by the court conference and Board of Revenue in 1653, after a 700-a-tael rule of 1645. The 1684 ban held only on paper: late Kangxi was 770–880. The state's tools were releasing treasury cash (Kangxi, c. 1740s), official brokers (1737) and laws against private minting and melting. The market ran 700–1,200 in the 18th century and about 2,000 by 1837 (412 A5–A6, 414). Legal discounts set by kind of coin: old light coin at 0.7 (1702).
- **Xianfeng:** the state itself priced its notes at 2,000 cash a tael. Notes were redeemed at 70% in Beijing, and fell to about 3% (Morse) or 2.6–5.2% (Wei, at second hand) by 1861. The ten-cash piece passed for 2 standard cash, and only inside Beijing.
- **Ming:** paper was legally 1 guan = 1,000 cash = 1 tael (gold 1:4 against silver, 1375). It fell to 16% of face by the 1390s, a tenth under Yongle, and less than one cash by Chenghua. Enforcement ran from bans with exile, through fines, to the 1436 relaxation and the lifting of the ban under Tianshun.

## Q2. Who priced and paid in which metal

**S2 (明史 卷81)**, 1436: 「朝野率皆用銀，其小者乃用錢」 (silver for everything, cash for small sums; quoted in Q1).

**S6. 明史 卷78 食貨二 賦役** (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/明史/卷78, fetched this pass). Evidence 1436–1581.

- 1436, the 金花銀: 「遂仿其制，米麥一石，折銀二錢五分。…折銀百萬餘兩，入內承運庫，謂之金花銀。其後概行於天下。自起運兌軍外，糧四石收銀一兩解京，以為永例。」 (translation) Following that precedent, one shi of rice or wheat was commuted at 0.25 tael. Over a million taels went to the inner treasury, called "gold-flower silver". The practice later spread to the whole realm: apart from grain shipped to the troops, four shi of grain were taken as one tael and sent to the capital, as a permanent rule.
- The Single Whip, fully in force from 1581: 「一條鞭法者，總括一州縣之賦役，量地計丁，丁糧畢輸於官。…悉並為一條，皆計畝徵銀，折辦於官，故謂之一條鞭。…嘉靖間，數行數止，至萬曆九年乃盡行之。」 (translation) The Single Whip gathered a county's land tax and labour service, assessed by land and adult males, all paid to the officials, merged into one item and levied in silver by the mu, hence the name. Tried and dropped several times under Jiajing, it was fully applied in Wanli 9 (1581).

**S7. 清史稿 卷123 食貨四 鹽法** (archived, https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/清史稿/卷123). Evidence 1728–30. 「商課用銀，民間買鹽用錢。…今每兩合錢二千，而鹽價如故，亦有減至十三四文者，以錢易銀，不敷原數。應請部臣會同督臣詳議」。至十年，題准每斤加銀一釐。 (translation) Merchants pay the salt levy in silver; people buy salt with cash. Now a tael is 2,000 cash, but the salt price is unchanged, some even down to 13–14 wen; changing the cash into silver no longer covers the original sum. In 1730 an addition of 0.001 tael per jin was approved. (The 2,000 is probably in the doubled local count; see 412 A7.)

**S8. 清史稿 卷421, 朱嶟 傳** (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/清史稿/卷421, fetched this pass). Evidence 1846, his memorial on 劉良駒's proposal for one rule for silver and cash.

- 「今出納以銀，錢幾置諸無用。…方今鹽務疲敝，皆以銀貴錢賤為詞，以鹽賣錢而不賣銀也。…工次僱夫購料，俱系用錢」 (translation) Now the state takes in and pays out in silver, and cash is all but useless to it. The salt administration is failing, everyone blaming silver dear and cash cheap, because salt is sold for cash, not silver. At the works, hiring labour and buying materials are all done in cash.
- 「農民以錢輸賦，天下十居七八。地方官收錢解銀，每致賠累。」 (translation) Seven or eight in ten farmers across the realm pay their tax in cash. Local officials take in cash and must remit silver, and often make up the loss themselves.
- On paying soldiers in cash: 「餉銀每兩折錢多不過千七百，少不過千二百」. (translation) Pay is to be converted at no more than 1,700 and no less than 1,200 cash a tael. Also 「兵丁領銀，仍須易錢然後適用。每至兵領餉時，不准鋪戶抑價」 (translation) Soldiers paid in silver must still change it into cash to use it; when they draw pay, shops are not allowed to push the rate down.

**S3 (Morse 1908)**, describing late-Qing practice:

- Who paid in what: "The Kuping tael is the currency in which are collected all other dues to the government than Customs duties, excepting only those which are levied in kind (such as the grain tribute) or in copper cash."
- Weight standards and spreads:
  - "The normal standard Kuping tael is 575*8 grains of silver 1,000 fine ; this is the receiving rate (the paying rate being 0*2 per cent, lighter) at the Imperial Treasury, and the several provincial treasuries vary from this standard in some instances as much as one per cent."
  - "The Tsaoping tael has been found to weigh 565 65 grains".
  - At Peking: "three standards of tael weight — the Kung-fa of 5557 … grains, the Market of 552*4 grains, and the Metropolitan or Two-tael scale of 5417 grains — and two recognised standards of silver, 1,000 and 980 fine respectively".
  - Shanghai's standard was "944 of the China standard of 'pure silver'".
  - Against the Kuping tael (this pass): Tsaoping (漕平) is 1.8% lighter, the Market tael (市平) 4.1%, the Two-tael scale (京平/二兩平) 5.9%. Fineness 980 is a 2% discount, 944 a 5.6% discount.
- The bankers' spread: "this difference, usually between a quarter and a half of one per cent., is made not by charging a commission, but by boldly using two sets of weights, one for receiving and one for paying".
- Short counts of cash (evidence 1906): "A nominal 100 cash has for a long time been worth 97 in actual cash at Wusueh, 98 at Lung-ping…". In the north: "The price of an article being there quoted at 100 cash, you hand over 50 coins".

**S9. 清稗類鈔 022, 湘淮軍餉** (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/清稗類鈔/022, fetched this pass). Evidence 1859 onward, Hubei.

- 「所有湘軍餉銀，概發湘平，蓋自咸豐己未，所收庫平，每百申三兩六錢」 (translation) All Hunan Army pay was issued on the Xiang scale; from 1859, every 100 taels received on the Kuping scale made 103.6 on the Xiang scale.
- 「湖北銀少錢多，其時收款大宗，專在釐金，通以十足制錢為定」 (translation) Hubei had little silver and much cash; the bulk of revenue was likin, all reckoned in full standard cash.

**S5 (Thirst for Silver)**: "Silver, by contrast, served as the principal medium for tax payments, official salaries, large-scale commerce, and interregional exchange".

**Reading: ATTESTED.**

- **The split by metal:** silver for tax, salt levies, official pay and long-distance trade; copper for retail, salt bought by households, hired labour and materials. Ming says so for 1436 ("cash for small sums"); Qing for 1728 and 1846.
- **Tax in silver:** fixed in silver from the 1436 commutation and the Single Whip of 1581. Yet in 1846 seven or eight in ten farmers actually paid in cash, converted by the county.
- **Silver standards as percentages:** 漕平 1.8%, 市平 4.1% and 京平 5.9% under 庫平; treasuries vary up to 1%; banks' receiving/paying spread 0.25–0.5%, taken through two sets of weights. Fineness discounts 2% (980 fine) to 5.6% (944, Shanghai). The Xiang scale ran 3.6 per 100 against the Kuping scale.
- **What is missing:** these percentages are mostly late-Qing figures (1900s), from Morse. The fee a retail money shop (錢鋪/錢莊) took to change copper for silver is **SILENT**. Searched: "Qing cash shop money changer spread…" (English) and 钱铺/钱庄 兑换 (Chinese); only 朱嶟's ban on shops pushing the rate down touches it.

## Q3. Did a price depend on the metal?

- **Cash prices stuck while the rate moved:**
  - 1728 salt: 「每兩合錢二千，而鹽價如故」 (S7, above).
  - Zeng Guofan, 1852 (S10 below): 「東南産米之區，大率石米賣錢三千，自古迄今，不甚懸遠。」 (translation) In the southeast's rice country, a shi of rice sells for about 3,000 cash, and has not varied much from of old to now.
  - S5 note 12: "goods prices and wages were relatively stable in terms of copper coins … while the silver–copper exchange rate increased substantially over time."
  - 414: copper wages flat while the rate doubled.
- **A source the other way:** 朱嶟, 1846 (S8): 「夫損上必期益下，今錢值日賤，物價日貴，泉府費兩錢而成一錢，官兵領一錢則僅當半錢。」 (translation) Taking from the state should profit the people, yet now cash grows cheaper by the day and prices dearer by the day. The mint spends two cash to make one, and an officer or soldier drawing one cash gets only half a cash's worth.
- **The price depended on what you paid with, Xianfeng Beijing (S1):** 「民間得鈔，積為無用，京師持鈔入市，非故增直，即匿貨，持向官號商鋪，所得皆四項大錢，不便用」. (translation) Notes piled up useless in people's hands. Anyone who took notes to market in the capital found the seller either raised the price or hid the goods; taking them to the official shops got only the four kinds of big cash, which were hard to use. Also 「局錢亦漸惡…奸商因之折減挑剔，任意低昂。商販患得大錢，皆裹足」. (translation) Even mint coin grew bad; crafty merchants discounted and picked through it, setting its value at will. Traders feared being paid in big cash and stayed away.
- **Light or old coin at a discount:**
  - 1702 old coin at 0.7 tael per 1,000 (S1).
  - Ming Jiajing private coin at 30–40, later 60–70, to 0.01 tael against the legal 7 (412 A8).
  - 1905 Peking ten-cash "rated closely to the value of the metal contained in it" (S3).
- **Price in silver vs in cash, as policy:** c. 1740s (S1): 「物之定直以銀不以錢，而官民乃皆便錢不便銀，趨利之徒，以使低昂為得計…嗣是宜重用銀，凡直省官修工程，民間總置貨物，皆以銀。」 (translation) Goods are properly priced in silver, not cash, yet officials and people alike find cash convenient and silver not, and profiteers make their gain by moving the rate. From now on silver should be favoured: provincial public works and wholesale purchases of goods should all be in silver.

**Reading: ATTESTED.** Prices quoted in copper were mostly sticky while the silver rate moved: salt 1728, rice c.1850, Beijing wages (414). The one dissent is 朱嶟 in 1846 ("prices dearer by the day"). Prices did depend on the means of payment: the Beijing notes of the 1850s, the big cash refused or discounted, light coin legally at 70% (1702), Ming private coin at a fraction of standard coin. How fast cash prices followed a rate move is not measured beyond the 1728 salt case: salt was raised by 0.001 tael per jin in 1730, about two years after the complaint.

## Q4. Who won and lost

**S10. Zeng Guofan, 備陳民間疾苦疏** (text on https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/敗經/卷十六_亂敗, fetched this pass). This is a modern anthology page that names neither the memorial nor its date; the identification as Zeng's memorial of 咸豐元年十二月 (early 1852) is mine. Quoted as the page has it, mixed character forms included.

- 「即使漕糧或收本色，而幫費必須折銀，地丁必須納銀。小民力田之所得者米也，持米以得錢，則米價苦賤而民怨；持錢以易銀，則銀價苦昂而民怨。」 (translation) Even if the grain tribute is taken in rice, the transport fees must be paid in silver, and the land tax must be paid in silver. What the small farmer earns is rice. Selling rice for cash, he resents the low rice price; changing cash for silver, he resents the high silver price.
- 「昔日兩銀換錢一千，則石米得銀三兩。今日兩銀換錢兩千。則石米僅得銀兩五錢，昔日賣米三鬥，輸一畝之課而有餘。今日賣米六鬥，輸一畝之課而不足。朝廷自守歲取之常，小民暗加一倍之賦。」 (translation) Formerly a tael bought 1,000 cash, so a shi of rice brought 3 taels. Now a tael buys 2,000 cash, so a shi brings only 1.5 taels. Formerly selling 3 dou of rice more than paid a mu's tax; now 6 dou is not enough. The court keeps to its usual annual levy, and the small people silently bear double the tax.
- 「此外如房基，如墳地，均須另納稅課。準以銀價，皆倍昔年。」 (translation) House plots and graveyards owe their own levies too; at the silver price, all are double former years.
- 「故自道光十五年以前，江蘇尚辦全漕，自十六年至今，歲歲報歉，年年蠲緩…蓋銀價太昂，不獨官民交困，國家亦受其害也。」 (translation) Until 1835 Jiangsu delivered its full grain tribute; from 1836 to now it has reported dearth every year and had remissions and postponements every year. Silver being too dear harms not only officials and people but the state too.
- On Zhejiang: 「民愈抗廷，官愈窮窘，于是有"截串"之法」. (translation) The more the people resist, the more hard-pressed the officials, hence "cut receipts", collecting the next half-year's or next year's tax in advance.
- 「如湖廣之來陽、崇陽、江西之貴溪、撫州，此四案者…亦由銀價之倍增，官吏之浮收，差役之濫刑」. (translation) The four cases of Leiyang and Chongyang in Huguang, and Guixi and Fuzhou in Jiangxi arose also from the doubled silver price, officials' over-collection and runners' abuse of punishment.

**S8 (朱嶟, 1846)**: 「先時銀多，則官以收錢漁利；今時錢賤，則官以易錢賠累。」 (translation) When silver was plentiful, officials profited from collecting in cash; now that cash is cheap, they lose on changing it. (He proposes paying troops in cash at 1,200–1,700.)

**S9 (清稗類鈔, Hu Linyi, c.1859+)**: 「如放餉十兩，以五兩實銀、十千實錢為率，時市銀易錢，每兩千有五百文，糧臺漲價，竟至五百文，人不厭其重也。文忠意謂以錢易銀，徒為商賈謀利，不如暗益員勇」. (translation) Pay of ten taels was issued as five taels in silver and 10,000 cash. The market rate was 1,500 a tael, but the commissariat raised the rate by as much as 500 (to 2,000), and men did not mind the weight. Hu reasoned that changing cash into silver only enriched merchants, better to quietly benefit officers and troops.

**S3 (Morse, citing Jamieson's Honan report, 1905)**: "in another the amount in taels is converted into cash at 2,600 to the tael, and converted back into taels at 1,105, being an addition of 135 per cent, and then 50 per cent, is added for 'cost of collection.' … cases are common and well known where the conversion into cash was at the rate of between 5,000 and 6,000, with the effect of increasing the land tax to over five times the statutory amount." Also: "This, if too much, provokes a riot".

**S5 (Thirst for Silver)**, model outputs, not measurements: "the silver shock reduced China's aggregate welfare by 1.16%, with fiscal rigidity accounting for most of the loss"; a county at the 75th percentile of trade cost to Canton had "about 24% more conflicts than a county at the 25th percentile."

**Reading: ATTESTED ONLY FOR** taxpayers, local officials, the state and soldiers.

- **Taxpayers lost:** the tax measured in rice doubled, 3 dou to 6 dou a mu (Zeng, c.1852), with the rate going from 1,000 to 2,000. In 1905 Henan, the conversion rate used by the county added 135% (Morse).
- **Local officials:** lost on remitting silver (1846). They had profited when silver was cheap.
- **The state lost revenue:** Jiangsu arrears every year from 1836.
- **Soldiers paid in silver gained:** Hu Linyi paid at 2,000 against a market of 1,500.
- **Responses:** riots and tax resistance (four named cases; Zeng mentions 抗欠); advance collection (截串); state remissions; proposals for big cash and paper, adopted in 1853.
- **Wage earners:** a real-wage effect is only the inference from 414 and S5 n.12 (cash wages and prices flat); no new measurement.
- **Xianfeng inflation:** losers were holders of notes and big cash (Beijing 1850s); no measured real-wage figure was found.
