# Search pass, feature 428 Q2: were guards and scribes hired by the job or kept on by the year?

**Guards, China:** agency escorts (镖师) were kept-on staff on a monthly wage. They made about three or four trips a year, and between trips the agency hired them out to guard houses.
**Guards, Japan:** no armed escort hired just for one trip was found. Cash went with the courier house's own convoy leader (宰領飛脚), who carried a sword himself. The "hired for a run, then back to his trade" pattern does exist, but only for unarmed village couriers.
**Scribes, China:** writing was paid by the document, so a day or two of work is attested.
**Scribes, Japan:** the writers found were kept on by the year, apart from legal petitions written for a fee per case. No scribe hired by the day was found.

It is a modern scholar's summary of Liu Jiansheng 2014 and Ji Kongrui 1986, covering the Qianlong reign (1736–95) onward.

**Scribes, China**

8. **You Chenjun 尤陈俊, 「清代讼师贪利形象的多重建构」**, https://www.aisixiang.com/data/98382.html. This is the best-attested case: every fee is per document.
   - 「在四川南部县，道光三十年（1850）规定"每张止给笔资钱一百文"」 (translation: in Nanbu county, Sichuan, 1850, the rule was "only 100 cash per sheet for writing").
   - 「道光年间南汇县的官代书顾治…为人誊写呈状，每张只收一百文钱，但如果是操刀为人作词，则是每张收取洋钱一元或者四五百文钱」 (translation: in the Daoguang era, 1821–50, Nanhui's official scribe Gu Zhi charged 100 cash a sheet to fair-copy a plaint, but a silver dollar or 400–500 cash a sheet to compose one).
   - 「徽州地区嘉庆年间…官代书每写一份词状要收取"写状银五钱"」 (translation: in Huizhou in the Jiaqing era, 1796–1820, an official scribe charged 0.5 tael for each plaint).
   - 「清代各地官府所定的官代书写状收费标准…通常都是在钱100文至300文之间。一名官代书如果完全照此收费标准靠写状谋生，那么通常只能维持类似于雇工的低下生活水准」 (translation: the official rates were usually 100–300 cash a plaint; a scribe living on those alone would have about a hired labourer's standard of living).
   - On the casual trade: 「还有很多偶尔为人代写词状的下层识字人士其实只收取较低的酬劳」 (translation: many lower-class literate people who occasionally wrote plaints for others charged little). Also 「算命先生那样偶尔也替人撰写词状的卑微之辈」 (translation: humble people such as fortune-tellers who sometimes also wrote plaints).

## Reading

**Guards, China: ATTESTED ONLY FOR the escort agency's own kept-on staff (Qing, especially 1736–1914).**
- The 镖师 was the agency's man, with board and lodging and about 4–5 taels a month plus a year-end share. That figure is undated and from a popular source.
- He made three or four trips a year of about three months each (Chircop-Reyes). Clients paid the agency per trip or by contract, settled each first month.
- In idle spells the agency hired him out as a house guard. A blocked-road escort might instead stay home teaching pupils (倪孝, 1869–70).
- "Hired by the job" applies to the agency, not the man: clients engaged an agency for one trip, and the agency's men could all be "out on hire". Hiring loose fighters for one trip is attested only for the early Qing (Pu Songling's twenty lancers). Chircop-Reyes says merchants came to prefer agency men from known martial lineages over "vagrants".

**Guards, Japan: ATTESTED ONLY FOR the courier house's own convoy.**
- Cash (500 and 900 ryō shipments robbed in 1856 and 1860) travelled under the courier house's convoy leader, and the cash courier wore his own short sword.
- No armed escort hired for a single trip, and no rōnin hired to guard a shipment, was found. 用心棒 is attested only as a word, from the 1740s.
- The "hired when needed, other work in between" pattern is ATTESTED, but for unarmed couriers: a rice-cake maker, a tinker and a Buddhist-image maker were paid per run (64 mon in 1852) in 1849–62. The village's regular courier, by contrast, was on a yearly stipend (4 ryō in Kiryū in 1820; 12 kan 800 mon in Fukushima village in 1866).

**Scribes, China: ATTESTED.** Writing was paid by the document, 1796–1900:
- 100–300 cash a plaint by official rate, often more in practice; 0.5 tael a plaint in Jiaqing-era Huizhou.
- Gu Zhi charged 100 cash a sheet to copy and a silver dollar to compose.
- Fortune-tellers and others wrote plaints now and then on the side.
- A scribe living only on official fees earned about a labourer's living.
- No street letter-writer's per-letter price from before 1912 was found.

**Scribes, Japan: ATTESTED ONLY FOR petition-writing by the case**, by litigants' inns and illegal suit-brokers, for a fee whose size was not found. Every other writer found was kept on: the Edo ward clerk (from 1721, on a ward wage plus watch money) and the village courier (yearly pay). No scribe or copyist hired for a day was found; 0305 already records no Japanese copyist's rate. What I searched:
- **Archive terms:** 代書, 代筆, 筆耕, 祐筆, 書役, 日傭, letter writer, write letters.
- **Queries:** 江戸 代書 / 代筆 料金, 筆耕 一丁 賃, 村 書役 日当 雇, 自身番 書役 給金, plus English queries on Tokugawa letter-writing.
- **Pages read that did not bear:** kotobank 筆耕 (its per-sheet rate is from 1900); kotobank 町飛脚 and 宰領 (they define the jobs, not the terms of hire).
- **Not used:** an archived Skinner page puts a letter-writer on a market circuit, but that is his 1949–50 Sichuan fieldwork, a modern observation, so I left it out.
