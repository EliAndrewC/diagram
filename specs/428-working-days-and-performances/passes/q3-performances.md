## Feature 428 Q3: how often players performed, by kind of troupe

Japan's licensed city theaters played by daylight only. They staged a handful of named programs a year, not one a month, and closed in the 6th and 7th months. Shrine-ground theaters had licenses of 100 fair-weather days. Country troupes played short festival runs of one to fifteen days. No source shows anyone playing every night. The only steady six-days-a-week playing I found is London's companies, and even they stopped for Lent, plague and summer.

### Sources

**(a) City playhouses, Japan**

1. **新版歌舞伎事典「興行」** (Shinpan Kabuki Jiten, "Performances"), https://japanknowledge.com/contents/kabuki/sousetsu/06kogyo.html. Modern reference work describing Edo-period practice. Saved at /tmp/l7r-pass/q428-jk-kogyo.
   - 「毎年一一月が新年度の初めで、役者との契約期間も、一一月から翌年一〇月までの一年間とした。そこで、年度最初の興行を〈顔見世興行〉と名づけ」
     (translation) "Every year the 11th month began the new season, and actors' contracts ran for one year, from the 11th month to the 10th month of the next year. The season's first program was called the kaomise program."
   - 「江戸の例でいうと、初春興行の曾我狂言に立てた大名題はなるべくそのまま残し、三月の〈弥生興行〉、五月の〈皐月興行〉には、一番目の不評の場を抜いて二番目、三番目を出していき、五月二八日の曾我祭まで行くことができれば大成功としたものである。しかし、三月三日初日で…《助六》などを出し、五月五日からまた狂言を差し替えることが多かった。」
     (translation) "In Edo, the main title of the New Year Soga play was kept as long as possible. For the 3rd-month (Yayoi) and 5th-month (Satsuki) programs, unpopular scenes were cut and second and third parts added. Running all the way to the Soga festival on 5/28 counted as a great success. Often, though, a new bill opened on 3/3 (Sukeroku and the like) and was changed again from 5/5."
   - 「六、七月は原則として芝居を休んだが、後には〈夏芝居〉といって、ふだんは大役をもらえない低い地位の役者を中心にし、入場料の安い勉強芝居を興行することがあった。文化・文政期になると、大立者も参加して…九月は〈秋興行〉…年度における最後の興行であることから〈御名残興行〉ともいった。」
     (translation) "As a rule the theaters rested in the 6th and 7th months. Later there were 'summer plays': cheap practice programs built around junior actors who did not usually get big roles. In the Bunka–Bunsei era (1804–30) leading actors joined in too. The 9th month was the autumn program, also called the farewell program because it was the last of the season."
   - 「六月の土用休み」
     (translation) "the dog-days rest of the 6th month"
   - 「劇場が毎月一興行を行うようになったのは大正以後のことで」
     (translation) "Theaters began staging a new program every month only after the Taishō era began [1912]."

2. **ja.wikipedia 顔見世** (Kaomise), https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/顔見世. Edo-period practice. Saved at /tmp/l7r-pass/q428-kaomise.

   - 「顔見世興行はたいてい10日間ほどの短期で、次に出す狂言を「二の替り狂言」といい、このほうに重きが置かれていた。」
     (translation) "The kaomise program was usually short, about 10 days. The next play was called the 'second change' play, and it carried more weight."

**(b) Shrine-ground and small theaters**

**England (comparative)**

    - 1592 at the Rose: "played six days a week for a period of eighteen weeks, during which they only missed Good Friday and two other days… the hundred and five performances… Twenty-three plays in all were given, for any number of days from one to fifteen; the same play was rarely repeated in any one week." Except in Lent or at the beginning or end of a season, or occasionally… especially in December, plays were given upon every week-day… there were no plays on Sundays… The total number of performances during the three years was seven hundred and twenty-eight… new plays to the total number of fifty-five, and at the average rate of one a fortnight."

### Reading

**(a) City playhouse — ATTESTED.**
- **Per day:** one program a day, by daylight only. The kaomise first drum was at 4 a.m.; a modern museum gives about 6:00 to 17:00. Night playing was impossible until gas light came in 1878. No source I read states the exact afternoon closing hour.
- **Per year:** five or six named programs: kaomise (11th month, about 10 days), then the "second change" bill, New Year, 3rd month, 5th month, and the autumn farewell program in the 9th month. A good run lasted from New Year to 5/28; often the bill changed on 3/3 and 5/5. A new program every month began only after 1912.
- **Idle months:** the 6th and 7th months by rule (the dog-days rest), later partly filled by cheap summer plays with junior actors. The 10th month of contract-making was probably dark too, but that is my inference from the calendar; no source says so.
- **Rain and fire:** no play in rain. Actors went touring when fire shut the house.
- **Mourning:** music-and-dance bans (narimono chōji) on a shogun's death are ATTESTED. How many days they closed the theaters is SILENT.
- **Puppet theaters (Takemoto-za, Toyotake-za):** SILENT on their calendar. I searched 竹本座 in the archive and one web query.

**(b) Shrine-ground theaters — ATTESTED ONLY FOR the license term.** Licenses were for 100 fair-weather days, granted each time, sometimes renewed back to back. They existed in the three cities and at provincial shrines. How many licenses a provincial town got a year is SILENT. The record's Kotohira figure (about 3 runs a year before 1835) is the nearest.

**(c) Country troupes — ATTESTED ONLY FOR short festival engagements.**
- Kinds of engagement: village plays at the tutelary festival, rain prayers or Bon, one or three days each; a bought-in troupe for 3–4 days, twice a year (Shima, 1855–67); a market-town run of about 9 fair days with about 15 shows day and night (1728).
- Frequency: Hamanoichi bought in kabuki 15 times in about 45 years.
- Not found: how many engagements a troupe filled in a year, and an Edo-period 100-day license for touring troupes (the samurai-archives page with that claim returned 503 and was not read).

**China — ATTESTED.**
- **Beijing theaters (c. 1800):** played from after noon to about 5–7 p.m., no night plays. Before 1900 troupes rotated among theaters every four days.
- **Shanghai (late Qing):** night shows existed. Trade fell off in the 12th month.
- **Village and temple-fair troupes:** hired for about 3 days. Fairs lasted 1–15 days and clustered in the 3rd–5th months. Guild-hall stages: SILENT.
- **Mourning:** imperial mourning stopped plays; at least about 100 days in 1881.

**England — ATTESTED.**
- **Time and week:** one performance a day at 2 or 3 p.m. (Platter 1599, Dawes 1614), six days a week, no Sundays.
- **Repertory (1592):** 23 plays in 18 weeks, the same play rarely twice in a week. **New plays (1594–97):** one about every fortnight.
- **Per year:** about 243 performances a year (728 over three years; my arithmetic).
- **Closures:** about 5–6 weeks for Lent and 2–3 months in summer (spent touring), plus plague bans: playing was allowed once deaths fell to 30 a week (c. 1604 patent).
- **Winter house:** the King's men used the Blackfriars as winter quarters from 1608.

**Canon — NOT ATTESTED.** The canon says a county troupe earns most of its money on the weekly market day and tours the village districts the rest of the week. No Japanese or Chinese source shows a weekly rhythm. Every rural passage shows episodic, festival-tied hires of one to fifteen days, a few times a year per place, concentrated in particular seasons. What is attested is that touring troupes existed and were bought in by villages and towns. The only regular weekly playing found anywhere is London's permanent companies, and they too stopped for Lent, plague and summer.
