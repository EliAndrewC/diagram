Feature 428, Q1: how many days a year each kind of worker worked, and their days off.

Only two kinds of worker have a day count: Japanese farm servants, and Japanese villages' collective rest days. The other three come back without a count. The Allen et al. 250 days is an assumption, not a count. I could not get at the one Chinese study that counts days by trade.

## Sources that bear on the question

**A. Kumon, Yuzuru, "The Labor Intensive Path: Wages, Incomes and the Work Year in Japan, 1610-1932"** (CIRJE-F-1154 working paper, August 2020)
- URL: https://www.cirje.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/research/dp/2020/2020cf1154.pdf
- I read it with curl and pdftotext into `/tmp/claude-1000/-diagram/9418dde1-1f91-4bdd-8ff9-f511af914058/scratchpad/cirje/k.txt`, because `make source-pages` could not read the PDF.
- The published version is in the Journal of Economic History, titled "...in Japan, 1610-1890". I did not read that version.
- Period of the evidence: rest-day clauses in servant contracts from Harima, Kai and Kanto villages, 1780-1840. The paper claims about 325 days a year from 1700 to 1910.

Passages:
- "I can investigate this using a small subset of these wage contracts (83 observation) which include stipulations on the number of days of rest with which I can estimate the length of the work year. Most of these contracts would state the number of days of rest per month resulting in large clusters of observations between 2-4 days of rest per month. Unfortunately, it is unclear if servants additionally rested during the national holidays and these are not added in my estimates. If they additionally got these holidays, they would have perhaps rested for an additional 3 days for new year, 3 days to honor the spirit of their ancestors during the bon holidays, and 5 days for the gosekku coming to a total of 11 additional days."
- "Such contracts often specified 15 days a month of work but there was some variation." This describes a separate kind of contract: a servant hired for about half the days of each month.
- "They worked for 310-330 days a year which is significantly more than the standard in modern society ... the estimates suggest Eastern Japan had a shorter work year than Western Japan by perhaps 10 days."
- "The day wage is often considered to include a risk premium, as such laborers were not guaranteed jobs on any given day."
- Figure 9 source line: "Servant wage dataset, Clark and Van Der Werf (1998), Abe (1998) Saito (1998)". These are the Saito village surveys from the brief; I only saw them through Kumon.

**D. Allen, Bassino, Ma, Moll-Murata and van Zanden: the 250 days.**
- Archived copy of LSE wp123, https://www.lse.ac.uk/asset-library/information/wp123.pdf
  - "We assume that one year's work consisted of 250 days – roughly full time work allowing for holidays, illness, and slack periods."
  - Also: "A woman spinning and weaving cotton for 200 days per year, which Li and Pomeranz both reckon was about the maximum possible, could earn 14.61 taels". This is a scholars' estimate for Qing Jiangnan, not a count.
- Bassino and Ma (archived, econhistdbm), note 6: "daily nominal wage multiplied by 250, assuming 250 working days and no other income".
- Liu 2022, LSE WP-339 (archived):
  - "the welfare ratio approach assumes a fixed length of the working year (250 days) for all countries."
  - "Li (2008) suggests that average working days may have considerably increased in the first half of the nineteenth century."
  - On Ming-Qing government accounts: "'yearly' payments in local gazetteers are day rates multiplied by a theoretical length of 360 days working ... a year-long labour service was usually divided into three or four terms, and each term can be 10 days or a season long (90 days)." This is a bookkeeping convention, not a count of days worked.

## Reading, per kind of worker

- **(5) Farm servants: ATTESTED.** In Harima, Kai and Kanto, 1780-1840, live-in term farm servants' contracts gave 2-4 rest days a month, about 24-48 a year, which means 310-330 working days. Kumon reckons eastern Japan about 10 days shorter. Kumon says it is unclear whether the roughly 11 New Year, Bon and seasonal-festival days came on top.
- **Village rest days, Japan: ATTESTED, but only through summaries of 古川.** Collective village rest days were about 20 a year around 1700. Later in the Edo period they were 20-30, or 30-60 depending on the source. After 1800 they rose in places to 50-80, partly because of demands from hired servants and young men's associations.
  - Do not add these to Kumon's figures. Kumon's are one servant's contract rest days; these are rest days for the whole village. The sources do not say whether a hired servant got both.
- **(2) Live-in servants: ATTESTED ONLY FOR the two yabuiri days.** Merchant-house servants got 1/16 and 7/16, Edo period, sometimes "around" those dates. ニッポニカ says they were not the only days off, but no source gives a yearly total for shop servants. Rural term servants are covered by Kumon above.
- **(3) Porters and packhorse drivers: SILENT on a count or on how steady the work was.** The only seasonal evidence (Meguro): official traffic peaked in the farming season, so drafted villagers were called up exactly when they were busiest. Travel time was unpaid. Each station office took 10-day turns.
- **(4) Carpenters: SILENT on a count.** Only attested: weather idled them and their pay was not daily. The "rain days plus the 1st and 15th plus seasonal festivals" claim appeared only in search-result summaries. I could not find it on any page I read, so it is not reported as evidence. The Waraku article had nothing on rest days.
- **(1) Day laborers: SILENT on a count.** The only statements are Kumon's "not guaranteed jobs on any given day" and the weather sentence above.
- **China: the 250 days is an ASSUMPTION** in Allen et al., Bassino and Ma, and Liu. Qing government accounts used a notional 360 days. The only Chinese figure is the 200 days a year Li and Pomeranz reckoned as the most a Jiangnan woman spinner could work, which is an estimate. Otherwise SILENT, including on hired hands' (長工) days off.
