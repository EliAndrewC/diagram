Found: in north China a house kept the drip strip (dishui) on its own ground - 2 or 3 chi in Jiaxiang (Shandong, a custom "not Jiaxiang's alone"), 6 or 8 cun in Pingyao (Shanxi), from pre-1949 custom surveys the study calls disputed, and 3 chi behind a house in a Baoding deed of 1884 - and one Shanxi writer recalls his village's tiled houses' back eaves reaching out 2.2 chi and not a cun more; deeds that meet a street or road (Baoding 1809, Shanghai 1883, Taichung 1825, Huizhou 1692) name the road as the edge with no strip; in Japan a village house in Mino (Ono district) stood back about 3 ft from the boundary of a road or of cultivated land ("shade-withdrawal", kage-hiki; Wigmore 1892 p. 30, the digit checked on the page image), other districts set a village house back so its rain fell on its own land, and town houses stood close together or up to the highway line; no page gives how far a Japanese farmhouse's eaves projected.
