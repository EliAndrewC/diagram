R40 ACCURATE - Edo domains set boat watch posts (funabansho) at busy landings to inspect boats and collect taxes, and the best-recorded (Nakagawa, 1661) was a fenced compound of about 155 by 100 ft with ten spears and a watch hut at the river's edge, manned by retainers; the setting taxes goods where sold, not in transit (canon), so a watch here inspects and takes no toll (a deviation); no page describes a watch at a local office's own landing - `river watch` on Hayakawa is the river-edge watch hut, cited to funabansho-jawiki and koto-nakagawa-funabansho, its size and its place at an office's landing a guess, Entry cities/river-cities 'Who kept watch on the river at an official's landing, and what did the watch post look like?'.
R41 KNOB - the funadama is the boat's own guardian, kept aboard at the foot of the mast (the encyclopedias differ: bow or midships) and only sometimes given a shrine on land; what river boatmen kept ashore, where recorded, was a water god (suijin, a Suitengu) - `boatmen's altar` on Hayakawa is one small shrine whose dedication (a land shrine to the boats' guardian, or a boatmen's water-god shrine) is a knob that names it; its place at the landing and size are a guess; Entry cities/river-cities 'Did the boatmen keep a shrine at the landing?'.
R42 ACCURATE - the stepped landing (gangi) is the norm and the pier the exception for a shelving bank (pointing at river-cities 040); on Hiroshima's rivers there were public and small private stepped landings, a back-gate landing was the merchant quarters' form, and samurai residences stood back from the revetment behind their walls with no gated landing - `dock` and `revetment` on Hayakawa: the compound's wall stands back behind the bank street, its landing is steps cut in the revetment across that street, reached from a gate in the wall, never a back gate onto the steps; the compound's own (not public) landing is a guess; Entry cities/river-cities 'Does an official's compound have its own stepped landing, or a pier?'.
R43 CONTRADICTION-RESOLVED - a bale (tawara) had no standard size before Meiji, rice included (2 to 5 to by time and place; 4-to and 6-to bales), and charcoal was packed by grade at 4, 8 or 10 kan; a 60 kg rice bale is about 75 by 47 cm (2.5 by 1.5 ft) and a museum charcoal bale 63 by 39 by 32 cm (about 2 by 1.3 by 1 ft) - urban-features 150 said the charcoal bale had "no standard weight ... unlike rice", uncited; corrected in this session (rice bales varied too; charcoal packed by grade), its absence note converted to a tawara-unit-jawiki citation - `tax barge` and `charcoal bales` on Hayakawa and Ubame: a bale drawn at ~4 ft is a convention, real length ~2.5 ft (charcoal ~2 ft); Entry urban-features 'How big is a bale of rice or charcoal?'.
R44 ACCURATE - the balance served valuables; everyday and bulk goods were weighed on the steelyard (Japan: in use from Edo, sizes to 16 kan; China: the traditional tool, an old one 2.2 m long to 175 kg); the platform scale came only in Meiji - `balance beam` on Ubame should be a steelyard (beam with a hook for the bale and a sliding weight), its frame or post a guess; the kind's name should change; Entry urban-features 'Was bulk charcoal weighed on a balance beam or a steelyard?'.
R45 SILENT - no page read says charcoal was kept in a plastered storehouse or gives a gap between a storehouse and its neighbors (absence note dated 2026-09-27); what is read is that the storehouse was built to protect its contents against fire (gunpowder among them), with ~1 ft walls and fire doors, and the 30 ft gap is this project's derivation for an open stack - `charcoal store` on Ubame: a sealed plaster kura takes ordinary spacing, not the 30 ft (labeled a guess resting on the storehouse's recorded purpose); Entry urban-features 'Does the charcoal yard's 30 ft fire gap apply to charcoal kept in a plastered storehouse?'.
R46 ACCURATE - Edo Japan kept carts in the towns and off the highways (ways 020), but the hand cart spread through the castle towns and beyond by late Edo, its bed about 8 by 2.5 ft; the GM's canon puts wagons and carts on the roads between towns (caravan wagons, a cartload of charcoal on a road, ox-drawn haywagons) - `cart yard`, `side gate` and `road` on Hayakawa and Ubame are a deviation from Edo Japan, from the setting's canon (cite l7r-wagons), no longer a conflict with ways 020; a ~6 ft cart lane is a guess; Entry ways 'Could a cart use the roads here, and where would it go?'.
R47 SILENT - no page read gives the width of the road before an official's or samurai residence's gate (absence note dated 2026-09-27); the highways were 3 to 4 ken (18 to 24 ft), 5 ken near Edo, and the Saigoku road through Hiroshima about 2.5 ken (15 ft) - `road` on Ochiba, Hayakawa and Ubame: a compound's front gate takes the width of the road it stands on (15 to 24 ft for a highway, researched), any wider forecourt and a side or cart lane (~6 ft) a guess; Entry ways 'How wide is the road at a compound's gate?'.
