I found useful figures for a city star's pay, for what a village paid to hire a touring troupe and how big that troupe was, and for Qing troupe sizes. I found nothing for what a street performer took in a day or what any instrument cost, and stage costumes only at the extremes. Nothing was edited in the repository. The ledger now has three outcomes from me: 06kogyo (nothing-found), guocuijingju (unreadable) and crd.ndl (rejected). The pages that bear on the question are left for you to record as cited.

## Sources that bear on the question

**1. A city star's yearly pay: Japan, Edo period**

- *Shinpan Kabuki Jiten* (a kabuki encyclopedia), https://japanknowledge.com/contents/kabuki/sousetsu/04yakusha.html
  - Covers the whole Edo period, with a cap in the Kansei era (1789-1801).
  - 「役者の給金（→身上(しんしょう)）は、江戸時代には年給で定められ、最高額の基準を千両としたことから〈千両役者〉の称も生まれた。実際には、千両を超す年給を得る役者もいた。寛政期（1789‐1801）に、あまりの年給高騰に音をあげた興行者側が幕府に願い出て、命令によって最高額を五百両に押さえるという事態も招いた。それでもなお、加役料・よない金などの名目で給金の上乗せが行われ、実質は七百両、八百両を取る役者が何人もあり、やがてもとのままに復してしまう。」
  - (translation) An actor's pay was set as a yearly wage in the Edo period, and the top benchmark was 1,000 ryō, hence "thousand-ryō actor." Some actors in fact earned more than 1,000 a year. In the Kansei era the theater managers, unable to bear rising pay, petitioned the shogunate, which capped it at 500 ryō. Even so, pay was topped up under names such as extra-role fees and night money, several actors took 700 or 800 in practice, and the cap soon lapsed.
  - The same page says actors were socially 「河原者・河原乞食…士農工商の四民以下」 (translation: "riverbed people, riverbed beggars … below the four classes"). This matches the canon's hinin.
- kotobank 千両役者 (Heibonsha encyclopedia, by 井草利夫), https://kotobank.jp/word/千両役者
  - Covers 1711-36.
  - 「江戸時代，それぞれの役者は11月から翌年10月までの1年間契約で劇場の専属となったが，その給金が1000両の高額に達した者を千両役者という。その最初は正徳・享保期（1711-36）の初世芳沢あやめや2世市川団十郎といわれる。」
  - (translation) Each actor was bound to a theater by a one-year contract from the 11th month to the 10th of the next year; one whose pay reached 1,000 ryō was called a thousand-ryō actor. The first are said to have been Yoshizawa Ayame I and Ichikawa Danjūrō II in 1711-36.
  - The *Nihon Kokugo Daijiten* on the same page gives the earliest use of the word as 柳多留 13 (1778).
- Fukami patent office essay, https://www.fukamipat.gr.jp/discusses/1908/
  - This is **tertiary**: a patent attorney's blog relaying 赤坂治績, who relays 『芝居乗合話』 (Kansei era). I did not read that primary source.
  - 「年間8000両…のあがりのうち、役者の給金におよそ6000両，道具類の制作費、衣装費用等に1000両を費やしたといいます。…座の役者はおよそ60名程度」 and 「一人年間25両から50両」 for ordinary actors. It adds that top actors supplied their own costumes and paid backstage staff.
  - (translation) Of about 8,000 ryō a year in takings, about 6,000 went on actors' pay and 1,000 on props, costumes and the like. A company had about 60 actors; ordinary actors got 25-50 ryō a year each.

**2. What a village paid a hired touring troupe, and its size: Japan, Shima province, 1855-1867**

- 橋本, 「近世・近代の鳥羽志摩地方における芸能興行の実態と変遷」, Mie University master's thesis, https://mie-u.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/7030/files/2015M004.pdf
  - The author's given name is garbled in the text layer, so I give the surname only.
  - `make source-pages` could not read the PDF. I extracted the text locally to /tmp/claude-1000/-diagram/9b9d9dec-de8d-4c07-9afd-3bc1cbbaa84b/scratchpad/mie/m.txt.
  - Quotes are as the text layer has it. 「梯」 is OCR for 払, so 請梯控帳 reads 請払控帳.
  - **These are puppet troupes (人形芝居), not acting troupes.**
  - 1857, the Ura village account book: 「一︑金弐両三歩ト拾弐匁壱分六厘 右者紙谷七太夫座人形十一日ヨリ十三日迄中飯迄雑用花代共梯申候」
    - (translation) 2 ryō 3 bu of gold and 12.16 monme: paid to the Kamiya Shichidayū puppet company for the 11th through the 13th, lunches, sundries and 花代 included.
  - 1860:
    - 「金三歩 右者紙谷七太夫花代梯申候」 (translation: 3 bu, 花代 to Shichidayū)
    - 「金壱両壱歩拾弐匁六分四厘 右同断人数弐拾人四宿雑用人梯申候」 (translation: 1 ryō 1 bu and 12.64 monme, the same company, sundries for 20 people for 4 nights)
    - 「十八匁 右同断蒲団借り賃」 (translation: 18 monme, hire of bedding, same)
  - 1867:
    - 「金壱両壱歩 右者山本善太夫座花代梯申候」 (translation: 1 ryō 1 bu, 花代 to the Yamamoto Zendayū company)
    - 「金三両壱歩九分九厘 右同断弐宿十八人造用梯申候」 (translation: 3 ryō 1 bu 9 bun 9 rin, the same company, costs for 18 people for 2 nights)
    - The author sums this up: 「十八人組であり︑雇入れ宿代などの費用も約四両以上村で支払っていた」 (translation: an 18-person company, for whose hire and lodging the village paid about 4 ryō or more).
  - 1855: 益口太夫座に「弐両五匁七分三厘」を「能代と二日間の泊り代」 (translation: 2 ryō 5.73 monme to the Masuguchi company, as the performance charge and two nights' lodging). The author adds 「年二回…村で三両以上の大金を支払っている」 (translation: twice a year the village paid out a large sum of 3 ryō or more).
  - 1832, 河内村: 「座本紙谷七太夫︑拾八人組ニ而今日興行仕候」 (translation: the company of Kamiya Shichidayū, eighteen strong, performs today).
  - **Caveats:**
    - The author frames 花代 as one of the sundries paid 「公演料以外に」 (on top of the performance fee). Yet no quoted line sets out a performance fee by itself; the nearest, 1855's 能代, is lumped in with lodging.
    - Entries mix gold and silver (ryō/bu with monme), so I did not convert them.
    - 「金三両壱歩九分九厘」 is ambiguous (the monme unit seems to be missing), so I did no arithmetic on it.
  - Arithmetic (mine): at the author's "about 4 ryō or more" for 18 people in 1867, the village's whole outlay was about 0.22 ryō or more per performer per visit, mostly board.
  - Post-1868, one line only: in Meiji the thesis shows small 小芝居 of 4-5 actors and puppet troupes of 3-6. These are modern and are not evidence for the Edo period.

**3. Troupe size and hiring by officials: Qing China**

- 清稗類鈔 (Qing anecdotes), 戲劇類, archived (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/清稗類鈔/089, /diagram/.specify/source-archive/7f/7f64d40f7e44/20261019T100004Z/text.txt)
  - Luantan opera, Qianlong era (1736-95): 「亂彈者，乾隆時始盛行之，聚八人或十人，鳴金伐鼓，演唱亂彈戲文」
    - (translation) Luantan became popular in the Qianlong era: eight or ten people gather, beat gongs and drums, and sing luantan plays.
  - Flower-drum opera, Shanghai, 1862-1908: 「同、光間，上海城中西園之隙地，有花皷戲，演者集三四人，男擊鑼，婦打兩頭鼓，和以胡琴、篴板」
    - (translation) In the Tongzhi and Guangxu eras, on open ground by the West Garden in Shanghai, there was flower-drum opera: three or four performers, the man beating a gong, the woman a two-headed drum, accompanied by fiddle and flute.
  - Costume upper bound, a past case: 「昔時排一新本，必以多金特製之。如淮商排《桃花扇》一劇，費至十六萬金之多」
    - (translation) Formerly a new play's costumes and props were made specially at great cost; when the Huai salt merchants staged *The Peach Blossom Fan* they spent as much as 160,000 in money.
  - Court stars, Guangxu era: 「名優均須進內當差…傳差一次，賞銀二十兩，若譚鑫培、羅百歲等，歲且食俸米二十石」
    - (translation) Famous actors had to serve at court; each summons was rewarded with 20 taels of silver, and the likes of Tan Xinpei received a stipend of 20 shi of rice a year as well.
- Prince Gong's Mansion Museum, 「清代王府演戏」, https://www.pgm.org.cn/pgm/xsyjou/200905/09b229c3dc224f7c9d56161babe0693d.shtml
  - Edict of 嘉慶四年 (1799): 「民間扮演戲劇，原以藉謀生計。地方官偶遇年節，雇覓外間戲班演唱，原所不禁。若署內自養戲班，則習俗攸關，奢靡妄費」
    - (translation) Popular play-acting is a way of earning a living. For local officials to hire an outside troupe now and then at festivals is not forbidden; to keep a troupe in the yamen is a matter of morals and extravagance.
    - This bears directly on the canon's special performances at the magistrate's manor.
  - Fragment of the 雍正二年 (1724) edict on officials keeping actors: the page's text breaks mid-sentence and resumes at 「二三十人，一年所費，不止數千金」 (translation: "twenty or thirty people; a year's cost no less than several thousand"). The join is broken, so the subject is not certain.
- Petcharapiruch, "Within the Palatial Compound", *Thammasat Review*, https://sc01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/tureview/article/download/40806/33798
  - I extracted the text locally to /tmp/claude-1000/-diagram/9b9d9dec-de8d-4c07-9afd-3bc1cbbaa84b/scratchpad/ql/q.txt.
  - It quotes 國朝宮史 (18th century) on the staffing of the court theater offices 南府 and 景山. These are eunuch posts, so this is the court eunuch performers' rate, not an open-market actor's pay.
  - Supervisor: 「總管一員，七品…每月銀五兩、米五斛」 (translation: 5 taels of silver and 5 hu of rice a month).
  - Rank-and-file eunuchs: 「太監，無定額。每月銀二兩、米一斛」 (translation: 2 taels and 1 hu of rice a month).

## Reading

**ATTESTED ONLY FOR** the following cases.

- **City star pay (attested):** about 1,000 ryō a year in the 1710s-30s, sometimes more; capped at 500 in the 1790s but 700-800 in practice. Ordinary city actors got 25-50 ryō, but that comes only from the tertiary source.
- **Rural hire and troupe size:** attested only for puppet troupes in Shima fishing villages, 1855-67. A village paid 2 to 4-plus ryō per visit, twice a year, for 18-20 performers for 2-4 nights, covering 花代, board, lodging and bedding. How the company shared the money is not shown, and the fee itself cannot be separated from the board.
- **Troupe size in China:** a Qing touring luantan troupe was 8 or 10, and a flower-drum act 3 or 4. This sits squarely with the canon's 5-10, against the city companies of 43 and 26 in record 0266. Officials hiring outside troupes at festivals is attested in the 1799 edict.
- **Costumes:** only the extremes (the 160,000 *Peach Blossom Fan* outlay; city stars supplying their own costumes). The thesis records an 1862 Okina costume the village gave the company, but the amount is water-damaged. As a cloth proxy only, record 0261 gives a tan of cotton at about 4-9 monme (1781-90).
- **SILENT:**
  - A street performer's daily take. I ran three queries (大道芸/辻芸 稼ぎ, 清代 撂地 收入, 江戸 大道芸 投げ銭) and grepped the archived 願人坊主 and 広小路 texts; none gives a sum.
  - Prices for a shamisen, flute, kotsuzumi or taiko. Five queries in Japanese and English returned modern listings, a drum-skin receipt with no amount, and nothing on the Morisada text.
  - A Japanese ordinary-costume price.
