Feature 431, pass E: the proverb, and why women's household work went unrecorded.

I couldn't find the GM's proverb in its 360-day form. Both halves of it are documented, though, and the closest attestation is in a farming manual. On the second question, Japanese historians say plainly why women's work went unrecorded. For China I found only partial statements, and none reached the household-work claim itself.

## Q6: the "rise before dawn 360 days and the family will be rich" proverb

**Sources that bear on it**

1. **農桑衣食撮要 卷下, 「通俗直說」**, by 魯明善 (Yuan dynasty), https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/農桑衣食撮要/卷下. The Siku summary on the work's main page dates it: 「於延祐甲寅，出監壽郡，始撰是書」 (translation: "in the jiayin year of Yanyou [1314], when he went out to supervise Shou commandery, he began writing this book"). It was reprinted in 1330. The passage closes the twelve-month farming almanac:
   「通俗直說四方風土氣候不同，凡務本者，宜順時而動。一家之計在和。父慈子孝，兄友弟恭，夫婦和睦，此家之肥也。一生之計在勤。"欲求生富貴，須下苦工夫。"人之常談，甚有理。一年之計在春。當春不用力，秋收何及？宜布種發生之時。一日之計在寅。晏眠[B083]早起，三朝當一工。居家以勤謹爲先。」
   [B083] is a character Wikisource could not encode, so I have not filled it in.
   (literal translation) "Plain words in common speech: climate differs in every region; whoever works at the root occupation [farming] should act with the seasons. A household's plan lies in harmony. Father kind, son filial, elder brother friendly, younger brother respectful, husband and wife in concord: this is what fattens a household. A lifetime's plan lies in diligence. 'If you want wealth and honour in life, you must put in bitter effort': people say this all the time, and it is very true. A year's plan lies in spring. If you do not exert yourself in spring, how can there be an autumn harvest? It is the time to sow and sprout. A day's plan lies in the yin hour [about 3-5 a.m.]. Go to bed late, [unreadable] rise early: three mornings make one day's work. In keeping a household, diligence and care come first."

2. **樓鑰 (Lou Yue, 1137-1213), 攻媿集 卷九, the poem 「午睡戲作」**, https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/攻媿集_(四部叢刊本)/卷第九. The text is Southern Song.
   「早起三朝當一工老來貪睡不相同偶然一次五更起卻用重眠到日中」
   (literal translation, title "Written in jest on a midday nap") "Rising early three mornings equals one day's work; / but old and greedy for sleep, it is not the same for me. / Once, by chance, I got up at the fifth watch, / and then slept again until noon."
   He quotes the line as something everyone already knows, so the saying is older than about 1200.

3. **通俗編 卷三「時序」**, a Qing proverb dictionary, https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/通俗編/03. The page is filed under Qing. I am supplying the author, 翟灝, from memory; the page I read does not confirm it.
   - 「【早起三朝當一工】〔宋樓論詩〕。」 (translation) "'Rising early three mornings equals one day's work': [from] a Song poem by Lou." The source tag is printed as 「宋樓論詩」, probably a garbling of 樓鑰詩.
   - 「【一年之計在於，一日之計在於晨】見〔梁元帝延纂要〕。〔婁元禮田家雜占〕作一日之計在於寅。」 (translation) "'A year's plan lies in [spring], a day's plan lies in the morning': see Emperor Yuan of Liang's *Zuanyao* [6th century]. Lou Yuanli's *Miscellaneous Divinations of the Farm Household* has 'a day's plan lies in the yin hour'." The page omits 春 and adds a stray 延; I have quoted it as printed.

4. **增廣賢文**, https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hans/增廣賢文. This is a composite text: the core is Ming, and it picked up additions under the Qing and in modern times. I did not check which layer these lines belong to.
   - 「一年之计在於春，一日之计在於晨；一家之计在於和，一生之计在於勤。」 (translation) "A year's plan lies in spring, a day's plan in the morning; a household's plan in harmony, a lifetime's plan in diligence."
   - 「早起三光，迟起三慌。」 (translation) "Rise early and three things go smoothly; rise late and three things go flustered."
   - 「勤俭持家富」 (translation) "Run the household diligently and thriftily and it grows rich."
   - 「田地勤耕足养家」 (translation) "Till the fields diligently and you will have enough to support the household."
   I have not dated the last three lines as Ming or Qing. The session should not cite them as premodern until that is checked.

5. **朱子家訓 (朱用純, born 1617, late Ming / early Qing)**, https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hans/朱子家訓.
   「黎明即起，洒扫庭除，要内外整洁。既昏便息，关锁门户，必亲自检点。」
   (translation) "Rise at first light, sprinkle and sweep the courtyard, keep inside and out clean and tidy. When dusk falls, rest; bar and lock the gates, and check them yourself."
   This is a gentry household rule. It says nothing about getting rich.

6. **Japan: 慶安御触書**, archived copy of https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/慶安御触書. This is Wikipedia's **modern Japanese paraphrase**, not the original wording. The same page records that the "1649" text is disputed, and that 山本英二 identifies its source as the 1697 Kōfu 「百姓身持之覚書」.
   「早起きをし、朝は草を刈り、昼は田畑を耕作し、夜は縄を綯い、俵を編むなど、それぞれの仕事を油断無く行うこと。男は農耕、女房は機織りに励み、夜なべをして夫婦ともよく働くこと。」
   (translation) "Rise early: cut grass in the morning, till the fields by day, twist rope and weave rice-bales at night, and do each task without slackening. The man should work hard at farming and the wife at the loom, working at night as well, husband and wife both labouring well."

**Reading.** **SILENT** for the proverb as the GM dictated it: "360 days" and "make the family rich" never appeared together, and I found no 三百六十 form. **ATTESTED ONLY FOR** its two halves. The first is "rising early adds labour": 早起三朝當一工 is already a commonplace when Lou Yue uses it around 1200. The second is "diligence makes the household rich": 一生之計在勤 / 欲求生富貴，須下苦工夫. Both sit side by side in Lu Mingshan's farming almanac of 1314, addressed to 務本者 (those who work the land). So the GM is right that this is a farming saying. The nearest premodern wording would be something like 一日之計在寅…早起，三朝當一工…居家以勤謹爲先 (1314). Japan has no premodern proverb on record here. The only dictionary entry I read for 早起きは三文の徳 (Kotobank) has a definition but no origin or date. A search summary says one origin theory traces it to Lou Yue's line, but I did not read the page behind that, so it is unverified.

## Q7: why women's household work went unrecorded, and how historians reconstruct it

**Sources that bear on it**

1. **牧原成征 (Makihara Shigeyuki), introducing 高埜利彦編『近世史講義：女性の力を問いなおす』 (ちくま新書, 2020)**, https://www.u-tokyo.ac.jp/biblioplaza/ja/F_00081.html. The subject is Tokugawa Japan.
   「最大の原因は史料にある。文字はもともと、支配者層が支配や統治のために普及させてきたという側面があり、現在までに残される史料も、当然、支配や統治、政治に関するものが多くなる。…そのため百姓の家も男性当主の労働が中心を占め、領主の御用や村の自治に関わる村役人もみな男性であった。近世には村に膨大な古文書が残されるようになるが、それを書いたのも男である村役人である。…こうした家や村、武力や力仕事が重視された社会では、男性が公的な役割を独占して史料を残し、女性の姿は見えにくくなる。」
   (translation) "The biggest cause lies in the sources. Writing spread partly because rulers needed it to govern, so the documents that survive are naturally mostly about rule, administration and politics. ... In the peasant household too the male head's labour was central, and the village officials who dealt with the lord's business and village self-government were all men. In the early-modern period villages came to leave enormous quantities of documents, but the people who wrote them were male village officials. ... In a society like this, which valued the household, the village, arms and heavy labour, men monopolised public roles and left the records, and women become hard to see."
   The book's table of contents also lists a chapter on 『孝義録』, the official compilation of commendations for virtuous conduct, as a source for women.

2. **一橋大学附属図書館 TZ〈ほんの窓〉 第11号「働く女性の江戸時代」 (2007)**, https://www.lib.hit-u.ac.jp/images/2019/12/tz_011.pdf. The subject is Tokugawa women's labour.
   - 「農業労働における女性の地位と役割を論じた長島淳子、都市の女子奉公を採り上げた片倉比佐子、名主の日記から妻や娘の家内での働きを明らかにする河野淳一郎と大口勇次郎の論文」
     (translation) "Nagashima Junko's paper on women's status and role in farm labour, Katakura Hisako's on urban female service, and the papers of Kōno Jun'ichirō and Ōguchi Yūjirō that bring to light the work of wives and daughters inside the house **from village headmen's diaries**."
   - 「文字として書かれ、現在まで残存しているのは行政や経済に関わる記録が中心であり、当主である男性の名前が数多く登場します。…史料の中から女性に関する記述が丹念に拾い出されてきました。」
     (translation) "What was written down and survives is mainly administrative and economic records, in which the names of male household heads appear in great numbers. ... Mentions of women have been painstakingly picked out of these sources."
   - The article also names historical demography based on **宗門改帳** (temple population registers; 速水融, 1988) and 薮田貫's work on women's service and marriage age. It summarises 長野ひろ子 (2003): when the small peasant household formed, 「女性労働が管理労働の大半から排除されていく」 (translation: "women's labour came to be excluded from most managerial work").
   - Comparative point only: Duby and Perrot's *Histoire des femmes* 「女性は公的・政治的な記録には残らない」 (translation: "women do not remain in public and political records"), and Joan Scott's argument that dividing labour by sex was itself a nineteenth-century discourse.

3. **Ann Waltner, review of Francesca Bray, *Technology and Gender* (H-Asia, April 2000)**, https://china.usc.edu/node/21628. The subject is China, about 1000-1800.
   "Textiles bound the inner quarters to the state, quite literally, through tax policies which until the Single Whip Reform of the late Ming collected tax in kind (grain and cloth) rather than in cash. When bolts of cloth were no longer demanded by the state as taxation, the meaning of women's work shifted, and, in Bray's words, 'the spatal seclusion of the inner quarters took on new meanings of separation and dependence (p.263).' These changes are accompanied by a gradual masculinization of textile manufacture. Bray does not argue that women were less involved in textile production, but rather suggests that their economic activity was masked."
   ("spatal" is a typo on the page.) This is Bray at second hand, through a reviewer.

4. **Kenneth Pomeranz, review of Li Bozhong, *Agricultural Development in Jiangnan, 1620-1850*** (archived; eh.net, text.txt lines 44 and 56). The subject is Ming-Qing Jiangnan. The date was not in the parts I read.
   - "women increasingly exited agriculture (in which they had never been very productive anyway), and earned more per day by moving into rapidly-growing textile trades"
   - "his claim that both male and female labor productivity rose significantly ... (The problems with these estimates are that a) the documentary base is fairly narrow ...)"

5. **李世众 (Li Shizhong), 「列女书写、妇德规训与地域秩序——以明清乐清县志为中心的考察」, 华东师范大学学报 2016 (4): 81-91**, https://xbzs.ecnu.edu.cn/CN/Y2016/V48/I4/81 (abstract only).
   「不同版本的明清县志《列女传》，在传记内容和叙事策略上存在着隐晦的差异。这些差异往往呈现出士绅的妇德塑造脉络」
   (translation) "Different editions of the Ming-Qing county gazetteer *Biographies of Exemplary Women* differ subtly in content and narrative strategy; these differences often reveal the gentry's project of shaping female virtue."

**Reading.** **ATTESTED** for Tokugawa Japan, **ATTESTED ONLY FOR** parts of the China claim. For Japan, Makihara (2020) and the Hitotsubashi survey (2007) both say directly that surviving records are administrative documents written by men for governing, organised around male household heads. They name what historians use instead:
- village headmen's diaries
- temple population registers (宗門改帳), via historical demography
- official commendation records (孝義録)
- prescriptive texts such as 女大学, read against the grain
- painstaking extraction from administrative papers

For China I have three narrower points:
- Bray, through Waltner's review: women's cloth was recorded as tax in kind until the late-Ming Single Whip reform, after which their economic activity was "masked".
- Pomeranz: Li Bozhong's estimates of women's productivity rest on "a fairly narrow" documentary base.
- Li Shizhong: gazetteer biographies of women were shaped by gentry ideas of virtue, not by women's daily work.

I found no China passage stating outright that women's unpaid housework "went unrecorded"; I could not reach Mann, Ko, Uno or Walthall. The 1649/1697 farm-household rule (Q6, item 6) also shows the prescriptive genre where wives' night weaving does get written down, as an instruction, not as a record.
