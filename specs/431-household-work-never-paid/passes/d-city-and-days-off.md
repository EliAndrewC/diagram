**Pass D (feature 431): what city households bought, and what workers did on days off**

The evidence is good for Edo and thin for Kyoto and Osaka. Rice polishing, firewood, seasonings, used clothing and baths were all bought in Edo. Laundry and mending were not shown to be bought. Nothing read tells a single tenement man's housework apart from a family man's, beyond one unsourced modern line. On days off, only the merchant-house live-ins are documented.

**What the record already holds.** 0311 has yabuiri (藪入り, the servants' two home-leave days) from the Kotobank entry and the Tokyo library's festival calendar, and Kumon's farm-servant rest days. 0307 has the Mitsui clerks' pay in kind and that clerks "were not allowed to have a household" until nearly 30 (40 in a great house). 0183 has the Edo street lot with back tenements behind the shop. None of these was searched again.

## Q4: what an Edo household bought, by item

**1. Rice polishing.** Archived copy `kotobank.jp/word/搗米屋` (World Encyclopedia, by Itō Kōichi). Evidence dated 1727 and 1744.
- 「江戸には1744年（延享1）当時18組，およそ2100人の搗米屋が存在し，臼5500ほどを使って営業していた。このほか搗米を営むものとして大道搗が，1727年（享保12）当時1100人ほどいた。」
- (translation) "In Edo in 1744 there were 18 groups, some 2,100 rice-polishers, working about 5,500 mortars. Besides these, street polishers numbered about 1,100 in 1727."
- 「消費者への米の販売は，河岸八町米仲買と脇店八ヵ所組米屋が玄米を，搗米屋が白米を売ること以外にはできなかった。また1744年以降は，農村で白米にして江戸市中へ売ることも禁じられていた。」
- (translation) "Rice could be sold to consumers only as brown rice by the dealers, or as white rice by the polishers. After 1744 it was also forbidden to polish rice in the villages and sell it in the city."

**2. The Bunsei-era household budgets (1818-30).** Archived copy `toyo.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/6852/files/sportskenkokagaku11_055-077.pdf`. This is a modern academic paper. It summarizes 『文政年間漫録』 (citing 未刊随筆百種 vol. 1, pp. 297-298); the original text was not reached.
- The vegetable peddler (a family man): 「彼は毎日仕事から帰ると米代として200文，味噌・醤油代として50文，子供の菓子代12〜13文をその日の稼ぎから取られ，残金は100〜200文程度であったという。」
  - (translation) "Every day when he came home from work, 200 mon for rice, 50 mon for miso and soy sauce, and 12-13 mon for the child's sweets were taken from his day's earnings, and 100-200 mon was left."
  - The paper places this peddler among 「江戸の裏長屋の住人を主な担い手とする棒手振」 (translation: "the peddlers, mostly residents of Edo's back tenements").
- The carpenter: 「文政期頃の大工の日収は飯料込みで銀5匁4分（540文）」; 「毎月の支出は，店賃(家賃）が10匁(1000文）で，食費は夫婦に子ども1人として約30匁(3000文),調味料と薪の代金が合わせて58匁（5800文)，その他に道具代，家具代，衣装代等の諸々を含めると月々の総支出額はおよそ128匁(12800文）」
  - (translation) "A carpenter's day's income around the Bunsei era was 5.4 monme of silver (540 mon), meal money included. Monthly spending: rent 10 monme (1,000 mon); food for a couple and one child about 30 monme (3,000 mon); seasonings and firewood together 58 monme (5,800 mon). With tools, furniture, clothing and the rest, the monthly total was about 128 monme (12,800 mon)."
- Saving against bad weather, quoting 『柳庵雑筆』 (same period): 「積て風雨の日の心充てにや貯ふるらん」
  - (translation) "they pile it up and save it, perhaps, as something to count on for days of wind and rain."

**3. Single versus married peddlers.** `https://www.nippon.com/ja/japan-topics/g01009/`. A 2020 popular article on Morisada; the sentence below is the journalist's own and cites no source.
- 「妻帯者は妻と子を養う義務があったので日々仕事に励んだが、独身者の多い江戸には、気楽なその日暮らしの棒手振も星の数ほどいた」
- (translation) "Married men, who had to support wife and children, worked hard every day; but in Edo, with its many single men, there were countless carefree hand-to-mouth peddlers."
- The same article, after the peddlers' backers (親方): 「雨が降ったら休む者もいた。気が乗らなくて休む日もあった。」 (translation) "Some rested when it rained. Some days they rested because they did not feel like working."
- On starting out: the 親方 lent 600-700 mon of stock money plus pole and baskets, repaid with 2-3 mon interest per 100 mon (credited to 文政年間漫録).

**4. Bathhouses (湯屋).** `https://www.nippon.com/ja/japan-topics/g01098/`. Evidence is Morisada, 1837-53. The only verbatim Morisada line is 「京坂ニテ風呂屋ト云イ、江戸ニテ銭湯アルヒハ湯屋ト云フ」 ("In Kyoto and Osaka they say furoya, in Edo sentō or yuya"). The rest is the article's paraphrase:
- 「京坂は浴室のある家が多く、小さな借家の住人が風呂屋を利用する程度だった。一方、江戸は…長屋住まいの庶民が頻繁に利用したため、天保〜嘉永から幕末に至る時期には約600軒を数えた。」
  - (translation) "In Kyoto and Osaka many houses had a bath room, and bathhouses were used only by the residents of small rented houses. In Edo, tenement dwellers used them often, and there were about 600 from the Tenpō-Kaei years to the end of the shogunate."
- Fees, as the article reports Morisada: 「湯銭（入浴料）は大人10文くらいだったが、天保12（1841）年の天保府命…によって大人8文、子供6文、小児4文に定められた」; 文久2〜3年 (1862-63) 「薪（まき）の高騰によって大人12文に値上がり」; 慶応元（1865）年16文、翌2年8月24文.
  - (translation) "The fee was about 10 mon for an adult; in 1841 an order fixed it at 8 mon adult, 6 child, 4 small child. In 1862-63 it rose to 12 mon because firewood got dearer; 16 mon in 1865, and 24 mon in the 8th month of 1866."
- Back-scrubbing (三助) was a separate charge: 「別料金の『流し代』」.

**5. No bath, a shared well and stove in the tenement.** Archived copy `ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/長屋`. Modern, describing Edo practice.
- 「路地に共同トイレがあり、風呂は無い。水は共同の井戸が有ったが、これは地下水をくみ上げるものではなく、神田上水から供給されていた水道水の取水口である。」
- (translation) "There was a shared toilet in the alley, and no bath. Water came from a shared well, which drew not groundwater but the piped water supplied by the Kanda aqueduct."

The same layout appears at `https://president.jp/articles/-/85985` (excerpt of 磯田道史 ed., 『新版 江戸の家計簿』), which gives a water jar, sink and stove at the entrance of the 9-shaku-by-2-ken unit. The one period price on that page: 「明和9（1772）年の炭の平均価格は1俵で銀4.1匁」 (translation: "in 1772 charcoal averaged 4.1 monme of silver a bale"). Its yen figures are the book's conversions and should not be used.

**6. The well as the wives' cooking and washing place.** `https://www.nippon.com/ja/japan-topics/g01041/`. Modern article; the scene is late Edo.
- 「井戸水は飲用であると同時に、洗濯・行水（風呂）や、米をといだり、釜炊き用…としても使われた」; 「井戸はいわば長屋の住人共同の炊事・洗濯場だったため、女房たちが集い」
- (translation) "Well water was for drinking and also for washing clothes, bathing, rinsing rice and cooking." "The well was the tenement's shared cooking and washing place, so the wives gathered there."
- It cites a late-Edo picture-book showing 「米をとぐ長屋の女房」 (translation: "a tenement wife rinsing rice").

**7. Water sellers.** Archived copy `kotobank.jp/word/井戸` (Nipponica). Covers the period up to early Meiji, all Japanese cities in general.
- 「日本の多くの都市では、近代的水道の始まる明治初年まで、用水路から日常の用水を求めたり、水売りから川の水を買った所も少なくない。」
- (translation) "In many Japanese cities, until modern waterworks began in early Meiji, there were not a few places that took daily water from channels or bought river water from water sellers."

**8. Used clothing.** `https://imidas.jp/jidaigeki/detail/L-57-117-08-04-G252.html`. Yamamoto Hirofumi, 2009.
- 「江戸時代の庶民は、主に木綿を着用しており、その多くは古着だった。」 (translation) "Commoners of the Edo period mostly wore cotton, and much of it was used clothing."
- 「柳原土手は、古着の中では最下級品を扱って」 (translation) "The Yanagihara embankment dealt in the lowest grade."
- 「万治2年（1659）の振売札…交付の際には、220人が認可を受けている」 (translation) "When peddlers' licenses were issued in 1659, 220 used-clothes buyers were licensed."

**9. Rental shops (損料屋) and few possessions.** Archived copy `eonet.ne.jp/~shoyu/mametisiki/reference-8.html`. A popular site with no date or source given; use it as a lead only.
- 「下着から鍋、釜、布団、正装まで借りることのできる損料屋…に賃料を払って必要なものをレンタルするのも一般的」 (translation) "It was common to rent what one needed from rental shops, which lent everything from underwear to pots, kettles, bedding and formal dress."
- 「農村からの出稼人はほとんどが着の身着のままだった」 (translation) "Migrants from the villages came with little more than the clothes they wore."
- 「損料屋といえばいちばんの得意先は長屋であった」 (translation) "The rental shops' best customers were the tenements."

**10. Prepared food.** `https://www.nippon.com/ja/japan-topics/g00962/`. As the article reports Morisada: 「鮓1貫は8文で、玉子焼きは16文」 (translation: "a piece of sushi 8 mon, an egg omelette 16 mon"); eggs 7-20 mon each.

**11. Hired washing.** `https://intojapanwaraku.com/culture/294401/`. A 2026 article citing 落合茂『洗う風俗史』 (1984).
- 「元禄初年には洗濯を専門の職業とした『洗濁屋』がすでにあった。着物を預けると丸洗いや染みぬきといった一般的な手入れをしてくれた」
- (translation) "By the first years of Genroku [c. 1688] there were already washing shops; you left a garment and they washed it whole or took out stains."
- 「江戸の洗濯屋さんは女性が主に働いており、京都ではおおくが男性だった」 (translation) "In Edo the washers were mostly women; in Kyoto mostly men."

**12. Single men in barracks (samurai, not laborers).**
- Archived copy `kk-bestsellers.com/articles/-/3486/` (historian Andō Yūichirō, modern): 「食事は自分で用意しなければならず、各々自炊したり、外食している。風呂については、屋敷内に共同浴場があったが、外の銭湯に出かける者が多かった。」
  - (translation) "They had to see to their own meals, each cooking for himself or eating out. For bathing there was a shared bath in the compound, but many went out to the public bathhouses."
- Archived copy `sengoku-his.com/1638`: foot soldiers lodged in rooms 「九尺二間の庶民の裏長屋と同じサイズ」, 「相部屋で住み食事も自炊」 (translation: "the same size as the commoners' 9-shaku-by-2-ken back tenements"; "sharing a room and cooking for themselves").

**China: Beijing water.** `https://scholars.ln.edu.hk/en/publications/one-city-with-two-water-drinking-water-in-beijing-1644-1900/` (Lei Zhang, 2017 abstract; evidence 1644-1900): "Beijing was notorious for its salty water, which was mainly procured from wells… two different water carrier systems: the state carriers in the Inner City and the private carriers in Outer City."

**Q4 verdict: ATTESTED ONLY FOR Edo.**
- **Bought, with dates:** polished rice (white rice could be sold only by the polishers; ~1,100 street polishers in 1727, ~2,100 polishers in 1744); firewood and seasonings (58 monme a month for a carpenter's family, Bunsei era); bath fees (8 mon from 1841, rising to 24 by 1866); used cotton clothing (220 licensed buyers in 1659). Bought water is attested only generally, "in many cities until early Meiji."
- **Not shown to be bought:** a washing trade existed from about 1688, but whose clothes it washed is SILENT. The tenement sources put washing, rice-rinsing and cooking at the shared well, done by the wives. Mending is SILENT.
- **Single versus family:** the dated budgets are both for family men: the peddler handed over rice money and a child's sweets money daily, and the carpenter paid for a couple and a child. The only single/married contrast found is the nippon.com journalist's unsourced line (single peddlers idled on rain days; married men worked every day). The best-attested single men cooking for themselves or eating out are samurai in barracks, not laborers. Rental-shop use by tenements comes only from a popular site with no date or source.
- **Kyoto and Osaka:** only the paraphrase of Morisada that their houses mostly had baths and only small-rental tenants used bathhouses. Little was found for either city.
- **China:** SILENT on Beijing water prices; the 估衣 (used-clothes) leads seen were all late Qing to Republican, and none was read.

## Q5: days not worked for pay

**13. Apprentices and clerks living in.** Archived copy `ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/丁稚`, modern, describing Edo practice.
- 「休暇で親元に帰れるのは正月明けと盆の年間数日のみであり、これを藪入りといった」 (translation) "The only leave to go home to their parents was a few days a year, after New Year and at Bon; this was called yabuiri."
- 「お盆・暮れの年2回の藪入りの際、小遣いや実家への手土産、新しい衣服（お仕着せ）などが支給されることがあった」 (translation) "At the two yabuiri, at Bon and year's end, they were sometimes given pocket money, gifts for home and new clothes."
- 「丁稚と手代までは住込、番頭より上は自宅を構え家族をもつこともあった」 (translation) "Apprentices and clerks lived in; from head clerk up, some kept a house and family."

**14. Shirokiya's Edo branch.** `https://www.shinchosha.co.jp/book/610242/`. These are the chapter titles of 油井宏子『江戸奉公人の心得帖』 (2007), a book drawn from about 400 of Shirokiya's own documents; Shirokiya was a Kyoto house with an Edo branch.
- 「故郷に初めて帰れるのは九年目の初登り」 (translation) "The first time home is the first 'going up' [to Kamigata] in the ninth year."
- 「結婚は退職後」 (translation) "Marriage after leaving service."
- 「店の用事で出かけて私用をしてはいけない」 (translation) "Do not do private business when out on the shop's errands."
- 「故郷の縁者にこちらから会いに行ってはいけない」 (translation) "Do not go to see relatives from home."
- 「新吉原や深川に通う奉公人」 (translation) "Servants who frequented the Shin-Yoshiwara and Fukagawa [pleasure quarters]."

**Q5 verdict: ATTESTED ONLY FOR merchant-house live-ins.** Their days off were the two yabuiri, spent at the parents' home or in amusement (already in 0311), with pocket money and new clothes given. They had no household work of their own, because they lived in until head clerk and married only after leaving. For a Kyoto house's Edo branch, home leave came first in the ninth year, so yabuiri as a home visit may not apply to staff from Kamigata. Side trading by servants or clerks is SILENT: the Shirokiya rule is about private errands, not trade, and the only side work found (傘張り, umbrella-making) belongs to lower 御家人 retainers, outside the class asked. Porters, guards and 中間 (samurai-house footmen) on days off are SILENT; the searches were 「中間 武家奉公人 内職 博打 部屋 非番」, 「中間部屋 賭博 折助」 and 「商家 店則 内証商い 禁止 手代」. China is SILENT: the Shanxi bank (票號) house rules on home leave were searched and not reached.
