# Search pass for feature 431 (diagram#21), PASS B: a Ming/Qing farm household's year and its unpaid tasks (China)

Short answer: the sources support the GM's "always working" picture for men's rainy-day work on one managed Jiangnan farm (c.1640). For hypothesis (3), they put a woman's textile year about level with a man's field year in mid-Qing Jiangnan, not below it. Every count of textile days I found measures cloth made for sale. None measures the time spent making the household's own clothes, so that part of the hypothesis has no evidence either way. Nothing was edited or committed.

## Sources

**1. 沈氏农书 (Shen-shi nongshu)**, c.1640, Gui'an, Huzhou. Archived copy `/diagram/.specify/source-archive/29/29913b34ce12/20261004T222822Z/text.txt` (agri-history.ihns.ac.cn/books/bns.htm). This is a landlord-manager's farm worked by hired men (长年 year-hands, 忙月 busy-month hands). It shows what hired men did, not an owner-cultivator family's own calendar.

The monthly calendar (逐月事宜) has a separate row for rainy days (阴雨):
- 正月 (1st month): "阴雨　修桑刮蟥　罱泥　载壅　罱田泥　劈柴　撒蚕草　秧界绳　编蚕帘　蚕箦" (translation): rainy days: prune mulberry and scrape off pest eggs, dredge mud, haul manure, split firewood, spread straw for silkworms, make seedbed lines, weave silkworm screens and trays.
- 二月 (2nd month): "阴雨　修桑括蟥　做塍修泼　锹沟　罱泥　修圩岸　劈柴　…锯车扉　罱田泥　载壅　捆桑绳　架山绳　撒柴" (translation): rainy days: … repair dikes, split firewood, saw water-wheel paddles, bind mulberry rope …
- 九月 to 十二月 (9th to 12th months): "绞绳索" (twist rope) appears every month. Also "做米　打米" (husk and pound rice), "缚囤" (tie grain bins), "押床箦" (press bed mats), "挑泥砖到家" (carry mud bricks home). Under 杂作 (odd jobs): "修船" (repair boat), "编篱笆" (weave fences), "做絮" (make cotton wadding, 9th month).

From the section on working fields (运田地法):
- "(十) 天时，大约晴七雨三，晴雨各有生活。…人家雇长年，天雨无生活可做，不得已而垦田，若有船可以罱泥，定须开潭罱泥，消磨雨工" (translation): roughly seven days fine to three rainy; fine and rainy days each have their work. … A household hiring year-hands, when it rains and there is no work, should, if it has a boat, dredge pond mud to use up the rain-days.
- "切不可贪阴雨闲工" (translation): never take advantage of idle rainy-day labor [to plough wet ground].
- "(九) 把桑绳…生活人扌刍绳，上等一日七八斤，酌中五斤" (translation): mulberry-binding rope: a worker twisting rope makes 7–8 jin a day at best, about 5 on average.
- Rations: "轻省及阴雨留家，全无 [酒]" (translation): light work, or staying home on rainy days, gets no wine. Also "每人日一升五合；妇人半之" (translation): 1.5 sheng [of rice] per person per day; women half that.

The household also bought cloth goods: "买麻苎布" (buy ramie cloth, 5th month); "蓑衣、箬帽" (rain capes and bamboo hats, bought in the 1st month); "买絮骨" (buy wadding, 9th month).

**2. 补农书 (Bu nongshu)**, Zhang Lüxiang, 1658, Tongxiang. Same archived file.
- "男耕女织，农家本务。…其常规：妇人两名，每年织绢一百二十匹…若家有织妇，织与不织，总要吃饭，不算工食自然有赢" (translation): men plough and women weave; this is the farm household's basic work. … The usual rule: two women weave 120 bolts of silk a year. … A household with a weaving woman must feed her whether she weaves or not, so leaving her food out of the costs it naturally profits.
- "西乡女工…东乡女工，或杂农桑，或治纺织。…男治田地可十亩，女养蚕可十筐，日成布可两匹，或纺棉纱八两" (translation): … in the eastern townships women's work is mixed with farming and sericulture, or is spinning and weaving. … A man can work ten mu; a woman can raise ten baskets of silkworms, or make two bolts of cloth a day, or spin eight liang of cotton yarn.
- "吾里地田，上农夫一人止能治十亩" (translation): in our village a top farmer alone can work only ten mu.
- "四月一日阴雨…男子劳于外，妇女忧于内，盖昼夜皇皇也" (translation): on rainy days in the 4th month [silkworm season], men toil outside, women fret within, anxious day and night.

**3. Buck, *Chinese Farm Economy*** (1930; data from 1921–25). Archived: archive.org `chinesefarmecono0000john`, `/diagram/.specify/source-archive/b6/b647a7ae9f1a/20261002T151359Z/text.txt`.
- Definition: "A man-work unit is the amount of work the average farmer accomplishes in a ten-hour day … on productive farm enterprises such as crops or animals." This is a standardized day, not an observed one.
- "The median … shows an average per farm of 190 man-work units, and this is performed by an average of two man-equivalents per farm … there are the equivalent of several idle months each year for each farm operator … While there is some other work to be done, of course, and equally of course some rainy days and some holidays … these could scarcely account for only about one-fourth of the year being utilized directly upon productive enterprises."
- "A little over one-fifth of all hired and family labor on the farm is performed by farm women. In East Central China it is 31.6 per cent compared with 11.8 per cent in North China. This real difference is probably because foot binding is much more prevalent in North China."
- Su Hsien, Anhwei: the early-June peak "would amount to over 16 hours of work per day for the 1.75 man-equivalent."
- "The problem of winter work for the farmer in China … women and children members of the farm family may be seen making winter shoes for sale from cow's hair." This is side work for sale, not household work.

**4. Fei Xiaotong, *Peasant Life in China*** (fieldwork 1936, published 1939; Kaixiangong, a silk district). Archived: `/diagram/.specify/source-archive/85/857b0fd46d16/20261002T122701Z/text.txt`.
- "children go to the farm only occasionally and women are entirely free from it. Agriculture is chiefly men's occupation. This division of labour … is a characteristic of the silk-producing area … women were busy in reeling while men were busy in preparing the soil." Women only work the irrigation pump in urgent periods.
- "Girls learn the technique of the silk industry, sewing and other household work from their mothers."
- "The domestic industry of weaving in the village has been practically ruined … only two of them still working. The material for clothing thus largely comes from the outside." "Sewing is women's work, except for tailors. Most of the women have skill enough to make ordinary clothes for their husbands and children." Clothing materials cost about thirty dollars a year.
- Women "have duties in household work and care of the children."
- Leisure periods. A schoolmaster says "from January to April and from July to September." The informant's letter says "from Ch'u Shu to Han Lu … about two months … [and] from Ta Hsüeh to the end of the year … During this leisure time, we go out for trade ventures." The two do not agree.
- House upkeep: "Every two or three years the wooden parts of the house must be oiled … this costs on the average ten dollars a year." That is paid work, so house repair here is not unpaid household labor.

## Reading

**ATTESTED ONLY FOR the managed Jiangnan farm, c.1640 (men's work); NOT ATTESTED for "fieldwork larger in total"; SILENT on clothing made for the household's own use.**

**(1) The year by season.** 沈氏农书 shows that on one managed Huzhou farm the year was organized so rainy days had their own lighter work. Its own words are "晴七雨三，晴雨各有生活" and "消磨雨工". That work was rope-twisting (9th to 12th months), firewood, husking and pounding rice, bin-tying, mat and screen weaving, and repairs to boats, dikes and water-wheels. Buck's productive-work count (1921–25) points the other way: about 95 standardized ten-hour days per worker, "only about one-fourth of the year", several idle months, and a "problem of winter work". Buck excludes household and repair work ("some other work … of course"). So the two are reconciled this way: the farm year left much slack, and householders filled it with maintenance, crafts and trading trips (Fei's two leisure periods "we go out for trade ventures"). For women, by season, I found only the 4th-month silkworm crisis (补农书) and Fei's reeling season. How their days split season by season is SILENT.

**(3) The GM's hypothesis.**
- The only quantified evidence is for mid-Qing Jiangnan, and all of it counts textile work *for income*. A woman's spinning and weaving year topped out at about 200 days (Li 1998). A man's field year was "probably no more than 200 labor days per 10 mu farm" (Li p.139, via Pomeranz's draft). Pomeranz concludes "Jiangnan women probably worked almost as many days for income as their husbands."
- So in Jiangnan one woman's textile time roughly equalled one man's field time. "Fieldwork larger in total" is not shown there. Women were mostly out of the fields (Fei; 补农书's 东乡 mixed districts excepted), so combined fieldwork across the household was about the same as the textile work, not more.
- Elsewhere the balance shifts. Buck finds women doing 31.6% of farm labor in East Central China and 11.8% in North China. Lu Village (1938) had women doing more fieldwork than men.
- The GM's actual claim is about making the household's *own* clothes. No source times that. Two point against home cloth-making in the commercialized areas: the Shen farm bought ramie cloth and rain capes, and by 1936 Kaixiangong bought its cloth ("weaving … practically ruined").
