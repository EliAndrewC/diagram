Feature 431, pass A: a Tokugawa farm household's year and its unpaid tasks (Japan).

I ran pass A (feature 431, Japan). On your third question the record splits by fibre. In the cotton era it points against clothing being the largest claim on a woman's time; for the earlier hemp and ramie era it is plausible but rests on one estimate. Nothing I found gives hours per family member.

## Sources that bear on the questions

**1. Seiryōki 清良記, book 7 (親民鑑月集), Iyo. Text dated 1629 but written in the mid-17th century. Two copies, neither of them the original text.**

(a) The Sukumo city history's summary of it, archived copy: https://www.city.sukumo.kochi.jp/sisi/057801.html (`/diagram/.specify/source-archive/d7/d7ed597872cc/20261003T021514Z/text.txt`)
> 「麦跡…１反に付２５人宛／古堅田…１反に付３３人宛／水田…１反に付２７人宛／山田　１反　３８人役／合計　１町　２９３人役」「この男子の２９３人役の外に女子の「２百人役余も入るべく」とあって…此の外に夜業などは計算されていないのである。」
(translation) A total of 293 man-days for 1 chō of paddy, and besides those 293 male days "over 200 woman-days will also be needed". Night work is not counted.

> 「なおこのほかに人夫役として養蚕２０人役、燃料１２０人役、家屋修理２０人役、用水路浚渫２０人役、茶拵１０人役、農具整備(莚萱取、蓑葉萱取、農具手入、鍛治)１２人役、牛馬飼育１２０人役、肥料用草苅４０人役の合計３６２人役となり、田畠１町２反５畝を耕作するに要する人夫役の総計は８１１人役となるのである。」 Upland fields: 「合せて９０人役で畑２反５畝」.
(translation) In addition: silkworms 20 man-days, fuel 120, house repair 20, ditch dredging 20, tea-making 10, tool upkeep (cutting reeds for mats and rain-cape thatch, tool care, smithing) 12, horse and ox care 120, cutting grass for fertilizer 40. That is 362, and with the 90 days for 2.5 tan of dry fields the total for 1 chō 2 tan 5 se is 811 man-days.

These are the only per-task, per-year figures I found.

(b) The text itself, as quoted in Nagashima Junko 長島淳子, 「江戸時代における農村女性の労働・生活とジェンダー」, 『農業および園芸』 92(7), 2017, p. 574: https://agriknowledge.affrc.go.jp/RN/2010912071.pdf (an image PDF; I read the page images)
> 「…百姓の妻は、独狂言をする如く働かされは叶はさる。子細は朝夕の食物を調へ、春夏(秋)両度の帷子を拵る布を織り、麦をこなし、田を植、秋は稲をこき、貢物を調へ、冬は又正月の着る物の木綿を織り、夫銭、返(段)銭の価をせては叶はす、薪悪しければいとまなし、料理の心なきは食物の味ひなし、かかる事諸人合点あるは希也、侍方には尚以御存知あるましく候…」
(translation) "A peasant's wife must work like an actor playing every part alone. She prepares the morning and evening meals; she weaves the cloth for the unlined robes made twice a year; she threshes the barley, transplants the rice, strips the rice in autumn and makes ready the tax goods. In winter she again weaves the cotton for the New Year clothes, and must find the money for the corvée and *dansen* levies. If the firewood is poor she has no respite; without care in cooking the food has no taste. Few people grasp this; samurai least of all."

I copied the bracketed (秋) and (段) as they appear in the article. They look like editorial readings set beside the text, and I could not settle that from the image.

**2. Nagashima 2017, the same article. Modern scholarship on Tokugawa practice.**
- p. 574, ramie before cotton: 「苧麻は栽培から衣料生産までの工程に非常に手間がかかり，木綿の10倍の労力を要したといわれている」「女性は麻布生産の貴重な働き手であり，大部分の労力がそこに注がれた」「木綿への移行で省力化した女性の労働時間を，小農経営のための農業労働に投入できたからこそ，「夫婦かけむかい」の近世農業が実現できた」
  (translation) Ramie took great labour from growing to finished cloth, "said to require ten times the labour of cotton". Women were the key workers in hemp-cloth production, "and most of their labour was poured into it". Only because the switch to cotton freed women's labour time for farm work could the husband-and-wife farming of the early modern period come about.
- p. 576, who did what and how it was valued: 「男性は耕起作業や代掻きなど瞬発力を要する筋肉労働に従事するのに対し，女性は手先の繊細さの必要な，それほど筋力を要しない単純作業に従事する傾向にあった」「種まきや土寄せ，脱穀調整などの女性労働は安価であり，特に炊事・掃除・洗濯などを担当する下女労働はさらに安く見積もられた」
  (translation) Men did muscular work such as ploughing and puddling. Women did dexterous, less strenuous work. Women's sowing, earthing up and threshing were valued cheaply, and a maidservant's cooking, cleaning and laundry more cheaply still.
- p. 576, the Imai household of Shinano, account book of 1821: 「女性のみの労働をあげると，4月と閏4月の庭ごみ広げ，干草広げ，刈敷広げ…5月には粽作り，稗より，油荏うるいなど」 and the wages 「男性でもっとも高額な者は年に金3両1分…通常の男性は金2両2分…女性は金1両と四季施…日雇い手間賃は男性が銭50文，女性が銭16～33文程度」
  (translation) Women-only tasks: spreading yard refuse, hay and green manure in the 4th month and intercalary 4th month; in the 5th month making rice dumplings, sorting barnyard millet, and similar. Annual pay: the best man 3 ryō 1 bu, an ordinary man 2 ryō 2 bu, a woman 1 ryō plus her seasonal clothing. Day rates: men 50 mon, women 16-33 mon.
- p. 575-576, the Muramatsu house rules, Noto, late 18th to early 19th century: 「三度の飯時には，女は寝間にいようと，庭や台所で仕事をしていようとも呼び出して，男の食事支度や配膳を行う」「休日には女たちは総出で，諸品の洗い拭きを行う。半日の遊び日であっても朝夕二度は手伝いをすること」
  (translation) At all three meals the women are called away from whatever they are doing to prepare and serve the men's food. On holidays the women all turn out to wash and wipe the household goods, and even on a half-day off they help morning and evening.

**3. Tanimoto Masayuki, "Cotton and the Peasant Economy" (a draft marked "Not to be quoted or cited without permission"): https://www.lse.ac.uk/Economic-History/Assets/Documents/Research/GEHN/Helsinki/HELSINKITanimoto.pdf. Evidence: Niikawa district (Etchū), mid-19th century.**
> "Table 2 … (Working days per tan) Spinning 2 day / Preparation 1 day / Weaving 1 day / Total 4 day … Annual production for self consumption 12.8 tan … Production for sales, per year 157.6 day / Production for self consumption, per year 51.2 day / Total 208.8 day" — "The average household devoted one woman for about 7 months to prepare the clothing for the household members, supplying 39.4 tan to the market and 12.8 for her own family." "Since the average number of the household member was slightly less than 5…"

The table cites no source, so the 4 days per tan may be Tanimoto's own figure. It covers spinning and weaving from bought ginned cotton only. Growing, ginning, sewing, washing and unpicking robes (araihari) and mending are not in it. The same paper (note 9) cites Nagahara Keiji, 『苧麻・絹・木綿の社会史』 (2004), p. 212, for "the labour input for producing the cloth from raw ramie per tan … reached nearly ten times more than that of cotton cloth".

**4. Tomioku village history, Kanazawa: http://tiikijiten.jp/~digibook/tomioku_kyoudo/keitai.php?no=0017 (archived). This is a 20th-century memory of Meiji and Taishō practice; only the weaving passage claims domain-era (藩政) practice.**
> 「男達は朝前仕事にかかるが女達は食事や家事、育児の仕事にかかるのがふつうで…男達は満腹すると昼休み（昼寝）するが、嫁は食事の跡始末、夜飯（よめし）の準備、せんたく、幼児に腹一杯の授乳をしなければならなかった。この男達の昼休みは苗代頃から秋の取り入れ頃まで毎日続き、秋は昼休みは無く、さらに夜なべ（夜業）が加わった。家により年中夜なべをし、農閑期でも薄暗い囲炉辺でわら仕事に家族全員精出す家もあった。」
(translation) Before breakfast the men went to work while the women cooked, kept house and minded children. After the midday meal the men napped, but the young wife cleared up, prepared supper, did the washing and nursed the baby. The men's nap ran from seedbed time to harvest. In autumn there was no nap and night work (*yonabe*) was added. Some households did night work all year, the whole family making straw goods by the dim hearth even in the slack season.

> 「藩政の頃ほもちろん、明治の中頃になっても機（はた）を織る農家が相当多くあった…おもに農閑の冬季に女達の手で根気よく織られた。」
(translation) In domain times, and still in mid-Meiji, many farm households wove, mostly by the women and patiently, in the slack winter.

**5. Kumon Yuzuru, CIRJE working paper 2020 (already archived by the project): https://www.cirje.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/research/dp/2020/2020cf1154.pdf. Evidence 1610-1890, from servant contracts and village surveys.**
> "They worked for 310-330 days a year … Other surveys of the work year in these villages also suggest at least 300 days of work per year. However, there was certainly some regional variation and the estimates suggest Eastern Japan had a shorter work year than Western Japan"

**6. Gendai Business, 2017, summarizing Furukawa Sadao (archived): https://gendai.media/articles/-/53636. Evidence from the 17th to 19th centuries.**
> 「17世紀後半から18世紀初めの農民の休日は…年間20日前後程であった。」「「遊び日」は、年間で30日～40日台から、ところによっては50日台、60日台以上、最大80日までにも達している。」
(translation) Peasants had about 20 rest days a year in the late 17th and early 18th centuries. In the 19th century, festival rest days ("asobi-bi") reached 30-40s a year, in places 50-60 or more, up to 80.

**7. Saito Osamu, LSE conference draft (marked "Draft. Please do not cite"): https://www.lse.ac.uk/Economic-History/Assets/Documents/Research/GEHN/GEHNConferences/conf6/Conf6-Saito.pdf. Evidence: early Meiji, Yamanashi 1879.**
> "26 per cent of the working population were dually occupied, which reflected that about a quarter of both male and female agricultural population had a non-agricultural side occupation." "The proportion of female farm family members having non-agricultural, domestic by-employment, such as reeling and weaving, was in the range of 13-22 per cent." Quoting Smith 1986/88: households shifted labour "back and forth from farming to by-employments, not only seasonally but from day to day and within the day".

**8. The 慶安御触書 (Keian edict).** I could only read the archived ja.wikipedia page (`/diagram/.specify/source-archive/16/1609f529296e/…`), which gives a modern paraphrase, not the text: 「男は農耕、女房は機織りに励み、夜なべをして夫婦ともよく働くこと」 (translation: the man farms, the wife works hard at weaving, and husband and wife both do night work). The Gunma prefectural archives page (https://www.pref.gunma.jp/site/monjyokan/130131.html) dates the text that circulated to an 1830 Iwamura print, reissued in 1838. It says no original of 1649 has ever been found and its authenticity is still disputed. Treat it as a late-Tokugawa official ideal, not as a 1649 fact.

## Verdicts

**Q1 (how each member's time split by season): ATTESTED ONLY FOR the outline, not for hours.** The record shows:
- a work year of 310-330 days, with 20 holidays rising in places to 50-80 by the 19th century;
- 293 male and 200-plus female field days per chō, with night work explicitly left out (Seiryōki);
- the daily pattern: women cook, wash and nurse while men nap in summer, and night work is added in autumn and in some houses all year (Tomioku, a Meiji memory);
- winter weaving by women;
- about a quarter of farm people with a side trade in Yamanashi in 1879.

I found no split of days or hours by member (husband, wife, children, elders, servant). The Choshu by-employment figures (Smith 1969, Nishikawa 1978) are paywalled.

**Q2 (task list with days): ATTESTED ONLY FOR the Seiryōki's annual man-days.** Those are fuel 120, horse and ox care 120, grass for fertilizer 40, silkworms 20, house repair 20, ditch dredging 20, tool upkeep and mat and rain-cape materials 12, tea 10, upland fields 90. Cloth is attested at 4 days per tan of cotton (spin 2, prepare 1, weave 1), about 51 days a year for a household of about 5 (Tanimoto, mid-19th century).

I found nothing per unit of work, or per year, for cooking, miso or soy, pickling, water carrying, rice hulling, straw rope, sandals, mats, rain capes, sewing, araihari or child care.

**Q3 (the GM's clothing hypothesis): NOT ATTESTED for the cotton era; ATTESTED ONLY FOR the hemp and ramie era, and only as an estimate.**
- **Cotton era.** One woman's household cloth came to about 51 days a year (4 days per tan × 12.8 tan) plus unmeasured sewing. Against that stand 200-plus female field days per chō, and Nagashima argues cotton moved women's time into fieldwork. So fieldwork was the larger claim even on the woman.
- **The exception.** In weaving districts that sold cloth, one woman gave about 209 days a year to spinning and weaving. Most of that was for sale, so it is a paid trade, not household work.
- **Hemp and ramie (17th century and in places with no cotton).** Nagahara's tenfold estimate and Nagashima's 「大部分の労力がそこに注がれた」 support the GM's view. However, that is a scholarly estimate, not a measured household.
- **The GM's second clause** (more time on farming than on clothing, counting everyone) fits all the evidence.
