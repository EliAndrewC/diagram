I've answered all three parts with evidence. Edo: no source ties rice, salt, firewood, wheat or barley prices to foreign trade, and only lamp oil and 雑穀 get an 1859-60 tie, from an encyclopedia. Beijing: SILENT. Tokyo 1882: the low soybean/rice ratio holds in every year from 1879 to 1895, so it is not an 1882 rice spike. It starts exactly where the price series switches source in 1879.

## Verdicts

**(a) Edo 1860-67**
- **Rice: NOT ATTESTED** as a trade effect. Export was banned by the treaties, and the first rice-import surge was in 1870. Rice fell 3.1% in 1859 itself, and its big rise was late (1866: +195.8%). Shinbo ties the general rise to the money supply.
- **Lamp oil (水油) and 雑穀: ATTESTED ONLY FOR 1859-60, on an encyclopedia.** Nipponica says export diversion cut shipments to Edo and drove up prices "centred on export goods", then says exports of the four non-silk goods fell sharply from 1861. No series isolates the oil price. In Shinbo's Kansai data seed and white oil rose less than the general index (2.93-2.94 against 3.39). By 1868-75 vegetable oil was an import, so the direction had reversed.
- **Soybeans: SILENT** on a trade tie. No source says 雑穀 covered 大豆. Their relative rise in Shinbo (3.43) and in Bernhofen-Brown's 1869 comparison comes with no trade mechanism, and Bernhofen-Brown suggest poor weather.
- **Salt, firewood, wheat: NOT ATTESTED.** No source names a trade mechanism.
- **Barley: SILENT.** Shinbo excludes it for lack of data.
- **Wax: ATTESTED** as a trade-raised export good, but only in Bernhofen-Brown's 1869 comparison. It is not on your list.

Two warnings for whoever uses this:
- **Shinbo's period ratios can't separate trade from money.** Raw silk, the textbook trade good, has III/II of only 2.95, below the general 3.39, because it jumped once in 1859. So the ratios above are context only, not tests.
- **All of Shinbo's prices are Kyoto/Osaka, not Edo.**

**(b) Beijing 1900-1919: SILENT** on foreign trade. Meng & Gamble's series show no break for rice in 1913-19; millet swings between $3.72 and $5.91 with no pattern tied to imports. The only cause anyone states is the 1920 civil-war cut of the railways, which is domestic and falls outside the window. Rice reaching Beijing through Tianjin would mostly be southern Chinese rice, which is domestic trade, not foreign. I could not find the Meng & Gamble 1926 text itself (archive.org search: nothing; HathiTrust: no record).

**(c) Tokyo 1882: ATTESTED** that it is neither the 1880-81 rice spike nor an 1882 peculiarity.
- 1882 itself is 0.86, the highest ratio in 1879-95. The 0.65 is Bassino-Ma's 1882-86 average.
- The ratio is at or below 0.86 in every observed year from 1879 to 1895. The drop below the Tokugawa range happens exactly at Kumon's source switch to LTES Tokyo retail in 1879, so it may be a series or grade difference rather than a real change.
- The Matsukata deflation deepens it: 0.57-0.58 in 1884-86, because soybeans fell harder than rice. That trough sits inside Bassino-Ma's averaging window.
- The LTES soybean prices move only in steps of 1/129 yen per kg. That fits retail quotes in whole sen per 升 (about 1.29 kg of soybeans), so the soybean series is coarse.
- Imports: beans were among Japan's 1868-75 imports, but in a small category (4.2% of imports). When Manchurian soybean imports began is **SILENT**.
