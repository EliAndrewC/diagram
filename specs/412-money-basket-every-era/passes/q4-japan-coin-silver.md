(translation) "Tokugawa Japan also had several currencies, but under a more orderly system. It is generally thought that converting at the market gold-silver rate and copper price raises no great problem, but the late-Tokugawa drift toward a gold standard and the relative fall of the silver unit (銀目) need attention. That leads on to the sharp fall of silver's price after the Ansei opening, which makes converting from silver monme to yen hard when Tokugawa prices and wages are joined to the Meiji period. For now the sound method seems to be to link the Kinai silver series to the Kanto gold series at 1830, and the Kanto gold series to the Meiji yen series." (the text layer reads 1830年目リンク; [で] is my reading of the garbled character)

## Derived figures (this pass's arithmetic; 1 monme = 3.75 g)

Pure silver per monme of chogin, prescribed (assayed): Keicho 1601-1695 3.00 g (2.97); Genroku 1695- 2.40 (2.42); Futatsu-takara 1706- 1.875 (1.90); Eiji 1710 1.50; Mitsu-takara 1710 1.20; Yotsu-takara 1711 0.75; Shotoku/Kyoho 1714-1736 3.00 (2.99); Genbun 1736-1820 and the 1765 5-monme piece 1.725 (1.69); Bunsei 1820-1837 1.35 (1.32); Tenpo 1837-1859 0.975 (0.977); Ansei 1859-1868 0.49-0.525 (0.506). From 1714 to the end of 1722 coins of different fineness circulated side by side, each valued at its silver content.

The ryo by the monme route (market monme a ryo x g a monme): Keicho, 1692-94 about 60-64 monme, so 180-190 g (early period at 43-50 monme, 129-150 g); Genroku around 1700, 48 monme, so about 115 g; Kyoho 1722-35, 57-59 monme, so 170-176 g; Genbun 1740s 107 g, 1750s 104, 1760s 109, 1770s 109, 1780s 96-100, 1790s 104.5, 1800s 111, 1810s 111; Bunsei 1820s 86 g, 1830-36 83; Tenpo 1837-39 60 g, 1840-43 61, 1850-54 62.5, 1855-59 69.
Check against the koban's gold: Keicho 15.0 g gold, Genbun 8.62, Bunsei 7.40, Tenpo 6.39, Ansei 5.11, Man'en 1.87. The implied gold:silver ratio is about 1:12 for Keicho and Genbun, 1:11.5 for Bunsei and 1:9.6 for Tenpo, matching the 金銀比価 page's 「小判と丁銀の含有率に基づく比価は1:10前後を維持した」 (translation: "the ratio by the koban's and chogin's metal content stayed near 1:10").

The ryo by the coin route (the silver in the coins that actually paid it): 8 Meiwa nanryo = 79 g (the wiki's 21.6 monme = 81 g) against 103.5-109 g by the monme route; 8 Bunsei nanryo = 58.7 g; 4 Tenpo ichibu-gin = 34.1 g (the wiki's 9.2 monme = 34.5 g, against Tenpo chogin's 15.6 monme = 58.5 g); 4 Ansei ichibu-gin = 30.8 g. The 1773 order valued a ryo at 24 monme of pure silver (90 g), and so the circulating monme at 1.5 g.
Bu = 1/4 ryo: ichibu-kin carries the koban's gold at 1/4 weight. Shu = 1/16 ryo: nanryo gives about 4.95 g a shu (1772) and the Bunsei nishugin 3.67 g.
Copper, using 0277's mon a monme: a mon was about 0.016 g of silver in the 1790s-1810s (Genbun), 0.0124 in the 1820s-30s (Bunsei), 0.0090 in the 1840s and 0.0093-0.0103 in the 1850s (Tenpo). A kanmon is 1,000 times that.

## Reading
ATTESTED, for fineness and weights: the prescribed silver fineness of each chogin (from 『旧貨幣表』 and 大蔵省 1875, via jawiki) and the Meiji mint's assays; the koban's and ichibu's prescribed weight and gold fineness; the nanryo's and ichibu-gin's weight and assay. The gold-silver market rate is ATTESTED ONLY FOR 1787-1859 at primary-series quality (RIEB, in 0277); 1722-1786 rests on a hobbyist transcription of 『日本史資料総覧』, which agrees with RIEB to within about 1 monme where both run. For Bassino & Ma's 3.11 g the verdict is SILENT on its basis: both versions of the paper state the constant with no coinage, year or citation, and it is applied unchanged from 1741 to 1871. It matches no Edo coin: 3.11/3.75 = 82.9%, above even the Keicho/Kyoho 80% (3.00 g); it is 1.8 times the Genbun 1.725 g that circulated in their 1745-54 benchmark, 2.3 times Bunsei and 3.2 times Tenpo. That 3.11 g is one tenth of a troy ounce is a numerical coincidence, not a finding. The co-authors' paper of the same year (BMS 2005) recommends linking the silver series to the gold series at 1830 rather than using any fixed silver weight. Because the constant multiplies both wage and basket, it leaves B&M's Tokugawa welfare ratios unchanged; it affects only their gram-silver levels and the silver-wage comparisons.
