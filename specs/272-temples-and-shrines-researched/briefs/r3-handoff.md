- A138 D64 KNOB (history) + deviation (setting) - 500: there were three arrangements side by side. Not every one of the ~65,000 villages had a temple, and households chose one by nearness; a folklorist judged the commonest form to be a village's houses split among several temples outside it. Other villages raised a hall of their own into a temple, and some kept several (Sugaura, ~70 households: 3 temples and 12 hermitages). A village temple's parish was 24-216 households. The share of each arrangement is SILENT (absence note). - maps: none. The village keeps its shrine and no temple, following the canon (the country monk serves in the village shrine), and 500 records this as a deviation. The hamlets served by the village's shrine are the setting's form of "the neighbor's temple".
- A139 ACCURATE - 510: five village temples' main halls were 4-6 ken, ~24-36 ft a side. The smallest temple had a hall ~27 x 18 ft, a kuri ~12 x 15 ft and a site of 185 tsubo (~6,600 sq ft). A hall and a bell tower were the small temple's whole plan; lesser halls and a graveyard could stand in the precinct. One temple stood "at the edge of the houses, on slightly higher ground". The Fukuoka "temple land" figure is sometimes smaller than the hall, so it is not used; the precinct band is 460's Saitama register. A general siting rule is SILENT (absence note). - maps: none. The village draws no temple; the figures calibrate the country shrine's hall and dwelling (120).
- A140 D61 B103 KNOB - 520: dosojin and jizo stand at thresholds: a village's entrance, a pass, a crossroads, a bridge foot, graveyards. Where the stones are thick, each hamlet has its own at its entrance, often in a group with a koshin tower. The Fukuyama tsujido halls stood about one per 20-30 households, ~6 ft square on four posts (12-18 ft on Shodoshima, 1862), and were regional. In Kinki, towns kept a jizo per block; Edo had Inari "everywhere". The Ming ordered an altar per 100 households, with ~1 earth-god temple per ten-odd lowland villages (the author doubts that count). A stone runs from palm-sized to ~6 ft tall. - maps: a hamlet and a village draw a group of 1-3 wayside stones at each place a road enters, drawn ~4 ft across (a convention). Per seed, a village may also keep open wayside halls, 6-18 ft square, one per 20-30 households at crossroads or by the road (even odds, a guess). A town draws a street shrine per block (a guess outside Kinki). The city's three-by-a-temple-cluster floor stands.
- A144 KNOB + SILENT - 530 (and a pointer and a rule clause in 190): where Japan cremated (the Shin regions), each settlement burned its own dead at its own simple open ground. That ground was kept with the graves (Nanjo town history; hearths attached to the settlement's graveyard) or stood apart at the hamlet's edge, the sanmai. In two-grave Wakasa villages the ground called sanmai lay on average ~370 m out; that is a body-burial ground. Six jizo were set at one cremation ground.
