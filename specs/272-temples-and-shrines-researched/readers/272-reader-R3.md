## Q1. Did an Edo-period village keep its own Buddhist parish temple (danna-dera/bodaiji)? Statistics.

### Danna-dera / bodaiji: definition and universality of the obligation

Kotobank, digital dictionaries aggregator, entry "檀那寺":
<https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%AA%80%E9%82%A3%E5%AF%BA-95362>

- デジタル大辞泉: "自分の家が帰依して檀家となっている寺。菩提寺ぼだいじ。"
  (EN: "The temple to which one's household has devoted itself and become a parishioner [danka].
  Also called bodaiji.")
- 精選版 日本国語大辞典: "その家が帰依して檀家となっている寺。江戸時代は、すべての庶民が檀那寺を
  持つことを強要された。"
  (EN: "The temple to which a household has devoted itself and become a parishioner. In the Edo
  period, all commoners were compelled to have a danna-dera.")
- 百科事典マイペディア: "自家が帰依している寺をいう。江戸時代，キリシタン禁制の実施手段として
  寺請(てらうけ)制度がとられ，すべての士・庶とも檀那寺をもたねばならなくなった。"
  (EN: "The temple to which one's household has devoted itself. In the Edo period, the terauke
  [temple-certification] system was adopted as the means of enforcing the Christian ban, and all
  samurai and commoners alike were required to have a danna-dera.")

None of the three entries states whether the "household's" danna-dera sat inside its own village
or a neighboring one; the obligation is stated at the household level, not the village level.

### English academic source: Tamamuro Fumio, "The Development of the Temple-Parishioner System,"
Japanese Journal of Religious Studies 36/1 (2009), pp. 12-19 (Nanzan Institute for Religion and
Culture). PDF: <https://nirc.nanzan-u.ac.jp/journal/6/article/1268/pdf/download>

- p.17-18: "Under the supervision of this office [jisha bugyō, est. 1635], the bakufu approved
  administrative head temples (furegashira jiin 触頭寺院) for each Buddhist sect that would
  require their branch temples to expose Christians in all villages. Through an elaborate,
  pyramid-like structure of head and branch temples, the bakufu instituted a complex network for
  monitoring religion..."
  - This documents that the bakufu's design required a branch temple's reach into "all villages"
    (i.e., temple coverage was engineered to be universal at the village level), but does not give
    a temple-count or village-count statistic.
- p.20: describes shūmon aratame yaku (investigator of religion office, est. 1640), whose role
  included having "village officials produce registries of religious affiliation (shūshi ninbetsu
  aratamechō 宗旨人別改帳) for everyone in their village" - again village-level administration, no
  national temple/village count given in the portion I read.

No numeric national statistic (temples vs. villages, e.g. ~90,000 vs ~63,000) was found on any
page I could actually open. See "Blocked / not found" below - this specific figure could not be
sourced to a public, fetchable page in this pass; treat it as UNCONFIRMED, not as researched fact.

## Q3. Wayside shrines: jizō, dōsojin, ishigami/sekishi, kōshin-tō, roadside Inari

### Dōsojin (道祖神)

Japanese Wikipedia, "道祖神": <https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%81%93%E7%A5%96%E7%A5%9E>
- "村境、峠などの路傍にあって外来の疫病や悪霊を防ぐ神である"
  (EN: "A deity placed at village boundaries, mountain passes, and other roadsides, to ward off
  epidemic disease and evil spirits coming from outside.")
- "集落の境や村の中心、村内と村外の境界や道の辻、三叉路などに主に石碑や石像の形態で祀られる"
  (EN: "Enshrined mainly in the form of stone steles or stone statues at the boundary of a
  settlement, the center of a village, the boundary between inside and outside the village, road
  crossings [tsuji], and three-way forks.")
- Size: ranges from small hand-sized pieces up to standing monuments roughly 2 m tall; usually
  carved from locally available stone (granite, river stone, basalt).
- Dating/spread: "道祖神が数多く作られるようになったのは18世紀から19世紀で、新田開発や水路整備が
  活発に行われていた時期である"
  (EN: "Dōsojin came to be made in large numbers in the 18th-19th centuries, a period of active
  new-field [shinden] development and canal/waterway construction.")

Ninomiya Town (Kanagawa Prefecture) official page, "道祖神":
<https://www.town.ninomiya.kanagawa.jp/0000000028.html>
- "このページでは二宮に見られる石造物の中から、町内に43基あるセーノカミをまとめました。"
  (EN: "This page compiles the 43 dōsojin [locally called 'seenokami'] found within the town.")
  - 43 dōsojin across the whole town (a multi-village municipality), i.e. a real per-municipality
    count, not per-village, but the only concrete count found on a public page.
- "いつ作られたかわかっているもので、最も古いのは元禄14(1701)年で、中里にあります。"
  (EN: "Among those whose date of making is known, the oldest is from Genroku 14 [1701], in the
  Nakazato district.")
- "「道祖神」などと刻まれた文字碑は一般的には江戸時代の終わり頃になって多く作られています。"
  (EN: "Text-only steles inscribed simply '道祖神' [dōsojin] etc. generally became common only
  toward the end of the Edo period.")

### Kōshin-tō (庚申塔)

Japanese Wikipedia, "庚申塔": <https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%BA%9A%E7%94%B3%E5%A1%94>
- Placement: "道の交差している箇所や村落の入り口などに" (EN: "at points where roads cross, and at
  the entrances to villages, etc."); also used as boundary markers between communities along
  roadsides.
- Distribution: nationwide except Okinawa/the Southwest Islands; more numerous in Kantō, relatively
  fewer in Kansai.
- Example count cited on the page: 77 stone kōshin-tō identified in Tajō district, Hyōgo
  Prefecture, most erected from the 18th to early 20th centuries.
- Historical timeline: construction became widespread from early Edo (Kan'ei era) onward; Edo
  period through mid-Meiji was the main construction phase; many were later removed in Meiji
  modernization and again in postwar road-construction/economic-growth-era clearances.

Uemura Shigeki, "地域の埋もれた文化財「路傍の石仏」の保全と利活用に関する考察 - 木更津の庚申塔を
例に -" [A study on preserving and using the "buried local cultural assets" that are roadside
stone Buddhist images - Kisarazu's kōshin-tō as an example], Kisarazu National College of
Technology Bulletin No. 49 (2016), pp. 83-. PDF:
<https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/aijax/374/0/374_KJ00004066730/_pdf> — actual working URL
used: search result PDF at jstage `bnitk/49/0/49_83` (fetched and read via pdftotext):
- "道標として街道沿いに置かれるものや，村や里の境界に造立されるものもあった"
  (EN: "Some [kōshin-tō] were placed along highways as road markers [dōhyō], and others were
  erected at the boundary of a village or hamlet [sato].")
- On origin/spread: "16世紀ごろから庚申塔の造立が進んできたという。一般的には，庚申講を3年間，18回
  続けた記念に庚申塔を造立する"
  (EN: "It is said that the erection of kōshin-tō progressed from around the 16th century.
  Generally, a kōshin-tō is erected to commemorate having kept the kōshin vigil group [kōshin-kō]
  for three years, eighteen sessions.")
- Meanings inscribed on the roadside stone, per the same source: safe childbirth, child safety,
  safe travel, memorial for children who died young, memorial for cattle/horses (once valuable),
  a barrier (kekkai) protecting the village from epidemics and evil, and a monument to kōshin-kō
  religious observance.

## Q4. China: village earth-god shrines (土地庙), wayside stupas/steles

### General prevalence language ("每村有庙" pattern), North China pear-belt

传统民间文化与新农村建设——以华北梨区庙会为例 [Traditional folk culture and new rural
construction: temple fairs in North China's pear-growing region as an example], 中国农业大学学报
[Journal of China Agricultural University] (社会科学版), full text:
<http://html.rhhz.net/society/html/20080610.htm>
- "村各有庙，庙各有会" (EN: "Every village has its temple(s), and every temple has its fair.")
  This phrase is used in the abstract characterizing the historical condition of villages in the
  pear-growing region of central North China.
- Types of temples named as present in this region by the early 20th century: 老母庙 (Old Mother),
  关帝庙 (Guan Yu), 三官庙 (Three Officials), 真武庙 (Zhenwu), 玉皇庙 (Jade Emperor), 五道庙 (Five
  Paths) - described as simple in structure ("简单的"), functioning as village landmarks and
  centers of community life tied to villagers' life-cycle observances.
  - NOTE: this article's list of "village temples" is the broader category of folk/village
    temples generally (various deities), not specifically 土地庙 (earth-god shrines); it documents
    that villages routinely had temples, but does not isolate a 土地庙-specific count.

### A specific ratio for 土地庙 specifically, Jiangnan (lowland vs. hill country) - CONFIRMED by
direct read (superseding an earlier unverified note from a search summary)

王健, "明清以来江南民间信仰中的庙界：以苏、松为中心" [Temple boundaries (miaojie) in Jiangnan folk
belief since the Ming-Qing: centered on Suzhou and Songjiang], 上海社会科学院历史研究所 [Shanghai
Academy of Social Sciences, Institute of History]. PDF (very slow server, ~1 KB/s; a 500s
background curl eventually completed a clean 619,729-byte download; read via pdftotext -layout):
<https://ih.sass.org.cn/_upload/article/files/85/9b/3c530d694fb39357215c6e822c8c/a8324f93-0201-498d-8e0f-c2086ec92055.pdf>

- Ming institutional baseline, quoting 万历《明会典》 [Wanli-era Ming Compendium of Administrative
  Statutes], juan 94: "凡各处乡村人民每里一百户内立坛一所，祀五土五谷之神，专为祈祷雨炀时若，
  五谷丰登，每岁一户轮当会首，常川洁净坛场。遇春秋二社，预期率办祭物，至日约聚祭祀。"
  (EN: "For village people everywhere, within every li of one hundred households one altar shall
  be erected, to sacrifice to the gods of the five soils and five grains, specifically to pray for
  timely rain and sun and an abundant grain harvest. Each year one household shall take its turn as
  altar-head, keeping the altar grounds constantly clean. At the spring and autumn she [earth-god]
  festivals, sacrificial goods shall be prepared in advance, and on the day [villagers] shall
  gather to hold the sacrifice.") - i.e., the Ming state's own rule was ONE ALTAR PER 100
  HOUSEHOLDS (a li), directly on point for a 40-100-household village: at the upper end of that
  range a single settlement would be exactly one li-she unit; smaller villages would share.
- On the transition from bare altar to housed shrine: "里社的'坛而不屋'被偶像崇拜所替代，如顾禄
  在《清嘉录》中所说'吴中土谷之神，分配古贤名姓，塑像奉祀，如任彦昇、蒋子文、张翰、王洵之类
  皆是'。"
  (EN: "The li-she [altar without a building] was replaced by idol worship, as Gu Lu writes in the
  Qing-dynasty Qingjialu: 'The earth-and-grain gods of Wu [Suzhou] are assigned the names of
  ancient worthies and worshipped as sculpted images - Ren Yansheng, Jiang Ziwen, Zhang Han, Wang
  Xun, and the like are all such [examples].'") - i.e. the bare altar became a housed 土地庙 with an
  image, and it is this later, housed form that the shrine-boundary (miaojie) analysis concerns.
- The measured ratio (from Hamashima Atsutoshi's gazetteer-based calculation, as reported and
  engaged with in this paper): "他以湖州乌青镇为例计算出当地大体上每十村强有一个土地庙，这是
  低地的例子；而地处东部微高地的盛桥里则大约40个自然村才有一个土地庙。"
  (EN: "Using Wuqing township in Huzhou as an example, he calculated that in that area there was
  roughly one 土地庙 for every ten-odd villages - this is a lowland example; whereas in Shengqiaoli,
  located on slightly elevated ground to the east, there was only about one 土地庙 for every forty
  natural villages.")
- Settlement-size contrast behind that ratio: "高地乡村一般是形成了小村或散村的聚落形态，如盛桥
  里212个自然村落的平均户数为7.7户；而低地乡村则是形成了集村的聚落形态，如相城69个村落的平均
  户数为112.9户。"
  (EN: "Hill-country villages generally took the form of small or dispersed settlements - for
  example, the 212 natural villages of Shengqiaoli averaged 7.7 households each - whereas lowland
  villages formed nucleated settlements, e.g. the 69 villages of Xiangcheng averaged 112.9
  households each.") This is a directly useful calibration: a 40-100-household village corresponds
  to the LOWLAND nucleated type in this source's own terms, i.e. the "roughly one 土地庙 per ten
  villages" ratio, not the sparser hill-country ratio.
- A concrete sub-village worship-group example, citing 费孝通 [Fei Xiaotong], 《江村经济》 [Peasant
  Life in China]: "在当地有着奉祀神道'刘皇'（即刘猛将）的地域性群体，由大约30户组成，被称为
  '段'。江村共有11个段，每个段都有自己的刘皇偶像..."
  (EN: "There was a territorial group in the locality that worshipped the god 'Liu Huang' [i.e.
  Liu Mengjiang], made up of about 30 households and called a 'duan' [segment]. Kaixiangong
  village had 11 such duan in total, each with its own image of Liu Huang...") - i.e. within one
  large village, worship was organized at the sub-village level in ~30-household units, each with
  its own separate icon, distinct from the village-level/inter-village 土地庙.
- A second example table (泖口 administrative village, Liantang township, Qingpu county, from
  Hamashima's fieldwork as reported here), "表1：青浦县练塘镇泖口村的社" [Table 1: the she of
  Maokou village, Liantang township, Qingpu county] gives, per natural village: 泖口 hamlet 87
  households (2 dates given: 436/87 and 270/71 population/households) sharing 3 社 [she shrine
  units]; 夏家村 hamlet 54 households sharing 1 社; 尤家村 hamlet 36 then 58 households sharing 1
  社. (Figures as printed in the source table; two columns evidently represent two different survey
  dates, not fully labeled in the extracted text - flagging that the table's column headers were
  not cleanly recovered by the layout-preserving text extraction and should be checked against the
  PDF's actual table/image if this level of precision is needed.)

This source is far and away the strongest single quantitative find of this pass for Q4: it gives
an explicit, cited, gazetteer-derived village-to-shrine RATIO (not just a qualitative "every
village had one"), and it explicitly distinguishes a Ming-dynasty administrative ideal (1 altar /
100 households) from the later, unevenly-distributed reality (1 housed 土地庙 per 10-40 natural
villages, depending on terrain and settlement nucleation).
