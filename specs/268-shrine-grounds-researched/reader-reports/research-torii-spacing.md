# Torii spacing and sandō length - research findings

Question: how far apart were torii on a small shrine's approach, and how long was a village
shrine's sandō? Context: our village shrine has 7 torii at 30 ft (~9 m) spacing, innermost 20 ft
from the hall; the record currently has only two attested poles (dense donation rows at ~0.1-0.2 m,
and ranked ichi/ni/san-no-torii hundreds of meters apart) with nothing attested in between, and the
30 ft pitch is an invented house rule.

All quotes below were fetched from the live page at the URL given (via WebFetch), not recalled from
search snippets. Japanese originals are given with my English translation.

## 1. Spacing of torii in short/dense donation rows

**Fushimi Inari Senbon Torii** (large-scale, not a village shrine, but the canonical dense-donation
case):
- Multiple secondary sources converge on **10-20 cm** gaps between adjacent torii in the packed rows.
  (Search-result synthesis, not a single verbatim fetched quote for this specific figure - flagging
  per the rules that this one is search-snippet-derived, not page-fetched verbatim.)

**Yutoku Inari Shrine (祐徳稲荷神社), Saga** - fetched
<https://www.sagatv.co.jp/kachiplus/media/archives/903653>:
- "境内全体で300基弱" - "just under 300 torii across the whole precinct."
- "223基" - 223 torii counted along the path from the main hall up to the inner sanctuary (奥の院).
- "ほぼ、10センチ間隔で並んでいるようです" - "They appear to be lined up at intervals of almost
  10 centimeters."
- "300メートル" - the path from the main hall to the inner sanctuary at the mountain peak is
  ~300 m.
- ARITHMETIC (mine, not the source's): 223 torii distributed somewhere within a ~300 m climb is
  consistent with the stated 10 cm pitch only for a short packed cluster; over the *whole* 300 m the
  mean pitch would be ~1.3 m if evenly spread. The source does not resolve this - the 10 cm figure
  describes a locally dense stretch, not the whole climb. Flagged as uncertain.

**Manzo Inari Shrine (萬蔵稲荷神社), Shiroishi, Miyagi** - a genuinely small, rural, forest shrine
(founded c. Tenmei 5 / 1785) - fetched <https://note.com/suzu_ki6155/n/n4563cc3b74a3>:
- "帰り道、鳥居がいくつあるのか数えてみました。途中、数を見失いそうになりながらも、数え上げ…結果114個でした！"
  - "On the way back I tried counting the torii. I nearly lost count partway through, but tallied
    them up - the result was 114!"
- "入り口から７分ほど歩くと景色が開けてきました。" - "After walking about 7 minutes from the
  entrance, the view opened up."
- No page gives the approach's length in meters. ARITHMETIC (mine): a ~7-minute walk on an unpaved
  forest path (roughly 50-70 m/min) is very roughly 350-500 m for 114 torii, i.e., a mean pitch on
  the order of 3-4 m - but this is my inference from a walking-time anecdote, not a measured
  figure, and I could not find a page stating the shrine's sandō length directly. Treat as
  suggestive only, not evidence.

**上色見熊野座神社 (Kamishikimi Kumanoza Shrine), Kumamoto** - initially a promising lead (famous
mossy stone-torii-tunnel photos) but checked and RULED OUT: the "97 along the ~200 m approach"
figure that appears in search results is for **stone lanterns (石灯籠)**, not torii - confirmed by
fetching the Wikipedia page (no torii count/spacing given there at all) and by a follow-up search
that explicitly resolved the ambiguity ("石造灯籠...97基...200メートル余りの参道"). Do not use this
shrine as a torii-spacing data point; if useful at all, it is a lantern-spacing data point
(~200 m / 97 ≈ 2 m per lantern), which is a different fixture with different siting logic.

**Conclusion so far on dense rows:** the only page-verified per-torii spacing figure for a real
donation row is Yutoku Inari's ~10 cm, i.e., gates essentially back to back (post to post), which
matches the existing record's "dense donation row, ~0.5-1 m" pole (a bit tighter, if anything).
I found no page giving a spacing in the 2-9 m range for any donation row, dense or sparse.

## 2. Distance from innermost torii to haiden/honden; typical sandō length at small/village shrines

- Nippon.com's own Japanese-language explainer of sandō
  (<https://www.nippon.com/ja/views/b05203/>, fetched) gives no length figures at all - it only
  defines sandō as "境内の鳥居から社殿へと向かうまでの道" ("the path from the precinct's torii to
  the shrine buildings") and discusses walking etiquette (staying off the center/seichū line).
- Jinja Honcho's own page (<https://www.jinjahoncho.or.jp/omairi/sandou/>, fetched) is likewise a
  general explainer with no figures: "参道とは、境内の鳥居から社殿へと向かうまでの道のことを言います。"
- Kotobank-sourced search synthesis notes the extreme end of the range: Tsurugaoka Hachimangū's
  outermost torii stands 1.8 km from the shrine precinct - useful only as an upper bound for a major
  shrine, not informative for a village shrine.
- Nagao Shrine (長尾神社), Katsuragi, Nara - English Wikipedia, fetched
  <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagao_Shrine>: "the outermost gate, Ichi-no-torii, leads to the
  second gate, Ni-no-torii, through a 200 meter long path called a sandō." This is a mid-size
  shrine's ichi-to-ni-no-torii leg, not a village shrine's whole approach, and it is squarely in the
  "ranked gates, hundreds of meters apart" pole the record already has.
- Kashihara Jingū (a major state shrine, not a village shrine, but the only page I found with an
  internal torii-to-torii breakdown) - fetched <https://kashiharajingu.or.jp/point/8277.html>:
  "橿原神宮の鳥居は表参道に2基、北・西参道に各1基の計4基あり" - four torii total, two on the main
  approach, one each on the north and west approaches; the second (innermost) of the main-approach
  pair is the largest, along a main approach of about 300 m total. No sub-distances between the two
  main-approach torii are given on the page.
- I could not find any page (municipal cultural-property listing, shrine's own site, or travel
  writing) that states a village/hamlet-scale shrine's full sandō length or its innermost-torii-to-
  haiden distance in meters or ken. This appears to be a genuine gap in what is publicly written up
  online for small shrines specifically (as opposed to famous/major shrines, which are the only ones
  that get their sandō measured and published).

## 3. Where the first torii stands relative to the road

This is the best-supported point. Two independent, page-fetched, citable sources converge:

- English Wikipedia, Torii (<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torii>, fetched): "The road leading to a
  Shinto shrine (sandō) is almost always straddled by one or more torii, which is one aspect that
  could help distinguish a Shinto shrine from a Buddhist temple." And: "Other torii can be found
  farther into the shrine to represent increasing levels of holiness as one nears the inner
  sanctuary (honden)."
- English Wikipedia, Sandō (<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sand%C5%8D>, fetched): "A sandō...is the
  road approaching either a Shinto shrine or a Buddhist temple. Its point of origin is usually
  straddled in the first case by a Shinto torii...gates which mark the beginning of the shrine's...
  territory." Also: "There can also be stone lanterns and other decorations at any point along its
  course."
- Kokugakuin University Encyclopedia of Shinto, Torii entry
  (<https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9635>, fetched): "When multiple torii are
  present, the largest one is normally called the 'first torii' (ichi no torii), and stands at the
  approachway to the grounds of the shrine." Additional torii "may also be found at various points
  within the precincts to indicate increasing levels of holiness as one approaches the main
  sanctuary (honden)."

Together these three independent, fetched, citable pages support: the first (outermost/largest)
torii sits at the point where the approach road becomes shrine territory - i.e., at the edge of the
grounds/grove, not partway up a flight of steps and not deep inside the precinct - and any further
torii are placed at intervals further in to mark rising sanctity toward the honden, with no fixed
distance rule given anywhere I found.

## 4. Edo-period dating of multiple-torii donation; did it reach village shrines before 1868?

- Reference-service page on Fushimi Inari's Senbon Torii
  (<https://crd.ndl.go.jp/reference/entry/index.php?page=ref_view&id=1000130073>, fetched):
  "これらの鳥居は，江戸～明治時代に参拝者の奉納により，建てられはじめました。" - "These torii began
  to be erected from the Edo into the Meiji period, through donations by worshippers." This
  confirms the *custom itself* (donating a torii as thanks for a granted wish, on the 願いが「通る」
  pun) is Edo-into-Meiji, but the page is specifically about Fushimi Inari and does not say whether
  or how fast the practice reached ordinary village shrines.
- Separate, single-torii Edo-period donations at ordinary/village-scale shrines ARE well attested by
  municipal cultural-property pages, but these are individual stone torii, not donation rows:
  - Kato City page on the stone torii at 河高住吉神社
    (<https://www.city.kato.lg.jp/kakukanogoannai/kyouikushinkoubu/shogaigakushuka/bunkazai/siteibunkazai/kensiteibunkazai/1510384600965.html>,
    fetched): "柱間が2.5m、高さが2.8mを測るもの" (post span 2.5 m, height 2.8 m), "室町時代末期～江戸時代
    初期の石鳥居と考えられています" ("believed to be a stone torii from the late Muromachi into the
    early Edo period") - i.e., a single ordinary-shrine torii, not a row, dated very early Edo at
    latest.
  - Search-result synthesis (not independently page-verified for all three, flagging accordingly)
    of other municipal listings: Fukusaki Town's stone torii, 3.28 m total height, 4.96 m overall
    length, dated Keichō 16 (1611); a Fukui City torii at Hachiman Shrine, post span 1.2 m, height
    "just over 2 m," dated Keichō 18 (1613). Both are single torii at ordinary shrines, both very
    early Edo.
  - A separate search synthesis (not page-fetched) reported village/ujiko-funded single stone torii
    at Kamata and elsewhere in 1774, 1790, and 1800 - again single gates donated by parishioners or a
    local lord, not rows.
- Net finding: I found solid, page-verified evidence that (a) the *single* memorial/donation stone
  torii at an ordinary village shrine is a well-attested Edo-period (even quite early Edo) act, and
  (b) the *row-of-many-donated-torii* custom associated with Inari worship is documented as
  Edo-into-Meiji at Fushimi Inari specifically. I found NO page - after searching specifically for
  village-shrine multi-torii donation rows, Edo-period village Inari donation customs, and
  cultural-property listings for torii rows rather than single torii - that documents an ordinary
  village shrine acquiring a *row* of several donated torii before 1868. The absence is notable: it
  is consistent with (but does not prove) the row-donation custom being a phenomenon of larger,
  well-trafficked Inari shrines that could draw many individual donors, something a small village
  shrine with one parish would be much less likely to accumulate before the Meiji era at the
  earliest. I would call this an absence note, not a settled negative.

## 5. Shrine-architecture guidance on torii placement

- Beyond the Encyclopedia of Shinto and Wikipedia passages already quoted in section 3 (torii marks
  the start of the sacred precinct at the road; further torii mark rising sanctity toward the
  honden), I could not find a public page giving a numeric design rule (e.g., "space torii N ken
  apart" or "the sandō should run N meters"). Searches for 宮大工 (shrine carpenter) design guidance
  and for a generic 神社建築 placement standard returned only general descriptive pages (Jinja
  Honcho's own site, general shrine-anatomy explainers) with no numbers. This looks like a genuine
  gap in what shrine-carpentry manuals put on the open web, as opposed to something specialist print
  manuals might contain but that I cannot access or verify as public web pages.

## Bottom line for the map decision

- No public page attests a torii-row pitch anywhere in the 2-9 m band our 30 ft (~9 m) spacing sits
  in. The one page-verified per-gate spacing for an actual donation row (Yutoku Inari, ~10 cm) is
  essentially gate-to-gate, far tighter than 30 ft. The one page-verified ichi-to-ni-no-torii leg
  (Nagao Shrine, 200 m) is far looser. The Manzo Inari 114-torii/7-minute-walk anecdote is the
  closest thing to a "several meters apart, informally, at a real small forest shrine" data point I
  could find, and even that yields (by my own walking-speed arithmetic, not a stated figure)
  something closer to 3-4 m than 9 m.
- The GM's suspicion that 30 ft is too far apart for a row of 7 donated torii is not contradicted by
  anything found, and the nearest real analogue (Manzo Inari, arithmetic-only) points toward a
  tighter pitch, on the order of a few meters, being more representative of an informal small-shrine
  donation row than 9 m.
- The "20 ft from innermost torii to the hall" figure has no attested anchor either way; I found no
  village-shrine page stating this distance at all (see section 2's gap).
