# Research: what an ordinary village shrine's precinct contained

Scope: village-scale Shinto shrines in premodern (pre-1868) Japan, with a China comparison for
village temples (村廟/土地廟). All quotes below were fetched live from the URL given (WebFetch or
WebSearch-with-fetch); none are from memory. Where a search only returned an aggregated snippet and
the underlying page could not be fetched (403/500 errors), this is flagged explicitly and the claim
is NOT treated as a verified quote.

---

## 1. 拝殿 vs 本殿 arrangement at small shrines - was there often only one building? 覆屋?

**Finding: YES - many small/village shrines have NO separate haiden, only a honden protected by a
覆屋 (ooiya/oiya, "covering house"), which sometimes doubles as the worship space.**

- ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/神社建築 (fetched):
  > "小型の本殿では、風雨から守るために覆屋をかける場合もある。"
  ("For small-scale main halls, a covering structure is sometimes placed over them to protect them
  from wind and rain.")
  > "一部の神社では山や岩を神体として崇めるため、本殿を持たず、神体を直接拝むための拝殿のみがある
  ところ（大神神社・金鑚神社など）や拝殿も持たない（檜原神社や湯殿山神社）ところもある。"
  (Some shrines venerate a mountain or rock as the go-shintai and have no honden at all, only a
  haiden for direct worship, or lack even a haiden.)

- WebSearch summarizing multiple pages (kojiyama.net "3. 神社の建物構成と名称" and others; the
  kojiyama.net page itself returned HTTP 500 on two direct fetch attempts, so treat the following as
  a search-snippet claim, not an independently re-verified quote - flagged per the rule that a page
  that cannot be read is not citable as a quote):
  > "旧村社などは本殿覆屋が拝殿も兼ねている場合があり... いつ頃からか、本殿の雨風雪による損傷を補う
  ために本殿を包む屋根を設けて囲うようになった。これを本殿覆屋といっており、これが拝殿のない小社の
  例として挙げられています。" with named real examples: 式内阿須々岐神社本殿覆屋 (Ayabe, Kyoto Pref.)
  and 子守神社（式内 巨椋神社）本殿覆屋 (Uji, Kyoto Pref.).
  ABSENCE NOTE: I could not re-fetch the kojiyama.net page directly (500 error both times) or find
  an independent cultural-property page for these two examples with construction dates in the time
  available, so this specific claim rests on the search engine's own summary of that page, not a
  verbatim-verified quote. It should be treated as directionally correct but not citation-grade
  until the source page is re-fetched successfully.

- kotobank.jp/word/村社-555866 (fetched), on 村社 (village-shrine rank, an official Meiji-era
  classification but describing the pre-existing社 it absorbed):
  > "旧制度の社格の一。郷社ごうしゃの下、無格社の上。多くは村の鎮守の社などが列格され、社掌しゃしょう
  が置かれていた。" and "村の鎮守社など、各地に鎮座する小規模な神社。"
  Confirms 村社 = the village's existing tutelary shrine (鎮守), officially described as small-scale.

**Assessment: STRONG support for "one small building, no separate haiden" as a real, attested
form at village scale**, though I could not pin an exact prevalence percentage or firm pre-1868
dating for when 覆屋-only became typical versus honden+haiden+heiden as one continuous range (the
three-part range described for large shrines like Mishima Taisha, 1866, is explicitly a "複合社殿"
[compound hall] and not representative of village scale).

## 2. 手水舎 / 手水鉢 - roofed pavilion vs plain stone basin; when did it reach village shrines?

**Finding: The purification RITE is ancient; the ROOFED BUILDING is an Edo-period development,
starting at grand shrines, and it is NOT clear the roofed form was universal at village scale -
many small/unstaffed shrines have only a plain stone basin, or none.**

- note.com/liga/n/na05eabb89521 (fetched):
  > "手や口を清めるという行為そのものは、非常に古い起源を持ちます" (the act of purifying hands/mouth
  is very ancient)
  > "屋根つきの建物としての手水舎となると、歴史はぐっと新しくなります" (as a roofed BUILDING, the
  history is much newer)
  Identifies Nikko Toshogu's 御水屋 (mizuya) as Japan's earliest such roofed pavilion, water basin
  1618, building 1636, driven by "江戸の平和がもたらした参詣ブーム" (the Edo-peace pilgrimage boom).
  The article explicitly does NOT address whether small shrines had roofless basins or when they
  got pavilions - I flag this as an open question, not resolved.

- www.city.higashimatsuyama.lg.jp (Yakyu Inari Shrine cultural-property page, via search snippet,
  not independently re-fetched in full - the search tool's summary states): the shrine's 手水鉢 dates
  to 文政九年 (1826), i.e., late Edo, as an inscribed dated example of an ordinary (not grand
  imperial-tier) shrine's basin.

- homemate-research (fetched, glossary page on 手水鉢): confirms the basin was "originally located
  within a small roofed structure called 手水舎," but the page does NOT state whether small/village
  shrines commonly lacked the roofed form - explicitly a gap in this source.

**Assessment: MODERATE support.** Solid dating that the roofed chozuya is an Edo-period elite
innovation (Nikko, 1636) spreading via the pilgrimage boom; the specific claim that a village shrine
would have had only a plain stone basin (no roof) is plausible and consistent with what unstaffed/
small shrines look like today, but I found no page that states this outright for the pre-1868
period. Recommend treating "plain basin, no pavilion, at ordinary village shrines" as the informed
default with an ABSENCE NOTE (searched: multiple queries on 手水鉢のみ/手水舎がない小さな神社;
found only modern anecdotal Yahoo-chie-bukuro material, not usable as a citation).

## 3. 狛犬 - when did stone komainu reach ordinary village shrines?

**Finding: CONFIRMED - stone komainu donation is an Edo-period, and specifically a COMMONER
phenomenon, i.e., consistent with the user's belief that it's a mid/late-Edo-onward, not ancient,
feature at village scale.**

- ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/狛犬 (fetched):
  > "江戸時代以降は庶民の奉納による石の狛犬の例が増え、その形態も子獅子を連れたものや玉を持ったものな
  ど多様化していく" (From the Edo period onward, examples of stone komainu donated by commoners
  increased, and their forms diversified.)
  > On earlier Kanto ceramic komainu (Kamakura-period onward): "神社に奉納されたものの多くは小型で、
  庶民による奉納と考えられる" (most were small-scale, thought to be commoner donations) - but this is
  about ceramic, not stone, komainu, and is a much earlier and rarer phenomenon.
  The article does not give exact dimensions for typical Edo commoner stone komainu.

- WebSearch aggregate (not independently re-fetched as a single page, general encyclopedic
  consensus repeated across komainu.net and others): stone komainu donation became a popular
  commoner practice specifically because, unlike expensive stone lanterns, they were an affordable
  status marker for a newly-prosperous but non-elite Edo-period village populace.

**Assessment: STRONG support for "mid/late Edo onward" as the period when stone komainu reach
village shrines**, consistent with the user's prior. Recommend NOT including komainu, or including
them only as a late-Edo option/knob, at any village shrine meant to read as older or as poor.

## 4. 石灯籠 - stone lanterns, donated

**Finding: Stone lanterns are much OLDER in origin (Nara-period import) but at village scale they
appear as VOTIVE, dated, inscribed commoner donations, well attested at ordinary shrines by
mid/late Edo.**

- WebSearch aggregate citing a Niiza City local-history page (city.niiza.lg.jp bunkazai-kenbun-024,
  not independently re-fetched line-by-line in this pass, but the search tool quoted an inscription
  directly from the page):
  > inscription on a stone lantern base: "奉納 氷川大明神 寛政十一未歳六月吉日" / "奉納御寳前武州新座
  郡石神氏子中" (Dedicated to Hikawa Daimyojin, an auspicious day in the 6th month of Kansei 11
  [1799]; dedicated by the ujiko of Shakugami, Niiza District, Musashi Province) - an ordinary
  village shrine (石神村の鎮守氷川神社), not a famous large shrine.

**Assessment: MODERATE-STRONG.** Origin story (Nara-period, from Baekje) is generic/encyclopedic and
not itself about village shrines, but the Niiza inscription is a genuine village-shrine example
dated 1799, i.e., late Edo, and fits the same "commoner votive donation" pattern as komainu. Not yet
independently re-fetched at full page level - flagged as search-snippet sourced pending a direct
fetch if this becomes load-bearing for a spec's citation.

## 5. 神楽殿 / 舞殿 / 農村舞台 (kagura hall / dance hall / village kabuki-noh stage)

**Finding: 神楽殿 as a purpose-built roofed stage is a Heian-late/Kamakura elite development;
農村舞台 (the FARMER kabuki/joruri stage, most relevant to an ordinary village) is a much later,
Edo-period (specifically Bunka-Bunsei era, early 1800s) commoner phenomenon, overwhelmingly built ON
shrine grounds.**

- ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/農村歌舞伎舞台 (fetched):
  > "全国に1777の農村舞台（能舞台・人形浄瑠璃舞台含む）が確認されている" (1,777 rural stages,
  including noh and puppet-theater stages, have been identified nationwide.)
  > "神社敷地に建設される場合が多く、寺院に設けられる場合もあるが、独立敷地の例は少ない" (Most were
  built on shrine precinct land; some at temples; independent-site examples are rare.)
  Permanent stages date from "文化・文政期" (Bunka-Bunsei era, roughly 1804-1830), i.e. late Edo, and
  the practice of village kabuki performance itself spread from the mid-1700s. Example: the 中山農村
  歌舞伎舞台 at 春日神社 (Shodoshima) - thatched hip roof, 6.5-ken frontage x 4.5-ken depth, with a
  revolving stage and trapdoor (すっぽん).

- WebSearch aggregate (not independently re-fetched): 常設の神楽殿 (permanent kagura halls) appear
  "平安末期には見られ、鎌倉時代より普及した" (seen in the late Heian period, spreading from Kamakura).

**Assessment: STRONG support, with an important DISTINCTION the user should keep**: 神楽殿/舞殿
(dedicated kagura-performance halls) are the OLDER, more elite-associated form; 農村舞台 (the
village kabuki/joruri stage, closer to what a real farming village would have) is a LATE-EDO
addition and not universal - only ~1,777 documented nationwide, concentrated in Tokushima, Hyogo
and Nagano. For a generic village shrine this should be a KNOB (present with modest rolled
probability, weighted toward late-Edo dating), not a default fixture, and should not be conflated
with a formal 神楽殿.

## 6. 摂社・末社 / 境内社 / 祠 (subsidiary shrines in the precinct)

**Finding: The concept is well-documented for shrines generally, but I could NOT find a source that
states village-scale prevalence versus the phenomenon being concentrated at grand/prestigious
shrines.**

- ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/摂末社 (fetched):
  > "摂末社（せつまつしゃ）とは、神社本社とは別に、その神社の管理に属し、その境内または神社の附近の
  境外にある小規模な神社のことで、摂社（せっしゃ）と末社（まっしゃ）と併せた呼称である。"
  (Sessha/massha collectively denotes small-scale shrines administratively attached to a main
  shrine, located within its precinct or nearby.)
  The article explicitly discusses only large, prestigious shrines' examples and does NOT address
  village-scale prevalence when asked directly.
- jinjahoncho.or.jp/omairi/smallkeidai/ (found via search, not independently fetched in this pass):
  states such small shrines within a precinct are "珍しくなく、特に歴史のある格式高い神社に多くみら
  れます" (not rare, but seen especially at shrines with a long history and high formal rank) - this
  actually argues AGAINST assuming they are equally common at low-status village shrines.

ABSENCE NOTE: searched "境内社 祠 村社 小さな神社 摂社末社 由来" and variants; every source discusses
sessha/massha definitionally or via famous-shrine examples. No source found stating a village
tutelary shrine (mura no chinju) typically had one or more subsidiary shrines/祠 versus not.
**Recommendation: treat a 境内社/祠 at the drawn village shrine as a KNOB/calibrated liberty, not a
default, given the explicit "especially at high-rank shrines" statement above, which cuts the other
way from assuming ubiquity.**

## 7. 御神木 with shimenawa (sacred tree)

**Finding: Very well attested as ancient and near-universal in concept (shrines are traditionally
founded AT a sacred grove/tree, not the reverse), with shimenawa as the standard marking device.**

- ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/神木 (fetched):
  > States shrines were "originally built at locations with sacred trees (神籬 himorogi)" - i.e. the
  tree/grove precedes the building, not vice versa, for most shrines.
  > Common species named: cedar (スギ), sakaki (榊), nagi, mochi-noki.
  > "神依木 (kami-yori-gi) and 勧進木 (kanjin-boku) are treated as 'seats for deities' with shimenawa
  ropes marking them as special." (paraphrase-quote from the fetch tool; the underlying Japanese for
  this specific line was not returned verbatim by the fetch, so treat the ENGLISH framing as a
  paraphrase, not an exact quote - a re-fetch would be needed for the precise Japanese wording.)
  > Dating: cites Man'yoshu book 4, poem 517 (Nara-period, 8th c.) as already reflecting a taboo/
  divine-punishment belief about touching sacred trees, i.e., the belief long predates any Edo-era
  village-shrine question.
  > The article itself flags "出典がまったく示されていないか不十分です" (sources are entirely absent
  or insufficient) - Wikipedia's own quality flag, noted for honesty.

**Assessment: STRONG support for including a goshinboku with shimenawa as a near-default feature**,
ancient in concept, generic across scale (a single old tree with a fence/rope, not an elite-only
luxury). Species should be locally appropriate (sugi, sakaki, camphor, zelkova - see #10 below on
the grove) rather than invented.

## 8. 絵馬殿/絵馬堂, 神輿庫, 社務所, 籠り堂/参籠所 (village gathering/retreat hall)

**Finding: Mixed. 絵馬殿/絵馬堂 and grand 社務所 buildings are attested chiefly at large/famous
shrines in the sources I could reach; 籠堂/参籠所 (retreat-and-lodge hall) IS well attested as a
generic religious-institution feature and fits the user's instinct that village shrines had a
villagers'-use gathering hall, tied to the 宮座 (miyaza) institution.**

- kotobank.jp/word/籠堂-2040496 (fetched):
  > "神社、仏閣に付属して、信者、行者がこもって祈願、修行する堂。おこもりをするお堂。" (A hall
  attached to shrines or temples where believers/practitioners seclude themselves to pray and train;
  a hall for "okomori.") First attested usage cited from the 1667 joruri text "Sanshō Dayū."

- ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宮座 (fetched):
  > "宮座とは、地域の鎮守もしくは氏神である神社の祭祀に携わる村落内の特権的な組織及びそれを構成する
  資格者の集団。" (Miyaza = a privileged village organization, and its qualified membership, that
  conducts the rituals of the local tutelary/ujigami shrine.) Confirms miyaza used shrine-attached
  fields (宮田・座田) to fund rites, i.e., a real economic/institutional tie to the shrine grounds.
  The article, when asked directly, did NOT mention a 籠堂/参籠所 by name in connection with miyaza -
  this is a GAP, not a contradiction: the institutional tie (miyaza <-> shrine) is confirmed, but the
  specific building link (miyaza meetings happening in a named 籠堂 on-site) was not found in this
  page and needs a dedicated local-history source to confirm for a specific village type.

- okunijinja.or.jp/facilities/equipment/ (fetched, a grand shrine, Totomi Ichinomiya - used only as
  a check on terminology, NOT as evidence for village scale): describes 社務所 as the shrine's
  administrative office; the page does not mention 神輿庫 or 絵馬殿 at all, which is itself mildly
  informative - even a large shrine's public facilities page doesn't foreground these as a fixed set.

ABSENCE NOTE: I searched "絵馬殿 絵馬堂 神輿庫 社務所 村の神社" and every readable hit (Konpira-gu,
Yasaka Jinja, Dazaifu Tenmangu, Takemikumari Jinja) is a large, famous shrine's 絵馬堂. I found NO
source describing an ordinary village shrine having a dedicated 絵馬殿 building (as opposed to ema
simply being hung on a wall or under the haiden eave, which is the more plausible village-scale
form). Likewise 神輿庫 as a SEPARATE building is attested at grand shrines but I found no statement
either way for ordinary villages - many small shrines likely store the mikoshi at the shrine
monk's/caretaker's dwelling or under the honden's covering roof rather than in a dedicated kura.

**Recommendation:**
- 籠堂/参籠所-as-villagers'-gathering-hall: keep as a real, well-founded feature, but note the
  precise architectural form at village scale (is it the same building as the resident monk's
  dwelling already in the plan, or separate?) is NOT resolved by what I found - given the existing
  plan already has "a hall that doubles as the resident shrine-monk's dwelling," the simplest,
  best-supported move is to have THAT hall also serve as the miyaza's 籠堂, rather than adding a
  fourth building, unless further research turns up a village shrine with a distinct komoridō.
- 絵馬殿, dedicated 神輿庫: DO NOT default-include at village scale; no support found. If ema exist,
  hang them under the haiden/honden eave, not a dedicated hall.
- 社務所 as a distinct building: only attested at large shrines in what I found; a village shrine of
  this scale plausibly has NO separate office, folding that function into the resident monk's hall.

## 9. 土俵 (sumo ring), 石碑, 力石, stone steps, 参道 surface

**Finding: All well attested as genuine village-shrine-festival features, mid/late Edo onward for
the specific inscribed/dated examples.**

- www.kokugakuin.ac.jp/article/50375 (fetched, Kokugakuin University, a strong academic-adjacent
  source):
  > "土俵には神が宿るとされている為です" (the dohyo is held to have a kami dwelling within it) - a
  consecration rite (土俵祭) with buried offerings (salt, kelp, dried squid, victory chestnuts,
  washed rice, kaya nuts) as "鎮めもの" (grounding offerings).
  > "全国の神社において奉納される相撲においても同様に土俵祭がおこなわれています" (the same dohyo
  ritual is performed for shrine-dedicated sumo nationwide) - explicitly extends beyond grand
  shrines to ordinary shrine-dedicated sumo. No fixed dimensions given for a village dohyo (a modern
  competition dohyo is a raised, roofed structure - a village festival dohyo is normally a simple
  ground-level ring, not assumed identical to that).

- www.city.nerima.tokyo.jp (Hikawa Jinja chikaraishi, fetched):
  > "氷川神社の参道わきには、だ円形をした河原石（かわらいし）が八つ置かれています。" (Eight oval
  river stones stand beside the approach path of Hikawa Shrine.)
  > "特に55貫（約200キログラム）と刻まれている力石は、区内では大きな部類に入ります。" (One stone
  inscribed 55 kan, about 200 kg, is among the largest in the ward.)
  > Era: "江戸時代後期から明治時代にかけて" (late Edo through Meiji) - used by young men of a
  farming village as festival-day strength contests; the heaviest lifted stone was dedicated to the
  shrine. Location confirmed as BESIDE THE SANDO (approach path), not at the honden itself.

- WebSearch aggregate on 参道 surface (not independently re-fetched at the specific-village level):
  gravel (玉砂利) and paved stone (石畳) are the two standard sando surfaces; a dated example
  (Mt. Oyama's stone-paved sando) originates in the Kansei era (1789-1800), late Edo. No source
  found giving a simple-dirt/unpaved sando as the more common village default versus gravel - this
  remains an open question; gravel is cheap and plausible as the default even for a poor village
  shrine, but this is inference, not a found citation.

**Assessment: STRONG for 力石 (dated, located beside the sando, plausible village addition,
late-Edo/Meiji) and for dohyo-as-consecrated-ground (extends to ordinary shrine sumo). MODERATE for
参道 surface (gravel/paved stone attested generically, not tied to a village-poverty gradient in any
source found). No page found specifically on 石碑 (memorial stones) at village shrines as a category
- flagged as an open search, not yet run to completion given time.

## 10. 鎮守の森 (the grove)

**Finding: The grove is definitionally what a shrine IS built around (tree/grove precedes
building), commonly forms a ring but not universally total encirclement, and typical species are
regionally-appropriate evergreens.**

- ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/鎮守の森 (fetched):
  > States the grove is "set and maintained" to surround the shrine, without stating this is always
  complete 360-degree encirclement (a rice-field-adjacent grove example in the article's own image
  captions shows a partial/asymmetric arrangement).
  > Species: "Camphor trees (Cinnamomum camphora), Zelkova, and Muku tree are seen in many shrines,"
  with camphor specifically flagged as possibly NOT originally native to the region in question,
  implying deliberate planting/selection rather than pure remnant-forest preservation everywhere.
  > No acreage figure specific to ordinary vs. grand shrines was found in this article; a separate
  reference-desk page (crd.ndl.go.jp) exists on total sacred-grove area nationwide but was not
  independently fetched in this pass.
- WebSearch aggregate citing forestry data (風致林/風致保安林 category, not independently re-fetched):
  total area 28,000 ha, 0.2% of all protected forest nationwide - a NATIONAL aggregate, not a
  per-shrine figure, so not directly usable for sizing one village shrine's grove.
- WebSearch aggregate on species by climate zone: "暖帯の低地では、クスノキ、カシ、シイなどが多く"
  (warm-belt lowlands: camphor, oak/kashi, shii/chinkapin oak predominate); "本州中部以南の多くの鎮守
  の森では、タブノキ・クスノキ・シイ・カシなどの常緑広葉樹が重要な構成要素" (evergreen broadleafs -
  tabu-noki, camphor, shii, kashi - are the key component of most sacred groves south of central
  Honshu).

**Assessment: MODERATE-STRONG.** Good species guidance (region-appropriate evergreen broadleaf:
kashi/shii/tabu-noki/camphor in warm lowlands; sugi/hinoki conifer stands elsewhere per the same
source) with the caveat that total encirclement is NOT confirmed as universal - a grove that wraps
part but not all of the precinct (e.g., backing and flanking, open at the torii approach) is at
least as well supported as a full ring, and is also the more common REAL-WORLD visual pattern in
the photos referenced by these pages.

## 12. Shrine-temples (神宮寺/別当寺) at village level - layout

**Finding: Real and common (a bettoji-monk, not the shrine priest, held real power over the shrine
until Meiji); at village scale ONE bettoji often administered SEVERAL village shrines, which argues
against every small shrine having its own resident dedicated temple complex - fits the plan's choice
of a single hall doubling as the monk's dwelling rather than a separate temple compound.**

- ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/別当寺 (fetched):
  > "神社の境内に僧坊が置かれて渾然一体となっていた" (monks' quarters were placed within the shrine
  precinct, forming an integrated whole [with the shrine]) - directly supports "hall that doubles as
  the resident shrine-monk's dwelling" being sited ON the shrine grounds, integrated rather than a
  separate walled temple compound.
  > "神仏習合の時代から明治維新に至るまでは、神社で最も権力があったのは別当であり、宮司はその下に
  置かれた" (from the shinbutsu-shugo era until the Meiji Restoration, the betto held the greatest
  power at a shrine, with the shrine priest ranked below him) - i.e. at a real premodern shrine of
  this kind, the resident Buddhist monk-figure typically outranked any Shinto priest, consistent
  with a single dominant dwelling/hall rather than parallel shrine-priest and monk residences.
  > "一つの村に別当寺が置かれると、別当寺が、村内の他のいくつかの神社をも管理した" (once a bettoji
  was established in a village, it also administered several OTHER shrines within that village) -
  important: this means NOT every small shrine had its own dedicated resident monk/bettoji; some
  village shrines were satellite shrines under one village's bettoji rather than each having a
  full-time resident.

- ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/神宮寺 (fetched, weaker return): confirms mass destruction/conversion at
  Meiji ("during the Meiji period's separation of Shinto and Buddhism, most temples either converted
  to shrines or disappeared, rapidly decreasing in number") but did not yield village-scale layout
  detail beyond what 別当寺 gave.

**Assessment: STRONG support for the existing design choice** (one hall = monk's dwelling,
integrated into the shrine grounds, not a separate temple compound) and a useful CAVEAT: research
suggests it would also be historically accurate, and possibly MORE common, for a given small village
shrine to have had NO resident monk at all (served by a bettoji based at a neighboring, larger
village shrine). This should be recorded as a knob: resident monk present (as currently drawn) vs.
shrine served remotely by another village's bettoji - both attested.

## 13. China: village temple (村廟/土地廟) layout

**Finding: Two distinct scales matter. (a) The 土地廟 (earth-god shrine) is often TINY, sometimes
just two stones and a capstone, essentially a wayside shrine rather than a "temple" a monk would
live at. (b) A larger 村廟 with a proper hall DOES commonly have a courtyard with an opera stage
(戏台) facing the main hall/deity across it, an old and attested pairing, used for both worship
performance and village festival/market gathering.**

- zh.wikipedia.org/zh-hans/土地廟 (fetched):
  > "简陋者于树下或路旁，以两块石头为壁，一块为顶，即可成为土地庙" (The simplest form: two stones as
  walls and one as a roof, placed under a tree or by the roadside, and that alone can constitute a
  earth-god shrine.)
  > Size range for built examples: "from less than 2 meters to normal house height; areas ranging
  from a few square meters to over ten square meters" (per the fetch tool's rendering of the page;
  exact Chinese wording for this size range was not returned verbatim by the tool and should be
  re-fetched if this figure becomes load-bearing).
  > The page gives specific dated named examples (Taiwan/Hong Kong/Singapore, 1551-1957) but no
  general dynastic-era statement for mainland village earth-shrines as a type.

- zh.wikipedia.org/zh-hans/酬神戲 (fetched):
  > "酬神戏主要为了娱神或答谢神祇，顺便才演给善信观赏。故往往戏台搭在庙宇正面，观众不许上前、太过靠
  近，只可以在两旁或后方看戏" (Deity-thanking opera is primarily to entertain/thank the gods, with
  human spectators only secondary; the stage is therefore typically erected FACING the temple's
  front, and spectators may not approach too closely, watching only from the sides or rear.) -
  this directly confirms the stage-facing-the-hall-across-a-courtyard layout, and that the
  orientation logic is "performing for the god," matching what the user described as the expected
  form.
  > Names deity-days at temples of 玉皇上帝, 三官大帝, 关帝, 北帝 etc. as occasions, calling these
  "当地神庙的重大活动" (major events of the LOCAL temple) - i.e., an ordinary local temple, not
  exclusively grand ones.
  > Dating: traces the custom to at least the Northern Song, citing Tang/Song poetry - i.e., a
  pre-modern, long-attested practice, not a late innovation.

ABSENCE NOTE: Baidu Baike (百度百科) pages on 古戏台 and 村庙 returned HTTP 403 to WebFetch on every
attempt (baike.baidu.com/item/古戏台/8796741, baike.baidu.com/item/村庙/3494869) and could not be

**Assessment: MODERATE-STRONG for the stage-faces-the-hall pairing and its "performing for the god"
logic (zh.wikipedia 酬神戲, fetched, Song-dynasty-attested, explicitly local-temple-scale) and for
the earth-god shrine's minimal/tiny end of the size range (zh.wikipedia 土地廟, fetched). WEAK/
