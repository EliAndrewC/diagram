# Was a small village shrine's precinct enclosed, and by what?

Research pass for: village shrine (jingūji/bettō form, resident shrine-monk) drawn as a
140 x 99 ft rectangle enclosed by a continuous fence around a 60 x 48 ft hall. GM doubts a small
farming-village shrine had any wall or fence at all.

All quotes below are verbatim from pages actually fetched (WebFetch), with URL. Japanese/Chinese
given with original text + my translation. Search-only snippets that were never opened are NOT
quoted as fact - they are listed under "Searched, not usable" at the end.

---

## 1. What bounded the keidai (境内) of an ordinary village shrine?

### 1a. Terminology: tamagaki/mizugaki are usually HONDEN-adjacent fences, not precinct walls

**Kotobank, dictionary aggregation, https://kotobank.jp/word/%E7%8E%89%E5%9E%A3-562692**

- デジタル大辞泉: "皇居・神社の周囲に巡らした垣。垣が二重にあるときは外側のものをいう。"
  ("A fence encircling the imperial palace or a shrine; when there are two, this is the outer one.")
- 精選版日本国語大辞典: "神社・皇居の周囲にめぐらした垣。斎垣(いがき)。瑞垣(みずがき)。"
  ("A fence around a shrine or the imperial palace. Also igaki, mizugaki.")
- 改訂新版世界大百科事典: "神社の社殿もしくは境内の周囲にめぐらす垣。" (at Ise, innermost = mizugaki,
  outer = tamagaki) ("A fence around a shrine's buildings OR around the precinct.")
- 日本大百科全書 (Nipponica): "神社の聖域を囲む垣。" (at Ise: outer to inner = aragaki, tamagaki,
  mizugaki) ("A fence enclosing a shrine's sacred zone.")
- 百科事典マイペディア: "神社の本殿の周囲または境内の境界にめぐらした垣。"
  ("A fence around a shrine's main hall, OR around the precinct boundary.")

Note the dictionaries themselves are split between "around the honden" and "around the precinct" -
both usages exist, and multi-shrine sources default to describing the LAYERED case (Ise Shrine,
four concentric fences) as the model, which does not generalize to an ordinary shrine.

**ja.wikipedia.org, 玉垣, https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%8E%89%E5%9E%A3**

- "玉垣（たまがき）とは、神社・神域の周囲にめぐらされる垣のことである。瑞垣（みずがき）ともいう。"
  ("Tamagaki is a fence set up around a shrine or sacred precinct. Also called mizugaki.")
- "瑞垣（みずがき）、斎垣（いみがき、いかき、いがき）、神垣（かみがき）は玉垣と同様にすべて同じ意味を持つ。"
  ("Mizugaki, imigaki/ikagi/igaki, and kamigaki all carry the same meaning as tamagaki.")
- "概ね一番内側の垣を「瑞垣」とよぶことは一致している。伊勢神宮を例に挙げると、四重の垣がめぐらされ
  内側から順に、瑞垣、内玉垣（うちたまがき）、外玉垣（そとたまがき）、板垣と呼んでいる。"
  ("Sources broadly agree the innermost fence is called mizugaki. At Ise Shrine, for example, four
  concentric fences are set up, called from the inside out: mizugaki, inner tamagaki, outer
  tamagaki, and itagaki.")

**nippon.com, "神社に行こう！ 神社空間を読み解く⑧玉垣", https://www.nippon.com/ja/views/b05208/**

- "玉垣を巡らして、外界と区切っている。すなわち玉垣の外側が俗界で、内側が神域である。"
  ("[Fences] are run around [the honden] to divide it from the outside world; outside the tamagaki
  is the secular realm, inside is the sacred realm.")
- "最も内側のものを瑞垣（みずがき）と称し、外側のものは玉垣・荒垣・板垣などと称する。"
  ("The innermost is called mizugaki; the outer ones are called tamagaki, aragaki, itagaki, etc.")
- Ise example again given as the paradigm case (four concentric fences).

**Reading:** where a shrine has one fence, "tamagaki" describes a fence hugging the HONDEN, not a
precinct wall. A precinct-wide enclosure only shows up as the OUTERMOST of several rings at a large,
richly-endowed shrine (Ise being the standard illustration every source reaches for). This supports
distinguishing an honden-hugging 瑞垣/玉垣 from a whole-precinct boundary, as the GM's rule asks.

### 1b. Real small-shrine examples: honden-only fences, and one shrine with NO enclosure at all

**Yahira Shrine (八平神社), Shinshiro City official page,
https://www.city.shinshiro.lg.jp/kanko/minzokugeino/yahirajinjya.html** (municipal registered
cultural property, registered 2013-12-24)

- Honden: "木造平屋建、流造の建物である。棟札から、元禄13年（1700年）に一宮大明神、八剣大明神、
  八幡大菩薩を合祀し、三社の社殿として本殿が建てられた" (single-story wooden nagare-zukuri honden,
  built 1700, enshrining three deities).
- Tamagaki: "大正7年に建設された屋根付きの板塀である。木造の塀に桟瓦の屋根をのせる。玉垣は、その周辺
  から本殿の屋根が望めるように、高さを抑えて建てられている。" ("A roofed board fence built in 1918
  [Taishō 7]. A wooden fence topped with a tiled roof. The tamagaki is built low, so that the
  honden's roof can still be seen from around it.")
- This fence is explicitly around the HONDEN, deliberately kept low so the roof stays visible from
  outside it - i.e., a decorative/ritual boundary around the building, not a precinct wall meant to
  screen or enclose the grounds.
- Designation reason given: "登録理由：国土の歴史的景観に寄与しているもの" (registered because it
  contributes to the region's historical landscape) - i.e., this fence was notable enough (unusual/
  well-preserved enough) to be separately registered, which cuts against it being an unremarkable
  default feature of every village shrine.

**Shirahata Shrine (相州藤沢 白旗神社), "境内案内～石玉垣～",
https://www.shirahata-jinja.jp/blog/%E5%A2%83%E5%86%85%E6%A1%88%E5%86%85%EF%BD%9E%E7%9F%B3%E7%8E%89%E5%9E%A3%EF%BD%9E/**

- "「昭和三年十一月十日」の日付と「御大典紀念建之」の文字が刻まれており、昭和三年の昭和天皇の即位礼に
  合わせて建立されたことがわかります。" ("Carved with the date 'November 10, Showa 3 [1928]' and
  the words 'built to commemorate the Great Ceremony [of Enthronement]', showing it was erected to
  coincide with Emperor Showa's enthronement in 1928.")
- Donor list: over 50 names across twelve named neighborhoods (領家町, 白旗横町, 西坂戸町,
  南仲通一～四丁目, 西仲之町, 東仲之町, 相生町, 車田町, 臺町) - i.e., a substantial, multi-neighborhood
  town shrine, not a single hamlet's shrine.
- "階段を登り切った社殿の周囲を石玉垣が取り巻いています" ("The stone tamagaki surrounds the shrine
  building [shaden] at the top of the stairs") - again HONDEN/shaden-only, not the whole precinct.
  This is a Showa-era (1928) commemorative donation, not an Edo-period or ordinary feature.

**Yasuda Hachiman Shrine (安田八幡宮), national cultural property database,
https://kunishitei.bunka.go.jp/heritage/detail/101/00006725** - one of the few genuinely
Edo-period (dated "江戸末期" / "1830-1867", late Edo) stone tamagaki I could find:

- "安田八幡宮玉垣" "江戸末期" "1830-1867"
- "神社参道の両脇に設けられた石造玉垣。間口４．６ｍ、長さ１６ｍ、高さ０．６ｍで、親柱を９間に立て、
  各間小柱５本で笠石を受ける。上手の石段部分は長さ３．０ｍで、両端に親柱、中間に中柱を置き、小石柱
  ５本で笠石を受ける。参道景観を整える施設である。"
  ("A stone tamagaki set up on both sides of the shrine's approach path. 4.6 m wide opening, 16 m
  long, 0.6 m high overall, nine main pillars each supporting a capstone via five small pillars
  between. The upper stone-step section is 3.0 m long... A facility that shapes the view of the
  approach path.")
- Reading: even this Edo-period example runs LOW (0.6 m) along the SIDES OF THE APPROACH PATH
  (sandō), not around the whole precinct perimeter - it frames a walkway, it does not wall the
  grounds. "Hachiman-gū" also implies a shrine of some local prominence, not necessarily the
  smallest hamlet shrine.

**National Diet Library reference-service record (crd.ndl.go.jp), full Q&A verbatim,
https://crd.ndl.go.jp/reference/entry/index.php?id=1000040750&page=ref_view**

- Question (patron's, verbatim): "神社の鳥居の周りにある囲い、玉垣・瑞垣などについて由来をしりたい。
  近所の神社に鳥居しかないので、なにか作ろうかと考えているらしい。他の神社で生垣だったり、石だったり
  しているのでいわれなどしりたい。" ("I want to know the origin of the enclosures around shrine
  torii - tamagaki, mizugaki, etc. My neighborhood shrine has ONLY A TORII [and nothing else], so
  [someone] is apparently thinking of building something. Since other shrines have hedges, or stone
  ones, I want to know the background.")
- Answer given (verbatim): "垣根　524.8/ヌ　（1984年刊、法政大学出版会）に『垣と日本文化』として
  神社などの周りに作られる"瑞垣"は『みずみずしい垣』『青い垣』の意味や"玉垣"の玉は霊の意味で、したがって
  御霊を守る垣のことなど垣について詳しく書いてある。" (points to a book, "Kakine" (1984, Hōsei
  University Press), for etymology; does not give the patron a rule for whether to build one.)

  **This is the single most direct piece of evidence found: a real, named local shrine (the
  questioner's own neighborhood shrine) with literally only a torii and nothing else - no fence, no
  hedge, no wall - treated by both patron and librarian as an unremarkable, ordinary state of
  affairs that simply prompted curiosity, not as something anomalous needing repair.** The librarian
  could point to no rule requiring an enclosure.

### 1c. The grove itself as the boundary (鎮守の森)

**ja.wikipedia.org, 鎮守の森, https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%8E%AE%E5%AE%88%E3%81%AE%E6%A3%AE**

- Article frames chinju-no-mori as the grove "神殿や参道、拝所を囲むように設定・維持されている森林"
  (forest established/maintained so as to surround the shrine hall, approach, and worship area) -
  i.e. the GROVE, not a built fence, is presented as the thing that encloses/separates the precinct
  from the secular world.
- Footnote/definitional exclusion: "祠を含む小さな神社で、神殿のすぐ隣まで人家などが建てられている
  神社は除く。" ("Excludes small shrines, including shrines that are just a hokora [small
  roadside/field shrine], where houses etc. are built right up next to the sanctuary.") - implies
  that at the small end of the shrine spectrum, dwellings can abut the sanctuary directly with no
  grove and (by implication) no other boundary either; such shrines are simply excluded from the
  "chinju-no-mori" category rather than described as having a substitute wall.

I could not find, on any page I actually fetched, an explicit general statement of the form "an
ordinary village shrine's keidai was bounded by nothing but the edge of its grove" - the closest
support is (a) the definitional exclusion above, and (b) the NDL reference case of a real shrine
with only a torii. This is suggestive but not a citable general rule; flagging it as such rather
than overstating it.

## 2. When did stone tamagaki around WHOLE precincts become common?

Findings point toward: rare/local in the Edo period (and even then, typically flanking an approach
path or ringing the honden, not the whole precinct), and common as Meiji-Taisho-Showa donation
projects, often tied to specific commemorative occasions (imperial enthronements) rather than being
a routine, load-bearing feature every shrine needed.

- Yasuda Hachiman Shrine: late Edo (1830-1867), but flanks the sandō, not a precinct wall (see 1b).
- Yahira Shrine: Taishō 7 (1918), around the honden only (see 1b).
- Shirahata Shrine: Shōwa 3 (1928), explicitly built "御大典紀念" - to commemorate Emperor Shōwa's
  enthronement - around the shaden only, funded by 50+ named parishioner donors across twelve
  neighborhoods (see 1b). This matches the GM's suspicion that datable stone tamagaki cluster in
  Meiji-Taishō-Shōwa, often as one-off commemorative/fundraising projects, not inherited Edo-period
  fabric.
- note.com, "神社を守るということ" series, https://note.com/jinja_mamoru/n/nf6c05b296cea: "「玉」は
  「美しい」「尊い」を意味する言葉で、古くは木の柵や生け垣でしたが、時代とともに石造りのものが広まりました。"
  ("'Tama' means 'beautiful'/'noble'; anciently these were wooden fences or hedges, and over time
  stone ones became widespread.") This confirms a wood/hedge -> stone progression but gives no
  specific era for the changeover, and I could not find a page that dates it more precisely for
  ordinary (non-flagship) shrines. Treating the Meiji-Taishō-Shōwa dating as SUPPORTED BY EXAMPLE
  but not by an explicit general historical statement.

None of the three dated examples found (1830s Edo, 1918, 1928) show a stone fence around a WHOLE
precinct - all three enclose either an approach path or the honden/shaden. I found no dated example
anywhere of a whole-precinct stone tamagaki at an ordinary rural shrine; the only whole-precinct
account (Ise Shrine's four concentric rings) is the flagship imperial shrine, not a village-scale
comparandum.

## 3. Small rural temples / village halls (村の堂, 惣堂, 辻堂): walls or not?

**Kyoto University of the Arts graduation research site, "日常と信仰の歴史的建造物 備後地方に点在する
辻堂", http://g.kyoto-art.ac.jp/reports/6415/**

- "田畑や道路の脇に建てられた、壁のない四本柱の屋根のある簡素な木造のお堂である。" ("A simple wooden
  hall built beside fields and roads, with four pillars and a roof but NO WALLS.")
- "一般的に「辻堂」というと壁のある建物を指すが、備後地方の辻堂は壁がないものがほとんどである。"
  ("Generally 'tsujidō' refers to a walled building, but in the Bingo region, most tsujidō have no
  walls.") - Note this source itself flags that tsujidō ELSEWHERE (outside Bingo) are typically
  described as walled buildings; Bingo's open-sided type is presented as a regional variant, not the
  nationwide default. So the general dictionary sense of tsujidō is a small walled hall; this
  regional case is evidence that "small roadside religious buildings can also be unwalled small
  structures with no fence at all," but should not be read as proof that tsujidō/sōdō nationwide
  lack walls.
- "多くは二畳ほどの広さで" (most are about two tatami mats / roughly 3.3 sq m).
- Dictionary definitions of 辻堂 (Kotobank, https://kotobank.jp/word/%E8%BE%BB%E5%A0%82-99226):
  Daijisen "道ばたに建っている仏堂。" ("A Buddhist hall built by the roadside"); Nikkoku Daijiten
  "道の辻などに建てられている仏堂。" ("A Buddhist hall built at a crossroads or similar.") Neither
  dictionary entry mentions walls, fences, or enclosures one way or the other - they describe the
  BUILDING itself, not a precinct around it. I found no dictionary or page describing a tsujidō/
  sōdō having a SEPARATE precinct fence distinct from the building's own walls (or lack of them).

I was not able to find a page specifically discussing 惣堂 (sōdō) construction/enclosure; searches
for "惣堂 村堂 江戸時代 建物 構造" returned nothing usable (see "Searched, not usable" below).

## 4. China: did a village earth-god temple (土地廟/土地庙) or small village temple (村廟/村庙) have
a courtyard wall (院墙)?

**zh.wikipedia.org, 土地庙, https://zh.wikipedia.org/zh-cn/%E5%9C%9F%E5%9C%B0%E5%BB%9F**

- "土地庙因神格不高，且为基层信仰，多半造型简单，简陋者于树下或路旁，以两块石头为壁，一块为顶，即可
  成为土地庙" ("Because the earth god's rank is low, and it is a grassroots-level cult, tǔdìmiào are
  mostly simple in form; the crudest examples, under a tree or by the roadside, need only two stones
  as walls and one as a roof to count as a tǔdìmiào.")
- "也有简单以水泥或砖块砌成小室，现今台湾甚至有工厂开模以水泥灌制大量生产" ("Some are simply built as
  small cement or brick chambers; today in Taiwan factories even mass-produce them cast in cement.")
- "属于微型建筑，高度从不到2米至正常房屋高度；面积大小小则几平方米，大则十几平方米"
  ("These are micro-structures, from under 2 m to normal house height; area from a few square meters
  up to십여 [ten-odd] square meters at most.")
- "也有土地庙因香火鼎盛，逐渐中大型化者" ("Some tǔdìmiào, because of heavy incense/worship traffic,
  gradually grow into medium- or large-scale ones.")
- **No sentence on this page states or implies a courtyard wall (院墙/围墙) around an ordinary
  tǔdìmiào.** The page's own description is of the SHRINE BUILDING ITSELF being reduced to almost
  nothing (two stones and a capstone) at the low end - there is no room in that description for a
  separate enclosing wall; the "wall" language used (两块石头为壁) refers to the walls of the tiny
  building, not a courtyard around it.

**Field study, 澎湃新闻/The Paper, "土地与神祇｜村庙作为公共空间：对浙北某拆迁村庄的田野考察",
https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_4855063** (case study of one village temple, 甘棠庙, in
northern Zhejiang before its demolition - flagged as a single-site field account, not a general
architectural authority)

- "甘棠庙原坐落于缸窑桥东，是一座工匠木雕古建筑，庙院占地2.5亩左右。" ("Gantang Temple originally
  stood east of Gangyao Bridge; it was an old wood-carved building by craftsmen, with the
  temple-and-courtyard [庙院] occupying about 2.5 mu.") - the term 庙院 (temple courtyard) implies
  SOME enclosed courtyard existed for this particular temple, but the fetched text does not state
  what bounded it (wall vs. hedge vs. nothing) - I could not confirm a described wall.
- "天气好的时候，老太太们就坐在右翼念经堂外晒太阳"，"在前院里，老爷子们就坐在条凳上" ("On good-weather
  days, the old women would sit outside the right-wing sutra-chanting hall sunning themselves"; "in
  the front courtyard, the old men would sit on benches") - confirms a 前院 (front courtyard) existed
  at this specific, apparently sizeable (2.5-mu) village temple, but again no statement of what
  physically bounded it.
- This is a single named example of one somewhat substantial village temple (not a bare tǔdìmiào);
  it should not be generalized to "small farming-village shrines" broadly, and I could not confirm a
  wall/no-wall statement from it either way.

I could not get a usable fetch of Baidu Baike's 村庙 entry (HTTP 403) or of Zhihu answers on
城隍庙 vs 土地庙 (HTTP 403 on both questions tried) - see "Searched, not usable."

**Provisional finding for China:** the clearest, best-supported statement is the zh.wikipedia one:
an ordinary village-level 土地庙 is often reduced to almost nothing (a couple of stones), with NO
separate courtyard wall implied or needed; only where a temple grows large/prosperous ("中大型化")
does it start to resemble a proper templar complex (implicitly, at that point, of the kind that
might have a courtyard). I found no page directly asserting or denying an 院墙 for a mid-size or
prosperous 村庙 specifically (as opposed to a bare tǔdìmiào), so that middle case remains open.

## 5. Were shrine/temple walls marks of rank or wealth?

**ja.wikipedia.org, 築地塀, https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%AF%89%E5%9C%B0%E5%A1%80** (this is
about TEMPLE/aristocratic earthen walls specifically, tsuiji-bei - not the Shinto tamagaki/mizugaki
discussed above, but directly on point for "walls as rank markers")

- "泥土をつき固めて作った塀。単に「築地」（ついじ）ともいう。" ("A wall made by pounding earth/mud
  solid. Also simply called 'tsuiji'.")
- "版築」という方法で作られる物が多く、塀の上に屋根として簡便な小屋組を設け、瓦や板などで葺いたものも
  多くある。" ("Many are built by the rammed-earth (hanchiku) method, with a simple roof frame on top,
  often finished in tile or board.")
- "古来より公家の邸宅や寺院、官舎などに特に見られ" ("Seen since ancient times particularly at court
  nobles' residences, temples, and government offices [i.e., high-status institutions specifically].")
- **"寺では定規筋という白い横線を入れた筋塀を築き、5本（五条）を最高位にして寺格を表すようになった。"**
  ("At temples, 'suji-bei' walls were built with white horizontal lines called jōgi-suji, with FIVE
  lines as the highest rank, used to express the temple's RANK [jikaku].")
- "定規筋の白い線は5本が最上で、御所と門跡寺院にしか許されません。" (from the earlier search summary
  of the same topic, consistent with the fetched page: "Five lines is the highest, permitted only to
  the Imperial Palace and monzeki [imperial/noble-abbot] temples.")
- "規模の大きい物は「大垣」と呼ばれ、平城京の南面の築地塀は高さ12メートルに達したといわれている。"
  ("Large-scale examples are called 'ōgaki'; the tsuiji-bei on the south face of Heijō-kyō is said to
  have reached 12 m high.")

**This directly and explicitly supports the GM's suspicion for TEMPLES**: an earthen wall (tsuiji-bei),
and especially a striped (suji-bei) one, was a recognized, graded mark of institutional rank -
reserved at its highest grade for the Imperial Palace and monzeki temples, i.e., the top of the
status hierarchy, not an ordinary village hall/temple. I found no equivalent explicit "rank marker"
statement specifically for Shinto tamagaki/mizugaki (the shrine-fence sources in section 1 describe
sacred/secular boundary function and donor-commemoration, not a graded rank system), but the temple
tsuiji-bei case is a clean, well-supported example of "wall = status," which is consistent with
(though not directly proof for) the same logic applying to shrine precinct walls.

## Summary of what is and is not supported

1. **Fences called tamagaki/mizugaki are attested at real shrines mostly as an ENCLOSURE OF THE
   HONDEN/SHADEN OR A FRAME FOR THE APPROACH PATH, not as a wall around the whole precinct** - every
   dated real example found (Yahira 1918, Shirahata 1928, Yasuda Hachiman Edo-late) is one of these
   two, never a whole-precinct wall. The whole-precinct multi-ring case (Ise) is the flagship
   imperial shrine, not a comparandum for a small village shrine.
2. **A real small shrine with literally nothing but a torii - no fence, no hedge - is directly
   attested** (NDL reference-service patron's own neighborhood shrine), and treated by both the
   patron and the librarian as unremarkable.
3. **Dated tamagaki examples cluster in Taishō/Shōwa** (1918, 1928, the latter explicitly tied to an
   imperial enthronement fundraising drive), with one genuine late-Edo example, supporting - by
   example, not by an explicit general historical statement - the idea that whole-precinct stone
   fencing is a later, donation-driven phenomenon rather than inherited from a "shrines have always
   been walled" baseline.
4. **Small rural halls (tsujidō, Bingo region) are explicitly attested as WALL-LESS**, four-post
   open structures, though the source itself notes this is a regional departure from the more common
   (walled) tsujidō elsewhere - I could not find material specifically on 惣堂/村堂.
5. **China: an ordinary village 土地庙 is attested as reducible to a couple of stones, with no
   courtyard wall implied**; a single field-studied, larger (2.5-mu) village temple did have SOME
   kind of courtyard, but I could not confirm what physically bounded it.
6. **Walls as a rank/status marker are explicitly and strongly attested for TEMPLES** (tsuiji-bei,
   graded by the number of suji stripes, five stripes reserved for the Imperial Palace and monzeki
   temples) - this is the single best-supported "walls = high status" statement found, though it is
   about temples, not Shinto shrine fences specifically.

**Overall, the record found supports the GM's doubt**: nothing found shows a small farming-village
shrine's whole precinct enclosed by a continuous fence as a default/attested condition. What IS
attested, for small/ordinary religious sites, ranges from no enclosure at all, to a modest wall-less
hall, to (at larger or Meiji/Taishō/Shōwa-refurbished shrines) a low fence around the honden alone or
flanking the approach - never a 140 x 99 ft precinct-wide fence as drawn. A precinct-wide wall reads,
on this evidence, as something closer to a large/wealthy/high-rank institution's feature (explicit
for temple tsuiji-bei; consistent, though not textually proven, for shrine tamagaki) than as an
ordinary village shrine's default.
