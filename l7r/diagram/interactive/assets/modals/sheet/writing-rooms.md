About: The writing rooms were the two rooms of the office hall where the office's documents were made up: a room
used only for writing, where they were drawn up, and the room beside it, where they were bound. At Takayama, the one
intendant's office that survives, the documents sent to the shogunate were drawn up in such a writing room, the
kakiyaku-beya, and bound in the room next to it, both rooms of the office hall. Whether a small office had
either room is not recorded; the rebuilt Iijima jin'ya, a modern reconstruction, has a writing room as the first room in from the entrance.

The writing room was worked by the office's apprentices, the kakiyaku: mostly the sons of officials and of village and town
officials, taken on to learn the office's work before they rose to be its clerks. At Iijima they kept night duty in the
writing room while they trained. How many worked there is not recorded.

Like the hall they lay in, the rooms were of timber: Takayama's office was a single-story wooden hall roofed with wooden
shingles. What the two rooms looked like inside, and how large they were, is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That a county office kept a writing room and a binding room of its own: the offices found with them are Takayama's,
  which governed a whole province, and Iijima's modern reconstruction; a county office's hall is far smaller.
- Their size on the plan, about 10 to 12.5 ft wide by 17 to 20 ft deep each and at most about 210 sq ft, some 6 to 12 mats: no measured writing room was found,
  and the two together are drawn well under the one 26 to 28 mat room where Takayama's officials worked.

Depiction: The plan draws the writing room and the binding room as two rooms of the office hall's back row, west of the
clerks' room, side by side or one behind the other, their floors drawn in the hall's own color.
