About: The armory was where a magistracy kept its arms. A Chinese office counted it among the stores it could not do without, and kept one even in a county with no standing
barracks: Pingyao's county office in China had none under either of its last two dynasties, yet it kept
an armory, which its war office guarded. When that office was rebuilt,
its great hall was raised at five bays, the west end bay the armory and the east end bay the store for money and grain.
In Japan the gate range of a small domain's seat at Kaibara held a store of arms toward its south end, past a horse-viewing room, across the gate
from the guardroom.

Pingyao's armory stood in the office hall itself; Kaibara's stood in the range of rooms the main gate passes through.
Kaibara's took one or two of the three eight-mat rooms on its side of the gate, each about 12 ft square, though whether one or two is not recorded; how large or how deep Pingyao's bay was is not recorded. At Nirayama, the house of a line of intendants, the weapons store was a building of its own, about 18 by 12 ft, gabled, roofed in pantiles and entered at its gable end under a pent roof. What an armory's walls were built of is not recorded, nor how many arms it held or how they were racked.

Guesses:
- Its size on the plan, 14 by 16.7 to 14 by 20 ft in a hall: no account measures Pingyao's bays; of the stores of arms measured, Kaibara's took one or two of its eight-mat rooms of about 12 ft square, in a gate range rather than an office hall, and Nirayama's was a building of its own of about 18 by 12 ft.
- Its place in the hall's back row: no account says how deep Pingyao's armory ran; the whole end bay, front to back, is the more natural reading of a bay, and the plan draws only the back row, leaving the hall's front band, where the dais stands, whole.

Depiction: The plan draws the armory as a room at the west end of the office hall's back row, beside the clerks' room,
its floor drawn in the hall's own color; or, where the main gate is a range, as one of the range's rooms beside the passage; or as a small store of its own, a building apart, set wherever the courts have room. Each plan takes one of the places open to it.
