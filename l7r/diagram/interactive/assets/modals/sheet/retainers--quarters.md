About: The retainers' quarters housed the magistrate's retainers inside the compound, in dwellings that each held a household
or unmarried men sharing. A rural intendant's office kept the huts and rowhouses of its junior officials on its own grounds,
and a small domain's seat laid out its retainers' residences among its halls and storehouses; the great town magistracy of
Edo instead housed its constables in a district of their own. At Sakai's town magistracy the magistrate's own retainers are thought to have lodged in rowhouses inside its grounds, those of highest standing seemingly in a block of just two dwellings.

The form was the rowhouse, the nagaya: a long, narrow, single-story building under one roof, divided along its length into
dwellings. Each dwelling was an earth-floored entry for leaving shoes and a raised plank floor, and each had an entrance of
its own, closed by a lattice door. Such rows could be thatched, a gate rowhouse tiled, and Obata's row has earth walls as it stands today; what an office's staff row was walled and framed with is not recorded. No bed or bunk is known in a rowhouse before modern times.

The rowhouses measured ran about 80 to 300 ft long; the gate and foot soldiers' rows were 12 to 24 ft deep, built of bays about 12 to 18 ft wide. A foot soldiers' row at Shibata,
143 by 24 ft, held eight households under one thatched roof; at Obata each dwelling was about 12 by 21 ft,
one room, an entrance and a kitchen. A retainer of higher rank had more room: in the Sayama domain's rowhouses, dwellings 15 ft deep ran 24 to 36 ft along the row by rank. How many unmarried men usually shared a dwelling is not recorded: three
retainers on duty in Edo shared one, the two juniors cooking by turns, while each constable of Sakai's town magistracy had a dwelling of their own, about 12 by 18 ft.

Guesses:
- That a county office's whole staff lived on its grounds: an intendant's office is known to have kept its junior officials'
  quarters and a storehouse keepers' rowhouse there, and an intendant governed with about ten retainers of the shogunate and a few local hires, but no source says who else lived inside, and at one such office, Omori, a mid-ranking official lived in a house of their own in the town; at Sano, a domain's distant office, the staff lived in the town as a rule though rowhouses stood on its grounds.
- That a few men share each dwelling, and so how many bays the rowhouse runs to: the cases found run from one man to a dwelling, at a town magistracy, to three retainers to a
  dwelling.

Depiction: The plan draws the quarters as a rowhouse, long and narrow, never a square block, divided into dwellings, each with a door of its
own, and draws nothing in it as a bunk or a bunk room.
