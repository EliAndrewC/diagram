About: The servants' quarters lodged the household's domestic servants, the komono, who did its odd jobs. They were not
samurai but of peasant or townsman stock, most of them younger sons of peasants, hired for wages by the year or the half
year, some through a broker; they changed households on two fixed days a year.
How many a household had to keep was fixed by its assessed rice yield. They lived in, inside their master's walls; no
servant is known to have had a house of their own in the samurai quarter.

Where they slept followed the size of the house. On a large house the buildings along the street were long
ranges (nagaya), of one story or two, where the servants lived, the front gate cut through one of them so that the servants there kept it; a
smaller house had a wall in place of the ranges, and where its few servants slept is not recorded. Even a chief retainer's great house, at Aizu, kept the rooms of its maids and servants under its own roof, beside a range of retainers at its gate. One retainer's house, the Miwa house at Kaminoyama, kept its chugen in a hut outside the back door, by the kitchen.

The chugen, menservants ranked below the foot soldiers, lived together in large rooms: one
was two rooms of six mats, each about 9 by 12 ft, entered by a sliding lattice door. Where servants lived as
households, or a few men together, each dwelling had a door of its own, opening on an earth-floored entry before a raised
plank floor.

How big an ordinary range was is not measured. A long-house gate, with the gatekeeper's and servants' rooms on either side of its passage, was the usual front gate of an upper samurai's house; a large one in Edo ran about 72 by 15 ft, of two stories. A thatched row for eight households of a lord's lowest retainers or servants ran
about 145 by 24 ft.

Guesses:
- Its depth of about 15 ft, and its length: the depth is that of the large long-house gate measured in Edo, and that so large
  a gate stands for an ordinary range is the guess; no ordinary range's length was found measured.
- How many bays a common room runs to, and the sliding partitions sometimes drawn between them: the one room described
  names its two six-mat rooms, not what stood between them.
- That a large household's menservants and grooms slept in the range, and its maids and kitchen hands under the main roof: the Aizu house kept rooms for its
  maids and servants in the house, but no page says which servant slept where.
- That a small household's few servants slept under the main roof, in the storeroom and rooms off the kitchen: the one case recorded,
  the Miwa house, kept its chugen in a hut by the kitchen, and whether that house had a range is not said.

Depiction: The plan draws the servants' quarters as a range on the compound's street edge, its outer face the wall,
beside the gate or with a gate cut through it. It takes one of the two forms servants
lived in - a common room behind one door, its bays open to one another or parted by sliding partitions, or a row of
dwellings each with its own door - and never shows dwellings walled off from one another behind one shared door. The range
holds the menservants and the grooms, whose work is the gate, the stable and the outer court; the maids and the kitchen
hands are drawn in a maids' room under the main roof at the kitchen end, so the kitchen's hands never cross the guests'
gate or garden.
