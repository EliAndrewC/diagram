About: The kitchen, the daidokoro, was where a samurai household cooked, on a kamado, a clay range with fire-mouths. It was
part of the house, set to its side: at a thousand-koku samurai's house at Hagi the kitchen wing juts out beside the living
room. It had its own door from outside, the katteguchi; the family came and went by an inner
entrance of its own, and guests by the formal one. Who cooked in it, the household or its servants, and whether anyone
slept there, are not recorded.

The kitchen sat under the house's own roof, as one of its rooms or a wing of it roofed as the house was: thatch at a
Matsushiro house of 1794, pantiles at the Hagi house. In the great mansions the kitchen might be a building of its
own, with a smoke louvre in its roof; whether an ordinary samurai kitchen had one is not recorded. Its walls are not recorded, nor
what closed its outside door, nor whether that door was barred at night.

A samurai house was matted in nearly every room, but its kitchen was board-floored, with an earth-floored part, the doma,
smaller than a farmhouse's. The kamado as a rule stood on the doma, though perhaps at times on the boards of a samurai
kitchen whose doma was small; a district magistrate's house of 1794, its kitchen altered by about 1868, has a kitchen all
of board floor, its sink set on the floor. No sunken hearth is known in a samurai kitchen.

At the one house whose kitchen is measured, the Hagi house, its wing of board floor and doma was about 440 sq ft, about a
sixth of the house by one of its two official measures.

Guesses:
- That the kitchen has one door to the outside, on its doma where it has one: the kitchen door is recorded, often at the rear of the house and in the great mansions onto the service kitchen's doma, but not how many a kitchen had or
  whether an ordinary kitchen's door opened on its doma.
- A second outside door, or a way of its own to carry food to the rooms, wherever a plan draws one: no source shows either.
- The kitchen's size on the plan: one kitchen is measured, by one of two measures that disagree.
- That the kitchen and its service ground stand to the side and rear of the house, away from the gate: the kitchen is
  recorded within the house, to its side, and a secondhand summary of one book counts a kitchen yard among the four kinds of Kanazawa's samurai gardens, but no source says which side of the lot it took, and at one middle-rank retainer's house, at Shiroishi, the gate leads along the wall straight to the kitchen entrance.

Depiction: A residence's plan draws the kitchen board-floored, cooking on a kamado that stands either on a small doma at the
kitchen's edge or, on legs, on the boards - each plan takes one - and never on a sunken hearth. Its door opens into the work space of
the service ground, the reverse of a guest's door; the family's own door is the residence's inner entrance, or, where a guest is brought by a garden path to the veranda and not to a formal entrance, the kitchen door itself. A residence's kitchen is
drawn at about the size of the Hagi house's kitchen wing, about 24 by 18 ft or a little smaller, whatever the size of the house it serves, and always well short of the house itself; it stands to the side and rear of the house, away from the gate. At a village shrine the kitchen is the kitchen end of the country monk's dwelling, drawn as an earth floor (doma) with a kamado on it: the doma's stipple and the kamado's small dark square with its fire are map drawing conventions for an earth floor and a clay range, and the sunken hearth a farmhouse most often cut in its floored part is left off, so the plan shows one hearth.
