About: The garden of a samurai residence lay to the south of its formal rooms, before them, and was made to be looked at, from the room that
faced it or from its veranda. Its water took one of two forms: a pond garden, built around real water, or a dry landscape
(karesansui), which showed water without using any. Around the water a garden held set stones, made
hills and planting, with stepping stones, stone lanterns and fences.

Both forms stood beside samurai rooms. Two government offices, the office of the
shogunate's intendants at Takayama and the seat of a small domain at Okutono, both kept pond gardens; Okutono's lay on two
levels, the lower one seen from the formal room. Of seven samurai-house gardens at Chiran, one was a pond
garden and six were dry. At Matsushiro the samurai houses set their garden on the south side, generally with a pond, carp kept in it and a made hill planted with trees beyond it.

How large a residence garden's pond was is not recorded. A temple's dry garden beside its formal room, at the Daisen-in,
is a level plot of about 1,100 sq ft (100 sq m).

What a small residence's or an office's pond was banked with is not recorded either. The great pond gardens edged their
water with set stones and pebble beaches, using fewer set stones from the mid-17th century and smaller ones from the early 18th. At Obata, a small
domain town built around its lord's jin'ya, the surviving ponds on samurai-house lots made to be looked at are ringed by
large stones or planting, and some of the working ponds kept for fish and water are parted from their channel only by dry-laid stone;
but those gardens are undated, so they show the ponds as they survive, not as they were made. Two excavated gardens show
how a pond's bank was made: at Shunjuen, the pond-and-stroll garden of a hatamoto family's lower residence, the ponds
were edged in turn by a low stone wall thought to have served as a revetment, a neatly laid stone revetment and set edging stones, and at the Ninomaru of Nagoya Castle, a castle garden, the
North pond was faced and floored with tataki, earth and gravel beaten hard with lime; each shows that one garden, not the
rule for a small residence's or an office's pond.

Guesses:
- The pond's size: no residence pond's size is recorded, so it is drawn small, within sight of the rooms that face
  the garden.
- How often an office kept a pond rather than a dry garden: no source counts the two at government offices, and samurai
  houses went both ways (Chiran's gardens mostly dry, Matsushiro's mostly with a pond). Both forms are taken as accurate,
  though the two government offices whose gardens could be read, Takayama and Okutono (Okutono's as restored from an
  Edo-period drawing), kept a pond: each plan takes one of the two, and its notes say which.
- A pond on the way from the gate to the entrance, where a plan draws one: no account describes a pond there, so putting it there is our guess.

Depiction: Each plan takes one of the two forms, a small pond or a dry garden of white gravel and stones standing for water,
within sight of the rooms that face it; the plan's notes say which. The line drawn round a pond is where its water ends, a
map drawing convention: the bank is not drawn as a thing of its own. The bank itself is not recorded for a pond of a small residence or an office, though the great gardens set theirs with stones and shores curved to copy a coastline.

Name: garden pond
Covers: the pond, or the dry garden, in the inner garden
