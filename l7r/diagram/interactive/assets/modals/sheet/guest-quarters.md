About: A samurai house received its guests inside the house. The residence at the Takayama intendant's office
had a room for receiving guests among its rooms, and a middle-rank house kept its guest space - the formal entrance with its
low board step, the shikidai, the anteroom where a visitor was announced, and the zashiki, the guest room - within its one
main house, apart from the family's living rooms.

A guest of rank came by palanquin through the gate and across open ground, and the palanquin was brought right alongside
the step, so that the guest went into the building without setting foot on the ground. A middle-rank house could do without
such an entrance: its guest passed through a middle gate and along a walled garden path straight to the veranda of the
zashiki. No guest house standing apart is known at a samurai house; a traveling lord or official lodged instead at a honjin,
a post town's house for guests of rank, with its raised room of honor, and a lord's officials touring the villages lodged at the village headman's house.

The guest room was the house's formal room, finished for formality where the master's own living room could be finished
playfully. Like nearly every room of such a house but the kitchen, it was matted, and a wide veranda ran between it and the
garden, which lay to the south before the formal rooms. Along that edge the house closed with storm shutters, board doors slid out at night and against wind and rain, and stowed by day in a box at the end of their run.

How large a guest room was is not recorded.

Guesses:
- A guest house drawn as a building of its own: none is known at a samurai house, and the nearest form, the honjin's suite
  of honor, belonged to a post town's lodging house, not to an office.
- A garden between the gate and a guest's formal entrance, where a plan draws one: no account of a formal entrance puts one there, and the two drawings of that
  ground show it open; it is drawn from one loosely dated plate that runs a garden path from a second gateway to the entrance, and from the walled garden path by which a house without such an entrance led its guest.

Depiction: The plan draws the guest quarters as one labeled room, though the real guest space was the zashiki and the rooms beside it. It stands for the guest room of the residence, by the reception, or on some plans for a guest house of
its own. A guest's door opens onto the ground inside the gate - an open court a palanquin could cross, or on some plans a garden - never straight from the street or against the side
of a building.

Name: guest quarters
Covers: the guest room of the residence with its floor and label, or a detached guest house and its label
