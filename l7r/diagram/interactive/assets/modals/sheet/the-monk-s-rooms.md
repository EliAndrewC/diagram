About: The monk's rooms were where the shrine's monk lived, the living part of the monk's dwelling, the kuri.
A temple's kuri was where a temple's monks lived; it began as a kitchen, and at an
ordinary temple it often served as the temple's office as well. Until the Meiji era (1868) the monk of a shrine with a temple attached lived
at the temple. In Rokugan the shrine's keeper is the village district's country monk, who lives on a plot of tax-free land
and may have a few acolytes, lent part-time by the village's larger families to help farm it. The monk also keeps the district's records of births and deaths.

At an ordinary temple the kuri was often much like an ordinary house, and even the largest measured, at a temple of standing, resembles its region's
farmhouse, whose rooms stood on a raised floor beside an earth-floored end. At another temple of standing, in
Kawasaki, the head priest's living room was a room of twelve mats. What a village kuri's walls and roof were built of, and how its doorways closed, is not recorded.

The rooms were one monk's home; whether acolytes slept in them is not recorded, nor how large a village shrine's rooms under its hall's roof were. The smallest kuri measured, at a small hall in a Fukuoka farming town,
was about 12 by 15 ft, and a village temple's, in a register made after 1868, about 39 ft across its front; those of temples of standing ran far larger, 9 by 5 ken (about 54 by 30 ft, at a ken of about 6 ft) at Chonen-ji and 12 by 8 ken (about 72 by 48 ft) at
Tokuun-ji.

Guesses:
- The rooms' size on the plan, which with the kitchen end comes to about a farmhouse's floor: the one small kuri measured,
  at the Koshin hall, stood apart from its hall, and no dwelling under its hall's roof was found measured at a village, so
  the dwelling is reckoned from the farmhouse it resembled.

Depiction: The plan draws the monk's rooms between the villagers' hall and the earth-floored kitchen end, under the one roof,
because at a village shrine one building serves as both the monk's home and the place the villagers come to; a hall and
dwelling under one roof was known, though rare. The rooms are drawn with no sunken hearth, though a common house most often cut one in the middle of its floored part; the plan shows one hearth, the kitchen's, a map drawing convention. The district's registers are kept in a writing room in the dwelling,
so no office building is drawn.
