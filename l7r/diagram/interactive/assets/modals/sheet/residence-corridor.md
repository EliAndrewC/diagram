About: A watari-roka was a corridor built between two buildings to join them. Such corridors grew up in the mansions of the
shoguns and the daimyo of the late 1500s and the Edo period, built as many halls joined one to another. Farmhouses,
townhouses and the houses of middle and lower-ranking samurai had almost none: such a house was one block under one roof.

A great house could also grow hall by hall. At the Katsura villa four halls stand stepped back one behind the next,
like geese in flight, and they went up in three phases over about half a century, in the early Edo period: halls set in
echelon were how this house grew, hall added to hall.

How such a corridor was built, how wide or how long it ran, and how Katsura's own halls are joined, is not recorded.

Guesses:
- How the plan draws a corridor: about 3 to 6.5 ft wide to walk, half a ken to one ken as Katsura's inner corridors and a
  dictionary's one-ken corridor are, running up to about 36 ft along the rooms it serves; a band of this project's,
  a guess, not a measurement - no account of a samurai house's corridor's width or length was found.

Depiction: The plan draws the corridor joining each hall of a residence set in echelon to the hall before it, putting the great
houses' corridors together with Katsura's stepped halls; a residence drawn as one block has none between its halls. A residence
drawn this way reads as a house that has been added to. A kitchen is drawn against the house it serves, with no corridor. Inside a house the plan draws a corridor as a passage, not a
room: a lighter board-floor strip with a dotted edge, drawn over the dashed partitions of the rooms it runs between, so its edge reads
as a dash-dot line apart from the plain dashed partitions between the rooms - a drawing convention of the map, so a passage reads apart from the rooms it serves.
