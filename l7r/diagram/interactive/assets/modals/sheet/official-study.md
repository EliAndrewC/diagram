About: The official study was a magistrate's own working room in the office hall, where the office's
papers would be read and answered; no account names such a room.

At Takayama, the one intendant's office that survives, the entrance hall, examination room, working office
and great hall were rebuilt together in the Edo period, most likely as one block. The officials sent by the shogunate worked in a
room of the office, partitioned from the room of those hired mostly from the district, and the documents sent to the shogunate
were drawn up in a room used only for writing and bound in the room beside it. An intendant governed with about ten
retainers of the shogunate and a few local hires.

Takayama's office was a single-story timber hall roofed with wooden shingles, built with great formality to show the
office's rank. In a private house, a study was the room with the built-in writing desk, the tsuke-shoin; what a magistrate's own working room looked like inside, and how large it was, is not recorded.

In China, at the governor-general's seat at Baoding, the official's study and inner office stood past the inner-residence
gate, on the residence's side of the line between the court of business and the home. In a Japanese lord's palace the lord's own rooms, a room for daytime work and a bedroom, lay behind the formal front, and Edo-period drawings of the shogun's palace count the shogun's own office as part of that front.

Guesses:
- That the magistrate had a working room of their own in the office hall, in the back row behind the dais band, at its
  east end: no account names or places such a room; it is reckoned
  from where an office's paperwork had to be done, since a private study in the residence would stand on the household's
  side of the line between office and home.

Depiction: The plan draws the official study as a room of the office hall's back row, behind the dais band, east of the day office, its
floor drawn in the hall's own color.
