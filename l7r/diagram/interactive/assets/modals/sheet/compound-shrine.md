About: A government office seems to have kept a shrine inside its walls, as a household kept the god of its own ground in a corner of its
lot. Even the shogunate's branch post at Jōge, with just three officials, had an Inari shrine and a
Tenjin shrine within its walls, and a Chinese county office at Neixiang kept shrines to the god of the office, the god of the
land and the god of its jail. At both, a shrine was part of the office's equipment; what differed was how many each kept and which
gods they served. A samurai house, too, could keep an Inari shrine in its grounds.

What an office's shrine looked like is not recorded. A household's god was mostly kept in a small shrine of stone or of wood, some under a thatched roof,
almost never a hall like an ordinary shrine's (one measured stone shrine was about 16 in each way), or at an old tree or a stone
taken as the god's seat; one carefully kept might be given a small hall, and even a torii. A shrine often kept more than one kami: several could share one hall, or a kami brought in from
elsewhere could be given a small shrine of its own on the same ground.

How large an office's shrine was is not recorded, nor whether a garden was laid out around it. The few small halls measured, a
rural shrine's worship hall among them, ran from about 13 ft square to about 32 by 35 ft; a sanctuary was often a single bay,
about 6 ft square at one rural shrine.

Guesses:
- That a compound's shrine serves Inari unless its plan says otherwise: an Inari shrine was among the Jōge post's, the one
  Japanese office whose shrines are known, and that from a drawing alone, since the dig on the site found none.
- That the shrine hall is kept smaller than the residence: no size of an office's shrine was found, and no account ranks a
  shrine among a compound's buildings.
- A garden around the shrine, wherever a plan draws one: no account describes a garden around a household's or an office's
  shrine.

Depiction: The plan draws one shrine inside every compound, since what the offices known had in common was a shrine, not
how many each kept. It stands in a corner of the grounds, where a household kept its god, beside a tree: a household might keep its god at an old
tree in place of a shrine, and the plan gives the shrine its tree as well.
Where a compound's shrine serves more than one
kami, they are housed in one of three forms, picked at random for each plan - altars side by side, as on a household's altar shelf of three shrines; one altar for the main kami, as on a household's one-shrine shelf; or a
second small shrine - and each altar is drawn as a feature of its own, as is any torii before it.

Name: compound shrine
Covers: the shrine hall or halls, their edging and dividing rail, and the shrine labels
