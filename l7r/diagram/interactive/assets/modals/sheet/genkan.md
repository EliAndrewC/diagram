About: The genkan was a samurai house's formal entrance, a room or building at its front, kept for the head of the household and honored guests; the rest of
the household came and went by an inner entrance, and the kitchen had a door of its own. In the Edo period the word meant an
entrance with a shikidai, the low board step where guests were bowed in and seen off.

A guest of rank came by palanquin, and the palanquin was brought right alongside the step, so that its rider went into the
building without setting foot on the ground. So an arrival was staged: the gate, then open ground a palanquin could cross, then
the step. At the office compound at Jōge, the entrance and its step faced the open ground inside its main
gate. Where an intendant's office and residence shared a compound, at Takayama, the genkan was on the office; whether a guest went on from it to the residence indoors in the Edo period is not recorded.


Not every house had one, and it marked standing. The houses of senior retainers did; a village headman was allowed a
shikidai where other farmers were not; and at Kakegawa's castle palace only the lord and senior retainers came in by the
formal porch. At Takayama the office's entrance room, as rebuilt in 1816, kept a great alcove marking a rank of 100,000 koku and a wave pattern that even daimyo refrained from using. A middle-rank house could do without, and bring its guest through a middle gate and along a walled garden
path to the reception room's veranda.

Behind the step an office could have a genkan room, a matted room a step up from the shikidai, and it was for official
greetings: at Takayama the intendants newly arrived from Edo came in through it, and there the intendant met the shogunate's
envoys; at a Kaga foot soldier's house the household greeted guests and received messages there, a brief caller going no
further in. The step itself was timber, a low, broad board platform across the whole entrance; Kaibara's entrance gable was
roofed with cypress bark. What a genkan's walls and roof were otherwise built of is not recorded.

How large an ordinary house's genkan was is not recorded; at the council hall a small domain built at its Sayama seat in 1775, the genkan was 8 mats and its shikidai probably about 12 by 9 ft, and at Kawagoe's castle palace, a great lord's, the great entrance and carriage porch ran 19 by 5 ken, about 113 by 30 ft.

Guesses:
- Its size on the plans, about 10 by 6 ft to 12 by 9 ft, with a door block 5 to 6 ft wide: no account read gives the
  size of a samurai house's or an intendant's office's genkan; the one shikidai found measured, at the council hall the Sayama domain built at its jin'ya in 1775, was 2 ken and 6 mats, probably about 12 by 9 ft, and the castle palaces' are far larger.
- The genkan room's size where a plan draws one, about 12 by 38 ft, some 25 mats: no account read gives a genkan room's
  size at an office; the only mat counts found, the Sayama council hall's genkan of 8 mats, shikidai of 6 and next room of
  8, are a list from a domain's hall, not rooms in their order on a plan, and the room is drawn larger than all three.
- {0104#genkan-room-office-hall}
- That where the office and the residence share a compound, the way to the residence runs on through the office: whether a
  guest went on from the genkan to the residence indoors is not recorded, and it is drawn as today's visitors' route runs over the restored compound at the
  Takayama jin'ya.

Depiction: Where the office and the residence share a compound, the plan draws the genkan on the office hall, and the way to
the residence runs on through the office; where the residence stands alone, the genkan is on the residence. The genkan's door
opens onto open ground a palanquin can cross, never onto the street or against the side of a building. A plan whose
house brings its guests in by a garden path draws no genkan. Where a plan draws the room behind the step, the genkan room, it
is a room at the office hall's end, between the porch and the working rooms. The small dark block on the porch's outer edge is the genkan's door, drawn as every door on the plans is; it is not the step, which was a low, broad board platform across the whole entrance. Where a plan draws the porch in a fill darker than its hall's, that is a drawing convention so the porch reads apart from the hall, not the color of the step's timber boards.
