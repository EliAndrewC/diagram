About: The residence was the house where the magistrate's own household lived, inside the walled compound of the office
they ran. In a Chinese county office the home stood behind the office by regulation;
at Takayama, the one Japanese intendant's office that survives, the residence stood beside it.

Such a house was usually one block under one roof; halls joined by corridors belonged to the mansions of shoguns and
daimyo. In a lord's palace the
rooms ran from the reception at the front, through the master's own rooms, to the family's beyond; a smaller house could
set its formal room deepest in.

A veranda 3 to 6 ft wide ran along the rooms, closed at night and in bad weather by sliding board storm shutters. What the
house's walls and roof were made of is not recorded; nearly every room was matted, but the kitchen was board-floored, its earth-floored doma small. The formal garden lay south of the formal rooms, and a guest of rank
crossed open ground inside the gate to the genkan, the formal entrance, though a middle-rank house could do without one and bring its guest in along a walled garden path instead.

A 150-koku district magistrate's house at Matsushiro had about 1,740 sq ft on grounds of about 0.8 acre; a
thousand-koku samurai's house at Hagi ran somewhere between about 1,470 and 2,630 sq ft. Takayama's restored
residence, for an intendant over a whole province, is about 6,400 sq ft. A samurai household's servants lived in, inside the master's grounds - at a large house in the ranges along the street, at the Takayama residence in rooms of its own. A household had to keep as many servants as its koku required; how many lived in a magistrate's house, family or servants, is not recorded.

Depiction: The plan sets the residence in an inner court behind the office hall, with a gate between the two courts - the
Chinese county office's order, kept for every plan as a simplification, though at Takayama the residence stood beside the office. Where a plan draws a formal entrance, it is on the office hall, not on the house, the way to the house running on through the office as at Takayama; other plans draw none and bring a guest through a middle gate and along a walled garden path to the formal room's veranda. It draws the
house either as one block under one roof or as two or three halls stepped back one behind the next, each joined to the
last by a corridor, as the great houses joined their halls; a house drawn so shows one its holders have built onto, hall by hall, as the Katsura villa grew. The house is measured against a district magistrate's house - on the county magistracy's plan about 1,780 sq ft with its kitchen and bath - not against an intendant's far larger residence; a plan may draw a richer posting's house larger, up to about 2,400 sq ft. The reception stands at one end of the house, with
the garden before it on the sunny side where the buildings allow, and each labeled room - the master's, the family's, the
inner rooms - stands for a suite of several rooms. The pale strip along the garden face, carried round one or two more faces on some plans, is the veranda, drawn open: the plan shows the house by day, its storm shutters stowed in their boxes, as they stood in daylight.
