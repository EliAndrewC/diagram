About: The tax archive was the earth-walled storehouse (dozō) where an office kept its papers, in a building made to
come through the fires that took wooden halls. It was a store, built to keep what it held safe from fire, damp and
theft. A Chinese official's seat, the yamen, likewise kept an archive for its documents beside its storehouses. Office
halls were timber, and they burned: the Sado magistracy burned and was rebuilt five times, though Takayama's office
never did.

Its walls were thick earth, plastered, often a foot thick or more, its doors faced with earth and plaster or made to be
shut in a fire, and most such storehouses were roofed with tile. In a fire, Edo's townspeople put what they could not
carry out into storehouses of this kind, and from 1720 Edo encouraged earthen building against fire.

The intendant's office at Takayama, which governed the whole province of Hida, kept its documents in a book storehouse
built in 1841, with a floor of about 450 sq ft; it survives. The same office's kitchen storehouse is larger, at about
740 sq ft. No other office's records store is known by its size, and how long and how deep any ran is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That an office kept its papers in a storehouse rather than a room of its hall because of fire: no account says why.
- That a county office's records store was no larger than Takayama's: Takayama's served a whole province, so one
  governing less is reckoned to have needed no more; no other office's was measured.
- The proportions the plan draws: no account gives a records storehouse's length and depth.

Depiction: The plan draws the archive as a plastered storehouse, pale and solid. No fire-water tub stands at it: the tubs
stand at the wooden buildings, and the archive, like every plastered storehouse, was built not to burn. It stands well below the
staff's rowhouse in size, as Takayama's small storehouses did. Its door is one small dark block on its wall, standing for
the pair of plastered leaves that closed a storehouse. Like every building on the plan, it stands a little further from its neighbors than a real office's buildings did, so that each reads apart.
