About: Fire-water tubs, tensuioke, "heaven-water tubs", held rainwater kept standing against fire. Wooden halls burned - the
Sado magistracy burned and was rebuilt five times, though Takayama's office never did - and the defense was water kept ready
where a fire might start. Edo's townspeople kept rainwater tubs at the front entrance and on the roof as a daily habit, with
soaked fire-beaters for falling sparks, and a town order required tubs of water kept ready against fire. The earth-walled
storehouses were the buildings made to resist fire.

The tubs were fed from the gutters, and stood on the roofs, at the eaves and at the street corners; they became general, it
is said, after the Kansei era (1789-1801). A large tub at a street corner had hand buckets stacked on top of it, ready for
use. In the Qing, the Forbidden City in Beijing kept great water vats instead, 308 of them by its statutes, before its halls
and in its courtyards wherever a timber hall stood far from water.

How much a corner tub held is uncertain: either some 285 gallons (1,080 liters) or a tenth of that, about 29 gallons (108
liters).

Guesses:
- Which buildings get a tub, and how many, the kitchen, whose cooking fire burned all day, among the first: the habit and
  the town order are recorded, but not a rule for every building or a ranking of buildings by risk.
- A tub's size, about 2.5 ft across: no source gives a rain tub's width.
- Tubs at a village shrine at all: no page names them there; the shrine is drawn as the magistracy is.

Depiction: The plan draws each tub as a small blue disc, the water seen from above, beside a door of a wooden building,
since the water was kept at the entrance, clear of the doorway: two at the kitchen's door, a reception's beside the stone
its guests step up from, on the bath's yard side against its hearth, at most one at a wooden granary, and none at an earth-walled storehouse,
which was built not to burn. The tubs kept on the roofs are not drawn: no tub stands inside a building's outline on the plan. The tubs are drawn only on building plans: at a town's scale a tub is too small to
draw, and a walled town or city map shows its fire defense as a fire watch instead, a tower or a watch house with a ladder on its roof.
