About: The sanctuary, the honden, was where a shrine's kami was kept: a small building at the back of the shrine,
behind the hall where the villagers prayed and on the same line.
A shrine often kept several kami, one of them the main one, housed together in one sanctuary or
each given a bay of its own, behind a door of its own; which was usual at a village shrine is not recorded. In Rokugan the deity a village shrine
houses is a Fortune.

The commonest form of sanctuary across Japan was the "flowing-roof" style, nagare-zukuri, most often one bay wide or three.
A village's new sanctuary of 1841 stood on a stone base set by masons, and its carved wall panels were paid for by a few of its leading families. Another, of 1858, about 4 ft wide and 4 ft deep, was built wholly of unpainted zelkova, a hardwood, and carved from its wall panels down to its posts.
A small sanctuary
was sometimes housed under a covering building, to keep off the wind and rain. Before modern times a fence round a
sanctuary is known only where a lord or a great shrine built it.

A village shrine's sanctuary was tiny: in one district of Musashi, one-bay sanctuaries ran from about 4 ft wide to about 8. One built in 1789 is about 6 ft square, a fifth of the width of its worship
hall; another village shrine's, two bays by one, comes to about 12 by 6 ft at a bay of about 6 ft, though bays varied.

Depiction: The plan draws the sanctuary one, two or three bays wide - about 6 ft square for one bay, the size of the one built in 1789, 12 by 6 ft for two, 18 by 6 ft for three - which of them picked at random for each plan, behind
the hall on the approach's line, in a small clearing where the shrine's wood stands behind the hall (which sides of the hall the wood takes differs by settlement; the title card says which), with no fence round it, and names the Fortune it
serves beside it. It fills the sanctuary vermilion, with a gray strip along its front. Four of these are drawing conventions. The vermilion marks the kami's house apart from the hall and the dwelling; the one built in 1789 had pillars of plain squared timber with almost no ornament. The gray strip is the front of the plinth the plan sets it on. The approach runs on the same line as the hall and the sanctuary: a
sanctuary stood behind its hall on the hall's line, and the plan lays the approach on that line as well. And the
clearing keeps the building clear of the trees so it shows, where a real shrine's buildings stood under the trees of its wood. The plan never draws a
sanctuary under a covering building with no worship hall before it, because the worship hall's roof also houses the country
monk who keeps the shrine.
