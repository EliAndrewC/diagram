About: The sanctuary, the honden, was where a shrine's kami was kept: a small building at the back of the shrine,
behind the hall where the villagers prayed and on the same line.
A shrine often kept several kami, one of them the main one, housed together in one sanctuary or
each given a bay of its own; which was usual at a village shrine is not recorded. In Rokugan the deity a village shrine
houses is a Fortune.

The commonest form of sanctuary across Japan was the "flowing-roof" style, nagare-zukuri, most often one bay wide or three.
A village's new sanctuary of 1841 had carved wall panels, paid for by a few of its leading families.
A small sanctuary
was sometimes housed under a covering building, to keep off the wind and rain. No fence ringed a village shrine's
sanctuary: before modern times one is known only where a lord or a great shrine built it.

A village shrine's sanctuary was tiny: in one district of Musashi, one-bay sanctuaries ran from about 4 ft wide to about 8. One built in 1789 is about 6 ft square, a fifth of the width of its worship
hall; another village shrine's, two bays by one, comes to about 12 by 6 ft at a bay of about 6 ft, though bays varied.

Guesses:
- The size the plan gives a sanctuary three bays wide, 18 by 6 ft: no sanctuary that wide is measured, so it is reckoned from
  the measured sanctuaries' 6 to 12 ft wide and about 6 ft deep.

Depiction: The plan draws the sanctuary one, two or three bays wide - about 6 ft square for one bay, the size of the measured
village sanctuary, 12 by 6 ft for two, 18 by 6 ft for three - which of them picked at random for each plan, behind
the hall on the approach's line, in a small clearing where the shrine's wood stands behind the hall (which sides of the hall the wood takes differs by settlement; the title card says which), with no fence round it, and names the Fortune it
serves beside it. Two of these are drawing conventions. The approach runs on the same line as the hall and the sanctuary: a
sanctuary stood behind its hall on the hall's line, but where a village shrine's approach ran is not recorded. And the
clearing keeps the building clear of the trees so it shows, where a real shrine's buildings stood under the trees of its wood. The plan never draws a
sanctuary under a covering building with no worship hall before it, because the worship hall's roof also houses the country
monk who keeps the shrine.
