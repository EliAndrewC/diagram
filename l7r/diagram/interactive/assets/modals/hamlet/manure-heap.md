About: A manure heap was where a farm rotted its muck into fertilizer before it went onto the fields, since fresh dung
harmed the crop. Stable manure (kyuhi) was the dung of a household's cattle and horses, trodden by the beasts into straw
and fallen leaves until it rotted. The household's night soil was rotted too, in an earthenware jar buried in the ground
or a plastered pit, beside the privy or out by the household's fields.

Farm manuals of Song China and Edo-period Japan told the farmer to keep a house for the manure; the Chinese manual wanted
it low-eaved, because muck left under the sky lost its richness. In the manuals swept earth, ash, chaff, broken straw and
fallen leaves went into it with the night soil and the kitchen's and the bath's dirty water; where there were many beasts, their dung
was piled layer on layer like a small mountain.

How big a heap was, where in the yard it stood, and how many farms kept one before modern times are not recorded; the
manuals called for a manure house on every farm, and a compost shed was one of the outbuildings that could stand inside a
farmstead's enclosure. A count of three hamlets in northeastern Japan in 1972 found about one
compost shed to every four farms whose house was built before 1945. Some settlements kept their muck in jars sunk in the
ground instead; the title card says which this one keeps.

Guesses:
- That the muck lies in an open heap rather than in a roofed manure house: the manuals called for a house, but no count
  from before modern times says how many farms built one, so the open heap is taken to have been the commoner.
- That the heap stood just beyond the privy: no account says where in the yard it stood, and a Chinese latrine of the Han
  dynasty was built over the pigsty and drained to a cesspool, so privy and muck are taken to have stood together.
- Its size on the map, 8 by 6 ft: no account measures a heap.
- That 40 to 70 farmsteads in 100 keep a heap: no count of any date gives it, so the share is set below the manuals' manure
  house on every farm.
- That a settlement keeps its muck in heaps or in sunk jars at even odds: both forms are recorded, but not how many
  villages used each.

Depiction: The map draws a heap as a small dark-brown mound streaked with straw, just beyond the privy on the side away from
the house, or behind or beside the house where there is no privy. Where the privy stands on the sunny side and a neighbor
lies that way too, a heap may stand nearer the neighbor's house than its own; a click on it says whose it is.
