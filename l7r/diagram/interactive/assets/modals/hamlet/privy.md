About: The privy was a farm household's toilet. On a farm it was usually a building of its own, apart from the
house, with a urinal and the privy under one roof. It stood outside because what it gathered was manure for the fields,
and many stood south or southeast of the house, in the sun, where the contents rotted quickly; others stood under the
eaves in front of the stable or out in the yard.

What its walls and roof were made of is not recorded. The night soil was kept in a tank beside it or in a pit out by the
fields, and rotted in a jar buried in the ground or a plastered pit. At its
simplest, at several farms of one northern village, the privy was only a tub sunk in a barn's floor with boards laid
across it.

Nearly every farm had one: in the Edo period most houses of one village had a privy outside the house, and at the period's end all
sixteen households of another; in one northern district a new branch household was given at least a house, a stable and a
privy. In that sixteen-house village privies ran from about 5 by 5 ft to 27 by 15 ft, most 18 by 12 ft or smaller. A
privy joined to the house was at first an official's: in six villages only the headman's or other officials' houses had
one until 1771, and about one house in ten by 1824.

Guesses:
- How often each of the places a privy stood is drawn: no source says how often each was used.
- Where in the yard an outhouse stood, and how far out from the house a privy in the front yard stood: no source says.
- How far from its house a privy on the sunny side may stand: no source gives a distance, so it is kept near enough to read
  as its own house's.

Depiction: The map draws a privy on most farmsteads as a small roofed building at one of the sixteen privy sizes counted in
one village, with a dark circle at one end standing for its night soil, so it does not read as a wood shed; the real night soil was kept
in a tank beside it or in a pit out by the fields. A privy is sought first on the house's sunny side, as about three real privies in four stood,
but the work yard takes much of that ground, so fewer than half end up there; most of the rest stand behind the house, because the work yard covers the ground in front of the house and under the stable's eaves, where real privies stood too.
