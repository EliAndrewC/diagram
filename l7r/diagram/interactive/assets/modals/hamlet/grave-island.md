About: A grave in the fields held a family's dead out in the farmland, apart from any shared burial ground. Around
Shanghai many villagers buried their dead one by one in the middle of the open fields, at a spot a Taoist priest or a
geomancer chose. In parts of Japan a household's burial grave once stood on its own land beside the bunds of its fields,
until an order of 1872 forbade burial at a bund's edge; elsewhere a household's grave
stood in a corner of its field, or in the middle of a field along a road.

A column of soldiers marching into Shanghai in 1842 found graves in every field, as mounds of earth. Where a Japanese
family kept two graves, the body's grave was marked with no more than a stone. Whether any grave stood out in the middle
of a flooded paddy is not recorded: no Japanese grave is known in mid-paddy, and the Chinese accounts do not say what the
fields grew.

On level farmland in parts of 1920s China, graves took about 2.6 percent of the farm land, and up to 9.1 percent on
fertile land; where no hill land could take them, they stood anywhere in a field. A field grave's own size is not
recorded, but the graves of one Edo-period temple graveyard ran about 2 to 8 ft long. How common such graves were is not
recorded either, though where the custom held they seem to have been common.

Guesses:
- [grave_form=island] That a grave stood as an island inside a flooded paddy plot: the Chinese accounts show graves
  inside working fields without naming the crop, and no Japanese grave read stood in mid-paddy.
- That a hamlet takes the island or the corner form at even odds: no source weighs one against the other.
- The mound's size on the map, about half its plot's width for an island and smaller in a corner: no size of a grave in the
  fields was found.

Depiction: The map draws a grave in the fields in one of two forms, and each hamlet takes one; the title card says which.
About three in ten maps of valley-floor, terraced or narrow-valley paddy draw one; no rate is known, so the share is a
choice.

[grave_form=island] Here the grave is a mound with two or three stone markers on an island inside a plot, the paddy's
basins cut back to run up round it, which is the Chinese form.

[grave_form=corner] Here the grave is a small mound with one or two stones in a plot's corner against its bunds, which is
the Japanese form. The map sets the mound a short step in from the corner, clear of any ditch or path along the plot's edge, so it reads as standing in the corner, and carries the plot's bund round it; such a grave stood in a field's corner, beside its bunds.
