About: A small grass hill was sometimes set aside for one paddy's sole use, its grass cut for that paddy alone; it
was called a tazuke kusayama.

A village's paddies needed far more grass than their own bunds gave: the grass and young shoots
for green manure were cut in early spring and plowed straight into the fields, and on one farm in the late Edo period about two
horse-loads went on every tan (about a quarter acre) of paddy. Most of that grass came from the village's common grass ground, the grass hills past
the fields; a grass hill kept for one paddy was the exception.

Grass hills were cleared of their trees and kept for their grass, though their cover ran from open grass to a thin wood, chiefly of pine, up to about 10 to 13 ft tall. This one's area, and where it lay against its paddy, are not
recorded.

Guesses:
- That about one hamlet in three keeps one: the record says only that it was done sometimes.
- That it is about the size of one paddy plot: no record gives its size.
- That it stands on the slope just above its paddy, at the top of the field: where it lay against its paddy is not recorded.

Depiction: The map draws the paddy's grass hill as a patch of cut grass - the scrub's tufts, half as many, with no brush
and no pine - standing on the slope just above one paddy at the top of the field. Like the commons it is one small block repeated
across its ground, with no outline. Its ground is tinted a fresh green, a map drawing convention so the cut patch can be told from
the rough grazing around it; a real cut slope would show only its shorter grass.
