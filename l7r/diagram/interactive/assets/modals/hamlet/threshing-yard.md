About: The threshing yard (niwa) was the open ground on the sunny south side of a Japanese farmhouse, where the
household threshed its harvest and dried the grain. The rice was drawn a handful at a time through combs set on straw
mats; the grain was then dried on mats spread across the yard, for a day to a week, and husked later
indoors, usually as night work. In south China some households had their own floor before the house, and some villages
shared one.

At harvest the yard lay under straw mats, each about 3 by 6 ft. On the San'in coast, where the autumn weather changed
easily, the rice-drying racks were gathered by the house so the threshing could be done at home; elsewhere they stood
mostly out on the fields. A south-China floor was hoed level, tamped and washed with cow dung and water before each
harvest; hardly one household in a hundred laid lime and sand over it.

The yard was the farm's open ground, and grain was not all it held. At Arakawa, a Shiga village swept by winds off the mountains toward Lake Biwa, in the
Meiji era, the storehouses and retirement houses stood southwest and west of the house as wind fences round
the open ground before the entrance, where rice was threshed and vegetables dried. Fruit trees were planted in part of a
yard; on the Shonai plain, where the rice was prepared indoors on the house's wide earthen floor, the front yard was
narrow and often planted with fruit trees, flower beds or vegetable beds. In northern Miyagi, once the bath and the privy
became huts of their own, a fashion of early Meiji, they were usually put in the front yard.

A household measured its yard in mats: forty to sixty on an ordinary farm, about 700 to 1,100 sq ft, and past a hundred,
over 1,800 sq ft, on a few. In one Shikoku district in the Taisho era the yard took a fifth of a lot averaging about 4,500
sq ft. Most yards were near the ordinary size and a few far larger; a larger household had a larger house lot, and probably a larger yard, though not in
proportion. In an Edo-period planned colony, every settler's homestead was the same.

Depiction: The map draws each yard beside its house as the harvest left it, part-covered in straw mats: between a third and
two thirds of the mats a real yard held, each with a little bare ground around it and most a little askew, as laid by
hand, since dozens of mats edge to edge would read as a textured floor rather than as mats. Each yard's size is drawn at
random about the ordinary middle, a few much larger, a larger household's mostly larger, unless the settlement gives
every farm the same yard. Every yard is kept in the sun, clear of the shade a neighbor's house, a tree or bamboo casts in
autumn, so a farm whose yard lies before its house keeps its persimmon behind it, though fruit trees stood in part of some yards. No racks are drawn out on the fields, where the seasonal racks stood in the month after the harvest.
