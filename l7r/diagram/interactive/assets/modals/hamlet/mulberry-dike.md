About: A mulberry dike, sangji, "mulberry dike", was the bank round a fish pond, heaped from the pond's own mud and planted
with mulberry to feed silkworms. The leaves went to the silkworms, the silkworms' droppings went to the fish, and the mud
dredged from the pond went back onto the bank.
In the Pearl River delta mulberry took the dikes over from fruit trees in the late Ming and early
Qing; such ponds are kept on the Yangtze delta too.

The mulberry was kept as low bushes, stripped for its leaves; in one plantation practice it was cut back each year to 5
or 6 ft, and on the Yangtze delta today it stands in rows along the bank on a low or medium trunk. Each time a pond was drained,
its mud was dredged up onto the bank, raising it and feeding its soil. The bushes stood bare in winter.

How thickly the mulberry stood depended on how low it was cut: from about a bush to the square foot in the Pearl River
delta's root-cut planting today to about one to 24 sq ft in a late-Qing field of mid-trunk trees. Modern dikes in the Pearl River delta are usually about
20 to 33 ft (6 to 10 m) wide, standing 2 to 3 ft (0.5 to 1 m) above the water, though modern practice also keeps a dike between ponds under 16 ft (5 m) unless it carries a sty or traffic; in one village dikes once 66 ft (20 m) wide have worn to under 13 ft (4 m) as the ponds
grew. A dike's width before modern times is not recorded. Water and bank were kept in proportion, so that the bank's mulberry could feed the fish beside it; today the split is given as about six parts water to four of bank, or the reverse, or seven to three, and how it ran before modern times is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That each of the five recorded splits of water and bank is drawn at even odds: the accounts carry all five, and may differ by region rather than in error,
  and every account found that writes the split as a ratio is modern.
- That one split holds for every pond of a village, so the bank is as wide as that split makes it: no width of a pond
  dike from before modern times was found, and the one modern account of how the split was set says only that in practice it was more complicated and generally governed by tradition.
- Each bush's crown, 4.4 to 7 ft across: no account gives how wide a mulberry bush grew.

Depiction: The map draws each bank as mottled earth under rows of low bushes, about 4.4 ft apart in the row, about one
bush to 24 sq ft: the late-Qing spacing, the one density dated before modern times. At that spacing the crowns run together in
stretches with earth showing between, where the far denser root-cut planting would fuse into one band of green. No
berries are drawn: a mulberry's fruit would be smaller than the map can show, and whether a bush stripped for its leaves
bore fruit is not recorded. A bush always stands on the bank, though its crown may lean over the canal at the bank's foot.
Each settlement's ponds are drawn at one of five splits of water to bank: six parts to four either way, seven of water to three of bank, one to one, or
three of water to seven of bank. The bank is drawn as a ring round the water, so pointing at a dike lights the bank and not the pond inside it. Every dike
of a hamlet carries the same crop, so each hamlet reads as one kind of dike-pond village; the title card says which.
