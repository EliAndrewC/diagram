About: A household grave held a farming family's dead on the family's own land, apart from any shared burial ground.
Many Japanese households once kept a graveyard of their own; a branch house might set its grave beside the main house's,
so that the kin shared one, and some set the grave within their own house land, a house-plot grave. Until the Second
World War many graves were still built on a corner of a household's own land.

In the former Hatara district, before 1688, one family kept its graveyard at the southeast corner of its
house plot, and another family's at the northwest corner, ground now counted as woodland. Of how such a grave looked, little is told: one family's descendant believed its dead were buried
without gravestones, in the grove behind the plot's shrine to its guardian god; whether a household grave had a fence, a hedge or a kept surface is not recorded. In that district many houses kept a graveyard beside the plot
or close by it. Elsewhere a household's grave stood in its own field, in a front garden, or in a corner of a field.

Such a grave stood beside the shared ground a settlement or a temple kept, not in place of it: temple graveyards and grounds held in common stood across the
country beside it, and how common each form was, village by village, is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That about half of hamlets keep household graves, and where they do, about a third of the farmsteads: the record says
  many households kept one, but gives no count.
- How large the grave is on the map, and its few upright stones, a small patch about 8 by 6 ft: no household grave was found
  measured, and the one grave described was believed to have had no gravestones.

Depiction: The map draws a household grave as a small cleared patch with a few upright stones, at a corner of the
farmstead's plot, the southeast or the northwest first, a short step off the corner of the house. A hamlet keeps such
graves or does not; where it does, about a third of its farmsteads draw one, and a farmstead whose corners are all taken
draws none.
