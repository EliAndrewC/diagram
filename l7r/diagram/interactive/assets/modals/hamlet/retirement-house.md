About: A retirement house (inkyoya) was where a farm's old couple lived once they had retired. Moving out was the main
form of retirement, one form of it being the old couple alone going to a house in a separate building, and the two often kept
separate meals and purses - one family living as two households. Whether any of the farm's work was done in a retirement house is not recorded.

Most retirement houses stood inside the family's own house plot, with an entrance of their own. In one windswept village
of Shiga, they stood west and southwest of the house with the
storehouses, sheltering the front yard from the wind. What one was built of, and how it was laid out inside, is
not recorded.

The custom was strong from the Pacific coast through the Inland Sea, above all in Kyushu and Shikoku, and thin in the
northeast and along the Sea of Japan coast, where the generations stayed together under one roof. Where it was kept
thoroughly, every house had one; in one village east of Edo, counted some years after the Edo period,
8 of 16 households had one.

A retirement house was small. That village's eight ran from 3 to 15 tsubo (about 110 to 540 sq ft), and one of 9 tsubo, 3 ken square (about 18 ft square), was a middling one.
A samurai house's retirement house of 1820, in the Edo period, in
Matsushiro, is about 25 by 15 ft (7.7 by 4.6 m).

Guesses:
- Its size on the map, about 18 by 15 ft: the record gives a range of sizes but no one plan, so the map draws one size
  inside that range, a little under the middling 9 tsubo, as a room or two with an earth-floored entry.
- How many of a settlement's homesteads keep one, which the title card states: no share across a region was found, so where
  the custom is kept the map gives one to some homesteads, not every one, since a household had a retired couple for only
  part of its life.
- {0028#side-farmhouse-stands-how}

Depiction: The map draws a retirement house as a small farmhouse, its roof in the farmhouse's own two shades of thatch,
standing off the back wall or one flank of its farmhouse with its door on the side away from it, its own entrance; what a
real one's roof was is not recorded. It belongs to its farmhouse's family, so it is not counted as a household; the title
card says whether this settlement keeps the custom, and how many of its homesteads have one.
