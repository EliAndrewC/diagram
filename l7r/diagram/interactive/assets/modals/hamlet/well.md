About: A well (ido) was a shaft dug by hand to the water below. Most were shared, because a well cost a great deal to
dig: a whole village, or a group of households within one, drew from a common well, and the wellside was where the
neighbors met, to draw water, do the washing and talk. A farm could have its own, probably where the water lay close under
the ground: in one low-lying Edo-period village most houses had a well outside the main house. A samurai household could
have a well of its own inside its fence.

[settlement_form=dispersed] On one plain where the water lay deep and wells were hard to dig, scattered farms often led a
small channel off the irrigation water into their grounds instead, for cooking, washing and drinking.

The shaft was about 3 ft (1 m) across, with a frame of wood or stone round its mouth, the curb. The bucket came up on a sweep, a pole worked like a seesaw over a post with a weight at its butt, where the water
stood shallow, and on a rope over a pulley where it lay deep. Some wells had a roof on posts over them.

In one coastal village the wells were dug at about one to every five to ten houses, and the ground round many of the
common ones was paved with stones. One well could serve several hundred: in late-Qing Beijing, at least about
650 inhabitants.

Guesses:
- That the curb the marker stands for was about 4 ft square: no premodern curb was found measured, so the figure is fitted
  round a premodern shaft about 3 ft across.
- That about three wells in four on shallow water take a sweep and three in four on deep water a pulley frame, and that a
  valley floor holds shallow water and an upland deep: the two gears and the depth that chose between them are known, the
  shares and the landforms are not.
- That about one well in three has a roof: no count of roofs over ordinary wells was found.
- The paved apron's width, about 4 ft round the curb: the paving is known, its width is not.
- That a village's well may stand on a lane's verge: wayside wells are known only in general, not in villages.
- [settlement_form=dispersed] That a scattered settlement's farms draw channels or wells of their own at even odds, and
  where in the dooryard a farm's well or channel ends: no source says how many areas used each, where a farm's well stood
  or where a channel ended.

Depiction: The map draws a well as a marker much larger than life, its curb about 19 ft across where a real well's mouth was about 3 ft (1 m), so a reader can find it:
the marker shows where the well stands, not how much ground it takes. Wells stand
among the houses they serve, never in the fields or on open common ground, and the map draws more of them than a real
village counted, and keeps every household within reach of water. A shrine set apart from the houses keeps a well of its own
beside its hall, and a hilltop shrine none; the shrine's water is drawn as a well rather than a ditch or a pond by the map's
convention, since which waters a shrine drew on is not known.

[settlement_form=dispersed] A scattered farm draws its own water, a well in its dooryard or a small channel led off the
nearest irrigation ditch into its grounds; the title card says which.

[settlement_form=linear] A row village draws either a well at every farm or wells shared along its street; the title card
says which.
