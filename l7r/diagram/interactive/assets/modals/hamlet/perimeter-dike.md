About: A polder's dike was a bank of earth heaped right round fields reclaimed from a lake, a river or a marsh, to hold the
water out: the ground inside lay below the water outside for part or all of the year, and drained through gates
when that water fell. In the dike-pond country of the Pearl River delta the dike was made of the mud dug out of the ponds.
In one Lake Tai village in the 1930s, each polder's raised margin was at once its dike, its mulberry ground and the
ground its houses stood on.

A dike could be planted along its length: on one great Song polder, rows of mulberry flanked the ridge and rows of
willows ran along it, with reeds below. Willow roots bind a bank, and willows were cut for
shoots woven into baskets, fish traps and fences. Willow and mulberry stood bare in winter. Polders took the shapes the
ground gave them, square, round, oblong, many-sided or irregular; around Lake Tai the rectangular polder is a modern form.

That great Song dike was about five times as broad as it was high, wide at its foot and narrow on top. A river dike
of early Edo Japan stood about 12 ft (3.6 m) high on an 18 ft (5.5 m) base. The Lake Tai margin's mulberry
strip was 33 to 98 ft (10 to 30 m) wide, more where houses stood. How high a village polder's dike stood is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That the dike's outer face followed the water's edge in gentle curves: polders took many shapes, but no account says
  how an outer dike ran along a shore.
- That a small polder's dike stood about 5 ft (1.5 m) high: no height for a small polder's dike was found, and the heights
  found, a great polder's and a domain's river dike, bound it only from above.

Depiction: The map draws the dike as a band of heaped earth all the way round the polder, 14 to 40 ft across and changing
in width along its length, mottled as mud patched year after year is. Where it runs broader than about 18 ft, the base of
the early Edo river dikes, it is drawn so on purpose, so that the dike holding the water off the whole polder reads as
the greater bank beside the crop dikes round the fish ponds. Two rows of trees run along it, willow on the water side and
mulberry on the field side; the maps set them on the bank's faces, where the willows of the great Ming river dikes stood on
the ground off the dike's foot, so they read as the dike's own planting. The willows are spaced about 8.5 ft apart,
looser than the roughly 5.5 ft spacing of the willows on a Qing border embankment, and the mulberry about 4.4 ft, so their
crowns read as touching trees rather than one hedge. The band and its rows are cut where a channel
crosses it, at a gate. The farmhouses keep their own windbreak groves besides, since the dike's low, bare trees did little
to shelter a village.

Name: perimeter dike
