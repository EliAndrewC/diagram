About: In a hamlet that dug its shared wells among the houses, each wellhead stood in the open ground between the
homesteads that drew from it, off the lanes. Most wells were shared, because a well cost a great deal to dig; a whole village,
or a group of households within one, drew from a common well, and the wellside seems to have been where the neighbors met to draw water, do
the washing and talk.

The shaft was about 3 ft (1 m) across under a curb of wood or stone, the bucket raised on a sweep or, from deep water, on a
rope over a pulley. Round many of Karihama's common wells the ground was paved with stones.

In Karihama, a water-short village on the Ehime coast, the wells were dug at about one to every five to ten houses. On the water-poor Musashino plateau, the 172 to 180 farms of Santome first dug 11 wells and shared them. On low, wet ground,
most houses of one village in eastern Musashi, in the Edo period, had a well outside the main house.

Guesses:
- That a village's shared wells stood among the houses that drew from them, and about as often as by the lane: it is read from Karihama, whose survey counts the houses to a well but does not map where the wells stood, and no account says how common each siting was.
- That the five to ten houses a Karihama well served stand for a hamlet's wells elsewhere: Karihama and Santome, whose 172 to 180 farms first shared 11 wells, are the only village counts
  read.

Depiction: The map sets each shared well in the open ground among about five houses, off every lane, with a house near it;
where a hamlet has fewer than five households to share a well, it stands among three, or two. The well is drawn as a marker
larger than life, about 19 ft across, so a reader can find it; a real well's curb was a frame only a few feet across round
a shaft about 3 ft (1 m) wide.
