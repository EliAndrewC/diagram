About: A scattered farm's water could come by a small channel led off the irrigation into its own grounds, and the household
used it for cooking, washing and drinking. The Tonami plain of Toyama, an alluvial fan, has over 7,000
farmhouses each in the middle of its own fields, a landscape over 500 years in the making: the farmers built there so that
each could manage the water for their own fields. On the fan the water table lay deep and a well was hard to dig, so in many
areas each house's grounds took a channel, bringing that same irrigation water home to the household.

What such a channel looked like, how it was lined and where in the grounds it ended are not recorded. Elsewhere in Japan, at
Harie on Lake Biwa, a channel ran alongside the houses, and each house washed its vegetables at a washing place of its own at the water.

How wide a farm channel ran is not recorded; at Hongcun, in Anhui, a village's channel to every household was nearly 3 ft
(1 m) wide. How the Tonami areas without channels drew their water is not recorded either.

Guesses:
- That a scattered settlement leads channels to its farms or digs each a well at even odds: no account says how many areas
  used each.
- That a channel may be led off the brook, standing for the irrigation water, or off a neighbor's channel: no account says
  where a channel was taken from, or whether neighbors shared one.
- That it ends in the dooryard, a step off the threshing yard: no account says where it ended.

Depiction: The map leads each farm's channel off the nearest irrigation ditch it draws, or the brook where that is nearer, never the drain, round the buildings,
yards and gardens and through the farm's own grove into its dooryard. Where the channel left the grounds again is not recorded, so it
is not drawn. It is drawn about 2.5 ft wide, the narrowest water the map shows legibly; how wide the real channel ran is not
recorded. A farm that no channel can reach is given a well of its own.
