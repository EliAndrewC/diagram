About: A notice board (kosatsuba) was the wooden frame on which the shogunate and the domains posted standing law - the ban on
Christians, the rules on coin, fire and packhorse charges - so that everyone would know it. The boards stood where traffic was
heaviest: at a town's central crossroads and its entrances, at the barrier stations and ports, and at the foot of bridges. Edo's
six great boards stood at bridge ends and gates, and a farming village's board stood at its center, at its way in, before the
gate of a village official's house, or at a bridge end.

The boards measured at two post towns were frames about 10 ft high and 16 to 18 ft along the road; the one rebuilt at Kanagawa
stands on a stone base inside a fence, under a roof, its notices hung high above the passers-by. A village's board could be
built the same way, but its size is not recorded.

Boards were most often set where people passed, at the village center or at a crossroads of main highways; how many villages kept theirs at a bridge instead is not recorded.

Guesses:
- Which of a settlement's bridges its board stood by: no source says, so the map takes the widest, a brook's bridge before a ditch's plank, and of two alike the one nearest the houses.
- That a village's board was the size of a post town's: no measurement of a village board was found.
- How often a settlement's board stands at a bridge rather than its center, its way in or an official's gate: the one source that ranks them puts the center first but gives no share, so the map rolls evenly among the seats a settlement can site - one in four where all four can be sited.

Depiction: Only a settlement whose map draws a bridge takes this seat. The map seats the board
on the way at the foot of the bridge, close by the way's edge where there is room, otherwise a little back from it, and turned broadside to it. It is drawn as a roofed
frame on a stone footing inside a fence. On a village or city map a frame the size of those measured at two post towns, about
16 ft by 6 ft, would be too small to see, so the map draws a larger marker in its place, footing and all, and its label says
what it is. A board may stand under a tree, and its label is drawn above every crown, so it shows where the board is even when a
crown hides it.
