About: In a clustered village, the households' own trees could stand among the houses themselves: clumps of
trees in the gaps between the farms, the dooryard copse. A Japanese homestead's wood was its own, grown on the lot where its
house stood, and it was a store as much as a shelter, giving firewood from its branches, timber from thinning and fallen
leaves for the hearth and for manure. In southern China villagers planted fruit trees and bamboo for their use in their fengshui woods.

Early in the twentieth century, round a farm's main house and outbuildings on the Musashino upland stood woods of cedar, bamboo, evergreen oak and
zelkova; in the farm groves of the Tonami plain persimmon was among the three commonest trees, and
persimmon, fig and flowering trees stood at a house's front.

In one Edo-period register, three households' woods covered about 6,000, 10,700 and 28,000 sq ft; homestead woods varied in form,
size and kinds of tree. Whether a village kept a wood of its own among its houses, apart from each farm's, is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That a clustered village's trees stand among its houses or against its back grove at even odds: no account describes the
  trees among a clustered village's houses, in Japan or in southern China.
- That each farm's wood, its share of the copse counted in, runs between about 6,000 and 28,000 sq ft: the range is the one
  register's three households, not a survey.
- That the copse is bamboo and fruit trees: no account describes it, so it takes the trees a homestead grove and a fengshui
  wood were planted with.

Depiction: The map spreads the copse through the gaps between the houses, each clump within a dooryard's reach of its house,
standing against the houses but never on a roof, a yard, a garden or a shed, out of the yards' and gardens' sun, and off the main road.
Where the ground near the houses is used up, the copse is drawn smaller than its households' woods.
