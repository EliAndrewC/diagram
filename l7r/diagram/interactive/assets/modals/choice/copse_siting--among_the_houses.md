About: In the loosely clustered villages common in the Kanto and the Tohoku, each house stood on a wide lot with its own grove, so the households' own trees stood among the houses themselves: clumps of
trees in the gaps between the farms, the dooryard copse. A Japanese homestead's wood was its own, grown on the lot where its
house stood, and it was a store as much as a shelter, giving firewood from its branches, timber from thinning and fallen
leaves for the hearth and for manure. In southern China villagers planted fruit trees and bamboo for their use in their fengshui woods.

Early in the twentieth century, round a farm's main house and outbuildings on the Musashino upland stood woods of cedar, bamboo, evergreen oak and
zelkova. In the farm groves of the Tonami plain, as surveyed in modern times, persimmon was among the three commonest trees, and
persimmon, fig and flowering trees stood at a house's front. A persimmon commonly stood in a farmhouse's dooryard, the yard before the house, for its fruit and its summer shade.

In one Edo-period register, three households' woods covered about 6,000, 10,700 and 28,000 sq ft; homestead woods varied in form,
size and kinds of tree. Whether a village kept a wood of its own among its houses, apart from each farm's, is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That a clustered village's trees stand among its houses or against its back grove at even odds: no account describes the
  trees among a southern Chinese village's houses, and in Japan only the loosely clustered villages of the Kanto and the Tohoku are recorded with each house in its own grove, the denser villages of the west with houses side by side behind walls.
- That each farm's wood, its share of the copse counted in, runs between about 6,000 and 28,000 sq ft: the range is the one
  register's three households, not a survey.
- That the copse is fruit and other broadleaf trees, without bamboo: no account describes a clump standing between the
  houses, so it takes the broadleaf and fruit trees a homestead grove and a fengshui wood were planted with, and leaves a
  household's bamboo to a stand of its own.

Depiction: The map spreads the copse through the gaps between the houses, each clump within a dooryard's reach of its house,
standing against the houses but never on a roof, a yard, a garden or a shed, out of the yards' and gardens' sun, and off the lanes and the main road, so no lane runs between a farm and its own trees.
Where the ground near the houses is used up, the copse is drawn smaller than its households' woods. The copse is drawn with fruit and other broadleaf trees only: bamboo grew round the houses too, but the map draws a farm's bamboo as a stand of its own, beside the house or inside its grove.
