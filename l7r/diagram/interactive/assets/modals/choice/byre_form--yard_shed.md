About: A farm that plowed with a beast kept an ox, a water buffalo or a horse - in Japan horses in the east and cattle in the
west, in China's rice country the ox and the water buffalo - and the beast lived with the household that kept it. Across much
of Japan its stall was a corner of the farmhouse's earth floor, but elsewhere it stood in a stable of its own: the outer
stable, a building apart from the house in the household's own plot. In some regions, southern Kanto among them, a stall in the
house was rare.

A farm stable's sides were plank walls, and its entrance was closed with removable wooden bars rather than a door;
whether a stable of its own had its front closed in the Edo period is not recorded. In some regions its floor was dug 2 ft
or more deep, and wild grass thrown in load after load, part eaten and the rest trodden with the dung into manure. A farm stable usually had a ceiling, with a servants' room or a storeroom above it, and one standing on its own usually had a privy and a shed built on with it.

Two such stables of the Edo period have been measured: single-story buildings under tile roofs, of about 320 and
370 sq ft (30 and 34 sq m), one with a frame of about 15 by 12 ft. In the Kinki region a farmhouse's stable
was about 10 ft (3 m) square; stables were wider where winters were long; how big an Edo-period smallholder's stable was is not
recorded: both measured stables stand on farmsteads registered as cultural properties.

Not every household kept a beast: in one western province from the early 1700s only about half did, and fewer as time went
on. In one village of 16 households, 8 kept a stable. In most of China a farm kept a draft animal or two.

Guesses:
- How far the shed stands from the farmhouse: no source gives it.
- That a settlement keeps its beasts in a shed in the yard, indoors, or in shared sheds, about three, six and one time in ten: no source counts the forms from one settlement to the next; a modern count found the beasts inside the house at about six old farmhouses in ten, and no shared shed was found.
- That between 35 and 50 in every 100 households keep a beast: the top is the half one province's households kept in the early 1700s, the bottom the fewer that kept one later; how many fewer no source gives, and one village of 1824 had a stable at about two houses in three, above the range.

Depiction: The map draws the stable as a small one-room shed of about 16 by 11 ft, with a plank roof and a dark open front to its stall so it reads apart from the storehouse and the dwellings, standing
on its own in the homestead near the farmhouse, only at the households that keep a beast. It is kept small, near the
surviving frame and under the surviving stables' full area, so it reads as a shelter for a beast or two rather than a barn of
many stalls. It is drawn the same whether the settlement keeps an ox, a water buffalo or a horse: the beast changes what the
stable held, not how it looks from above. The surviving stables were tiled.
