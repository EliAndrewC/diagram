About: A farming village took the higher, drier ground beside its paddy, where its houses stayed out of the wet. The old ideal of a site, from China, set a house with its back to the hill and its face to the
sun and the water, where the hills encircled and the water embraced; a farmhouse probably stood close up against its paddy
in just that way. On a river's lowland the old villages stood on the natural levee, a slight rise that drained well above the
wet ground behind it, and a lump of houses grew at the foot of a hill or plateau or on a river terrace.

A visitor walking up from the paddy found the houses along its upper edge, the hill or the rising ground behind them and the
fields falling away in front.

How often a village sat on the high edge rather than elsewhere beside its fields is not recorded, nor how far its houses
stood from the paddy.

Guesses:
- That the high edge on the windward side is one seat among several, and how often each comes up: which edge of a valley was
  driest depended on the valley, and no count of village seats was found.

Depiction: The map seats the cluster along the upper edge of the fields, with its back to the rising ground,
and keeps it off any drawn marsh. Every hamlet but a polder's takes this seat, its land laid to fall away from the winter wind or square across it, so its houses keep their back to the wind (a polder's village backs onto its landward shore instead); in reality it was one seat among several. Where a map sets its land to fall toward the wind, the houses still take the high edge, their back to the hill and off the wind.

Name: Where the houses sit: on the high edge of the fields
