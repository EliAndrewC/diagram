About: The wagon inn was the road inn (dian) of north China, built round a broad yard for the carters' wagons and animals.
Under the Ming dynasty the commonest Chinese inn was the plain "mule-and-horse" inn of the open country, whose guests were
mostly carters who often cooked for themselves, so its service was simple and its price cheap. A Korean traveler on the road
to Shanhaiguan in 1780 found inns built round courtyards "not less than several hundred paces", since nothing smaller could
hold the carts, horses and crowds; the wagon inns of the northeast, from the end of the Qing into the 1970s, stood on the
main roads and round a city's outskirts.

A visitor pulled into a yard full of animals. In 1780 an inn's stable held a long trough of stone or brick two or three
bays long, and dozens of wooden troughs stood in rows in the yard; in 1863 a traveler found horses, mules and donkeys loose
all over one inn's courtyard and slept in its dormitory. The later wagon inns kept open ground at the back for the carts
and a long stable with one very long trough; their buildings were single-story, of mud brick and reed thatch, and their
rooms held heated sleeping platforms (kang), each a common bed for about twenty.

No Chinese inn's frontage, depth or area is recorded.

Guesses:
- That an inn before modern times stood a single story: the 1780 account gives no height, so the single story is carried
  back from the wagon inns of the 20th century.
- That a town's inn is a wagon inn, at even odds with the two-story post-station inn: nothing says how common either was.
- Its size on the map, about 66 by 48 ft: no Chinese inn's size was found.

Depiction: The map draws the inn as one story, a single row of windows along its wall, fronting the road it serves with its
entrance turned to the roadbed, so that a caravan can pull up from the road it is already on. Its long stable stands close
by, with open ground beside it for the carts.
