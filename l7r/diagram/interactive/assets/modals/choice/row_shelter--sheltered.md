About: In a row village with farms on both sides of its road, the farms on the lee side of the road could lean on the
grove row across it for their windbreak. At Ogawa on the Musashino plateau, a farm south of its road grew its bamboo all along its south side and sold
it to basket makers: the row of farms with groves across the road to the north already broke the wind, so no bamboo was
needed on that side as a windbreak, its owners said; the farm still kept a grove north of its house, but a thin one, mostly
zelkova. At Minami-cho, on the old Yanagikubo new fields, another farm south of
its road had its bamboo on the south, likely because of the row of grove farms across the road. How deep the bamboo stood, and how far past the yard, is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That a row village takes this form or every farm's own grove at even odds: both are attested, and neither is counted
  against the other.
- That a sheltered farm's windward grove is one tree deep: the account calls Ogawa's "rather thin" and gives no depth.
- That a street sheltering its lee row runs within about 67 degrees of across the wind: no account gives an angle.
- The bamboo band's depth, 16 ft, and how far past the yard it stands: no account gives either.

Depiction: The map draws the farms on the street's lee side with a thin grove, one tree deep, on their windward sides, and
each with its bamboo in a band
along its whole south side, true south whatever the map's wind, just past its yard's and beds' sun ground, where the
bamboo would shade neither; where that ground is taken the band is moved further south, and a farm with no room left
keeps none. The farms on the street's windward side keep their groves.
