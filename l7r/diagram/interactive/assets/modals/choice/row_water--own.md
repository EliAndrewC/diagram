About: In a row with wells of its own, each farm dug a well on its own lot. Most wells were shared, because a well cost a
great deal to dig and in many places the ground made water hard to reach; a household could more easily have its own where
the water lay close under the ground, as on the loam uplands of the Kanto, where shallow wells were made. Villages were often
sited where water was easy to draw, along the spring line at a fan's foot and on natural levees, the very lines a row
followed.

A well was a shaft dug by hand about 3 ft (1 m) across, with a frame of wood or stone round its mouth, the curb. Where the
water stood shallow the bucket came up on a sweep, a pole worked like a seesaw over a post; where it lay deep, on a rope over
a pulley.

Whether the farms of a row on low ground dug their own wells or shared them is not recorded; the one row whose water is
known, Santome on the dry Musashino plateau, shared deep wells. Yet in one village on the low, wet ground of eastern
Musashi, recorded house by house in the Edo period, most houses had a well outside the main house, while one water-short
coastal village had about one well to every five to ten houses.

Guesses:
- That a row's farms each dig their own well or share wells along the way at even odds: no account says which a row on
  lower ground did.

Depiction: The map gives every farm of the row a well of its own, standing in the farm's grounds; the well is drawn as a
marker larger than life, about 19 ft across, so a reader can find it; a real well's curb was a frame only a few feet
across round a shaft about 3 ft wide.
