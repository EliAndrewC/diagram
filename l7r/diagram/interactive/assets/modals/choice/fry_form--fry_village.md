About: A fry village raised the young fish, the fry, that stocked other villages' ponds. In the Pearl River delta that village was
Jiujiang, a township of Nanhai. From the middle of the Ming its men held the fry landings on both banks of the
West River, several dozen of them, paying a levy on each; they netted the wild fry and supplied the delta's other pond
districts. Most of its households lived by the trade and were called fry households: "the men trade fish fry, the women feed
the silkworms".

In the late seventeenth century Jiujiang's land looked like a chessboard, the dikes its lines and the ponds its squares.
The color of a pond's water told what it held: the fry water was turbid, the water of the grown fish clear.

Its fish ponds took about eight tenths of its land, and of their water seven parts in ten raised fry and three grown fish.
Fry ponds were found only there: elsewhere the ponds mostly raised grown fish, and fry tried in them did not thrive. How
big its fry ponds were is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That about one dike-pond hamlet in four is a fry village: no source gives the odds, and fry ponds being found only in Jiujiang makes it the rarer form.
- That the smallest ponds hold the fry: which of Jiujiang's ponds held them is not recorded, so the choice follows a late-Ming method in which fry start in a small pond and move on to larger ones as they grow.

Depiction: The map gives a fry village's smallest ponds to fry until they hold up to seven tenths of its pond water (reading
the seven parts in ten as a share of the ponds' area), and draws them in grayer, muddier water than the clear ponds where the
fish are grown on, so the hamlet reads as a fry village at a glance; the exact shade is a drawing convention.
A fry pond is whatever size its plot is: no small nursery pond is drawn beside a big one, for that was a late-Ming way of inland Jiangxi, away from the delta whose ponds the map draws.
