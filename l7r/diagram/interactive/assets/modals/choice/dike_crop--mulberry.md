About: On a mulberry dike, 桑基 (sangji, "mulberry dike"), the bank heaped up round a fish pond was planted with mulberry and
its leaves stripped to feed silkworms; the silkworms' droppings fed the fish, and the mud dredged from the pond went back up
round the bushes. In the Pearl River delta the mulberry dike took the place of the older fruit dike in the late Ming and early
Qing and was the commonest dike of the Qing. Fish ponds ringed by mulberry were kept in Jiangsu and Zhejiang too.

Mulberry grown for its leaf was mostly kept as low bushes, in one plantation practice pruned once a year to about 5 or 6 ft.
In Jiangsu and Zhejiang it stands in rows along the pond bank, on a low or middling trunk, and in the delta today the leaves
are picked for seven or eight broods of silkworms a year.

How thickly it stood depended on how low it was cut. In a late Qing field in Zhejiang, mid-trunk trees stood about 300 to
the mu, one to about 24 sq ft; the delta's root-cut bushes today stand about one
to the square foot, their crowns touching. Whether a bush stripped for leaf bore fruit is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That about half the dike-pond hamlets plant mulberry: no source counts the dikes of each kind, so mulberry is weighted the commonest because it became the dominant dike of the Qing.
- A bush about 4 to 7 ft across: no source gives the width of a mulberry bush's crown.

Depiction: The map plants every dike of the hamlet with mulberry alone, as rows of round bushes at the late-Qing spacing of
about one to 24 sq ft; the delta's far denser root-cut planting, known only as today's practice, is not drawn. One crop to a hamlet is the
map's choice, so each hamlet reads as one kind of dike-pond village: in the 17th century fruit trees, tea and mulberry stood
on the same villages' dikes. No berries are drawn, and a bush always stands on the bank, its crown free to lean over the water.
