About: In parts of Japan a household once buried its dead on its own land, at the edge of its own fields. In the Tenryu river
basin in Shizuoka and at Otawa in Yamanashi the graves anciently stood on private ground such as the land beside the bunds,
until the Meiji government moved them to common ground; in 1872 a Finance Ministry notice forbade burying the dead at the bund
edge of one's own fields, having heard that it was being done.

A household's own grave served its family, and a branch house might set its grave beside the main house's so that the
kin shared one. In Ibaraki such graves are still often seen
in a corner of a field, as well as in the house garden or before the entrance, and where the house-plot graves of one district
seem to have been moved out, many went into part of the household's own fields or woods within the hamlet. What a grave at a
field's edge looked like is not recorded.

How many households buried in their fields is not recorded, nor how common the practice was when it was forbidden. Until the
Second World War many graves were still built on a corner of a household's own land.

Guesses:
- That about three paddy maps in ten draw a grave in their fields: no source gives a rate.
- That a field grave is in a plot's corner about half the time and an island inside a plot the other half: both are placements of one thing, and no source weighs one against the other.
- The mound's size, about 13 by 9 ft with one or two stones: no size of a field grave was found.

Depiction: The map draws the grave as a small mound with one or two stones in a corner of a paddy plot, standing against its
two bunds and just inside the plot, clear of any ditch or path along its edge. That short step in from the corner is a drawing convention, so the mound reads as standing in the corner; the real graves stood at a field's corner, beside its bunds. The bund is carried round the mound to the
corner, so the flooded basin runs up to it.
