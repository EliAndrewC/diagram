About: A shared well stood by the roadside or among the houses that drew from it, and a well by the roadside had a name of its own, the wayside well (tsuji-ido).

Most wells were shared, because a well cost a great deal to dig; a whole village, or a group of households within one,
drew from a common well, and the wellside was where the neighbors met to draw water, do the washing and talk.

The shaft was
about 3 ft (1 m) across under a curb of wood or stone, the bucket raised on a sweep or, from deep water, on a rope over a
pulley.

How many such wells a hamlet dug by its lanes is not recorded; in one water-short coastal village the wells were dug at
about one to every five to ten houses, a dozen of them shared, and a planned village on a dry upland first dug 11 wells and shared them among some 170 to 180 farms.

Guesses:
- That a hamlet sets its shared wells by the lane, or among the houses, at even odds: no account says how common each was.
- That a village well stood by the lane at all: the wayside well is attested for roadsides in general, not for a village's
  own lanes.
- The verge's width, the curb within about 8 ft of the tread: no source gives one.

Depiction: The map sets each shared well on a lane's verge, its curb clear of the tread, with a house near it. Where no lane
near a well's houses has room beside it - the lanes running between the farmsteads' yards and beds - the well stands in the open
ground among the houses instead. The well is drawn as a marker larger than life, about 19 ft across, so a reader can find
it; a real well's curb was a frame only a few feet across round a shaft about 3 ft (1 m) wide.
