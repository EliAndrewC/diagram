About: In a settlement that kept its manure in pits, each household stored its night soil in an earthenware jar sunk in the
ground until it went out to the fields; human manure was the most important fertilizer a farm had. In one silk village by
Lake Tai in 1936 the jars stood half buried behind the houses, and the public road along the stream was lined with them. In
the 18th century a British visitor saw large earthen vessels buried to the edge in various parts of a farm and by paths and
roads, for a laborer or a passer-by to use. A Chinese farming book of 1313 already has southern households ripening manure in
a brick-lined pit at the head of the field, and in Japan a storage jar for night soil is on record by 1681.

A jar buried to the rim showed only its open mouth at the ground; a half-buried one stood up out of it. A Ming-dynasty farm manual wanted its pits deep and large; a buried
Japanese jar was often about 3 to 5 ft (1 to 1.5 m) across the mouth.

How big a Chinese manure jar was is not recorded. How many households kept their night soil out by the fields varied widely:
in three Japanese villages surveyed in 1959, from 2 households in 83 to 15 in 18.

Guesses:
- That a settlement keeps its manure in sunk jars or in heaps at even odds: both forms are recorded, but no source says how
  many villages used each.
- That between about 2 and 85 households in 100 keep their jar out by a field or a road, sought within about 160 ft of the
  house: the share is the span found for Japan's field pits, carried over to the jar, since the field-head pits of south China were lined with brick,
  not jars, and no account gives how far out a jar stood.
- The jar's mouth on the map, 3.5 ft across: no Chinese manure jar's size was found; it falls within the 3 to 5 ft of a
  buried Japanese jar.

Depiction: The map draws each jar as its mouth seen from above, a dark disc inside a pale rim, flush with the ground, beside
its household's privy or out at the edge of the household's nearest field or by a road. A jar buried to its rim and one half
buried look the same from above, so both are drawn as one mouth.
