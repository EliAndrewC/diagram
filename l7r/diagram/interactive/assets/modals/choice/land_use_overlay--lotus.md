About: A village could grow lotus on its deepest, wettest ground, for its root or its seeds. Lotus grows in standing water
about 1 to 8 ft (0.3 to 2.5 m) deep, and a sixth-century Chinese farm manual plants it in the mud of a fish pond. In the Ming
and Qing, Wu county was the chief lotus-root district round Suzhou, and in Qing Hunan, seed lotus being dear, farmers gave up
rice fields to grow it. In Japan the lotus districts on record are late: at Iwakuni lotus is said to have begun in 1796 on
reclaimed land fit for no other crop, and the low wetland villages of Kadoma, near Osaka, grew it in the late Edo period.

A lotus field was standing water under a cover of broad leaves. Where the roots were dug in July, rice could be planted after
them in the same field.

How much of a village lotus took before modern times is not recorded. An ordinary village probably grew it in a few deep
plots, and only a lotus district gave up much of its rice to it.

Guesses:
- How much of a village's field lotus took: no source gives a share for an ordinary village in any period, and the nearest
  estimate, a few percent to about 10 or 15 percent of a lotus-growing village's field, is reckoned from a national average.
- How often a settlement grows this crop rather than ponds, tea or none: no source counts them, so the choices come up evenly
  where the ground allows them.

Depiction: The map draws lotus in standing water on low ground only, on some of the plots of the two lowest rows of the
valley floor, the rest left in rice. The share it takes is drawn larger than the record supports, so that the lotus shows,
nearer a village in a lotus district than an ordinary one. Lotus grown in turn with rice, a household's choice from year to
year, is not drawn.
