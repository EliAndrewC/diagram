About: A farm's grove on two sides stood on the windward pair of faces and left the others open. A homestead grove, the
yashikirin, was first a windbreak, so it stood on the side the wind came from. On the Sendai plain, where the winter wind
blows mostly from the north-northwest, often and hard, the groves stood on the north or west of the house and often left the
south or east open; when the plain's new fields were settled, the domain's first lord urged farmers to plant trees in many
layers on the north and west against the cold wind off the mountains. On the Izumo plain the ring of pines round each house
gave way after 1868 to a hook covering only the north and west.

So a farm stood open on its lee sides, its trees massed in layers behind it. Where the damaging wind came from elsewhere the
pair turned with it: in Okinawa the big trees stood mostly to the north of the house, and next to the east.

Three Sendai homesteads counted in modern times held 356, 122 and 126 trees, bamboo apart. In one register of 1684, three
households' woods covered about 6,000, 10,700 and 28,000 sq ft. How many farms took the two-sided form is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That a settlement's groves take two sides half the time, three sides three times in ten and all four twice in ten, and
  four sides more often on flood-prone ground: no source counts farms by the shape of their grove.
- How deep the windward stand runs, about one and a half house depths: no source gives a grove's depth, so it is reckoned
  from tree counts and crown sizes.

Depiction: Every farm in the settlement takes the same shape of grove, since the shape went by region. The map draws the
windward pair as a deep stand, tree by tree, every crown at its real size, keeps its trees out of the yard's and the kitchen
garden's sun, and leaves the other two sides open.
