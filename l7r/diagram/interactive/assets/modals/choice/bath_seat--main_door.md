About: A farm's bath, where it had one, was a small room of the main house where the household bathed in hot water, and in
some villages it stood under the eaves beside the house's main door. It was a luxury, since hot water at home meant a well,
fuel and a risk of fire: a farm manual of about 1680 has the household wash instead over a pit laid with bamboo slats, the
water collecting to thin the manure. Around 1770, when a bath first appears in village house plans, the room stood beside
the main door at four houses in one village and six in another, and beyond the stable at one house in a third.

The room was small, about half a tsubo (some 18 sq ft) around 1770, and 1 to 2 tsubo, about 6 by 6 to 6 by 12 ft, by the
1820s. What its walls and floor were like is not recorded.

Only a very few houses had a bath in 1770 and 1771; by 1824 and 1842 two or three houses in ten did, and by then most baths
stood out beyond the house's stable wing rather than by its door. No farm bath is known to have stood as a building of
its own before 1868.

Depiction: The map draws a bath room, at the two or three homesteads in ten that keep one, as a small room joined to the
farmhouse beside its main door; never as a shed standing
apart in the yard. A tub is drawn inside it so the room reads as a bath from above; the real tub stood under the house's
roof, unseen.
