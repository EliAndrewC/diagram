About: Apart from its houses, a farming settlement could keep a thicket of bamboo of its own, the take-yabu. In the Edo
period the villages of one district southwest of Kyoto owed bamboo as a tax and managed their own groves, some farmers
keeping bamboo shops on the side, and round Kyoto in the early Edo period settlements stood ringed by bamboo. A
thicket kept as a resource was cut in moderation and renewed itself from its own shoots. Bamboo grew only where the winters
were not too hard, and it wanted high, dry, level ground: it died in a low field once water reached it.

A thicket was dense and nearly all of one kind, its stems clones of one plant spreading by shoots from an underground
rhizome, and it shaded out almost everything beneath it. Madake, the common timber bamboo, grew a culm about 66 ft (20 m)
tall and at most about 4 in (10 cm) across.

Who held a village's thicket, and how large it was, is not recorded.

Guesses:
- A thicket of about 84 by 58 ft, a working stand cut and left to regrow: no source gives one's size.
- That the thicket stands on dry ground just beyond the back row of houses, and is held in common: an early-Edo screen paints
  settlements ringed by bamboo and an old Chinese farm manual wants it on dry ground, but no source says which side or who
  held it.
- How often a settlement's bamboo stands in a thicket rather than at its farmsteads, both or nowhere: no source counts them.

Depiction: A bamboo culm is far too slim to see at the map's scale, so the map draws the thicket as an area, its place and
size to scale, filled with paired culm strokes and a leafy fork in jade green over a light
jade wash - a mark taken from modern map legends, which stands for the bamboo and does not count it. It stands on dry
ground behind the houses, never on a paddy, a marsh or a pond, and clear of the houses, yards, gardens and lanes. A stand too narrow to read at the
map's full view is left out.
