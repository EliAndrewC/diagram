About: On unsettled land a village left some of its paddy to rest each year. In the late Heian period and the early medieval
age of great land clearance, much farmland was unstable for want of irrigation water or soil, and each year the
plots to work were chosen anew from the cropped and the resting ground, to let the soil recover and to fit the planting
to the water to hand. A plot left out of crop for a year was a kataarashi; it may have rested every other year, or only
now and then. In Rokugan a paddy once made can be cropped for centuries without tiring its soil, so a
plot rests, if at all, for want of water.

The resting plots lay mixed in among the worked plots, not in a block of their own. Nothing marked one off, and while it
rested it was common grazing for the village's cattle and horses. Resting ground was not abandoned ground: paddy lost
for good to poor soil, a flood or failed water works was another thing.

In the late Heian period only about three plots of paddy in five were worked on average. In 1102 the Oyama estate in
Tanba province worked about 110 of its 220 acres of paddy; about 64 acres rested that year, and the rest had gone
unworked for many years.

Guesses:
- That about one settlement in five takes this form: the settled form is taken as the commoner, but no figure weighs the
  two.
- That two to four whole plots rest in each field: the one estate measured rested nearly a third of its paddy, an upper
  bound rather than a hamlet's norm.
- That the resting plots lie toward the far end of the water's run: read from "short water", since no source says which
  plots of a village's paddy rested.

Depiction: The map draws each resting plot as a whole basin of grass with tufts inside its own banks, never a patch
within a basin, and never two resting plots side by side, so they read as scattered among the cropped ground.
