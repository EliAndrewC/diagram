About: A farm's grove on three sides closed every side of the house but its front. This was the form of the Tonami plain,
where more than 7,000 scattered farmhouses each stand in a grove, there called the kainyo, that kept off the cold winds and
snowstorms of winter and the strong south wind of early spring. The east was the front of the house, with its entrance and its garden, and there the grove was thin.

Tall trees, mainly cedar with oak and zelkova, stood from the south face of the house round to the west; hackberry and alder,
with a little bamboo, from the west round to the north; and on the open east front, flowering trees, persimmon and fig. The
trees ran 36 to 92 ft (11 to 28 m) tall, about 49 ft (15 m) on average, over an understory of saplings and shrubs, and every
homestead kept a stand of bamboo. The trees were planted in rows, thick enough to hide the house.

In 1987 the groves of Kashima, a hamlet of 46 households, held 1,542 trees at least 4 in (10 cm) thick, about 33 a household,
nearly half of them cedar. The plain's farmhouses stood in their groves by the 1650s, but when the groves took this shape is not recorded, nor how many farms elsewhere took three sides.

Guesses:
- That a settlement's groves take three sides three times in ten, two sides half the time and all four twice in ten: no
  source counts farms by the shape of their grove.
- That the third side's band beside the house closes only the house's own side, leaving the yard and garden in the sun, and
  is one tree deep: no source says how far it ran or how deep it was.
- How deep the windward stand runs, about one and a half house depths: no source gives how deep a grove stood beside its house (the one old measure gives whole homestead woods' sides and area, not where the house stood in them), so it is reckoned
  from tree counts and crown sizes.

Depiction: Every farm in the settlement takes the same shape of grove, since the shape went by region. The map draws each tree
at its real size, the windward sides as a deep stand and the third side as a thinner band of lesser trees, one tree deep,
leaving open the front away from the wind, where the yard and the way in are; on the Tonami plain that front held a thin planting of flowering and fruit trees - the map plants none there, and a farm
that dries its grain in its front yard keeps its persimmon behind the house. No tree stands in the yard's or the kitchen
garden's sun. Only the crowns are drawn: no tree is drawn under another's crown, so the understory of saplings and shrubs
does not show. Where a farm keeps bamboo, it is drawn as a patch of paired culm marks in jade green on the house side of each
windward stand, a mark standing for culms too small to see; under the crowns it is hidden.
