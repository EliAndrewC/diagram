About: A farm's grove on three sides closed every side of the house but its front. This was the form of the Tonami plain,
where more than 7,000 scattered farmhouses each stand in a grove, there called the kainyo, that kept off the cold winds and
snowstorms of winter. The east was the front of the house, with its entrance and its garden, and there the grove was thin.

Tall trees, mainly cedar with oak and zelkova, stood from the south face of the house round to the west; hackberry and alder,
with a little bamboo, from the west round to the north; and on the open east front, flowering trees, persimmon and fig. The
trees ran 36 to 92 ft (11 to 28 m) tall, about 49 ft (15 m) on average, over an understory of saplings and shrubs, and every
homestead kept a stand of bamboo.

A 1987 survey of Kashima, a hamlet of 46 households, counted 1,542 trees at least 4 in (10 cm) thick, about 33 a household,
nearly half of them cedar. When this planting began is not recorded, nor how many farms elsewhere took three sides.

Guesses:
- That a settlement's groves take three sides three times in ten, two sides half the time and all four twice in ten: no
  source counts farms by the shape of their grove.
- That the third side's band beside the house closes only the house's own side, leaving the yard and garden in the sun, and
  is one tree deep: no source says how far it ran or how deep it was.
- How deep the windward stand runs, about one and a half house depths: no source gives a grove's depth, so it is reckoned
  from tree counts and crown sizes.

Depiction: Every farm in the settlement takes the same shape of grove, since the shape went by region. The map draws each tree
at its real size, the windward sides as a deep stand and the third side as a thinner band of lesser trees, one tree deep,
leaving open the front away from the wind, where the yard and the way in are, as on the Tonami plan; no tree stands in the
yard's or the kitchen garden's sun.
