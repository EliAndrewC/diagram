About: A dike-pond was a fish pond ringed by a dike planted with mulberry, kept as low bushes: the leaves fed silkworms,
the silkworms' droppings fed the fish, and the mud dredged from the pond was banked back up round the mulberry. The fish were carp, bought as fry netted wild in the rivers. Ponds were dug out of low ground that
flooded, their mud heaped into the dike, in the Pearl River delta and in Jiangsu and Zhejiang.
In the delta they arose in the early Ming and reached their height at the end of the Qing. Most of the time ponds took only
part of a village's rice land, but in the 18th century, at Longshan, "outside the dwellings all was pond", three-quarters of the farmland.

The ponds lay as a mosaic, mostly narrow rectangles side by side, with squares and odd shapes pressed in to fit the ground.
They lay among the creeks and canals of the polder around them, watered and drained through sluice gates in the polder's own
dike. Modern ponds are about 1 to 1.5 acres (0.4 to 0.6 ha) and 7 to 10 ft deep, their dikes 20 to 33 ft wide.

In 1678, early in the Qing, the fish took half of one delta township's
land, and its ponds eight tenths. Water and dike were kept in proportion, so the mulberry could feed the
fish beside it: in modern times about six parts water to four of dike, or the reverse.

Guesses:
- That a village takes each of the five recorded splits of water and dike at even odds, one split for all its ponds: no
  account gives a share between them, and every account found that writes the split as a ratio is modern.
- A pond's bank as wide as the village's split leaves it, from about 15 ft to about 42 ft: no width of a pond dike before
  modern times was found, and the widest banks are broader than any modern figure but one village's former dikes.
- [dike_crop=mulberry] A mulberry bush about 4 to 7 ft across: no source gives a bush's crown width.

Depiction: The map draws the block as a polder converted nearly or wholly wall to wall into ponds. The ponds
are four-sided, set out for each settlement either as a mosaic, the lattice of canals bent out of line so the ponds skew to uneven shapes, or as a straight chessboard of ponds.
Each pond is ringed by a bank of mottled earth planted with one crop for the whole
settlement, mulberry, fruit trees or tea; the title card says which. A village's dikes could carry these crops side by side, but the map gives each settlement one, so it reads as one kind of dike-pond village. The sluices drawn are the polder's own, in its outer dike, not one in every pond,
and that outer dike is drawn as any polder's is. It is kept broader than the pond banks so it reads as the greater bank, the one holding the water off the whole block; how broad a village's dike was before modern times is not recorded. Its rows of willow and mulberry are set on its faces, so they read as its own planting, where a Ming river manual set such rows on the ground off its foot. A single canal runs round the inside of its foot: canals ran inside a polder's dike, but the one ring at its foot is the map's convention.
