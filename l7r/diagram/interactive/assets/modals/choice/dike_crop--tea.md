About: Tea was one of the plantings on the pond dikes of the Pearl River delta. Late in the 17th century a writer on Guangdong found the
villages of Guangzhou's large counties giving up good fields to make dikes and planting them with fruit trees, lychee most and
tea and mulberry next, so tea stood second on the dikes beside the mulberry.

How tea stood on a pond dike is not recorded. Elsewhere it was grown as rows of bushes: in north China in the 1840s tea grew
on the lower, fertile hillsides in rows about 4 ft apart, in small gardens of one to five acres, and in Japan it was often
planted as a single row along the banks of paddies and dry fields, where it marked the boundary with the neighbor, broke the
wind and held the soil.

How many dikes carried tea, or how much a household picked from them, is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That about one dike-pond hamlet in six plants tea: no source counts the dikes of each kind, and tea is weighted least because it is only named in a list.
- Two clipped hedges along each bank, each about 3 ft wide and broken every 7.5 ft or so: drawn from the tea bush's habit, since no source says how tea stood on a dike.

Depiction: The map plants every dike of the hamlet with tea alone, as two clipped hedges along each bank, broken at intervals
so they read as bushes set end to end rather than one solid band. One crop to a hamlet is the map's choice, so each hamlet reads
as one kind of dike-pond village: in the 17th century fruit trees, tea and mulberry stood on the same villages' dikes.
