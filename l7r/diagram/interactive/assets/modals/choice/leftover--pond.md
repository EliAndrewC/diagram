About: Where a district of the Pearl River delta went over to dike-ponds, fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes, the ponds could
take most of its ground. In the 18th century fields were abandoned to build them, and at Longshan "outside the dwellings all
was pond": the dike-ponds reached 30,000 mu (about 5,000 acres), three-quarters of the cultivated land. At Longjiang and
Longshan every pond was a mulberry pond, and in the Qing the farmers of Nanhai and Shunde were said to have "abandoned rice to
plant mulberry".

Such a place was pond after pond, each ringed by its planted dike, the dikes running up to the houses and the creeks and
canals of the polder winding between them.

The highest shares on record are a township's: three-quarters of Longshan's cultivated land, and in 1678 eight tenths of the
land of Jiujiang, another pond township. Whether any village gave up its rice entirely is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That every parcel of the block is a pond: the highest shares recorded are three-quarters and eight tenths of a township's land, so a block with no rice at all goes beyond them.
- That a wholesale dike-pond hamlet keeps no rice about half the time: no source counts the hamlets of either kind.

Depiction: The map lays the whole block out as dike-ponds and converts every parcel to a pond, so no paddy is left among them.
Each farmstead still keeps a yard before its house, drawn the same as a rice hamlet's threshing yard; with no rice to thresh,
what it was used for is a guess, most likely a work yard for sorting cocoons and handling leaves.
