About: A farming household could draw its water from a well of its own. Most wells in premodern Japan were shared by a
village or a group of households, because a well cost a great deal to dig, but a well might belong to one household, most
probably where the water lay close under the ground. In a scattered village each farmhouse stood apart in the middle of its
own fields. On the Tonami
plain, where the water table lay deep and wells were hard to dig, many areas led a channel into each house's grounds instead;
how the other areas drew their water is not recorded.

A well was a shaft dug by hand about 3 ft (1 m) across, with a frame of wood or stone round its mouth, the curb, about 4 ft
square on a surviving example. Where the water stood shallow the bucket came up on a sweep, a pole worked like a seesaw
over a post with a weight at its butt; where it lay deep, on a rope over a pulley hung from a frame. Some wells had a roof on
posts over them.

How many scattered farms kept a well of their own is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That the farms the channel did not serve dug a well of their own, and that a scattered settlement takes wells or channels
  at even odds: no account says how the other areas drew their water, or how many used each.
- Where on its lot the well stands: no account places a scattered farm's well.

Depiction: The map draws each farm's well in its own dooryard, inside its lot, near its work yard and off its way in, as a
marker larger than life so a reader can find it: about 19 ft across, where the real curb was about 4 ft square.
