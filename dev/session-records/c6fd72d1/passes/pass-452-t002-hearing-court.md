# Pass 452 / T002 - was a hearing court screened from the yard, and could lodgings face it?

QUESTION=none. China first (the county yamen's great-hall court), Japan as tiebreaker (the shirasu). Read-only pass.

## What the record already says (built on, not repeated)

- **0099 (the hearing court)** does not mention screening. It makes the shirasu a roofed court, or an earth floor inside the building, against the magistrate's hall. At Edo (plans of 1841/1842) it puts a long waiting strip (公事人溜) beside the gravel court, inside the compound. The parties' koshikake stands by or outside the main gate. No fence, wall, gate or screen around the court is named, and nothing on whether a hearing could be watched.
- **0097 (rowhouses)** and **0331 (where staff lived)** do not mention the court at all. They cover rowhouse size and build, and whether staff lived on the grounds (Sakai: retainers' rowhouses beside the magistrate's residence; doshin row on the lot adjoining to the north).
- Also already in the record: 0096 (Neixiang: past the main gate come the guest lodge, temple, runners' quarters and prison, and only past the ceremonial gate the great hall; Pingyao's west "ghost door"); 0163 (the six offices stand between the ceremonial gate and the great hall, east and west); 0104 (Japanese offices: the entrance faced the gate and the shirasu lay to one side; Edo North 1841: the shirasu in the area left of the front entrance).

## Reading, by numbered point

**(1) What enclosed the court. China: ATTESTED. Japan: ATTESTED ONLY FOR enclosure by the building and the compound; no fence, screen or palisade round a shirasu found.**
The great-hall court of a Qing county yamen was a courtyard of its own. On the south the ceremonial gate (儀門) closed it off, its middle leaves normally shut, with side doors for daily traffic (C4, C8, C9, C10; Pingyao's gate dates from 1619, Huozhou's is a Ming building). The six offices' side ranges walled it on the east and west (C4, C5 Neixiang 1666, C6 Jiexiu 1677 「堂下東西各列廊房」 and Ming Pingyao, C7 Yuanmou and Wuding, C8 Pingyao 1619, Ye county in 卷451 「堂前左右為六房」), and the hall itself stood on the north. The admonition arch or pavilion stood in the walk (甬道) between gate and hall (C3 Neixiang, 6.35 m wide; C6, C7, C11 Huozhou, 10 m north of the gate), with a terrace (月台) before the hall (C8, C11, C12). The whole stood inside the yamen's high wall (C10, C13). In Japan, the record already has the shirasu roofed or as an indoor earth floor (0099). At Takayama the civil court is an earth-floored room facing the goyoba (J4), so walls bound it. At Edo (1841) the gravel court is enclosed on all four sides inside a block of the building: hearing rooms on the west, rooms labeled 白洲 on the north and south, the parties' waiting strip on the east (J7). So it was closed to the yard and the gate by the building itself, not by a fence. No premodern source read names a 塀, 板塀, 柵 or 矢来 enclosing a shirasu, or a 白洲口 or 公事人門. The one 塀 at a shirasu is in the modern reconstruction of the Nagasaki magistracy's Tateyama office, which says it followed period drawings (J8). That is a lead, not evidence; the drawings are not cited. At Edo South, the rowhouses stood on the west of the site and the shirasu on the left of the entrance (J9). The other walls in the Japanese sources enclose a whole compound (J6) or a whole office.

**(2) Was a hearing open to view? China: ATTESTED (open, for great-hall hearings). Japan: NOT ATTESTED (closed).**
China: a magistrate who heard a case in the great hall had several hundred people standing below the hall to watch. Wang Huizu (C1, Ningyuan, c. 1786-90) urges it for its effect on onlookers and contrasts it with the inner office, where only the two parties were present. Huc found the court before a county hall at Guangji full of people watching a criminal trial with torture (C2, c. 1846). A late-19th-century album caption has the court crowded with runners and coolies (C15). Modern Neixiang accounts say the ceremonial gate was opened for public trials of major cases "to let the people come in by the middle gate" (C4), and that magistrates "welcomed the people to sit in" (C3). So the court was closed to the yard by a gate that was normally shut, but the side door was open to ordinary people (C8: 「一般人两门皆可出入」), and the hall court was where hearings were meant to be seen. The limits: idlers were barred at the main gate (C10); confidential cases were heard in the second or third hall or the inner office (C4, C9, C1); and at a circuit yamen in 1873-74 the crowds waited outside (C16). Japan: Tani (J1, 2008, on the shogunate's courts) says shirasu hearings and criminal examination were not public, "not a show", and only visiting officials sat in at the Hyojosho. One unsourced blog (J2) says the opposite and is contradicted by the plans in the record. The tiebreaker is not needed here, because China is attested. For a Japanese-style drawing, though, the Japanese evidence points to a closed court.

**(3) What faced the court, and could a dwelling open onto it? China: ATTESTED for the clerks' offices on both sides, and for runners', gatekeepers' and jailers' rooms (皂隸房, 班房, 快手房) on the hall court or by its gate. Whether those rooms were slept in is not stated. The clerks' lodgings (吏舍, 吏廨, 公廨), where named, stood in a side line or behind the office ranges, not opening onto the court. Japan: ATTESTED ONLY FOR offices and waiting rooms round the court; the only dwelling yards with a court in them are an official's own residence.**
China: the six offices lined both sides, sometimes with the constables', purchasers' and lamp-men's rooms in the same ranges (C6 Jiexiu). The gatekeepers', runners' and jailers' rooms stood just inside the ceremonial gate, on the west (C6), or the runners' room stood with the six offices (C6 Pingyao 1709, 「又修六房、班房」; C7 Yuanmou, twelve bays of 班房 inside the main gate). The tax-service rooms, guest lodge and jail stood outside the ceremonial gate (C7, C8). Staunton's hall stood "at the end of a paved court, surrounded by galleries" (C14). Living quarters lay behind the hall (前衙後邸; C12 has the family's flower-hall court beside the third hall). At Neixiang the clerks' lodgings (吏舍) are listed apart from the six offices and stood "before the registrar's office", with the jail before them (C5, Kangxi). The modern layout puts them in the west line, with the refectory, prison and registrar's office, not on the axis (C18, C11). At Ming Pingyao the office quarters (公廨) were "behind the three western offices" (C6, C19), and at Xiaoyi the clerks' quarters (吏廨) were "west of the hall" (C6). In both, a range of offices stands between the quarters and the court. Lin Qian (C17) has clerks left with "nowhere to shelter" when the six offices fell down in the early Qianlong reign. That may mean some clerks slept in the office ranges facing the court, but the text does not say so. No source read puts a dwelling's door onto the hall court. Japan: the civil court at Takayama faced the locally hired officials' office, with status-divided duty rooms, each with its own entrance, beside it (J4). At Edo (1841) the court's quarter held the judgment room, the examination rooms, clerks' duty rooms, the waiting place, the koshikake, the jail doshin's room and the temporary cells. The retainers' rowhouses stood at the far, north-west corner (J3, J7), and the court's block had no dwelling against it (J7). The only "dwelling" cases: the arson-and-robbery commissioner held court in the garden of his own residence (J5), and Danzaemon's shirasu lay south of his own dwelling, beside a jail whose keeper lived there (J6). In both it is the presiding official's own house, not junior retainers' lodgings. No source read puts junior staff's lodgings opening onto a court; the sources that bear put offices, waiting places and cells round it and the staff's dwellings elsewhere. Whether that rules on the drawn example is for the record's checks.

**(4) Measured or surviving examples with plans. ATTESTED ONLY FOR the Chinese survivals' layout and a few measures; the Japanese plans asked for could not be read here.**
Neixiang (rebuilt from 1892, museum since the 1980s): hall 27.22 x 12.37 m, admonition arch 5.2 m high x 6.35 m wide (C3); a 1666 layout in the gazetteer (C5). Pingyao: compound 203 x 131 m; the 1882 gazetteer plan (县治图) draws 5 tax-service bays a side outside the ceremonial gate, where the modern restoration built 6 (C8, C9). Huozhou: admonition pavilion 10 m north of the ceremonial gate (C11). Zhili governor-general: terrace 13 m square (C11). Ye county: zh.wikipedia 叶县县衙 (archive 5f/5f0caee6cf6c) gives only its dating (begun 1396; only the five-bay great hall survived the late-Ming wars, the rest is Qing). Its Kangxi-era entry in 古今圖書集成 職方典 卷451 (archive 78/7891046d8ec3, offset ~11369) gives the layout: 「葉縣治　在城內東街，大堂五楹。堂前左右為六房，為承發司、長庫，樓前為儀門。儀門外東為寅賓館，西為土地祠，又前為大門。……門內西為獄，大堂後有穿，堂後為縣廨，有大樓五楹。」 (translation) "Ye county office, on East Street in the city: a great hall of five bays. Before the hall, left and right, are the six offices, the dispatch office and the long store; in front of the tower is the ceremonial gate. Outside the ceremonial gate the guest lodge on the east, the earth-god shrine on the west, and further in front the main gate ... inside the gate on the west the jail; behind the great hall a passage, and behind the hall the county residence (縣廨), with a great tower of five bays." Edo North 1841: read (J7). The court is enclosed inside the building block, with the waiting strip on its east and the cells and the jail doshin's room across an open yard. The plan gives no measures. Takayama's 1851 plan (嘉永4年 御陣屋絵図, city of Takayama PDF) was read as an image, but its hand-written labels are illegible at the file's resolution, so neither court nor its bounds could be identified. The guide (J4) gives the rooms in words only. Edo South 1842: not re-read in this pass (0099 read its labels). Nirayama, Hita, Kurashiki: no page read describes a shirasu or its enclosure (Hita's 1825/1830 drawings exist, per a Rotary talk summary that does not describe them). Kusatsu honjin: no page read mentions a shirasu. SILENT for those.
