## Sources that give the thing asked

**A. ja.wikipedia 扇状地 (archived 4b/4b329c90c108; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/扇状地).** A modern encyclopedia.
> 伏流水は扇端部で湧水として現れ、その先に小河川を作ることが多い。
(translation) The underground water comes out as springs at the fan's toe and often forms small streams beyond it.
> 扇端部 湧水帯をなし水を得やすいため、古くから集落や農耕地（アジアでは水田）が立地する事が多い。
(translation) Fan toe: it forms a spring belt and water is easy to get, so settlements and farmland (in Asia, paddy) have often stood there since old times.
> 扇状地の特性として、扇端部付近以外では地下水位が深く
(translation) A trait of fans is that the water table is deep everywhere except near the toe.

**B. MAFF 土地改良事業計画設計基準 (水田), Table 7.2 (archived 51/51704ce60ec8; https://www.maff.go.jp/j/nousin/noukan/tyotei/kizyun/attach/pdf/nougyouyousui_suiden-1.pdf).** A modern standard. The quote is reassembled from a two-column old/new table with split cells.

(translation) Valley-floor plain: water table high throughout ... the part touching the hill foot or a terrace tends to be under artesian pressure.

## Reading

**Point 1, the fan: ATTESTED ONLY FOR the toe and front margin. NOT ATTESTED for a lateral seepage belt.**
- **Toe.** China (C: the Yongding fan, Northern Wei to Qing) and Japan (A, D, E) agree that a fan's springs and wetland lie at its toe and front margin, and that the lakes form in the fan-margin depressions at its front.
- **Sides.** Nothing found puts seepage or marsh along a fan's lateral margins. A says the water table is deep everywhere except near the toe, which points against it.
- **What was found on the sides.** F and G class the fan's sides (扇側部) with the toe as the watered periphery cleared first for paddy, on very large fans. They do not say seepage or wetland, and they show farmed ground, not avoided ground.
- **The rest.** The inter-fan depression (C) is only named, and Woods (H) says "perimeter" while describing a wetland at the base and toe.
- **Building.** SILENT for the sides.

**Point 2, the narrowing side: ATTESTED ONLY FOR a valley floor whose fields run against a hill or terrace foot. Japan only; China has only hill-foot springs (M) and cold spring-soaked hill fields (N), with no side position.**
- **Japan.** Seepage comes out where the valley slope meets the floor (I, B, J). At Sakaori it comes out along the steep side only, not the gentle one (L).
- **Dates.** The practice is described for the Edo period (L) and up to the 1950s (I), but every source describing it is modern.
- **What it makes.** Wet, cold paddy farmed up to the hill foot, with a warming catch channel along that foot (J, L). The one source naming a wetland type there names a spring wetland at the slope foot (I). None of these is a reed marsh lying outside the fields.
- **Building.** No source says hamlets kept off that strip. The one passage on it (O) has villages lined along the hill-foot spring line, so the houses stand at the line, not away from it.

**Point 3, the block `toe-on-tapering-flank`: on a fan, it stays a DEVIATION.**
- **On a fan.** The evidence puts the wet ground at the toe and front margin, which favours stopping the marsh at the fields' lower corners. The fan's sides were favoured farmland (F, G).
- **Along a valley wall.** If the record rules that the narrowing side is a valley wall or hill foot, a wet side is attested in kind, and at Sakaori, the one case found, only along the steeper wall (L). Even then the sources show cold wet paddy with a catch channel at the hill foot, not reeds outside the fields.

Suggestions for the record:
- Keep the DEVIATION label for fans.
- Make the data-guess say that the sources put a fan's springs at its toe, and its sides among its early-cleared farmland.
- Add the valley-wall finding (I, J, L, with B) as the one case where a wet side is attested. Whether the drawing's narrowing side is that case is for the source-applicability check.
