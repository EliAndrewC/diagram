# Compound programs

*Part of the Mode A compound/building docs - the scale, vocabulary, composition rules and checklist live in [`buildings.md`](buildings.md).*

**Load this file when:** you are drawing a compound whose TYPE has a documented program - today the county magistrate's manor and the country shrine. A building type with no program yet is drawn from the vocabulary and the composition rules, and gets its program here before its exemplar lands (`docs/buildings.md`, "Adding a building type").

---

## Compound programs

Per-building-type specs: the required program every instance shares, the knobs that make instances differ, and staffing/sizing anchors. The research reasoning behind each program lives on [`research/contents.json#compounds`](../research/contents.json#compounds) (the magistracy) and [`research/contents.json#religion-and-the-dead`](../research/contents.json#religion-and-the-dead) (the country shrine). (Planned next entries: samurai country estate, governor's compound, castle keep.)

**A type is declared once.** Each program's REQUIRED ITEMS - the label a check finds each by, the size band it is held to, the class of that band and the finding it rests on - are the declaration in [`l7r/diagram/buildings/types.json`](../l7r/diagram/buildings/types.json), and the table under each heading below is RENDERED from it by `make building-programs` (`CHECK=1` fails the gate when it is stale). Edit the declaration, never the table. The prose around a table - the composition rules, the knobs, the anchors, the staffing - is hand-written here and is what the reviewers read beside the table; the checks read the declaration (`tools/pack_audit/registry.py`, the sweep in `tests/test_mode_a_sheets.py`).

The seat of a County Magistrate (Rank 5): a walled compound in the county town combining court, tax office, granary, garrison, and the magistrate's household. [`pool/magistracies/ochiba-magistracy/ochiba-magistracy.svg`](../pool/magistracies/ochiba-magistracy/ochiba-magistracy.svg) is the worked example - it is this generic program plus Ochiba's particulars (the hall-scale two-altar Inari shrine, the Akami-fude and Fox-Fire Lantern relics, the cinnabar workshop colonnade, the Pact-Bowl threshold stone). A new magistracy should read as the same institution while sharing none of those particulars. [`pool/magistracies/hayakawa-magistracy/hayakawa-magistracy.svg`](../pool/magistracies/hayakawa-magistracy/hayakawa-magistracy.svg) is the validation instance: the same program with every knob set differently (rich river-landing county, staff-housing option (c), guest-wing annex, two modest shrines, cell by the gatehouse).

**Composition rules** (every county magistracy; the items themselves are the table below):

- **Realistic unless the GM says otherwise** (the GM, 2026-09-28: *"You should definitely draw with the things that your sources most safely support. Not just in this case, but in general"*): the buildings' shapes and how things sit within the compound follow the historical norm, in the narrowest reading the sources support - anything beyond it is labeled a guess. A sheet departs from the norm only where the GM has called for it (the vermilion threshold stones are the setting's invention; the buildings and their layout are not). A review rule never pushes a drawing past the record: where the record's form and a rule disagree, the rule is changed to admit the form.

- **Walled enclosure** with a formal main gate + gatehouse and at least one lesser side/rear door. One lesser door must serve the INNER court directly (a kitchen/service postern for deliveries and night-soil), so household traffic never crosses the hearing court; a busy OUTER court (stables, cell, granary) warrants a small service gate of its own too, so muck, night-soil, and prisoner transfers skip the formal gate. After placing buildings, walk the cart route from the gates to the granary and stables in your head - a route every diagram must actually have (a round-2 review caught a gatehouse-and-hearing-court squeeze that cut the granary off from the main gate). Walls mark jurisdiction more than they defend: most impressive on the public approach, plainer at the rear.
- **Guest doors feed courts, not flanks.** Every entrance intended for GUESTS opens into something suitable for receiving them - a court, a garden, a graveled forecourt - never directly into (or against the flank of) a building. Service doors deliberately do the opposite: the kitchen postern opens straight into work space. When adding any door, ask who comes through it and what they step into.
- **Two-court zoning**: public/administrative outer court at the gate; private inner court behind (residence, garden, shrine). The internal divider wall has its own gate - a household door (~24 px / 8 ft, a real nakamon, visibly narrower than the ceremonial main gate), customarily on the main axis directly BEHIND the office hall, which is deliberate: the hall itself is the privacy baffle. Formal visitors are received in the hall, and their way to the residence runs on through the office (research/questions/0104-the-formal-entrance-and-a-guests-arrival-genkan.drawing.html), never through the divider gate; the divider gate serves household traffic (the state/home hinge), and heavy service uses the kitchen postern, so the narrow alley behind the hall is a feature, not a defect. Checked: `two_court_zoning`.
- **The office hall is the compound's working heart**: rear day rooms (day office for tax and case business, plus the magistrate's official study), with the dais band on the south face - dais and flanking clerk positions overlooking the **roofed hearing court** with kneeling positions; its floor is a knob, white gravel or the local river cobbles, each plan taking one (research/questions/0099-the-hearing-court-shirasu.drawing.html: white gravel at the magistracies, river cobbles at Takayama), written in the notes. The residence's "private study" is for the household side of life; official business happens here. The clerks' duty room is a workroom for 3-4 heimen clerks who live in the county town and commute - a room of the hall on the drawn sheets, never clerk housing.
- **The residence** has rank-graded quarters and NO genkan of its own: where office and residence share a compound, the one formal genkan is drawn on the OFFICE hall and the visitors' way runs on through the office to the house (research/questions/0104-the-formal-entrance-and-a-guests-arrival-genkan.drawing.html; or no genkan at all, the roji form - buildings.md, 'Entry porch'); the family block and any detached dwelling (karo's house, guest house) reach the court by an informal door - no sealed boxes. **A rear service strip** behind it (the shady north side): the servants' nagaya plus utility (the household's storehouses, the attached family privy), a named rear yard of open working ground with the storehouses beyond it (the Higuchi house, research buildings 230), OR a narrow service alley - never a wide UNNAMED empty band (see Latrines and the rear service strip). **The kitchen garden** is two knobs (research/questions/0109-vegetable-gardens-at-a-samurai-residence-saien.drawing.html): its SITE among four - west of the residence, south beside the formal garden, in the rear service ground (on a Chinese list alone), or on a parcel of its own - any site ruled out where a full-sun bed gets under six hours of direct autumn sun (research/questions/0038-sunlight-and-shade-on-the-farm.drawing.html), and its SIZE between a soup-greens plot of about 1,100 sq ft and a field over about half the grounds; a hand-drawn plan takes one of each and records them in its notes.
- **The shrine is universal equipment**, Inari by default. Scale and dedication are the per-manor particular; Ochiba's full two-altar hall is the exception, justified by its priest-magistrate, not the norm - and even it stays subordinate in footprint to the residence (the hall-shrine ceiling, the `shrine` band below).
- **Wells** are distributed by use (kitchen, garden, stables); **latrines** roughly one per functional zone, the residence privy attached to the house, the rest against service walls near a gate; **fire-water tubs** (~8-12) at the fire-prone wooden buildings, weighted to the kitchen (2), none at the plaster kura (Fire discipline).
- **The notice board** just outside the main gate is the BENCH'S board (verdicts, edicts, bounties - the court's output, read by those who come to it); the settlement's own kosatsuba is a DIFFERENT board on the town map, posting the state's standing law at the busiest public point (`research/contents.json#trades-and-services`, the notice-board entry; GM 2026-07-24). Both existed at once. Checked: `notice_board_adrift`.
- **The practice ground** stands in the outer court beside the watch's lodging - swept patch + weapon rack + striking posts, sized to the resident platoon. The compound trains daily; only a provincial city and above keeps a dojo (see grounding).
- **The compound is mostly open** - building coverage in the jin'ya band, buildings ringing the courts with the court-spine (forecourt, oshirasu, garden) held open in the center, loose slack consolidated rather than the envelope shrunk (the packing grounding). Checked: `coverage_band`, `perimeter_hugging`.

**Staffing and sizing anchors** (the Harima worked example in [budgets.md](https://github.com/EliAndrewC/l7r/blob/main/setting/budgets.md) plus `/host-l7r-repo/gm-assistant/setting/demographics.md`): ~15 working samurai - the county's "full platoon" of magistrate, senior retainers, and junior bushi, with only a handful of non-working dependents; a standing ashigaru contingent; ~3-4 heimen clerks commuting from town; and ~10 servants (cooks, grooms, cleaners) living on-grounds. The magistrate's discretionary income is ~1,000 koku/yr, and the compound handles a ~6,400 koku/yr county tax flow, most of it passed up to the governor.

**Knobs** (the axes that make two magistracies read differently):

1. **Tier.** The county magistracy is the baseline. The capital-stationed Imperial / Clan / Family magistrates get larger urban compounds with yoriki duty rooms; where their staff live is a knob, each plan taking one (research/questions/0114-the-imperial-magistrates-compound-in-a-capital.html, which supports both): OUTSIDE the compound on plots of their own in the city, as Edo's town magistracy housed its yoriki and constables, or INSIDE it in row houses, as a domain's estate in Edo housed its lower retainers. The tiny end is a road checkpoint post (research/questions/0110-border-posts-and-their-crossing-court-kuchidome-bansho.html): a barrier gate, an inspection hall facing the road and the officer's residence behind it, as at Nuruyu, and at a larger post a lockup, stables and a notice board within its fence, as at Hakone; how many samurai kept one is not recorded.
2. **Posting wealth.** County magistracies are tax-farmed and exam-competed for their revenue. A rich posting shows fine timber, a guest house, generous ceremony space; a poor one is drawn poor - patched walls, weathered timber, a hall too small for its ceremonies - a GUESS: only that some offices ran short of money is attested, not how a short-funded one looked.
3. **Granary weight.** Where the county's tax rice waits, a knob (research/questions/0098-storehouses-for-the-tax-rice.drawing.html): a county on navigable water leans to a row of storehouses at the town's river landing, its rice moving through, and its office keeps one modest storehouse; an isolated county leans to the office's own storehouse, which keeps the rice. The office draws ONE storehouse either way - the landing row is off the compound's sheet. Read the county's Mode B geography to pick. An upland county's granary also shifts CONTENTS: more in-kind goods (soybeans, barley, local products), since less of its tax is rice (see grounding).
4. **Garrison emphasis.** Bandit country adds ashigaru, an armory, a watchtower; a quiet county keeps a token watch.
5. **Staff housing.** (a) Everyone in the barracks and residence wing - the default, per the mostly-unmarried working platoon; (b) plus a few attached family rowhouses inside the walls for married sergeants and corporals; (c) senior retainers keep their own houses in town and the compound holds only the duty watch - suits a wealthy or long-settled magistracy, and shows on the town's Mode B map as samurai houses near the manor.
6. **Tenure character.** A freshly-appointed exam magistrate keeps a standardized office; strong office continuity (a long line of magistrates, or inherited rites and relics like Ochiba's) accretes manor character - an ancestral alcove, mature gardens, accumulated oddities.
7. **Resident particulars.** The "Ochiba slot": shrine dedication (Bishamon for a garrison county, Daikoku for a market county), a relic, a workshop, a dojo (a personal distinction of the resident, never a default program building - see the dojo grounding bullet). Designed per-manor in conversation with the GM.
8. **Justice-front furniture.** How public-facing the bench is: a petition window separate from the trial court, a grievance drum or bell at the gate, the prominence of the notice board. Vary by clan culture.

The seat of a village district's **country monk** - the one tax-free religious figure of the district (an Adept-rank monk of the Ministry of Rites' order, per the campaign notes), who keeps the district's birth, death, marriage and travel records, performs the villagers' rites, and lives at the shrine. [`pool/country-shrines/hoshigaoka-shrine/hoshigaoka-shrine.svg`](../pool/country-shrines/hoshigaoka-shrine/hoshigaoka-shrine.svg) is the worked example, named for Hoshigaoka's district; its particulars beyond the program are open for the GM. The research behind every rule here is on [`research/contents.json#religion-and-the-dead`](../research/contents.json#religion-and-the-dead): "Does the country monk live at the shrine?" and "Inside a village shrine's precinct: halls, basin, sacred tree and offerings (keidai)".

**The monk lives here, and that is the historically accurate form** (GM 2026-09-19; the research pass of feature 254). Before 1868 a Japanese village's sacred site was ordinarily a shrine with a small temple attached where the shrine-monk lived and performed the villagers' rites in Buddhist form; the parish temple's priest lived in its kuri; a Qing village temple engaged a monk to reside and keep it. The two forms history also had - a pure Shinto village shrine with no resident, tended by the parishioners' rota, and the unstaffed rural temple with a commuting priest - are forms this setting does not draw. On the sheet, "lives here" means UNDER THE HALL'S ROOF by default (knob 1), not merely within the fence.

**Composition rules** (every country shrine; the items themselves are the table below):

- **The precinct is not enclosed: its arch and its wood mark it** (feature 268, research "Shrine woods and fences (chinju no mori, tamagaki)"). No dated fence round a whole village precinct is attested - fences ring the sanctuary alone, and date from Taisho and Showa donations - and a wall is a temple's rank mark. So no fence, wall or hedge rings the ground marked `id="precinct"`, and no fence rings the sanctuary either, at any wealth: before 1868 only a lord's or a great shrine's is known, and at a village shrine it is attested only in modern times (feature 280 M65/M81, research "Did a village shrine have a fence round its sanctuary before modern times?"). Checked: `no_precinct_enclosure`.
- **The precinct holds the grove** (research "How large was a village shrine's precinct, and how much of it was built on?" and "Shrine woods and fences (chinju no mori, tamagaki)"): the hall, the sanctuary and the privy stand in a small clearing of the precinct's open ground (`keidai-earth`, a plain fill; not claimed swept - feature 280 M66), the kitchen garden in it or on the open ground beside it, wherever it gets its sun; the shrine's wood stands on the sides of the hall its ground gives it (knob 8), its edge its crowns' own where it meets scrub or slope, and the rest of the precinct is open ground. The precinct's size is the village-shrine registers as found - Saitama's fourteen 55-708 tsubo (median 403), Tochigi's fifty 142-2,700 (median 655), Saga's hamlet shrines 50-662 (median 148) - with buildings covering some 2-14% of it; every register figure is Meiji or later, so it can understate an Edo precinct (the 1870s taking of shrine lands cut precincts to their core) or overstate one (later enlargements and mergers); the Edo returns agree with the band - Saga's cover the lower part of 150-650 (about 45-540 tsubo), and Kami-Nerima's of 1821 run from about 10 to about 3,350.
- **The approach runs from the arch to the hall**, and the sanctuary stands BEHIND the hall on the same axis: arch, then hall, then sanctuary, each marked by id (`approach`, `arch`, `hall`, `sanctuary`). The outermost arch stands where the way enters the shrine's ground, the precinct's edge, the further arches deeper in toward the hall, the innermost one pitch off the hall's face - the one built thing a way runs under. **The arch count follows the map** (the setting's 1, 3 or 7 - `research/contents.json#religion-and-the-dead`, torii are votive donations), **at the 12 ft pitch** (the GM 2026-09-27: "All maps, ~10-13 ft"; research "Torii spacing"), drawn in plan (the top beam across the approach, its posts marked). Every arch is marked `id="arch"`, the OUTERMOST first. Checked: `sanctuary_on_axis`, `arch_on_approach` (the outermost arch, at the precinct's edge), `matches_map` (every arch against the map's).
- **Under one roof (the default), the hall is also the monk's home, and that building is the precinct's largest**: the villagers' end toward the arch, the dwelling end behind with its privy, and its kitchen garden near it on open ground where neither the hall nor the wood takes its six hours of sun (research "How many hours of direct sun does a kitchen bed need?"; the sheet audit's garden check). Under the two-building form the dwelling is a farmhouse-class house beside the hall, and may out-foot it: the hall alone is farmhouse-sized or smaller (research "Inside a village shrine's precinct: halls, basin, sacred tree and offerings (keidai)"), so only the sanctuary is held smaller than the hall.
- **The well or basin stands beside the approach**, never on it and never under the arch - the purification stop on the way in: a plain stone basin at village scale (a village's basin is attested before 1868 - Nagao Shrine's, 1828 - and the one pre-1868 pavilion found is Yakyu Inari's, 1836, at a shrine with a following across the Kanto; the basin's roof is a guess). Where a map sites the shrine's well elsewhere, both are drawn. Checked: `well_clear_of_arch`.
- **A sacred tree beside the approach**, ringed by its rope (a shrine was often first built where one stood; how common at village scale a guess). The donated stonework - guardian figures, lanterns, strength stones - is on the wealth knob, none at average; a farmers' stage and a sumo ring are knobs, absent by default. Subsidiary shrines, an office, a votive-plaque hall and a portable-shrine store are not drawn, the drawing page's choice (research "Inside a village shrine's precinct: halls, basin, sacred tree and offerings (keidai)"): subsidiary shrines are sometimes seen in a precinct, the one village shrine found with an office - Rokusha's, 30 tsubo - is in a register of 1872 or after (research "Village shrines: where they stand and how big their grounds are"), and no votive-plaque hall or portable-shrine store was found at a village shrine.
- **The burial ground lies in the shrine's yard or apart from it, a knob** (research/questions/0226-shrines-temples-and-graves-in-each-size-of-settlement.drawing.html - the village rule on the village maps: both forms are recorded, not how many villages took each): a sheet on a map follows the map (knob 4); one on no map rolls yard or apart from its seed with equal weights. In the yard it stands outside the grove; apart, it is off the sheet.
- **No office building**: a small shrine kept none, and its business was done in the monk's own rooms - the district's registers live in a writing room or record chest in the dwelling. **No bell as a requirement** (knob 2). **The tax-free fields lie off the sheet**; the sheet shows the kitchen garden and the yard.
- **Fire-water** at the wooden buildings, as at the magistracy (a GUESS at a village shrine): each tub beside a door, the kitchen's two at its yard door.

**Knobs:**

1. **Hall-and-dwelling form**: `one roof` (the default - the hall is the monk's home and the villagers' rite-place in one building, attested at Kaie-ji and called rare there) or `two buildings` (the ordinary parish temple's hall and kuri). The default is set against the sources' "rare" on the GM's own words of 2026-09-19 - "the shrine is both their home and the place where the villagers come ... if there is any choice in the matter, then I want to make that the case". Written in the notes file as `**Form**: one roof` or `**Form**: two buildings`; the program check reads it.
2. **Bell tower**: absent (default) or present - the minimal temple was "a main hall and a bell tower", and a village hall may have neither.
3. **Dedication and its furniture**: the Fortune the shrine serves and what that puts on the sheet - the instance particular, designed with the GM. The default is Inari, the rice Fortune, the ordinary rural dedication (GM 2026-09-20); the exemplar's is Bishamon, the Fortune of Strength - Hoshigaoka's shrine is the country monk Otsuki's, on the GM's word of the same day. What a dedication puts on the sheet: at a Bishamon shrine the guardian pair, where the wealth knob draws one, is tigers in place of lion-dogs (attested at named temples, a guess at village scale; the record's "What the dedication adds" on [`research/contents.json#religion-and-the-dead`](../research/contents.json#religion-and-the-dead)); at average wealth nothing is drawn beyond the name at the sanctuary.
4. **Grove and burial-ground side**: by the map when the shrine stands on one - the sheet declares it (`**On map**:`) and draws a grove or a burial ground only where the map has one inside the frame, on the map's side (both are site items in the declaration; the GM, 2026-09-20: the diagram view matches what the larger map shows) - else by the site as the program describes it, the burial ground rolled `in the yard` or `apart` from the seed with equal weights. Written in the notes file as `**Burial ground**: in the yard` or `**Burial ground**: apart`.
5. **Wealth**: a poor district's thatch and plain timber against a rich one's tile - and the donated stonework, none at average: stone guardian figures (late-Edo villager donations; tigers at a Bishamon shrine), stone lanterns and strength stones beside the approach. A fence around the sanctuary is not a gift the knob draws: at a village shrine it is attested only in modern times (feature 280 M81).
6. **Farmers' stage**: absent (default) or present - real on shrine ground from the early 1800s, but regional (1,777 counted nationwide, lost stages and noh and puppet stages included, and over 200 in each of three prefectures). Absent unless the plan turns it on, the drawing page's rule: how many village shrines kept a stage is not recorded.
7. **Sumo ring**: absent (default) or present - the dedicatory bouts at ordinary shrines are attested; no size is recorded. Absent unless the plan turns it on, the drawing page's rule: how many village shrines kept a ring is not recorded.
8. **Grove form** (feature 279; research "Shrine woods and fences (chinju no mori, tamagaki)"): `behind`, `behind and sides`, `sides` or `all around` (accurate as forms; `behind and sides` is also a naturalist's general description, while `behind` and `sides` rest on one modern city survey alone, with its caveat). The ground names the candidates: a hall at the foot of its slope takes `behind` or `behind and sides`; a hall at the top of its slope, the approach climbing to it, takes `behind and sides` or `sides`; a hall on a rise or on flat paddy ground is `all around`; a hall midway on an even slope, with no break of slope at it, takes `behind`, `behind and sides` or `sides` (a guess - the record covers only a hall at a break). Between two or three candidates the settlement rolls from its seed with equal weights (a guess). The edge is irregular where the wood meets scrub or slope (a guess); where a paddy-plain wood meets its fields, its foot is a second knob, `field line` (along the fields' straight edge, as the one photograph shows) or `ragged` (its crowns' own), rolled from the seed with equal weights (a guess; the GM's ruling of 2026-09-28: evidence of one form without evidence it was the rule makes a knob). The hierarchy: under one roof the hall-and-dwelling building out-foots every other building on the sheet, under two buildings the hall out-foots its sanctuary and the dwelling may be the larger, and the sanctuary is the smallest building of the shrine proper (a privy is smaller, and is not one).

**Staffing and income** (the campaign notes): one country monk, with a few acolytes who help farm the tax-free plot on a part-time basis, on loan from larger families in the village; the monk's income is the plot's produce plus gifts and fees for the rites performed for the villagers - which is the parish temple's own economy, the danka's fuse and the temple's exempt land.
