# Compound programs

*Part of the Mode A compound/building docs - the scale, vocabulary, composition rules and checklist live in [`buildings.md`](buildings.md).*

**Load this file when:** you are drawing a compound whose TYPE has a documented program - today the county magistrate's manor and the country shrine. A building type with no program yet is drawn from the vocabulary and the composition rules, and gets its program here before its exemplar lands (`docs/buildings.md`, "Adding a building type").

---

## Compound programs

Per-building-type specs: the required program every instance shares, the knobs that make instances differ, and staffing/sizing anchors. The research reasoning behind each program lives on [`research/contents.json#compounds`](../research/contents.json#compounds) (the magistracy) and [`research/contents.json#religion-and-the-dead`](../research/contents.json#religion-and-the-dead) (the country shrine). (Planned next entries: samurai country estate, governor's compound, castle keep.)

**A type is declared once.** Each program's REQUIRED ITEMS - the label a check finds each by, the size band it is held to, the class of that band and the finding it rests on - are the declaration in [`l7r/diagram/buildings/types.json`](../l7r/diagram/buildings/types.json), and the table under each heading below is RENDERED from it by `make building-programs` (`CHECK=1` fails the gate when it is stale). Edit the declaration, never the table. The prose around a table - the composition rules, the knobs, the anchors, the staffing - is hand-written here and is what the reviewers read beside the table; the checks read the declaration (`rust/mode_a/src/audit/registry.rs`, the pool sweep in `rust/mode_a/src/pool_sweep.rs`).

The seat of a County Magistrate (Rank 5): a walled compound in the county town combining court, tax office, granary, garrison, and the magistrate's household. [`pool/magistracies/ochiba-magistracy/ochiba-magistracy.svg`](../pool/magistracies/ochiba-magistracy/ochiba-magistracy.svg) is the worked example of the program's composition - though NOT of its house, whose ~141 tsubo is a recorded deviation for Ochiba's wealthy magistrate (feature 433): a new sheet sizes its house by the residence item below - it is this generic program plus Ochiba's particulars (the hall-scale two-altar Inari shrine, the Akami-fude and Fox-Fire Lantern relics, the cinnabar workshop colonnade, the Pact-Bowl threshold stone). A new magistracy should read as the same institution while sharing none of those particulars. [`pool/magistracies/hayakawa-magistracy/hayakawa-magistracy.svg`](../pool/magistracies/hayakawa-magistracy/hayakawa-magistracy.svg) is the validation instance, its house drawn at the record's size (about 65 tsubo, feature 433): the same program with every knob set differently (rich river-landing county, staff-housing option (c), guest-wing annex, one modest shrine serving two gods, cell by the gatehouse).

**Composition rules** (every county magistracy; the items themselves are the table below):

- **Realistic unless the GM says otherwise** (the GM, 2026-09-28: *"You should definitely draw with the things that your sources most safely support. Not just in this case, but in general"*): the buildings' shapes and how things sit within the compound follow the historical norm, in the narrowest reading the sources support - anything beyond it is labeled a guess. A sheet departs from the norm only where the GM has called for it (the vermilion threshold stones are the setting's invention; the buildings and their layout are not). A review rule never pushes a drawing past the record: where the record's form and a rule disagree, the rule is changed to admit the form.

- **Walled enclosure** with a formal main gate + gatehouse and at least one lesser side/rear door. The household's court has a lesser door of its own: a samurai residence kept a rear wicket for carrying out night soil, the fujomon (research/questions/0101-privies-setchin.html, in general terms: no residence named, no size), so the collector never crosses the hearing court - that the program requires it, that it also takes the kitchen's deliveries, and its size and seat in the wall are a GUESS; a busy OUTER court (stables) warrants a small service gate of its own too, so muck and night-soil skip the formal gate (a GUESS, the record silent on the muck); prisoners do not have a gate of their own - they come by the formal gate, as at Pingyao, where those brought up for trial went through a side door of the ceremonial gate (research/questions/0093-the-main-gate-and-its-gatekeepers-nagaya-mon.html, research/questions/0096-holding-cells-agariya-and-roya.html; China first: 0101's dictionaries give the Japanese rear wicket for criminals too, the tiebreaker only, and our plans draw no ceremonial gate, so the main gate takes them); and the granary stands on the office side of the divider, never in the household's court (Pingyao built office buildings behind its war, justice and works offices "and north of them the county granary", research/questions/0123-the-magistrates-manor-in-a-town-where-it-stands-and-which-way-it-faces-jinya-and-yamen.html), at the head of an office-court flank column beside the hall's end, or just below a building that heads it (a GUESS on 0123's drawing page: the plan has no side-wing offices for Pingyao's seat to stand north of), so its carts come by the outer court's gates and the office reaches it without crossing the household. After placing buildings, walk the cart route from the gates to the granary and stables in your head - a route every diagram must actually have (a round-2 review caught a gatehouse-and-hearing-court squeeze that cut the granary off from the main gate). Walls mark jurisdiction more than they defend: most impressive on the public approach, plainer at the rear.
- **Guest doors feed courts, not flanks.** Every entrance intended for GUESTS opens into something suitable for receiving them - a court, a garden, a graveled forecourt - never directly into (or against the flank of) a building. Service doors deliberately do the opposite: the kitchen postern opens straight into work space. When adding any door, ask who comes through it and what they step into.
- **Two-court zoning**: public/administrative outer court at the gate; private inner court behind (residence, garden, shrine). The internal divider wall has its own gate - a household door (~18 px / 6 ft, a real nakamon, narrower than the ceremonial main gate's passage where its frontage is drawn above 7 ft, as the county example's is - no rule ties the two widths; the width a GUESS, searched and the record silent), directly BEHIND the office hall (its place a GUESS: no page we read records a jin'ya axis or places its middle gate; a generated plan, whose hall stands too near the divider for a gate behind it, opens it beside the hall instead, a DEVIATION research/questions/0090-magistrates-compounds-jinya-and-yamen.drawing.html records), which is deliberate: the hall itself is the privacy baffle. Formal visitors are received in the hall, and their way to the residence runs on through the office (research/questions/0104-the-formal-entrance-and-a-guests-arrival-genkan.drawing.html); the divider gate serves household traffic (the state/home hinge), and heavy service uses the kitchen postern, so the narrow alley behind the hall is a feature, not a defect. Checked: `two_court_zoning`.
- **The office hall is the compound's working heart**: rear day rooms (day office for tax and case business, the magistrate's official study at the household's end of the row (449 T392: 0113, Edo's yobeya at the join of office and residence), the clerks' room beside the day office, and between it and the armory's end bay (or at the row's end where the hall holds no armory, or where the study takes the armory's end, at the far end beside the day office, 449 T392) a writing room and a binding room, 0113's, Takayama's, a GUESS at a county office; each at its own size, never the row's remainder split - 449 T313), with the dais band on the south face - dais and flanking clerk positions overlooking the **roofed hearing court** with kneeling positions; its floor is a knob, white gravel or the local river cobbles, each plan taking one (research/questions/0099-the-hearing-court-shirasu.drawing.html: white gravel at the magistracies, river cobbles at Takayama), written in the notes. The residence's "private study" is for the household side of life; official business happens here. The clerks' duty room is a workroom for 3-4 heimen clerks who live in the county town and commute - a room of the hall on the drawn sheets, never clerk housing.
- **The residence** is sized by the record: about 49 tsubo with its kitchen, bath and corridor (research/questions/0091-samurai-residences-and-their-rooms-buke-yashiki.drawing.html: the 150-koku district magistrate's house the plan is measured against), up to about 2,400 sq ft (~67 tsubo) for a richer posting (research/questions/0118-samurai-house-lots-and-houses-by-rank-bukeyashiki.drawing.html: a senior lot's house covers about 1,200 to 2,400 sq ft; that a rich posting's magistrate keeps a senior house is a GUESS, recorded on research/questions/0091-samurai-residences-and-their-rooms-buke-yashiki.drawing.html); a larger house is a recorded DEVIATION with its reason (Ochiba's and Ubame's, the GM's ruling of 2026-10-26 that those magistrates are significantly wealthier than normal), and when the record's house leaves the compound under the coverage band, no building grows past its research size to fill it, and a wall moves in only when a house drawn at the record's size leaves coverage under the band and no other wall and no building at its research size can honestly close it - never as a default: a DEVIATION, recorded on 0116's drawing page with its reason and who chose it, as Hayakawa's north wall is (feature 433). It has rank-graded quarters and NO genkan of its own: where office and residence share a compound, the one formal genkan is drawn on the OFFICE hall and the visitors' way runs on through the office to the house (research/questions/0104-the-formal-entrance-and-a-guests-arrival-genkan.drawing.html; or no genkan at all, the roji form - buildings.md, 'Entry porch'); the family block and any detached dwelling (karo's house, guest house) reach the court by an informal door - no sealed boxes. **A rear service strip** behind it (the shady north side): utility (the household's storehouses, the attached family privy) - never the servants, who lodge in a range on the street edge, beside or through the gate (0115) - a named rear yard of open working ground with the storehouses beyond it (the Higuchi house, research/questions/0091-samurai-residences-and-their-rooms-buke-yashiki.drawing.html), OR a narrow service alley - never a wide UNNAMED empty band (see Latrines and the rear service strip). **The kitchen garden** is two knobs (research/questions/0109-vegetable-gardens-at-a-samurai-residence-saien.drawing.html): its SITE among four - west of the residence, south beside the formal garden, in the rear service ground (on a Chinese list alone), or on a parcel of its own - any site ruled out where a full-sun bed gets under six hours of direct autumn sun (research/questions/0038-sunlight-and-shade-on-the-farm.drawing.html), and its SIZE between a soup-greens plot of about 1,100 sq ft and a field over about half the grounds; a hand-drawn plan takes one of each and records them in its notes.
- **The shrine is universal equipment, and it is ONE shrine**, Inari by default: a compound draws one modest shrine however many a real office kept (0218's drawing page, a deliberate deviation). A second god is enshrined in that hall, or keeps a shrine of its own inside the precinct where the sheet's roll of the second kami's form says so (0218; the roll's group declares `data-form="own shrine"`); `one_compound_shrine` holds it. The shrine stands in a corner of the grounds, where a household kept its god (0218's drawing page); the tree beside it is a GUESS (0218's drawing page, `#shrine-tree`: the encyclopedia gives the god's seat as a small shrine or an old tree, not the two together; `shrine_tree`), and a garden laid out around it is a GUESS, no page describing one. Scale and dedication are the per-manor particular; Ochiba's full two-altar hall is the exception, justified by its priest-magistrate, not the norm - and even it stays subordinate in footprint to the residence (the hall-shrine ceiling, the `shrine` band below).
- **Wells** are distributed by use (kitchen, garden, stables); **latrines** roughly one per functional zone, the residence's two privies, the guests' and the family's, built into the house, the rest against service walls near a gate; **fire-water tubs** (~8-12) at the fire-prone wooden buildings, weighted to the kitchen (2), none at the plaster kura (Fire discipline).
- **The notice board** just outside the main gate is the BENCH'S board (verdicts, edicts, bounties - the court's output, read by those who come to it); the settlement's own kosatsuba is a DIFFERENT board on the town map, posting the state's standing law at the busiest public point (`research/contents.json#trades-and-services`, the notice-board entry; GM 2026-07-24). Both existed at once. Checked: `notice_board_adrift`.
- **The practice ground** stands in the outer court beside the watch's lodging - swept patch + weapon rack + striking posts, sized to the resident platoon. The compound trains daily; only a provincial city and above keeps a dojo (see grounding).
- **The compound is mostly open** - building coverage in the jin'ya band, buildings ringing the courts with the court-spine (forecourt, oshirasu, garden) held open in the center, loose slack consolidated rather than the envelope shrunk (the packing grounding; the one exception is a recorded DEVIATION - a wall moved in when the house is drawn at the record's size, 0116's drawing page). Checked: `coverage_band`, `perimeter_hugging`.

**Staffing and sizing anchors** (the Harima worked example in [budgets.md](https://github.com/EliAndrewC/l7r/blob/main/setting/budgets.md) plus `/host-l7r-repo/gm-assistant/setting/demographics.md`): ~15 working samurai - the county's "full platoon" of magistrate, senior retainers, and junior bushi, with only a handful of non-working dependents; a standing ashigaru contingent; ~3-4 heimen clerks commuting from town; and ~10 servants (cooks, grooms, cleaners) living on-grounds. The magistrate's discretionary income is ~1,000 koku/yr, and the compound handles a ~6,400 koku/yr county tax flow, most of it passed up to the governor.

**Knobs** (the axes that make two magistracies read differently):

1. **Tier.** The county magistracy is the baseline. The capital-stationed Imperial / Clan / Family magistrates get larger urban compounds with yoriki duty rooms; where their staff live is a knob, each plan taking one (research/questions/0114-the-imperial-magistrates-compound-in-a-capital.html, which supports both): OUTSIDE the compound on plots of their own in the city, as Edo's town magistracy housed its yoriki and constables, or INSIDE it in row houses, as a domain's estate in Edo housed its lower retainers. The tiny end is a road checkpoint post (research/questions/0110-border-posts-and-their-crossing-court-kuchidome-bansho.html): a barrier gate, an inspection hall facing the road and the officer's residence behind it, as at Nuruyu, and at a larger post a lockup, stables and a notice board within its fence, as at Hakone; how many samurai kept one is not recorded.
2. **Posting wealth.** County magistracies are tax-farmed and exam-competed for their revenue. A rich posting shows fine timber, a guest house, generous ceremony space; a poor one is drawn poor - patched walls, weathered timber, a hall too small for its ceremonies - a GUESS: only that some offices ran short of money is attested, not how a short-funded one looked.
3. **Granary weight.** Where the county's tax rice waits, a knob (research/questions/0098-storehouses-for-the-tax-rice.drawing.html): a county on navigable water leans to a row of storehouses at the town's river landing, its rice moving through, and its office keeps one modest storehouse; an isolated county leans to the office's own storehouse, which keeps the rice. The office draws ONE storehouse either way - the landing row is off the compound's sheet. Read the county's Mode B geography to pick. An upland county's granary also shifts CONTENTS: more in-kind goods (soybeans, barley, local products), since less of its tax is rice (see grounding).
4. **Garrison emphasis.** Every seat keeps an armory, a quiet county's too: in the gate range where the main gate is drawn as a range (Kaibara's held a store of arms, research/questions/0093-the-main-gate-and-its-gatekeepers-nagaya-mon.html), or in an end bay of the office hall (Pingyao's west end bay, at a seat with no standing barracks; at Jiexiu, whose later plan names a barracks, the great hall's two end bays held its gunpowder and weapons from 1591, research/questions/0123-the-magistrates-manor-in-a-town-where-it-stands-and-which-way-it-faces-jinya-and-yamen.html; the side varied across county offices, so which end bay, west or east, is a second roll from the map's seed, `<sheet>:armory end bay`, and the back row is drawn from the armory's end, 449 T367), whether or not the seat keeps a standing barracks - no source puts the arms in a barracks; or in a store of its own (Nirayama's weapons store a building of its own, about 18 by 12 ft, and Hamamashike's among its storehouses; Jiexiu's armory after 1677 is listed inside its side gates, though its account leaves open whether it stood there and whether it was a building or a room, 0123), under the sheet's `**Form**: armory as a store of its own`, standing where the sheet draws it (no source places one). The site is a knob rolled from the map's seed among the sites the sheet offers - the gate range where one stands, the hall's bay, a store - so a one-bay gate's sheet rolls between the hall's bay and a store. Bandit country adds ashigaru (a larger barracks) and a watchtower; a quiet county keeps a token watch.
5. **Staff housing.** (a) Everyone in the barracks and residence wing - the default, per the mostly-unmarried working platoon; (b) plus a few attached family rowhouses inside the walls for married sergeants and corporals; (c) senior retainers keep their own houses in town and the compound holds only the duty watch - suits a wealthy or long-settled magistracy, and shows on the town's Mode B map as samurai houses near the manor.
6. **Tenure character.** A freshly-appointed exam magistrate keeps a standardized office; strong office continuity (a long line of magistrates, or inherited rites and relics like Ochiba's) accretes manor character - an ancestral alcove, mature gardens, accumulated oddities.
7. **Resident particulars.** The "Ochiba slot": shrine dedication (Bishamon for a garrison county, Daikoku for a market county), a relic, a workshop, a dojo (a personal distinction of the resident, never a default program building - see the dojo grounding bullet). Designed per-manor in conversation with the GM.
8. **Justice-front furniture.** How public-facing the bench is: the prominence of the notice board, and what else is posted outside the gate (a bounty board). Every seat keeps a grievance drum at its main gate, as both county yamen we read of did (research/questions/0093-the-main-gate-and-its-gatekeepers-nagaya-mon.html), under the gate's roof and not drawn as its own mark (a map drawing convention); a petition window or a bell, which no page attests, is not a choice. Vary by clan culture.

The seat of a village district's **country monk** - the one tax-free religious figure of the district (an Adept-rank monk of the Ministry of Rites' order, per the campaign notes), who keeps the district's birth, death, marriage and travel records, performs the villagers' rites, and lives at the shrine. [`pool/country-shrines/hoshigaoka-shrine/hoshigaoka-shrine.svg`](../pool/country-shrines/hoshigaoka-shrine/hoshigaoka-shrine.svg) is the worked example, named for Hoshigaoka's district; its particulars beyond the program are open for the GM. The research behind every rule here is on [`research/contents.json#religion-and-the-dead`](../research/contents.json#religion-and-the-dead): "Does the country monk live at the shrine?" and "Inside a village shrine's precinct: halls, basin, sacred tree and offerings (keidai)".

**The monk lives here, and that is the historically accurate form** (GM 2026-09-19; the research pass of feature 254). Before 1868 a Japanese village's sacred site was ordinarily a shrine with a small temple attached where the shrine-monk lived and performed the villagers' rites in Buddhist form; the parish temple's priest lived in its kuri; a Qing village temple engaged a monk to reside and keep it. The two forms history also had - a pure Shinto village shrine with no resident, tended by the parishioners' rota, and the unstaffed rural temple with a commuting priest - are forms this setting does not draw. On the sheet, "lives here" means UNDER THE HALL'S ROOF by default (knob 1), not merely within the fence.

**Composition rules** (every country shrine; the items themselves are the table below):

- **The precinct is not enclosed: its arch and its wood mark it** (feature 268, research "Shrine woods and fences (chinju no mori, tamagaki)"). No dated fence round a whole village precinct is attested - the dated fences found at ordinary shrines ring the sanctuary alone, from Taisho and Showa donations, or line the approach - and a wall is a temple's rank mark. A fence along the approach is not drawn: the one dated before 1868, Yasuda Hachiman's late-Edo stone fence, is one shrine's, a guess no count settles. So no fence, wall or hedge rings the ground marked `id="precinct"`, and no fence rings the sanctuary either, at any wealth: before 1868 only a lord's or a great shrine's is known, and at a village shrine it is attested only in modern times (feature 280 M65/M81, research "Did a village shrine have a fence round its sanctuary before modern times?"). Checked: `no_precinct_enclosure` (a fence round the precinct, and since feature 449 T177 one round the sanctuary).
- **The precinct holds the grove** (research "How large was a village shrine's precinct, and how much of it was built on?" and "Shrine woods and fences (chinju no mori, tamagaki)"): the hall, the sanctuary and the privy stand in a small clearing of the precinct's open ground (`keidai-earth`, a plain fill; not claimed swept - feature 280 M66), the kitchen garden in it or on the open ground beside it, wherever it gets its sun; the shrine's wood stands on the sides of the hall its ground gives it (knob 8), its edge its crowns' own where it meets scrub or slope, and the rest of the precinct is open ground. The precinct's size is the village-shrine registers as found - Saitama's fourteen 55-708 tsubo (median 403), Tochigi's fifty 142-2,700 (median 655), Saga's hamlet shrines 50-662 (median 148) - with buildings covering some 2-14% of it; every register figure is Meiji or later, so it can understate an Edo precinct (the 1870s taking of shrine lands cut precincts to their core) or overstate one (later enlargements and mergers); the Edo returns agree with the band - Saga's cover the lower part of 150-650 (about 45-540 tsubo), and Kami-Nerima's of 1821 run from about 10 to about 3,350.
- **The approach runs from the arch to the hall**, and the sanctuary stands BEHIND the hall on the same axis: arch, then hall, then sanctuary, each marked by id (`approach`, `arch`, `hall`, `sanctuary`). The outermost arch stands where the way enters the shrine's ground, the precinct's edge - except where it and the innermost cannot both keep their places, when the innermost holds its 12 ft off the hall and the outermost moves inward with the run (0220's drawing page, a deviation from the first arch at the entry) - the further arches deeper in toward the hall, the innermost one pitch off the hall's face - the one built thing a way runs under. **The arch count follows the map** (the setting's 1, 3 or 7 - `research/contents.json#religion-and-the-dead`, torii are votive donations); a sheet on no map rolls it from the plan's seed at a village's weights, one, three or seven arches at 60, 30 and 10 in a hundred (a knob, research/questions/0220-shrine-gateways-and-the-approach-to-the-hall-torii-sando.drawing.html - a country shrine being a village's), and its notes record the roll; **at the 12 ft pitch** (the GM 2026-09-27: "All maps, ~10-13 ft"; research "Torii spacing"), drawn in plan (the top beam across the approach, its posts marked). Every arch is marked `id="arch"`, the OUTERMOST first. Checked: `sanctuary_on_axis`, `arch_on_approach` (every arch over the approach, one, three or seven of them, and the outermost - the one farthest from the hall, whatever its order in the sheet - at the precinct's edge, unless the innermost stands its 12 ft off the hall, which holds where both cannot: feature 449 T177), `matches_map` (every arch against the map's).
- **Under one roof (the default), the hall is also the monk's home, and that building is the precinct's largest**: the villagers' hall at one END of the building, on the approach, and the monk's dwelling (its rooms and its kitchen) at the other, never the hall between them, the dwelling end with its privy, and its kitchen garden near it on open ground where neither the hall nor the wood takes its six hours of sun (research "How many hours of direct sun does a kitchen bed need?"; the sheet audit's garden check). Under the two-building form the dwelling is a farmhouse-class house beside the hall, and may out-foot it: the hall alone is farmhouse-sized or smaller (research "Inside a village shrine's precinct: halls, basin, sacred tree and offerings (keidai)"), so only the sanctuary is held smaller than the hall.
- **The basin stands beside the approach at the innermost arch** (0222's drawing page), never on it and never under an arch - the purification stop on the way in, at every village shrine (feature 449 T182: the required item is the basin, where a well once stood in for it): a plain stone basin at village scale (a parish's basin is attested before 1868 - Nagao Shrine's, 1828, given by its parishioners - and the one pre-1868 pavilion found is Yakyu Inari's, 1836, at a shrine with a following across the Kanto; the basin's roof is a guess). Where the shrine keeps its own well (0196's drawing page: beside the hall, a shrine set apart from the houses), both are drawn, the well in addition to the basin; a hilltop shrine draws no well (0196's drawing page: its water from a spring or a cistern, a guess). Checked: `well_clear_of_arch` (the basin, `id="basin"`, required, and the basin and any well each off the approach and every arch: feature 449 T182); the basin's place by the innermost arch is the procedure's, which no check holds.
- **A sacred tree beside the approach**, ringed by its rope (a shrine was often first built where one stood; how common at village scale a guess). The donated stonework - guardian figures, lanterns, strength stones - is on the wealth knob, none at average; a farmers' stage and a sumo ring are knobs, absent by default. Subsidiary shrines, an office, a votive-plaque hall and a portable-shrine store are not drawn, the drawing page's choice (research "Inside a village shrine's precinct: halls, basin, sacred tree and offerings (keidai)"): subsidiary shrines are sometimes seen in a precinct, the one village shrine found with an office - Rokusha's, 30 tsubo - is in a register of 1872 or after (research "Village shrines: where they stand and how big their grounds are"), and no votive-plaque hall or portable-shrine store was found at a village shrine.
- **The burial ground lies in the shrine's yard or apart from it, a knob** (research/questions/0226-shrines-temples-and-graves-in-each-size-of-settlement.drawing.html - the village rule on the village maps: both forms are recorded, not how many villages took each): a sheet on a map follows the map (knob 4); one on no map whose hall stands at the top of its slope or on a rise (knob 8's grounds) stands on a hill and keeps its ground apart (research/questions/0236-where-a-village-buries-its-dead-its-own-ground-the-temple-yard-the-fields-or-the-home-plot.drawing.html: a village whose shrine stands on a hill always takes a ground apart; which grounds count as a hill a GUESS); any other on no map rolls yard or apart from its seed with equal weights. In the yard it stands outside the grove; apart, it is off the sheet.
- **No office building**: a small shrine kept none, and its business was done in the monk's own rooms - the district's registers live in a writing room or record chest in the dwelling. **No bell as a requirement** (knob 2). **The tax-free fields lie off the sheet**; the sheet shows the kitchen garden and the yard.
- **Fire-water** at the wooden buildings, as at the magistracy (a GUESS at a village shrine): each tub beside a door, the kitchen's two at its yard door.

**Knobs:**

1. **Hall-and-dwelling form**: `one roof` (the default - the hall is the monk's home and the villagers' rite-place in one building, attested at Kaie-ji and called rare there) or `two buildings` (the ordinary parish temple's hall and kuri). The default is set against the sources' "rare" on the GM's own words of 2026-09-19 - "the shrine is both their home and the place where the villagers come ... if there is any choice in the matter, then I want to make that the case". Written in the notes file as `**Form**: one roof` or `**Form**: two buildings`; the program check reads it.
2. **Bell tower**: absent (default) or present - the minimal temple was "a main hall and a bell tower", and a village hall may have neither.
3. **Dedication and its furniture**: the Fortune the shrine serves and what that puts on the sheet - the instance particular, designed with the GM. The default is Inari, the rice Fortune, the ordinary rural dedication (GM 2026-09-20); the exemplar's is Bishamon, the Fortune of Strength - Hoshigaoka's shrine is the country monk Otsuki's, on the GM's word of the same day. What a dedication puts on the sheet: at a Bishamon shrine the guardian pair, where the wealth knob draws one, is tigers in place of lion-dogs (attested at named temples, a guess at village scale; the record's "What the dedication adds" on [`research/contents.json#religion-and-the-dead`](../research/contents.json#religion-and-the-dead)); at average wealth nothing is drawn beyond the name at the sanctuary.
4. **Grove and burial-ground side**: by the map when the shrine stands on one - the sheet declares it (`**On map**:`) and draws a grove or a burial ground only where the map has one inside the frame, on the map's side (both are site items in the declaration; the GM, 2026-09-20: the diagram view matches what the larger map shows) - else by the site as the program describes it: a hall at the top of its slope or on a rise stands on a hill and its burial ground is `apart`, not rolled (0236's drawing page; which grounds count a GUESS), and any other's is rolled `in the yard` or `apart` from the seed with equal weights. Written in the notes file as `**Burial ground**: in the yard` or `**Burial ground**: apart`.
5. **Wealth**: a poor district's thatch and plain timber against a rich one's tile - and the donated stonework, none at average: stone guardian figures (late-Edo villager donations; tigers at a Bishamon shrine), stone lanterns and strength stones beside the approach.
