About: A field drain, an akusuiro, ran along the lowest ground of the paddies, across the fall of the land, gathering the water
each plot let out. That water was not wasted: where a channel was an old river course it carried the water on to be used
again, and elsewhere works were built to return it to the supply or use it directly. Ponds stood among
the paddies to store water for them, and in this form the drain runs into one below the fields.

A pond on low ground was the dish pond (sara-ike), made by ringing a hollow or a low wet place with an embankment and digging
out its bottom. On the plains it was shallow; such ponds are ringed today by reed, wild rice and cattail, their embankments
kept clear of growth to keep them strong, and an older Chinese manual had a pond's bank trodden firm by cattle.

Japan came to have over a hundred thousand ponds, more of them built in the Edo period than in any other, and before
its modern canal Kagawa, on the dry Sanuki plain, drew most of its farm water from them. The largest covered several hectares (a hectare
is about 2.5 acres); the smallest, about 33 ft (10 m) on a side, were owned and kept by a single owner.

Guesses:
- That a drain ends in a pond at all: every drain found emptied into a river or a natural watercourse, and none into a pond of its own.
- That a settlement's drain ends in a pond about two times in three: no source counts where drains ended.

Depiction: The map ends the drain in a pond at the foot of the field, with a fringe of reeds on wet ground at its shore and a
bare embankment beyond them; placing the pond there is how the map ends the drain, a drawing convention rather than a
surveyed example. Like every drain on the map, it is drawn at its true width, narrow at its head and widening to its
outfall.
