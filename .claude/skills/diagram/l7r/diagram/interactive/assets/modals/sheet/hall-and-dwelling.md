About: The shrine's one building held two things: the hall where the villagers came to pray to the shrine's kami, and the
home of the monk who kept it. Before 1868 many shrines had a Buddhist temple attached, whose monk lived there and performed
the shrine's rites in Buddhist form. In Rokugan every village district has a country monk, living on a plot of tax-free land,
who keeps its shrine and its records of births, deaths, marriages and travel; the monk's hall and dwelling stand under one
roof.

A monk's dwelling, the kuri, began as a kitchen, and at an ordinary temple it was much like an ordinary house; one
resembles its region's farmhouse, with an earth-floored kitchen and rooms laid with mats. A hall and a dwelling
under one roof are known once, at Kaie-ji, a temple in the city of Sakai, where the form was called unusual. One measured
hall of a village's size, a Kannon hall of 1778 in Saitama, was thatched.

Who lived there besides the monk is not recorded; in Rokugan the monk may have a few acolytes, lent part-time by the
village's larger families to help farm the shrine's land. The worship halls and small temple halls that
have been measured run about 20 to 35 ft on a side, and a village temple's main hall about 24 to 36 ft; Kaie-ji's hall and
dwelling together are 83 ft long and 26 to 43 ft deep, about 2,150 to 3,550 sq ft.

Guesses:
- Where the building's size falls inside the band set by Kaie-ji, the one hall and dwelling known under one roof: that
  example is a city temple's, not a village's, and no second one was found.

Depiction: The plan draws the hall and the monk's dwelling as one long building under a single roof, at its true size, the
hall facing down the approach with the small sanctuary behind it on the same line. The kitchen, the monk's rooms and the
writing room are drawn as parts of it.
