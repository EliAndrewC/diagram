About: Ponds laid out in a grid would lie pond after pond in straight rows and columns, the dikes running between them like
the lines of a board. In 1678 a writer likened the land of Jiujiang, a fry township of the Pearl River delta, to a chessboard,
its dikes the lines and its ponds the squares, though whether its ponds truly lay in rows is not recorded. Seen from the air
today, the ponds of Digang, on the south shore of Lake Tai, lie as a chessboard.

The old grid was the polder's. Under the Five Dynasties and the Song, the polders of the Lake Tai plain grew into the tangpu
system, a planned lattice of canals with a cross channel every five or seven li and a long channel every seven or ten li,
joining dredging, dike building, sluices and water transport. The grid was the canals'; the land between them was cut into
many small parcels. Around Lake Tai, too, farmers dug fish ponds out of the low ground and heaped the mud round them as dikes.

How many villages laid their ponds out in a grid before modern times is not recorded: the old grid was the canals', and the
ponds of the Pearl River delta lay as a mosaic.

Depiction: A dike-pond block is drawn as the mosaic, not as a grid of ponds, since ponds set out in straight rows are known
only from modern times. A rice polder keeps the planned grid of straight canals: its module lines run straight, while the
parcels inside them each take their own shape, never a grid of uniform rectangles.
