About: The servants' quarters lodged the household's domestic servants, the komono, who did its odd jobs. They were not
samurai but of peasant or townsman stock, most of them younger sons of peasants, hired for wages on terms of a year or half a
year; they changed households on two fixed days a year, and a household with no connections hired them through a broker.
How many a household had to keep was fixed by its assessed rice yield. They lived in, inside their master's walls; no
servant is known to have had a house of their own in the samurai quarter.

Where they slept followed the size of the house. On a large house the buildings along the street were long, single-story
ranges (nagaya) where the servants lived, the front gate cut through one of them so that the servants there kept it; a
smaller house had a wall in place of the ranges. The lowest servants, the chugen, lived together in large rooms: one
described is two rooms of six mats, each about 9 by 12 ft, entered by a single lattice door. Where servants lived as
households, or a few men together, each dwelling had a door of its own.

How big an ordinary range was is not measured; the Honda family's gate range at Okazaki once ran about 390 ft along its
front.

Guesses:
- That the servants' range stands in the shady rear behind the residence: the servants are recorded in the gate range on
  the street, and the rear is reasoned from the garden's place in the sunny south.
- Its size, a range for about ten servants: no measured example stands behind it.
- That a small household's few servants slept under the main roof, in the storeroom and rooms off the kitchen: no source
  says where they slept.

Depiction: The plan draws the servants' quarters as a building of its own, in one of the two forms servants lived in - a
common room behind one door, its bays parted by sliding partitions, or a row of dwellings each with its own door - and never
as dwellings walled off from one another behind one shared door.
