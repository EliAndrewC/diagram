About: The road at a magistrate's compound was the road or street the compound stood on, and its main gate opened onto
it; lesser lanes led to the side gates. A Japanese seat was often built beside a village or town already there, probably
at its edge, while the Chinese county yamen stood inside its town, on the main street. Under the Qing a local government
office faced south, by regulation; which way a Japanese seat's gate faced is not recorded.

Japan's great highways were lined with avenues of pine and cedar, with a mound raised at every ri, about 2.4 miles, to mark
the distance. They were earth dressed with sand and gravel, though never everywhere, and rain turned them to mud; a lesser
road was bare earth trodden hard by feet and hooves. Japan kept its carts to the towns and barred them from the highways to the end of the shogunate. In Rokugan
wagons and carts travel the roads between towns, so the road at a compound's gate carries them.

The highways were set in 1605 to about 30 ft (9 m), narrowed to about 13 to 23 ft in the mountains and 12 ft at passes
such as Hakone; most of the Five Highways ran about 18 to 24 ft. Through Hiroshima's castle town the highway was about
15 ft. How wide the road was just before an official's gate, or a lane to a side gate, is not recorded.

Guesses:
- A lane to a side or cart gate about 6 ft wide where carts use it: no lane's width was found, and the Edo hand cart's bed
  was about 8 by 2.5 ft, so six feet leaves room for the wheels.
- Any wider ground before a compound's gate, where a plan draws one: no account gives the width of the road there.

Depiction: The plan draws the road as a band of bare ground running from the sheet's edge to the main gate, and narrower
lanes to the lesser gates. A highway is drawn in the same earth tone as a lesser road: its dressing of sand and gravel was
earth-colored at a plan's scale.

Name: road
Covers: the approach road or town street, the lanes to the lesser doors, and a road across a border
