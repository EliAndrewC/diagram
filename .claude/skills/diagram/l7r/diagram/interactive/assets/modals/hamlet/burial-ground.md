About: A village's burial ground held its dead in common: a graveyard a settlement's inhabitants owned and kept
themselves. At the end of the Edo period it was one form among several, beside a temple's, a lineage's and a household's
graves, and as temples multiplied the dead went more often to a temple's ground. Where the dead had two graves, the body
most often lay in common ground on a riverbank, in the hills or by the shore, while the family visited a stone at a temple
or a hall in the village. In Rokugan the dead are cremated and their bones interred, and the country monk who keeps the village's shrine
performs the rites, so the village's one ground lies in the shrine's yard or on ground of its own at the edge of the houses.

The graves were small, the simplest at one excavated Edo graveyard mostly under 5 ft long. A burial grave was marked
with no more than a stone, and a ground might be divided into plots by household or by age or kept to no rule; none seems
to have followed a surveyed plan. By the Edo period six small stone jizo stood in a row at a graveyard's entrance. In
Rokugan, families weed and clean their ancestors' graves at the spring and autumn festivals.

How large a village's ground was is not recorded; it filled as its inhabitants died, some 18 to 30 in a thousand a year in
three Edo farming villages.

Guesses:
- Its size on the map, 7.5 to 18 sq ft for each inhabitant it serves: no premodern ground's area is recorded, so it is
  reckoned from the Edo death rates, an urn plot of 10 to 20 sq ft and a plot reused after about 30 years, neither of which
  is recorded.
- That a village's ground lies in the shrine's yard or apart from it at even odds: both forms are recorded, but not how many
  villages took each.
- That a village whose shrine stands on a hill always keeps its ground apart: no source says whether graves climbed a hill
  shrine's slope.
- That a ground apart lies downstream of the houses: that side is recorded only in villages as they stand today.

Depiction: The map draws one burial ground for each village, at its real size: an irregular patch of earth set with rows of
low grave markers and a taller memorial stone or two, with six stone jizo at its entrance, each drawn at least large enough to
be seen, though a real one stood about 2 ft tall. It lies in the yard of the village's shrine or apart from it, just beyond the last of the houses on the higher
ground to hand, never in water or a flooded field, and at no set distance, none being recorded from before modern times;
which form a village takes differs from one village to the next. A hamlet draws none: its dead lie in the main village's
ground.
