About: Wet rice was made from marsh. The earliest farmed rice in China grew on marshy ground near rivers, and farmers
went on making paddy by draining marsh, behind high dikes in China and on the low plains of Japan's rivers. Where the
draining stopped, the ground stayed wetland, and paddy let go turned back into it. Below a village's fields the wet
ground lay where the water came back to the surface: along the springs at the foot of an alluvial fan, or in the
backswamp behind a river's natural banks. Villages cut reed and grass for thatch every year from ground kept for it,
wetlands among it, used in common.

A marsh did not end in a line: from open water it graded through reed, then sedge and wet meadow, to dry ground. On
Lake Biwa the reed was cut each winter and its stubble burned in spring, and the cutting kept the bed open; left uncut,
old reed built up into soil and willow and alder grew on it. A village's own marsh was very likely worked the same way.
At a reservoir pond the reed, cattail and wild rice stood in the shallows and stopped at the foot of the embankment,
which was kept clear for its strength; a tended pond carried more kinds of water plant at its shore, not fewer.

The wet ground below a fan seems to have run as wide as the fan itself, following its spring line round its foot.

Guesses:
- That the marsh reaches a little past the fields on each side: no source gives how far seepage spread at a fan's

Depiction: The map draws the marsh on the undrained low ground below the fields, its upper edge following the foot of
the fields, and builds nothing on it. It runs straight along the slope rather than in the arc of a real fan's foot, its
reeds thinning toward the margin and its ends bowing outward downhill so the straight run does not read as an edge. The
reeds are one small block repeated over a pale wet haze; where the marsh meets the rough grass a narrow mixed band lets
grass and reed thin into each other along a rounded, waving edge, and no brush or pine stands inside. Where a stream or
ditch crosses it, the reeds run to the water and a band of denser, darker reeds lines each bank so the water stays easy
to follow. All of these are drawing conventions. A pond's reeds stand on the same wet haze, stop at the foot of its
embankment, and are drawn uncut, with no cut bed or drying racks, since no harvest is recorded at a pond.
