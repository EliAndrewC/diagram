About: A marsh was the wet ground below a village's fields: along the springs where the water that sank into an
alluvial fan came back up at its foot, or in the backswamp behind a river's natural banks.
Villages cut reed and grass for thatch every year from common ground kept for it, wetlands among it; reed stems also
made blinds. Wet rice was made from marsh: the earliest farmed rice in China grew on marshy ground near rivers, and
paddy was made by draining marsh, behind high dikes in China and on the low plains of Japan's
rivers. Where the draining stopped, the ground stayed wetland, and paddy let go turned back into it.

A marsh did not end in a line: from open water it graded through reed, then sedge and wet meadow, to dry ground. On
Lake Biwa the reed was cut each winter and its stubble burned in spring, and the cutting kept the bed open; left uncut,
old reed built up into soil and willow and alder grew on it. A village's own marsh was very likely worked the same way.
At a reservoir pond the reed, cattail and wild rice stood in the shallows and stopped at the foot of the embankment,
which was kept clear for its strength; a tended pond carried more kinds of water plant, not fewer.

The wet ground below a fan seems to have run as wide as the fan itself, following its spring line round its foot.

Depiction: The map draws the marsh on the undrained low ground below the fields, its upper edge following the foot of
the fields, and builds nothing on it, the ground being too wet. It runs straight along the slope rather than in the arc
of a real fan's foot, a simplification: its reeds thin toward the margin and its ends bow outward downhill so the
straight run does not read as an edge. The reeds are one small block repeated over a pale wet haze. Where the marsh
meets the rough grass, a narrow mixed band lets grass and reed thin into each other along a rounded, waving edge - a
drawing convention for the sedge and wet meadow that lay between the reed and dry ground - and no brush or pine stands
inside. Where a stream or ditch crosses it, the reeds run to the water, and a band of denser, darker reeds lines each
bank so the water stays easy to follow, another convention. A pond's reeds stand on the same wet haze and stop at the
foot of its embankment; no reed harvest is drawn at a pond, since none is recorded there.
