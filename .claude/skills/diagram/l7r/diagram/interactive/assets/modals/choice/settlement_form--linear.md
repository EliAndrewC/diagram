About: A row village, the resson, stood its houses one beside the next in a long line, on one side of the line or on both.
The line was a road, or the land's own: a river's natural levee, the spring line at a hill's foot, or the dike of land
reclaimed from the sea. On the new fields opened in the Edo period the road was laid first and the lots
set along it; on a levee or a dike the dry ground set the line. Villages in the hills of southwest Zhejiang, in China, grew
along rivers and roads in the same way.

On the planned rows of the Musashino plateau each farm fronted its road, house lot first, then its field running back in a
long strip, then woodland, so neighbors stood lot against lot, each house in its grove. On reclaimed ground a dike row's land
lay near the house and compact.

The measured rows are planned colonies. A farm there fronted its road for about 54 to 240 ft, and at Santome, opened in
1694-1696, each strip ran back about 2,240 ft; Santome held between 172 and 180 farms on roads laid like a go board, so
probably many rows rather than one. Which line was commoner, whether one side or both, and how long a row that grew by itself ran are not
recorded.

Guesses:
- How often a settlement is a row: two in ten on the maps, the fewest of the three forms, because the rows whose layout is
  measured were planned colonies; no share of the forms was found.
- Whether a row follows a street laid first or the dry edge of the paddy, and whether its farms take one side or both, at
  even odds: both forms of each are attested and none is counted against the other.
- Whether a row's farms dig a well each or share wells along the street, at even odds: no account says which a row on low
  ground did.

Depiction: The map strings the farms along a street, on one side or both, the street running on off the map as the road the
row stands on. A street laid first is drawn straight; a row along the dry edge curves with the field's margin, which stands
for a levee or a fan's foot; on low ground behind dikes the row always takes the dry edge, standing for the dike. The farms
stand one farmstead apart, lot against lot, never more than about 240 ft apart. The street is drawn 6 ft wide, a little wider than
the lanes off it; Santome's real roads were about 36 ft. When a street is full, the map lays another beside it with its own row, never a
second rank behind the first. On a two-sided row, the far farms have their holding drawn behind them as dry field; each
farm keeps its grove on its windward sides.
