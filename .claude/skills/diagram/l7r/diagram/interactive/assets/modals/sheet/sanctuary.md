About: The sanctuary, the honden, was where a shrine's kami was kept: a small closed building at the back of the shrine,
behind the hall where the villagers prayed and on the same line. It held the kami's emblem, usually a sacred mirror, and no
one but a priest went inside. A shrine often kept several kami, one of them the main one, housed together in one sanctuary or
each given a bay of its own; which was usual at a village shrine is not recorded. In Rokugan the deity a village shrine
houses is a Fortune.

The commonest form of sanctuary across Japan was the "flowing-roof" style, nagare-zukuri, most often one bay wide or three.
A measured village sanctuary of the late Edo period is of plain squared timber with almost no ornament. A small sanctuary
was sometimes housed under a covering building, to keep off the wind and rain. No fence ringed a village shrine's
sanctuary: before modern times one is known only where a lord or a great shrine built it.

A village shrine's sanctuary was tiny. That late-Edo one measures about 6 ft square, a fifth of the width of its worship
hall; another village shrine's, two bays by one, comes to about 12 by 6 ft.

Depiction: The plan draws the sanctuary about 6 ft square, one bay wide, the size of the measured village sanctuary, behind
the hall on the approach's line, in a small clearing of the shrine's wood, and names the Fortune it serves beside it. Two of
these are drawing conventions. That the approach runs on the same line as the hall and the sanctuary: a sanctuary stood
behind its hall on the hall's line, but where a village shrine's approach ran is not recorded. And the clearing, which keeps
the building clear of the trees so it shows; a shrine's wood was kept around its sanctuary. The plan never draws a
sanctuary under a covering building with no worship hall before it, because the worship hall's roof also houses the country
monk who keeps the shrine.
