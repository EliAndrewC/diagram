About: A frame weir held a brook back with a framework of stakes and logs, its gaps packed with clay. One was dug up
at the Kodera site, from the Kofun period: stakes driven at a slant into the stream
bed, logs laid along them and more stakes driven to hold the logs, the joints tied with wisteria vine and the gaps packed
with clay and gravel, the clay held down with woven grass. A fence in an 8th-century poem may
have been the same build. A hamlet set such a weir in its brook where the water fell too low for the intake to
draw it, and built it of the wood and earth that lay near; the first weirs were too fragile to stand anywhere but on small
streams.

A visitor saw a low bank of stakes, logs and packed clay across the water, often slanting upstream from the intake mouth,
as many old weirs ran: set so, it dammed the shallow riffle and stood off the fastest flood water. Old weirs mostly closed
only half a stream.

The Kodera weir stood in three tiers of stakes and crosspieces, about 14 ft (4.2 m) wide. How thick a village's frame weir
was, and how many small intakes had a weir at all, is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That a hamlet's weir is a frame, at even odds with the other three forms: no count of the forms was found.
- Its thickness on the map, about 5 ft: no thickness of a village weir was found, only of large river works.

Depiction: Like every weir on the map, the frame closes the brook from bank to bank, so that it can be seen, though
half-river closures were the common old form, and it slants upstream from the intake mouth.

Name: The weir: a timber frame
