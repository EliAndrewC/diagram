About: Small, irregular paddies were the ordinary rice land of Japan before modern times. Lords of manors, local families
and leading farmers opened the land piece by piece in small projects - a narrow strip of paddy watered from a pond built
in a ravine, terraces up a hillside - and each plot was fitted to its own patch of ground and water, so plots of every
size and shape lay side by side, and a household's paddy lay scattered over many of them.

On the ground such a field was a patchwork of level basins, each walled by a low bank of its own mud, stepping down with
the lie of the land. On a hillside each bed was held up by a riser of earth or stones 3 to 5 ft high and made as wide as
the slope allowed, usually 7 to 20 ft.

The smallest paddies were tiny. At Shiroyone Senmaida, on the Noto peninsula, 1,004 paddies cover about 10 acres of
slope, averaging about 200 sq ft (18 sq m), the smallest about 20 in square; an old counting song has the last paddy
found hidden under a farmer's straw raincoat. The excavated paddies of Yayoi and Kofun Japan, well over a thousand years
earlier, were mostly under about 1,100 sq ft (100 sq m), hundreds or thousands of them together.

Guesses:
- That a small-plotted field's basins are drawn at about seven-tenths the size of an ordinary basin: no average size of a
  small paddy before modern times was found, so the figure is set below the ordinary basin, itself reckoned.
- How often a settlement's paddies come out small rather than middling, large or in strips: no source counts how many
  villages had which.

Depiction: The map draws a small-plotted field as the same irregular patchwork as any other, with smaller basins and more
of them, each at its real size in feet. Since a real terraced paddy could be a few feet across, no basin is left off for
being small; only a sliver too narrow to level and wall is taken into the basin beside it.
