About: A farm's bath, where it had one, was a small room of the main house where the household bathed in hot water, and most
often it stood out beyond the stable wing that projected from the house's earth floor. In two villages in the 1820s and
1840s, 17 of one's 21 baths and 12 of the other's 15 stood there. One reason may have been that the bath's drain water
also served as manure.

It was a luxury, since hot water at home meant a well, fuel and a risk of fire. Home baths spread late in the Edo period,
partly as coopered tubs for hot water came to be mass-produced. The room was 1 to 2 tsubo, about 6 by 6 to 6 by 12 ft; what
its walls and floor were like is not recorded.

By 1824 and 1842 two or three houses in ten had a bath, where in 1770 and 1771 only a very few did. Whether the baths beyond
the stable were joined to it or stood a step off is not recorded, and no farm bath is known to have stood as a building of its
own before 1868.

Depiction: The map draws a bath room, at the two or three homesteads in ten that keep one, as a small room joined to the far
end of the farmhouse's stable wing, and against its floored rooms only where that wall has no room; never as a shed standing
apart in the yard. A tub is drawn inside it so the room reads as a bath from above; the real tub stood under the house's
roof, unseen.
