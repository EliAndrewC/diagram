About: The garden of a samurai residence lay to the south of its formal rooms, before them, and was made to be looked at, from the room that
faced it or from its veranda. Its water took one of two forms: a pond garden, built around real water, or a dry landscape
(karesansui), which showed water with set stones and gravel and used none. Around the water a garden held set stones, made
hills and planting, with stepping stones, stone lanterns and fences.

Both forms stood beside samurai rooms. The two government offices whose gardens are known, the office of the
shogunate's intendants at Takayama and the seat of a small domain at Okutono, both kept pond gardens; Okutono's lay on two
levels, the lower one seen from the formal room. Among the seven samurai-house gardens kept at Chiran, one is a pond
garden and six are dry. The house of a middle-rank district magistrate at Matsushiro has a pond in its grounds today.

How large a residence garden's pond was is not recorded. A temple's dry garden beside its formal room, at the Daisen-in,
is a level plot of about 1,100 sq ft (100 sq m).

Guesses:
- The pond's form and size: no residence pond's size is recorded, so it is drawn small, within sight of the rooms that face
  the garden.
- How often a small posting kept a pond rather than a dry garden: no figure weighs the two, and the pond is taken as the
  grander form, since both offices known to have one were far grander than a county post.
- A pond on the way from the gate to the entrance, where a plan draws one: no account describes one there.

Depiction: Each plan takes one of the two forms, a small open oval of water or a dry garden of white gravel and stones,
set among the planting within sight of the rooms that face it; the plan's notes say which.

Name: garden pond
Covers: the pond, or the dry garden, in the inner garden
