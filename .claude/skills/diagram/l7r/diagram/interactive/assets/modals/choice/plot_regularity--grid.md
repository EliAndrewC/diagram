About: A grid of fields came from a survey. The alluvial plains of the Kinki, the Inland Sea coast and northern Kyushu were
laid out in ancient times on the jori grid of paths, ditches and fields, whose paddies and canals went on working through
the medieval period; regular plots of that kind were widespread in western Japan up to the Meiji era. A grid needed a
planned field: the irregular patchwork that most villages' paddies grew into was not one.

The jori lines crossed at right angles at intervals of about 358 ft (1 cho, 109 m), usually north-south and east-west,
though not a few grids were tilted with the region or the ground. Within the squares, plots of a tan, about a quarter
acre, were laid out. The tidy grid of equal rectangles seen across Japan today is later: land consolidation, begun in
1872 and made law in 1899, squared the parcels so each touched a road and a channel. New land opened on a plan in the
Edo period was laid out in strips, and no grid of paddies is known from it.
