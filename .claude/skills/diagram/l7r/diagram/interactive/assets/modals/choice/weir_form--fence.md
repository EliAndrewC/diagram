About: A woven fence was the oldest way of holding back a brook at the mouth of a ditch: a row of stakes driven into the
stream bed, with brushwood or bamboo twined onto them from both sides. The fence, shigarami, was known by the 8th century,
when a poem imagined one laid across the Asuka River to dam it, and the oldest weirs dug up in Japan, from the end of the
Jomon period, are rows of stakes. A hamlet set one in its brook where the water fell too low for the intake to draw it, and
built it of the wood that lay near; so light a weir could stand only on small streams. One irrigation weir in Kagoshima,
built about 1638, is said to have begun either as stone or as plants woven onto wooden stakes, and its brushwood is still
laid anew every March.

A visitor saw a low, rough fence across the water, often slanting upstream from the intake mouth, as many old weirs ran:
set so, it dammed the shallow riffle and stood off the fastest flood water. It did not seal the brook; old weirs mostly
closed only half a stream.

A fence was as thick as its one row of stakes. How long or high a village's fence ran, and how many small intakes had one,
is not recorded.

Depiction: The map draws the fence about 1.5 ft thick, wider than its one row of stakes, so that it shows. Like every weir
on the map it closes the brook from bank to bank, so that it can be seen, though half-river closures were the common old
form, and it slants upstream from the intake mouth.
