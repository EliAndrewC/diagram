About: A privy, the kawaya or setchin, was in a samurai house part of the house: a urinal and a privy built as a pair,
attached to its principal buildings and in time set in a corner of the corridor, not a hut in the yard. A house might keep
two, an upper one for the head of the household and the men and a lower one for the women; a district magistrate's house,
built in 1794 and added to later, counts two. In townhouses and farmhouses a guest parlor had a privy at its rear, and one
domain's council hall had two upper privies, each with a board veranda. The row houses inside a compound had none each: a urinal and several privies were
gathered in a small building nearby.

What collected beneath a privy had a price. Night soil was the farmer's fertilizer, bought from a household by the year -
around Kanazawa a household of seven to ten sold its year's night soil for about 24 gallons (90 liters) of rice - and carted away by
collectors from outside; around Edo, farmers supplied firewood and charcoal to the castle and the daimyo estates for the
right to empty their privies.

What a samurai house's privy was built of, and how big it was, is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That the family's privy stood apart from the guests', toward the family's rooms: no source places a family privy in a
  samurai house before 1868, so this follows later town houses and a daimyo's retainers' row houses.
- About one privy to each part of the compound, three or four at a county office: no source counts them.
- Its size, 5 ft square for one seat: no source gives a privy's size.
- A hatch through the wall behind a privy that stands against the compound wall, for the collector: none is recorded.
- That a privy stands at least 15 ft from any well: no separation distance is recorded.
- A privy at a village shrine's dwelling for its country monk: no source names one at a village shrine, so it follows the
  farmhouse the dwelling is drawn as.
