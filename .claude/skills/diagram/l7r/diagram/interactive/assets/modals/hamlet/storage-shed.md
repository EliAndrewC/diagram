About: A storehouse (kura) was a farm's fireproof store, built to keep a household's goods safe from fire and theft;
one on Sado island held grain. Nobody lived in it, and it came to be a sign of wealth.

It was a small, solid block, commonly two stories high. Its walls were earth, laid coat after coat on a lattice of bamboo
and palm rope to 8 to 12 in (20 to 30 cm) thick and finished in lime plaster. It was roofed in tile - one headman-class farm's
storehouse had an earthen roof under its tiles - and by the end of the Edo period its doorway could be closed by a pair of
plastered leaves. In one district west of Edo storehouses stood in front of the house or behind it, on any side, and most
seem to have stood free of it.

The commonest size, in one district west of Edo, was about 15 by 18 ft; a village east of Edo had two of about 12 by 18
ft. A farm built one only once money had accumulated, so most farms had none. In that village of sixteen households, at
the end of the Edo period, two had a storehouse, both, it seems, among its leading families and with the second and third
largest houses, though the largest house had none. West of Edo, farms are said to have owned storehouses only from the last
years of the shogunate.

Guesses:
- Which of two farmhouses of the same size has the storehouse: the map draws lots between them, since nothing found says
  what set one household's storehouse apart from another's of the same size.

Depiction: The map gives a storehouse to about one farmhouse in eight, always the largest, so a well-off farm shows at a
glance; in fact storehouses went to large houses, but not strictly to the largest. It draws the
storehouse as a tiled annex against the farmhouse's shaded north wall or its west wall, leaving the sunny walls to the
garden, though real storehouses mostly stood free of the house. It is drawn at about a farm shed's size, so on a long
house it can be up to half again as long as a real storehouse. Every storehouse is tiled, even beside a thatched house, as
real storehouses were.
