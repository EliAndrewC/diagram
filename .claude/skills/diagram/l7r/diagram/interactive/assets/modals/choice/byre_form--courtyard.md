About: A farm that plowed with a beast kept an ox, a water buffalo or a horse - in Japan horses in the east and cattle in the
west, in China's rice country the ox and the water buffalo - and the beast lived with the household that kept it. Across most
of Japan its stall took a corner of the farmhouse's earth-floored work space: the inner stable, under the house's own roof.
In the Okayama region the cattle were mostly kept so, and in one mountain district of Hyogo partly because there was too
little flat land to spare for a separate shed.

In northern Japan's L-shaped farmhouse, the magariya, the stable was a wing of the house itself, warmed by the air from the
kitchen hearth; it was common among the better-off households, a headman's among them. In north China a wealthy courtyard
house often gave its east wing to a stable.

Not every household kept a beast: in one western province from the early 1700s only about half did, and fewer as time went
on. A household without one borrowed or hired one. How many households across the country kept the inner stable, against a
shed of its own, is not counted.

Guesses:
- That a settlement keeps its beasts indoors, in a shed in the yard, or in shared sheds, about six, three and one time in ten: no source counts the forms.
- That between 35 and 50 in every 100 households keep a beast: the top is the half one province's households kept in the early 1700s, the bottom the fewer that kept one later; how many fewer no source gives.

Depiction: A stall under the house's own roof cannot be seen from above, so the map draws it as a shed of about 16 by 11 ft
set against the farmhouse, an open-fronted stall under a plank roof; the beast really lived inside, in a corner of the earth
floor. Only the households that keep a beast have one.
