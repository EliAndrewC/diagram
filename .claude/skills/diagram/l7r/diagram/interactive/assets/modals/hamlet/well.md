About: A well (ido) was a shaft dug by hand to the water below, and most were shared, because a well cost a great deal
to dig. A whole village, or a group of households within one, drew from a common well, and the wellside was where the
neighbors met: the women gathered there to draw water, do the washing and talk. A samurai household could have a well of its
own inside its fence.

[settlement_form=dispersed] On one plain where the water lay deep and wells were hard to dig, scattered farms often led a
small channel off the irrigation water into their grounds instead, for cooking, washing and drinking.

The shaft was about 3 ft (1 m) across, wide enough for a digger to work in, with a frame of wood or stone round its mouth,
the curb. The bucket came up on a sweep, a pole worked like a
seesaw over a post with a weight at its butt, where the water stood shallow, and on a rope over a pulley hung from a frame
or a roof where it lay deep. Some wells had a roof on posts over them.

In one coastal village the wells were dug at about one to every five to ten houses, and the ground round many
of the common ones was paved with stones. One well could
serve several hundred: late-Qing Beijing had at least about 650 inhabitants to a well. The one curb found measured, undated,
is about 4 ft square.

Guesses:
- That the curb the marker stands for was about 4 ft square: no premodern curb was found measured, so the figure is fitted
  round a premodern shaft about 3 ft across.
- That about three wells in four on shallow water take a sweep and three in four on deep water a pulley frame, and that a
  valley floor holds shallow water and an upland deep: the two gears and the depth that chose between them are known, the
  shares and the landforms are not.
- That about one well in three has a roof: no count of roofs over ordinary wells was found.
- The paved apron's width, about 4 ft round the curb: the paving is known, its width is not.
- That a village's well may stand on a lane's verge: wayside wells are known only in general, not in villages.
- [settlement_form=dispersed] That a scattered farm draws a channel or a well of its own at even odds, and that the channel
  ends in its dooryard: no source says how many areas used each, or where a channel ended.

Depiction: The map draws a well as a marker much larger than life, its curb about 19 ft across, so a reader can find it:
the marker shows where the well stands, not how much ground it takes. It is a square curb on a paved apron with a sweep or a
pulley frame over it and, on some, a roof. Wells stand among the houses they serve, never in the fields or on open common
ground, and the map draws more of them than a real village counted, so that no household is left far from water. A shrine
set apart from the houses keeps a well of its own, and a hilltop shrine none.

[settlement_form=dispersed] A scattered farm draws a well of its own in its dooryard, or a small channel led off the nearest
irrigation ditch, round its buildings and into its grounds.
