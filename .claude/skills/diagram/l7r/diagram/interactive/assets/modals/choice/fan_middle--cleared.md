About: The middle of an alluvial fan, where the river sinks into its gravels and the water lies deep, was hard to use as
paddy, and across most of Japan it was long left wild. But that was a tendency, not a rule: in old heartlands such as the
Kinki and the Kofu basin, fans were cleared from early times, and elsewhere the clearing came from the end of the Edo
period into the Meiji era.

Cleared, the middle of a fan took crops that needed little water. It was dry field, wheat and barley among its crops,
and orchards; before the Second World War much of it became mulberry fields, above all in northern Kanto, and fruit
trees came after. Where canals could be brought onto it, a whole fan could be turned to rice: the Tedori fan on the Sea
of Japan coast, some 58 square miles (150 sq km), is almost wholly paddy.

Fans could be large. The Isawa fan, about 49,000 acres (20,000 ha) and one of the largest in Japan, was opened for fields
by order of the Sendai domain, with water drawn from its river through some 27 miles (43 km) of canals.
