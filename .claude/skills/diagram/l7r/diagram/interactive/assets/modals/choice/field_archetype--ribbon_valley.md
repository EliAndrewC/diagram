About: Where a stream had cut a narrow valley among hills, the paddy ran with the valley instead of filling a broad floor: the
yachida, a long, narrow paddy along the valley's wet bottom. Such paddies were made all over Japan, opened in small pieces
and watered by the valley's own spring water, and in Japan's middle ages, before the Edo period, they were its typical farm
landscape. They went by many local names, yatoda and sakoda among them. In the late Heian period a narrow valley probably
held one house or a few, each beside its own small valley paddy.

A visitor walking up such a valley saw a ribbon of paddy on its floor, hemmed in by the slopes on either side. The ground was
generally wet, and many valley paddies, lacking a good source of water, came to depend on rain alone. A basin flooded by hand
to an even depth can only be so large, so the ribbon was probably a chain of small paddies rather than one long sheet.

How long or wide a valley paddy ran, and how much rice it gave, is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That the brook ran down the middle of the paddy: every account places the paddy on the valley floor, and none says where
  the stream ran through it.

Depiction: The map draws the rice as one long, narrow field meandering down the valley floor. Banks across the field step it
down the valley, each holding one level stretch above the next, so the field holds water as the valley falls, and each
stretch is split across its width into small paddies, so the field reads as a chain of small paddies rather than one sheet. A drain runs across the foot of the field, gathering what runs out
of the lowest paddies, narrow where it starts and full where it leaves for the brook. The lowest stretches are tinted
blue-green as wet paddies, ground that stayed waterlogged; which plots take the tint is a convention of the drawing, not a
survey.
