About: Where a stream had cut a narrow valley among hills, the paddy ran with the valley instead of filling a broad floor: the
yachida, a long, narrow paddy along the valley's wet bottom. Such paddies were made all over Japan, opened in small pieces
and watered by the valley's own spring water, and one view holds that they were the most stable, standard farmland of
Japan's middle ages, before the Edo period. They went by many local names: yatoda or yachida in the east, tanada or yamada
around the old capital, sakoda further west.

A visitor walking up such a valley saw a ribbon of paddy on its floor, hemmed in by the slopes on either side. A basin
flooded by hand to an even depth can only be so large, so the ribbon was probably a chain of small paddies rather than one
long sheet.

How long or wide a valley paddy ran, and how much rice it gave, is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That the brook ran down the middle of the paddy: every account places the paddy on the valley floor, and none says where
  the stream ran through it.

Depiction: The map draws the rice as one long, narrow field meandering down the valley floor, with the valley's brook down
its middle. Banks across the field step it down the valley, each holding one level stretch above the next, so the field
holds water as the valley falls, and each stretch is split across its width into small paddies, sized like terrace paddies
on a slope as steep as the valley floor.
