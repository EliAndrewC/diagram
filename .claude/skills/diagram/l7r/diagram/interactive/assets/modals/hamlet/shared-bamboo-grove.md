About: Apart from the farmsteads, bamboo stood in thickets of its own, the take-yabu, "bamboo thicket". Farming households
used bamboo for baskets, fans, tools, building and food. In the Edo period the countryside round Kyoto was painted with its
settlements ringed by bamboo, and the villages of one district southwest of the city owed bamboo as a tax from the start of
the period, managed their own groves, and had farmers who kept bamboo shops on the side. Who held a village's thicket is not
recorded.

A thicket wanted high, dry ground: as early as the sixth century, Chinese farmers were taught to set bamboo on high, level
ground, near hills best, for in a low field it died once water reached it. Many thickets still stand on the outskirts of the
plain, where it meets the worked hills.

A thicket is one plant spreading by shoots from an underground rootstock: its stems are clones of that plant, a stand is most
often nearly one species, and it shades out almost everything else. Madake, the common timber bamboo, grows canes up to about
66 ft (20 m) tall and at most about 4 in (10 cm) across; the thickets painted round Kyoto were probably madake or another
bamboo, hachiku. Kept as a resource, a thicket was cut in moderation and renewed itself from its own shoots.

How large a village's thicket ran is not recorded.

Guesses:
- Its size, about 84 by 58 ft, a working stand: no source gives the size of a village's thicket.
- That it stands just beyond the back row of houses: no source says which side of a settlement a thicket took, beyond high,
  dry ground.
- That the thicket belongs to the village as a whole: no source says who held one, of any period.

Depiction: The map draws the thicket as an area true to scale in its place and size, filled with paired cane strokes and
a leafy fork in jade green over a light wash, one small block of marks repeated across it, as it draws a farm's bamboo;
a real cane, a few inches across, is far too slim to see at the map's scale. The mark is the one modern Japanese map
legends give bamboo; before then map-makers painted the growth itself. The thicket stands on dry ground just beyond the back row of
houses, never on a paddy. Whether a settlement keeps a thicket, bamboo on its farms, or both, the title card says.
