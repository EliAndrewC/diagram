About: A manure pit was an earthenware jar sunk in the ground, in which a household kept its night soil to rot before it
went out to the fields; fresh night soil harmed the crop, and in the silk villages human manure was a farm's most important fertilizer.
Some households used a plastered pit instead, which looked like a well. The jars stood behind the houses, about the farm
and along paths and roads, where a laborer or a passer-by could use one. Some were kept out by the fields instead: in
south China a brick-lined pit at the head of the field by the 1300s, and in Japan a pit by the fields, probably moved out
from beside the privy to make manuring easier.

What showed was the jar's open mouth: some jars were buried to the rim, others only half buried, their upper half standing
above the ground. The jar was often
unglazed earthenware, and it had to hold its liquid without leaking.

Its mouth was often about 3 to 5 ft (1 to 1.5 m) across, and one Ming-dynasty farm manual wanted its pits deep and large.
How many households kept theirs out by the fields varied widely from village to village: in three Japanese villages
surveyed in 1959, from 2 households of 83 in one to 15 of 18 in another.

Guesses:
- That a settlement keeps its manure in sunk jars or in heaps at even odds: both forms are recorded, but no source says
  how many villages used each.
- That a sunk jar was kept out by the fields as often as Japan's field pit was, from 2 to 85 households in 100 by
  settlement: the share is recorded only for Japan's field pit, and the field-head pits of south China were lined with
  brick, not jars.

Depiction: The map draws each jar as its mouth seen from above, a dark disc inside a pale rim, flush with the ground; a
jar buried to its rim and one half buried look the same from above, so both are drawn as one mouth, a map drawing
convention. A jar stands beside its household's privy, or out at the edge of the household's nearest field or by a road;
how many stand out by the fields differs from settlement to settlement.
