About: The office hall was the working building of a magistrate's compound, where the office's public business was
done and its cases heard. At Takayama, the one intendant's office that survives of more than fifty, the entrance hall,
the examination room, the working office and the great hall were rebuilt together in 1816, most likely as one block,
with the roofed hearing court against it; the clerks hired from the district worked in a room partitioned from that of
the officials sent by the shogunate. The magistrate's household lived apart, in a residence of its own, and the junior
staff in huts and rowhouses on the grounds.

Takayama's office is a single-story timber hall roofed with wooden shingles, built with great formality to show the
office's rank: its entrance room keeps a great alcove marking a rank of 100,000 koku and a wave pattern even daimyo
refrained from using. Wooden halls burned - the Sado magistracy burned and was rebuilt five times, though Takayama's
never did.

Takayama's office has a floor of about 8,100 sq ft, a quarter again the size of the intendant's residence, while a small
branch office's whole central building was about 2,850 sq ft; a county magistrate's hall probably fell between the two.
How long and how deep any office hall ran is not recorded.

Guesses:
- Its size on the plan, about 80 to 150 ft long and 20 to 45 ft deep: no account gives an office hall's length and depth,
  so the floor is set below Takayama's and, in all but its smallest form, above the branch office's.
- That the day office and the official study stood behind the dais: no account places those rooms behind the court; they
  are reckoned from what a working office had to hold.

Depiction: The plan draws the office hall as one long building in the outer court, its front band the magistrate's dais
over the roofed hearing court, and behind the dais the day office, the official study and the clerks' room, each its own
feature. It is the largest of the halls, larger than the residence, as Takayama's office was. The plan puts the office in
front and the residence behind it, the Chinese county office's order, a simplification for the Japanese form: at Takayama
the residence stood beside the office, on the lot to the west. Its buildings stand a little further apart than a real
office's did, so each reads on its own.
