About: The monk's rooms were where the shrine's monk lived, the living end of the monk's dwelling, the kuri, whose other end
was its earth-floored kitchen. A temple's kuri was "the place where a temple's monks live"; it began as a kitchen, and at an
ordinary temple it often served as the temple's office as well. Before 1868 the monk of a shrine with a temple attached lived
at the temple. In Rokugan the shrine's keeper is the village district's country monk, who lives on a plot of tax-free land
and may have a few acolytes, lent part-time by the village's larger families to help farm it.

At an ordinary temple the kuri was much like an ordinary house, and one that was measured resembles its region's
farmhouse, whose rooms stood on a raised floor beside an earth-floored end. At one temple of standing, in
Kawasaki, the head priest's living room was a room of twelve mats.

The rooms were one monk's home; whether acolytes slept in them is not recorded. The smallest kuri measured, at a small Koshin hall in a Fukuoka farming town,
was about 12 by 15 ft; those of temples of standing ran far larger, about 54 by 30 ft at Chonen-ji and about 72 by 48 ft at
Tokuun-ji.

Guesses:
- The rooms' size on the plan, which with the kitchen end comes to about a farmhouse's floor: the one small kuri measured,
  at the Koshin hall, stood apart from its hall, and no dwelling under its hall's roof was found measured at a village, so
  the dwelling is reckoned from the farmhouse it resembled.

Depiction: The plan draws the monk's rooms at one end of the one building, under the same roof as the villagers' hall,
because at a village shrine one building serves as both the monk's home and the place the villagers come to; a hall and
dwelling under one roof is on record, though rarely. The district's registers are kept in a writing room in the dwelling,
so no office building is drawn.
