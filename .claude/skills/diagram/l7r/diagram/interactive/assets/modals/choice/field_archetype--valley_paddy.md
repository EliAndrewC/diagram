About: Wet rice needs a level basin that holds water, and where a valley opened out among hills its floor was probably the
cheapest place to make one. Before modern times Japan's paddies there were small, irregular plots fitted to the lie of the
land and its water, opened piece by piece by lords of manors, local families and leading farmers, and each household's
paddy lay scattered over many of them. The tidy grid of equal rectangles seen today came only with land consolidation,
begun in 1872 and made law in 1899.

The water came by gravity from a river or from a pond set above the fields, down a head race that split into smaller and
smaller canals. Canals ran along the high ground, since water waters only what lies below it, and the water left by a drain
along the lowest line: when the Minuma reservoir was reclaimed in 1728, its supply canals were set along the edges and its
drain down the middle. In the old form the water went on from one paddy to the next rather than down a ditch to each.

A paddy parcel in the land surveys of the 1600s ran from about a tenth of an acre to under half an acre. In the Edo period
a village needed a little under a quarter acre of paddy for each inhabitant, and in the early 1900s a farm family worked
about 2.5 acres in all.

Depiction: The map lays the water out first and cuts the paddies between its lines, in a comb: a head race forking into two
supply canals along the high flanks of the fields, ditches leaving them at right angles down the slope, and one drain
gathering the water along the lowest line. Each canal narrows as its branches leave it, and no plot gets a ditch of its own;
below the last ditch the water passes from plot to plot. The plots are an irregular patchwork, and the field is sized to the
mouths it feeds.
