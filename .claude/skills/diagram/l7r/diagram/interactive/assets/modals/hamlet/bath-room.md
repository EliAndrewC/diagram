About: A farm's bath, where it had one, was a small room of the main house where the household bathed in hot water.
It was a luxury, since hot water at home meant a well, fuel and a risk of fire: a farm manual of about 1680 has the household
wash instead at a fixed place over a pit laid with bamboo slats, the water collecting to thin the manure. Home baths spread
late in the Edo period, partly as coopered tubs for hot water came to be mass-produced.

The room stood under the house's eaves beside its main door, or, most often, out beyond the stable wing that projected from
the earth floor, where its drain water may also have served as manure. A few, most of them in headmen's houses, were joined
to the raised, wood-floored living rooms. What its walls and floor were like is not recorded; the iron cauldron bath, the
goemon-buro, was later widely used in farm villages, though since when is not recorded.

The room was 1 to 2 tsubo, about 6 by 6 to 6 by 12 ft. In the earliest village house-plan registers to show one, of 1770 and
1771, only a very few houses had a bath; by 1824 and 1842 two or three houses in ten did. No farm bath
standing as a building of its own is found before 1868: the bath shed in the yard came in the Meiji period and after.

Guesses:
- That a settlement's bath rooms stand all beside the main door or all at the end of the stable wing, at even odds: both
  places are recorded, but not how often each was used from one village to the next.

Depiction: The map draws a bath room as a small room joined to the farmhouse, beside its main door or at the far end of its
stable wing, and against its floored rooms only where neither of those walls has room; never as a shed standing apart in the
yard. A tub is drawn inside it so the room reads as a bath from above; the real tub stood under the house's roof, unseen.
