About: In a clustered village the houses did not all stand on the main lane, and those set back from it had to be reached
some other way. One answer was side alleys: narrow ways branching off the main lane between the house plots to the houses
behind. Enoshima, a village crowded into a ravine, reached the houses behind its street by the narrowest of alleys, and a
traditional Manchu village in northeast China ran small blind alleys off its streets to its houses. Side lanes like these
suggest a village that grew over time, each household cutting its own way to the lane, rather than one laid out at once.

An alley was a strip of bare earth, trodden hard and muddy in rain, made for feet, carrying poles and the odd barrow or
packhorse. It was narrow and seldom straight; the households beside it probably took it over as space of their own. The
Manchu village's blind alleys were 7 to 13 ft (2 to 4 m) wide.

Not every house had a way of its own: in Japan a household whose land had no way to the road crossed a neighbor's land by
custom. How common side alleys were, against a back lane, is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That side alleys mean a village that grew, and a back lane one that was planned: a reading of the two forms, not the finding of any one source.
- That a settlement's lanes take side alleys or a back lane at even odds: no source counts villages of either form.

Depiction: The map lays the alleys after the houses are seated, each leaving a dooryard and running along the middle of the
gap between two plots to the main lane or an earlier way, joined at a T, and pulled as straight as the plots allow. A few
households may be reached across a neighbor's yard with no alley of their own. Alleys are drawn at the narrow width of a field
path, narrower than the measured Manchu alleys, since no lane in a wet-rice village was measured.
