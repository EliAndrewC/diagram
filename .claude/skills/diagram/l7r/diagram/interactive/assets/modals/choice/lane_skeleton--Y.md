About: A Y of lanes is two ways coming in and merging into one stem that runs on through a clustered village's houses.
A clustered village's shape followed its ground and its roads: houses gathered in a lump at a hill's foot or on a river
terrace, or strung out in a line along a natural levee, a spring line or a road. Out among the paddies the way ran on along the bunds between the plots, on a path the farmers called an
azemichi.

Each lane was a strip of bare earth, trodden hard and muddy in rain, made for feet, carrying poles and the odd barrow or
packhorse rather than carts. How wide a village lane ran is not recorded; the one old figure for a field road is about 3 ft.

The villages of China's northern plain ran two or three main streets east and west, with lesser lanes north and south;
beyond that, what shapes a village's lanes took, and how common each was, is not
recorded.

Guesses:
- That a village's lanes took one of a few shapes - a spine, a T, a Y, a crossroads or a lane along the water - and that a Y formed where two approaches merged, as a worn path takes the shortest or the easiest way and two such ways join where their walkers' routes run together: no source names these shapes or says where each formed.
- How often each shape comes up: no source counts them.

Depiction: The map seats the houses first and lays the Y after, fitted to where they stand: two arms from the upper side of
the cluster joining in a stem that runs down to its foot; the households' own ways join it. Each arm is drawn as a track of
bare earth a little wider than a footpath - a width of the map's choosing, since no village lane's width is recorded - pulled
as straight as the plots allow.
