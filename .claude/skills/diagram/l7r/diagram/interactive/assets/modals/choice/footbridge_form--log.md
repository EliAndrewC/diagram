About: The simplest crossing over small water was a single log, or a single board, laid from bank to bank. A Japanese
dictionary defines the log bridge as "just a single log laid across", and the one-log bridge as one laid across a brook
or a mountain stream; the Chinese single-log bridge, of one tree trunk or one board, let walkers or livestock cross narrow
rivers and gorges. A farm household could lay one itself: in a Chinese rice village of modern times, criss-crossed by
ditches, a grandfather found four log sections and laid them across a canal as a simple bridge, a common practice there.
What crossed a paddy ditch in older times, a log, a plank or earth over logs, is not recorded.

A visitor crossed in single file on a bare round trunk, or a board; logs over a ditch were slippery in the rain.

How long a log a ditch crossing took, how thick it was, and how often a crossing stood along a ditch are not recorded;
a statement of proportion for Japan's river bridges says nothing of the single log.

Guesses:
- That a settlement's ditch crossings are all single logs, at even odds with earth over logs and a planked deck: no count
  of the forms over a farm ditch was found.

Depiction: Every ditch crossing on the map takes this form, drawn as one rounded, bark-brown trunk across the water. Each
is drawn about 4 ft wide and 8 ft long so it reads as a single-file crossing and not a road bridge. Where a lane or a road
crosses water, the map draws a planked deck as wide as the way, whatever form the ditch crossings take; over real rivers,
logs under earth were the likelier.
