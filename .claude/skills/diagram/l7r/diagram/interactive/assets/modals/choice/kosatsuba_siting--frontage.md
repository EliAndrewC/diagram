About: A notice board (kosatsuba) was the wooden frame on which the shogunate and the domains posted standing law, so that
everyone would know it, and it stood where the traffic was heaviest: at a town's central crossroads, at its entrances, at the
foot of bridges, at the barrier stations on the highways and at ports. In a village it stood at the center or the way in, or
before the gate of a village official's house. Boards were most often set where people passed, at the village center or at
a crossroads of main highways.

Those who set a board up weighed the passers-by it would meet. Nakatsugawa's board on the Nakasendo highway stood not where
its replica stands today but some 66 ft (20 m) up the slope, at the crossroads with the Hida road, so that more passers-by
would see it. Every site known is on a road or street, and none seems to have stood on open ground set back from it.

How many villages turned their board to the busiest frontage rather than to some other mark is not recorded; traffic was not
the only reason, for a village divided between two lords set its board at about the same distance from each half's headman's house.

Guesses:
- That a settlement's board seeks the busiest frontage or the place water is drawn at even odds: no source counts where
  village boards stood.

Depiction: Among the seats its other choice on the title card allows, the map takes the one with the most houses and buildings near it, so the board meets the most passers-by. It stands a few feet off the way's
edge, turned broadside to it, and is placed last, once everything else stands.
