About: Where a hamlet's brook ran too low for its ditch to draw water off the bank, the farmers built a weir (seki) across it at
the intake, to hold the water back and raise it until it turned aside into the head race; some places set one up for the
irrigation season alone. China, too, built intakes "with a dam and without", and a Chinese farm treatise of 1313 has a palisade
built across a brook where the fields lay high, "causing it to issue out at the side". In old Japan most weirs closed only half
the stream, and the brook ran on below its intake.

A village weir was built of what lay to hand: a fence of stakes woven with brushwood or bamboo, a frame of stakes and logs
packed with clay, or a crib of timber packed with stone, and up to the late 16th century most intake works were small cribs of
this kind. Many old weirs ran slantwise upstream from the intake mouth, damming the shallow riffle rather than the deep pool,
which kept them off the fastest water in a flood. Weirs on small mountain streams go back more than two thousand years.

How many small intakes had a weir is not recorded, though in old Japan intake weirs were hardly built at all. How thick a village weir was is not recorded either: a woven fence was as thick as its row of stakes.
