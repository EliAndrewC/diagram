About: A clustered village's shape followed its ground and its roads. Houses gathered in a lump at a hill's foot or on a
river terrace, or strung out in a line along a natural levee, a spring line or a road. A T of lanes is one lane running
through the houses and a second meeting it at one end. Out
among the paddies the way ran on along the bunds between the plots, on a path the farmers called an azemichi.

Each lane was a strip of bare earth, trodden hard and muddy in rain, made for feet, carrying poles and the odd barrow or
packhorse rather than carts. How wide a village lane ran is not recorded; the one old figure for a field road is about 3 ft.

What shapes a village's lanes took, and how common each was, is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That a village's lanes took one of a few shapes - a spine, a T, a Y, a crossroads or a lane along the water - and that a T formed where a spur met the field track: no description of the shapes a village's lanes took was found.
- How often each shape comes up: no source counts them.

Depiction: The map seats the houses first and lays the T after, fitted to where they stand: one lane through the cluster and
a crossbar meeting it; the households' own ways join it. Each arm is drawn as a track of bare earth a little wider than a
footpath - a width of the map's choosing, since no village lane's width is recorded - pulled as straight as the plots allow.
