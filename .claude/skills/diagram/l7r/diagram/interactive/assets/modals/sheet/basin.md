About: The basin held the water worshippers purified themselves with before they prayed, rinsing the hands and the mouth.
A rinsing place, the temizuya, stood beside a shrine's approach, and the rite of rinsing is very old.

The roofed pavilion over a basin is much newer than the rite. The one found carved with a date before 1868, of 1836, covers a
basin of 1826 at an Inari shrine in Saitama whose following reached across the Kanto, both given by a donor living in Edo.
Whether a village shrine's basin stood in the open before 1868 is not recorded; many shrines today, unstaffed ones in the
provinces especially, have no pavilion at all.

A basin was a gift. In 1828 the whole body of Nagao Shrine's parishioners, in Kawasaki, gave it a stone basin inscribed
with their gift. How big a village shrine's basin was is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That a village shrine's basin is a plain stone basin with no roof over it: neither the open basin nor the roofed one is
  recorded at a village shrine before 1868.
- Its size on the plan, about 3 ft square: no basin's size was found.

Depiction: The plan draws the basin as a small square of stone holding a disc of water, beside the approach near the
innermost arch, with no roof over it.

Name: basin
Covers: the stone basin by the approach
