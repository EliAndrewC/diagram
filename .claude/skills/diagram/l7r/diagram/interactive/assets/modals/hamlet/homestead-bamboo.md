About: Many farming households kept a stand of bamboo of their own, wherever the winters were mild enough for it; where
they were hard, moso and madake, the big bamboos, did not grow. Its canes made baskets and fans, its sheaths wrapped
food, its shoots were eaten, and it went into houses, river-bank works, barrel hoops and everyday tools. On the farm it
grew low under the tall trees of the household's grove, filling the bare space beneath their branches against the wind;
on some farms it stood beside the storehouses, and at houses by rivers and on flood-prone ground it was planted for its
roots, which held the soil.

A stand of bamboo is a thicket of its own, not stems scattered through a wood. It spreads by shoots from an underground
rootstock, its stems are clones of one plant, most often nearly one species, and it shades out almost everything else.
Madake, the common timber bamboo, grows canes up to about 66 ft (20 m) tall and at most about 4 in (10 cm) across.
On the Tonami plain the farm stands were madake, moso, hachiku and yadake.

How large a household's stand was is not recorded, nor what share of farms kept one. On the Tonami plain there were once
many stands in the farmstead groves, and in one hamlet there every homestead still kept one in the late 20th century; it
seems to have been common, but not universal.

Guesses:
- That about three farms in five keep a stand: no source gives a share, and bamboo named as one of several lesser plants
  of a farm's grove reads as common but not universal.
- Its size, about 22 by 16 ft, both as a strip beside the house and as a patch inside a farm's grove: no source gives
  one.
- Which side of the house it takes - behind the house most often, then the windward side, then beside the shed, the far
  flank least: the sources give it with the storehouses, at a wet edge and on the wind side, name no seat behind the
  house, and give no share for any.
- That a farm with its own grove keeps a patch on the house side of every windward band: in the one grove described
  side by side, the bamboo and the tall windbreak trees shared only the west.

Depiction: The map draws a stand as an area true to scale in its place and size, filled with paired cane strokes and a
leafy fork in jade green over a light wash, one small block of marks repeated across it; a real cane, a few inches
across, is far too slim to see at the map's scale, and the marks stand for the bamboo rather than count it. The mark is
the one modern Japanese map legends give bamboo, first found on survey maps of about 1910; before then map-makers painted the
growth itself and left no symbol to use instead. A farm's stand is a strip set with its sheds and garden, kept off its lanes
and well, and on the windward corner when the wind comes on a diagonal. Where a farm has its own grove, the bamboo is a
patch inside it, showing only between the crowns and along the edge, since a crown drawn from above hides what grew
beneath it. No stand is drawn on a paddy, where standing water kills bamboo. Whether a settlement's bamboo stands on its
farms, in a thicket of its own, or both, the title card says.
