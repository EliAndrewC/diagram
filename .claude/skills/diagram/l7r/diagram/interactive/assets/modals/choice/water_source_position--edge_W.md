About: A village's water could come straight from a stream, tapped where it came down across the edge of the valley above the fields. A paddy was watered by gravity, so the water had to come in on the high side; in this form the stream reached the fields from the west. The intake stood on one bank, the side of the fields it served. Where along the bank it stood was chosen for the water it could take: where the current's deepest line ran against the bank, in a straight reach or on the outside of a bend, and a Japanese treatise of 1902 put the head works where the river left the mountains.

Where the stream ran high enough, the intake was only an opening in its bank; where its level fell, a low weir of stakes and brushwood, timber or stone was set across it to raise the water. The stream ran on past the intake, tapped rather than swallowed. From the intake mouth a head race ran down to the head of the fields and forked into the supply canals, each narrowing as smaller ditches drew water off it.

How far a village's head race ran before it forked is not recorded. Small mountain canals ran for short distances; the canal that watered the third-century paddies at Toro, about 19 acres (7.5 ha), was more than 1,200 ft (370 m) long.

Guesses:
- That the stream comes in from the west rather than another side: no source says from which side a village's water reached its fields, so the side is chosen by chance among those above the fields.

Depiction: The map draws the stream coming down the slope to the middle of the fields' west side, where it feeds the head of the canals. A stream is only ever drawn coming in where its water could run down onto the fields: one entering below them is never drawn, because water does not run uphill.
