About: At the end of the Edo period a Japanese village's dead did not all lie in one kind of ground. A temple's graveyard, a
graveyard the settlement held in common, a lineage's ground and a single household's grave all stood across the country,
each left to local custom, and as temples multiplied the dead went more and more to temple graves. A household was registered
with the temple that buried its members, often a temple outside its own village. In this setting the hall that tends a
village district's dead is the main village's shrine, kept by the country monk who performs the funeral rites for the whole
district, and its hamlets' dead are burned at the main village's cremation ground and lie in the village's ground.

Where the dead had two graves, the body lay most often in common ground, on a riverbank, in the hills or by the shore, below
the houses and never above them. A small temple graveyard filled close: in one excavated in
Sagamihara, burials overlapped.

That every hamlet kept a burial ground of its own rests only on records of the twentieth century. How many hamlets' dead went
to a village ground rather than one of their own is not recorded.

Depiction: The map draws no burial ground or cremation ground for a hamlet: they lie with the main village, off the map. A
household's own grave may still stand on its land.
