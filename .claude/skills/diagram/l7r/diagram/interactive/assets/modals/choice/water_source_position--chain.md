About: Water could be let down to a village's fields through a chain of ponds. Water released from a pond up the valley was stored in ponds on the flat and from there let onto the fields, and ponds strung together in a terrace-like series had names of their own in Japan, the stacked pond or the parent-and-child pond. Each stood higher than the ground below it, so the water ran on by gravity, and in this form it came into the fields midway along their high edge.

Each pond was ringed by its own embankment, its water drawn off through a pipe under the bank into the ditch that led to the next. In China a long canal linking many ponds was called a melon-on-the-vine system: its head likened to the root, the canals to the vine, and the ponds along them to the melons; the earliest is placed in the Warring States period.

A great pond could feed many: Manno-ike in Shikoku, the largest irrigation pond in Japan, watered about 11,000 acres (4,600 ha), and some 60 smaller ponds took water from it. How many ponds a single village's chain held is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That a small village keeps a chain of ponds rather than one reservoir: the stacked pond is a named form, but no source shows one at a single village's scale.
- That the water comes in midway along the high edge rather than at a corner: no source says where on a village's high ground its ponds lay, so the place is chosen by chance among those above the fields, a corner preferred where the houses themselves hold the high edge.

Depiction: The water comes into the fields just beyond the middle of their high edge, uphill of them. A pond is only ever drawn where its water could run down onto the fields: one below the fields it feeds is never drawn, because water does not run uphill.
