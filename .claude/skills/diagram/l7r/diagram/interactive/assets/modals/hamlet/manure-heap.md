About: A farm's muck was its fertilizer, and it was rotted before it went onto the fields, since fresh dung harmed the crop.
Stable manure (kyuhi) was the dung of a household's cattle and horses, trodden by the beasts into straw and fallen leaves
until it rotted. The household's night soil was rotted too, in an earthenware jar buried in the ground or a plastered pit,
which stood beside the privy or out by the household's fields or a road, where manuring was easier.

Farm manuals of Song China and Edo-period Japan told the farmer to keep a low-eaved house for the manure, because muck left
under the sky lost its richness. Swept earth, ash, chaff, broken straw and fallen leaves went into it with the night soil and
the kitchen's and the bath's dirty water; where there were many beasts, their dung was piled layer on layer like a small
mountain. In China, night-soil jars were sunk to the rim beside paths and roads, and a southern farmer kept a brick-lined pit
at the head of his field. A buried Japanese jar was often about 3 to 5 ft (1 to 1.5 m) across the mouth.

How big a heap was, where in the yard it stood, and how many farms kept one before modern times are not recorded; the manuals
called for a manure house on every farm. How many households kept their night soil out by the fields varied widely by
village.

Guesses:
- That the heap stood just beyond the privy: no account says where in the yard it stood, and a Chinese latrine of the Han
  dynasty was built over the pigsty and drained to a cesspool, so privy and muck are taken to have stood together.
- Its size on the map, 8 by 6 ft: no account measures a heap.
- That 40 to 70 farmsteads in 100 keep a heap: no count of any date gives it, so the share is set below the manuals' manure
  house on every farm.
- That a settlement keeps its muck in heaps or in pits at even odds: both forms are recorded, but not how many villages used
  each.
- That a field pit lies within about 160 ft of its house, and its mouth 3.5 ft across: no account gives either.

Depiction: The map draws a household's muck in one of two forms, the same for every farm of a settlement: an open heap, a
small mound just beyond the privy on the side away from the house (behind or beside the house where there is no privy), or a
night-soil pit, beside the privy or out at the edge of a field or a road. A pit is drawn as a dark mouth in a pale rim,
whether the real jar was buried to the rim or half buried, since from above both are the same mouth in the ground. Where the
privy stands on the sunny side and a neighbor lies that way too, a heap may stand nearer the neighbor's house than its own;
a click on it says whose it is.
