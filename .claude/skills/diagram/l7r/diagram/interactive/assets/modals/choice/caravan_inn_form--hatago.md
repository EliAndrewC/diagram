About: A hatago was a Japanese travelers' inn that gave its guests their meals, where ordinary commoners on the road mostly
stayed, the mainstay of a post station on the highways; below it stood the cheap lodging where travelers cooked for
themselves. A hatago was built on the plan of a
townhouse: past the big street door an earth floor ran to the back, beside it the board-floored shop and the kitchen, with
the guest rooms upstairs or at the back. It was much like a lord's lodging cut down, without its audience room, gate or
formal entrance.

A visitor saw a house on the street, at Akasaka on the Tokaido usually two stories on the road front; on the late Edo
Tokaido a projecting lattice on the upper story marked a house as an inn. One inn at Okabe, rebuilt about 1836, still
stands: wooden, two stories and tile-roofed, its upper floor the guest rooms. An Akasaka inn also held formal rooms, a bath
and a privy. No stable or cart yard of its own is recorded at a hatago before 1868.

The Tokaido's fifty-three stations held nearly 3,000 inns between them, on average 55 a station, and 27 on the Nakasendo.
Most were small, about 18 ft wide on the street or less; a large one had 30 ft or more. One at Hiratsuka was 33 ft wide
with about 2,040 sq ft of building, and the Ohashiya at Akasaka ran about 54 ft wide and 137 ft deep.
