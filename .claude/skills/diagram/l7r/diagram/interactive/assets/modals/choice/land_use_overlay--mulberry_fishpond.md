About: A village could give some of its low rice land to dike-ponds: fish ponds dug out of hollows that flooded, the mud
heaped round each to make a dike planted with mulberry. The leaves fed silkworms, the silkworms' droppings fed the fish, and
the pond mud was dredged back onto the dike. Around Lake Tai and in the Pearl River delta, where floods and waterlogging
were frequent, farmers made them so; in the delta they arose in the early Ming and reached their height at the end of the
Qing.

Such a village showed ponds in patches among its rice on the low ground, each ringed by its planted bank, the higher paddies
left in rice. Modern ponds are about 1 to 1.5 acres (0.4 to 0.6 ha) and 7 to 10 ft deep.

Most of the time the ponds took only part of the land. In the survey of 1581 the ponds of Longshan, one of the delta's two
earliest pond districts, came to 18% of its hill, dry land and ponds, its paddy left out of the count; by the 18th century
they had taken three-quarters of its farmland. As late as the 1980s a quarter of the delta's dike-pond heartland was still
in rice.

Guesses:
- That a pond was one household's work, and that ponds spread from a household to its neighbors, so that they lay in patches
  rather than evenly scattered: no source says who dug a pond or how ponds spread.
- That low ground alone did not make a dike-pond district: as low and wet as they were, the sandy fields of the outer delta
  are not recorded going over to ponds, and no source says why.
- How often a settlement grows this crop rather than lotus, tea or none: no source counts them, so the choices come up evenly
  where the ground allows them.

Depiction: The map converts some of the plots in the two lowest rows of the valley floor to ponds, in patches, and leaves
the rest in rice; how wide a valley's wet bottom ran is not recorded, so the two rows are taken so that the ponds show. A
village whose ponds took nearly all its land is drawn as a dike-pond field throughout, the title card's field form.
