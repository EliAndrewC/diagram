About: Where a brook ran high enough all through the season, a hamlet took its water through a plain opening in the bank, with
no weir: "If a river's flow is ample and its level always high, water can easily be taken just by providing an entrance for
it." In old Japan this was common: intake weirs were hardly built at all, the mouth being sought where water could be taken
naturally and a canal cut from it. China's intakes, too, came "with a dam and without", and the Ningxia district on the Yellow
River drew all its water without dams.

The intake stood on one bank, the side of the land it served, where the deepest current ran against the bank and the water
stood deep and steady. From it the dug head race ran down to its first fork, where the water was divided among the fields,
while the brook ran on below. What the mouth of a village's race was lined or shut with is not recorded; the heads of great
canals were closed with gates of timber or stone, or with boards lifted in and out.

How far a head race ran is not recorded either; the small mountain canals ran "for short distances". The mouth was small:
in 1657 a branch of the Tamagawa aqueduct serving one village left through an opening about 8 in (21 cm) square, and in
1722 one serving fourteen villages through one about 3 ft (91 cm) square.

- That a hamlet takes its water through an open intake about half the time: which form a hamlet built followed from its brook's level through the season, which the map does not model, and no source counts the two.
- How far above the fork the intake sits, chosen afresh for each map within a short run: no source gives how far a village's head race ran.
