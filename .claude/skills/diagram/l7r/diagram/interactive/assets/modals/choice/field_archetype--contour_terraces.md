About: Where a hillside left no valley floor, rice was grown on terraces, tanada: level paddies cut into the slope and
stacked in narrow strips, each held up by a wall between it and the next. In the Edo period, once the plains had little room
left, terraces were carried up steep mountain slopes in western Japan; in much of the east they were never cut that steep.
They were small work, not the planned projects of lords or great merchants, opened with little more than a hoe. China cut
them too, mainly in the mountains south of the Yangtze, in Guangxi and Yunnan.

Each terrace paddy was narrow and irregular, watered largely by the rain, on a mountainside reached by steep paths. The
steeper the ground, the higher the wall: western Japan built its walls of stone, able to stand on
a steep slope, and the east, which built fewer terraces, gentle banks of earth. A bench terrace in general, not only in
Japan, stood on a riser about 3 to 5 ft (0.8 to 1.5 m) high, its bed about 7 to 20 ft (2 to 6 m) wide.

A terrace paddy ran from about 200 sq ft (20 sq m) to a few thousand, smaller the steeper the slope. At Shiroyone some eight
hundred paddies average about 200 sq ft each; at Obasute, on a slope of one in seven, about 1,800 paddies spread over 185
acres (75 ha).

Depiction: The map draws the rice as stacked steps along the contour, stepping down the slope, each step divided along the
contour into small paddies. A slope carries at least eight steps, a count set for the drawing rather than taken from any
one hillside. A supply channel brings the water down and spills it from step to step into a drain at the foot, a route
drawn so the map shows where the water goes. The wall stands higher where the ground is steeper.
