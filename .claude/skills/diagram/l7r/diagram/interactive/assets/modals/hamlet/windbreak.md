About: A farming village sheltered behind a wood on the side the winter wind came from. In southern China it was the
fengshui wood, kept immediately behind the village and upslope of it, with the paddies open to the sun in front; in Japan a
grove stood on the north and west of each farmhouse, and in the Ryukyu islands a belt was planted round three sides of a whole
settlement. It did more than stop the wind: one Chinese lineage kept the hill facing its village planted because
bare rock there portended misfortune.

A southern Chinese village's wood belonged to no household. Its lineage held it in common, closed it to cutting by rules agreed
in council and carved on stone, and fined anyone who cut a tree - in one village, the cost of a performance of opera. A tree
the wind brought down was announced to all and taken for common use.

The Japanese grove mixed many kinds of tree, led by tall cedar and sometimes planted in rows, with bamboo low on its windward
side. A southern Chinese wood was mixed evergreen broadleaf, its canopy dense and its tallest trees over 65 ft (20 m) today,
dark within. The Ryukyu belt planted on royal orders in 1742 was about 50 ft (16 m) wide, of evergreen fukugi.

A southern Chinese village commonly kept about two such woods; the one behind it was usually about 2.5 acres (1 ha),
though some ran past 49 acres (20 ha).

Guesses:
- That a clustered village sheltered behind one shared wood, rather than a grove for each house: no source says which a
  village of farmhouses close together kept.
- The two forms the map draws, conifer-led and broadleaf, at even odds, the conifer the commonest crown of the first, and bamboo
  about one plant in twelve: no source gives a share, and every survey of the conifer-led form is of one farmhouse's grove, not
  a village's.

Depiction: The map draws the belt on the one or two sides of the settlement the winter wind comes from, the northwest unless
the place has a wind of its own, and never carries it round the houses. It is drawn in one of two forms - darker conifers set
in rows with lesser broadleaf among them, or an irregular wood of rounded crowns - with a little bamboo between the crowns;
the title card says which form this settlement has. The conifer rows are spaced so a reader can make them out;
how far apart real rows stood is not recorded. The belt runs on past the edge of the map, its planting resumes on both sides of a lane that crosses it, and its
trees are kept off the sun of the farmyards and kitchen beds.
