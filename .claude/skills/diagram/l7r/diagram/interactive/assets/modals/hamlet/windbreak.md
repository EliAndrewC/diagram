About: A wood on the side the winter wind came from sheltered a farming village in its lee. In southern China it was the
fengshui wood, kept behind the village and upslope of it, the paddies open in front; in Japan a
grove stood on the north and west of many a farmhouse, and in the Ryukyu islands a belt was planted round three sides of a
whole settlement.

In Huizhou's hills a village's wood belonged to no household. Its lineage held it in common, closed it to cutting by rules
carved on stone and fined anyone who cut a tree - in one village, the cost of a performance of opera; a tree the wind brought
down was announced to all and taken for common use. A Japanese household's grove gave it firewood, timber and fallen leaves
for manure.

The Japanese grove mixed many kinds of tree, led by tall cedar, sometimes in rows, with bamboo low on its windward
side. A southern Chinese wood was mixed evergreen broadleaf; surviving woods are dense and dark within, their tallest
trees over 65 ft (20 m). The Ryukyu belt, probably planted in 1742, was about 50 ft (16 m) wide, of fukugi and laurel, both
broadleaf evergreens.

A southern Chinese village commonly kept about two such woods; the one behind it is usually about 2.5 acres (1 ha) today,
though some run past 50 acres (20 ha). One Edo-period measurement puts a farmhouse's grove at roughly 6,000 to 28,000 sq ft.

Guesses:
- That a clustered village sheltered behind one shared wood, rather than a grove for each house: no source says which a
  village of farmhouses close together kept.
- That a settlement's wood is conifer-led or broadleaf at even odds, that conifers outnumber the other trees in the first, and
  that bamboo is about one plant in twelve: no source gives a village belt's share (in one Tonami hamlet's farmhouse groves
  nearly half the trees were cedar), and every survey of the conifer-led form is of one farmhouse's grove, not a village's.

Depiction: The map draws one belt for the settlement, not a grove at each house, on the one or two sides the winter wind comes
from - the northwest unless the place has a wind of its own - and never carries it round the houses. It is drawn in one of two
forms, darker conifers set in rows with broadleaf trees among them or an irregular wood of rounded crowns, with a little bamboo
between the crowns shown by paired strokes, since a real culm is too slim to see; the title card says which form this
settlement has. The rows are spaced so a reader can make them out, and the belt is drawn as a ragged band deep enough to read
as a wood rather than a row of trees; how far apart real rows stood, and how deep a village's wood ran, is not recorded. The
belt runs on past the edge of the map, its planting resumes on both sides of a lane that crosses it, and its trees are kept
off the sun of the farmyards and kitchen beds.
