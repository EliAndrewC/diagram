About: Which way a field's rows ran was a choice, and the land made it. A dry field, never flooded, was worked in ridges
and furrows with its crop in rows, and a tract's rows ran the way its ground called for. In a Chinese classic, when the
victorious state of Jin demanded in 589 BCE that every field row in Qi be turned to run east, Qi's envoy refused: the
early kings had ordered the realm by what suited the land, "south and east run its rows", and to turn them all east
served only Jin's war chariots.

On the ground, ridges laid along the contour checked the runoff and drained the surface together, and did it best on
gentle ground; in the rainy, low-lying south of China ridges were raised higher, with deeper furrows, to drain. Even
the surveyed jori grid of square fields, usually laid north-south and east-west, was not seldom tilted with the region
or the ground.

How far a village's rows turned from the contour is not recorded, nor how far apart they stood.

Guesses:
- How far the map turns a settlement's rows off the contour, up to 8 degrees either way on a hamlet and 12 on a
  village: no source gives the turn of a real field's rows, so it is chosen at random from a small range, and how often
  each turn comes up is a guess.
- That neighbors on one slope ran their rows alike, the direction changing where the land changed: no source describes a
  village's dry fields plot by plot; it is reasoned from the classical passage that put the direction in the land.

Depiction: The number on the title card is how many degrees the map turns the rows of a settlement's dry fields off
the contour of its slope. At 0 the rows start from the contour itself; a positive number turns them clockwise as the map
is seen, a negative one counterclockwise. Every dry field on the map takes the same turn, so two settlements on like
ground do not draw the same furrows. From that heading, each tract of two to four strips runs its rows either along it
or down the slope, turned a little to suit its own ground, and neighboring tracts differ by enough of a turn that the
strips can be told apart.
