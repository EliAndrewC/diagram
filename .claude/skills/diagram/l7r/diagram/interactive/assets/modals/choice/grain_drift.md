About: Which way a field's rows ran was a choice, and the land made it. A dry field, never flooded, was worked in ridges
and furrows with its crop in rows, and a tract's rows ran the way its ground called for. In a Chinese classic, when the
victorious state of Jin demanded in 589 BCE that every field row in Qi be turned to run east, Qi's envoy refused: the
early kings had ordered the realm by what suited the land, "south and east run its rows", and to turn them all east
served only Jin's war chariots.

On the ground, ridges laid along the contour checked the runoff and drained the surface together, and did it best on
gentle ground; in the rainy, low-lying south of China ridges were raised higher, with deeper furrows, to drain. Neighbors
on one slope probably ran their rows alike, the direction changing where the land changed. Even the surveyed jori grid of
the western plains, usually laid north-south and east-west, was not seldom tilted with the region or the ground.

How far a village's rows turned from the contour is not recorded, nor how far apart they stood.

Guesses:
- How far the map turns a settlement's rows off the contour, up to about 12 degrees either way: no source gives the turn
  of a real field's rows, so it is chosen at random from a small range, and how often each turn comes up is a guess.

Depiction: The map turns the rows of a settlement's dry fields by this angle from the contour of its slope, so two
settlements on like ground do not draw the same furrows. Within that, each tract of two to four strips turns its rows to
suit its own ground, either along the contour or down the slope, and tracts are set well apart so the strips can be told
