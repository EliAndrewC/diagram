About: A field drain - in Japanese an akusuiro, a channel for unwanted water - took the water away from the paddies
once it had watered them. It lay on the lowest ground of the fields, along their foot and across the fall of the land,
so that it gathered what ran out of every paddy above it. At Minuma, reclaimed for paddy in 1728, in the Edo period, the
supply canals ran along the raised edge and the drain down the lowest ground.

Not every district kept the two apart. Water could pass from paddy to paddy over the bunds, or one channel could both
water the paddies and take what they let out; a separate supply ditch and drain for every plot came only with modern
land consolidation. The drain let its water go into a river or a natural stream, where the villages below took it up
again - in a drought, all of it.

It was an earth ditch, in many cases no more than dug out and trodden firm. Water left each paddy by a small opening in
its bund; the lowest paddies' bunds probably ran along the drain's bank.

A drain probably needed little width where it began, carrying one paddy's runoff, and widened as it gathered water. The
oldest field channels with figures run from perhaps nine inches to 3 ft across, and a drain excavated among the earliest
Yayoi paddies was 1.5 to 3 ft (0.5 to 1 m) wide.

Guesses:
- A drain ending in a pond at the foot of its fields: every drain in the accounts lets its water go into a river or a
  watercourse, and none describes one ending in a pond.
- Its width where it leaves the fields, about 5.5 ft: wider than the one early drain measured, it is reckoned from what
  a drain must carry off after a storm, not from a period figure.

Depiction: The map keeps supply and drainage in separate ditches, a collector along the low line of each field and its
run onward - to the passing brook, or else to a pond at the field's foot or off the edge of the map, as the title
card says - so every hamlet has a
drain between its plots; the older forms, water passing from paddy to paddy or one channel both feeding and draining,
are not drawn. A drain is drawn at its true width, starting as narrow as the ditches that water the paddies end - its
finest stretch held to a floor of about 1.5 ft so it shows - and widening to its outfall, too gently to see along the
stroke. It runs across the slope, so on a map whose land falls at a slant its outfall can sit up the page while lying
lower on the ground: the water is not running uphill, the page is.
