About: A field drain - in Japanese an akusuiro, a channel for unwanted water - took the water away from the paddies
once it had watered them. It lay on the lowest ground of the fields, along their foot and across the fall of the land,
so that it gathered what ran out of every paddy above it. When the Minuma reservoir was reclaimed for paddy in 1728, in
the Edo period, its supply canals ran along the raised edge and its drain down the lowest ground.

Not every district kept the two apart. Water could pass from paddy to paddy over the bunds, or one channel could both
water the paddies and take what they let out; a separate supply ditch and drain for every plot came only with modern
land consolidation. The drain let its water go into a river or a natural stream, where the villages below took it up
again: in a drought, what one district let out was used in full by the district below it.

A drain needed little width where it began, carrying the runoff of a single paddy, and widened as it gathered water.
The oldest small field channels with figures run from perhaps nine inches to about 3 ft across, and a drain dug up among
paddies from the very start of the Yayoi period was 1.5 to 3 ft wide.

Guesses:
- A drain ending in a pond at the foot of its fields: every drain in the accounts lets its water go into a river or a
  watercourse, and none describes one ending in a pond.
- Its width where it leaves the fields, about 5.5 ft: wider than the one early drain measured, it is reckoned from what
  a drain must carry off after a storm, not from a period figure.

Depiction: The map keeps supply and drainage in separate ditches, a collector along the low line of each field and its
run onward - to the passing brook, to a pond at the field's foot, or off the edge of the map - so every hamlet has a
drain between its plots; the older forms, water passing from paddy to paddy or one channel both feeding and draining,
are not drawn. A drain starts as narrow as the ditches that water the paddies end and widens to its outfall. It runs
across the slope, so on a map whose land falls at a slant its outfall can sit up the page while lying lower on the
ground; there the brook bends out of the drain's mouth, so the junction reads as the drain turning down the valley.

Name: drainage ditch
