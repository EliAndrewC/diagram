About: A paddy was watered by gravity, so its water came in at the top. A hamlet's brook came down off the high ground and was tapped at an intake on its bank, on the side of the fields it served; in this form the water came in toward one shoulder of the head of the fields. Where along the bank it stood was chosen for the water it could take: where the current's deepest line ran against the bank, in a straight reach or on the outside of a bend, and Japanese engineering of the early twentieth century put the head works where the river left the mountains.

Where the brook ran high enough, the intake was only an opening in its bank; where its level fell, a low weir of stakes and brushwood, timber or stone was set across it to raise the water. The brook ran on past the intake, tapped rather than swallowed. From the intake mouth a head race ran down to the head of the fields and forked into the supply canals, each narrowing as smaller ditches drew water off it.

How far a village's head race ran before it forked is not recorded. Small mountain canals ran for short distances; the canal that watered the third-century paddies at Toro, about 19 acres (7.5 ha), was more than 1,200 ft (370 m) long.

Guesses:
- Where across the head of the fields the water comes in - toward one shoulder, in the middle, or toward the other, the middle as often as both sides together: no source says where across the head a village's water came in, so it is chosen by chance for each settlement.
- How far above the fork the intake sits, chosen by chance within the short distances the head race is known to have run: no source gives a figure.

Depiction: The map draws the brook coming down off the high ground past the head of the fan of paddies and running on down its flank. The intake stands on the bank nearest the fields, toward one shoulder of the head, and the head race leaves it at a slant pointing downstream and runs to the fork at the head of the fan; no intake is ever drawn below the fields, because water does not run uphill. The title card says whether this settlement's intake has a weir.
