About: A resting paddy (kataarashi) was a rice plot left out of crop for a year while the plots around it were worked. On
the unsettled land of the late Heian and early medieval periods, where the water ran short or the soil was thin, a
village chose its plots to work anew each year from the cropped and the resting ground, to rest the soil and to
fit the planting to the water. Resting ground was not abandoned ground; whether a plot rested every other year or
only now and then is uncertain. In Rokugan a paddy once made can be cropped for centuries without tiring its
soil, so a plot rests, if at all, for want of water, and resting paddy is the rarer case.

A resting plot lay in among the worked ones, not in a block of its own. Nothing marked it off; while it rested it
was common grazing for the village's cattle and horses.

In the late Heian period only about three plots of paddy in five were worked on average. In 1102 one estate in
Tanba province worked about 110 of its 220 acres of paddy; about 64 acres rested that year. From the Kamakura period on, the advanced
farming regions improved their water works and fields until
the land no longer needed resting, and the nucleated villages worked the same ground every year; one historian puts the
end of resting in the early modern period, when land surveys taxed paddy by its area.

Guesses:
- That four settlements in five rest no paddy at all: the settled form is taken as the commoner from the setting's
  nucleated villages and its canon, but no figure weighs the two.
- That a settlement whose paddy rests leaves two to four whole plots resting in each field: the one estate measured rested
  nearly a third of its paddy, an upper bound rather than a hamlet's norm.
- That the resting plots lie toward the far end of the water's run, where the water reaches last: read from "short
  water", since no source says which plots of a village's paddy rested.

Depiction: The map draws a resting paddy as a whole plot of grass with tufts inside its own bunds, never a patch within
a plot, and never two resting plots side by side, so they read as scattered among the cropped ground.
