About: A field drain, an akusuiro, ran along the lowest ground of the paddies, across the fall of the land, gathering the water
each plot let out, and let it go into a river or a natural watercourse. A drain of this kind in Saitama, dug for a district
that canals watered in the Edo period, began in the fields' spent water and ended in a river. In this form the drain lets its water
go on downstream, into the passing brook or a watercourse beyond the fields, rather than into a pond.

A drain was a dug channel, narrow where it started and widening as it gathered water. The finest field channels of an
ancient Chinese scheme were probably about 9 inches across, and an excavated drain from the start of the Yayoi period was 1.5 to 3 ft
(0.5 to 1 m) wide.

The water it carried off was used again below. In a drought, the drainage one district returned to the river was taken up in
full by the irrigation of the district below it, and in a valley paddy the water of the paddies and of daily life flowed on
into the river and in the end into the lake.

Guesses:
- That a settlement's drain runs on down its brook about one time in three: no source counts where drains ended.

Depiction: The map draws the drain at its true width as a dug channel along the low line of the field, narrow at its head
and widening to its outfall. Where the passing brook comes near its end it joins the brook; where it does not, it runs on as a ditch off the edge
of the map. It crosses the slope, so on a map whose land falls at a slant its outfall can sit up the page while still lying
lower on the ground.
