About: Even where a district went over to fish ponds nearly wholesale, some of its ground could stay in rice. In 1678 a writer
on Guangdong put the fish ponds of Jiujiang, a pond township of the Pearl River delta, at eight tenths of its land and its
fields at two. In the 18th century the ponds of Longshan came to three-quarters of its cultivated land, fields being abandoned
to dig them. As late as the 1980s a quarter of the delta's dike-pond heartland was still mostly in irrigated rice.

The rice that was left stood as ordinary paddy among the ponds and their planted dikes, inside the same polder, watered and
drained through the same creeks and sluices. Which plots stayed in rice, and why those, is not recorded; ponds were dug where
the ground lay low and flooded.

The shares above are a township's, not a hamlet's: how much rice a single dike-pond hamlet kept is not recorded.

Guesses:
- About one parcel in ten left in rice: chosen so a few plots read as standing rice among the ponds, below the two tenths of fields the 1678 writer gave one pond township.
- That a wholesale dike-pond hamlet keeps some rice about half the time and none the other half: no source counts the hamlets of either kind.

Depiction: The map lays the whole block out as dike-ponds and converts about nine parcels in ten, in patches; the parcels left
over are drawn as rice paddy, bunded and green among the ponds, not left as empty outlines.
