About: Every building had its ways in. A samurai house of standing kept a formal entrance, the genkan, for the head of the
household and honored guests; the family and those of the house came and went by an inner entrance; and the kitchen had
its own door from outside, the katteguchi, a word in use by 1666. A middle-rank house could do without the formal
entrance and bring its guests in through a garden. Who came in by the kitchen door before 1868 is not recorded.

A guest of rank came through the gate across open ground, and the palanquin was brought right alongside the entrance
step. In a Chinese courtyard house the servants' row turned its doors and windows inward, onto the court, its back a blind
wall to the lane. By the end of the Edo period a plastered storehouse could be shut by a pair of heavy plastered leaves,
and in the great fires of the period fire often never reached inside.

An ordinary door was probably no wider than about 3 ft, half a ken, and a farmhouse's big sliding door about 6 ft, with a
low wicket in it. The width of a kitchen door, a guest entrance or a storehouse's opening is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That a kitchen has one door to the outside, on its earth floor: the kitchen door is recorded, but not where it stood or
  whether a kitchen had two.
- A way by which food reached the rooms, where a plan draws one: no account shows one.
- A storehouse leaf about 3.5 ft wide: the one width found is a standard of today.

Depiction: The plan draws a door as a small dark block set on the wall it opens through, and a storehouse's pair of leaves
as one block. Its width is a drawing convention: the working doors of kitchens, lodgings, barracks and servants' rows are
drawn mostly two to three times the width of a real ordinary door, so that they read at the plan's scale.

Name: door
Covers: every small door glyph on a building's wall
