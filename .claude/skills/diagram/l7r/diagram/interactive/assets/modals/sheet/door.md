About: Every building had its ways in. A samurai house of standing kept a formal entrance, the genkan, for the head of the
household and honored guests; the family and those of the house came and went by an inner entrance; and the kitchen had
its own door from outside, the katteguchi, a word in use early in the Edo period. A middle-rank house could do without
the formal entrance and bring its guests in through a garden. A farmhouse had its everyday door at the front of its
earth floor and a second at the back, for cooking. Who came in by a samurai kitchen's door in the Edo period is not
recorded.

A guest of rank came through the gate across open ground, and the palanquin was brought alongside the entrance step. At
one old farmhouse the big door ran on wheels: it stood open by day, and at night and in wind and rain it was shut and
barred with a pole, and the household came and went by the low wicket set in it. By the end of the Edo period a
plastered storehouse could be shut by a pair of plastered leaves, and in the great fires of the period fire often never
reached inside.

An ordinary door was probably no wider than about 3 ft, half a ken, and a farmhouse's big sliding door about 6 ft. The
width of a kitchen door, a guest entrance or a storehouse's opening is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That a kitchen has one door to the outside, on its earth floor: the kitchen door is recorded, but not where it stood or
  whether a kitchen had two.
- A way by which food reached the rooms, where a plan draws one: no account shows one.
- A storehouse leaf about 3.5 ft wide: the one width found is a standard of today.

Depiction: The plan draws a door as a small dark block set on the wall it opens through, and a storehouse's pair of leaves
as one block. Its width is a drawing convention: the working doors of kitchens, lodgings, barracks and servants' rows are
drawn mostly two to three times the width of a real ordinary door, which was about 3 ft, so that they read at the plan's
scale. A guest's door opens onto open ground a palanquin could cross, never straight from the street; a kitchen door
opens into the work space.

Name: door
Covers: every small door glyph on a building's wall
