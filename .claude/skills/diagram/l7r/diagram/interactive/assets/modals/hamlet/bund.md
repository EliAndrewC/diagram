About: A bund (aze) was the low bank of earth heaped up between one rice paddy and the next. It held each paddy's water
in, it often marked the boundary where the owners on its two sides differed, and it was the path of the farm work:
farmers walked the bunds to weed and to fertilize, and rested on them. Two neighboring paddies probably shared a single bund, so a stretch of paddy was one connected network of
walls.

It was puddled mud, heaped from the paddy itself, with no set shape or size; along a ditch the paddy's bund was the
ditch's bank. Every late spring, before transplanting, its face was stripped of weeds and plastered with kneaded mud by
the hoe (azenuri), so that it held the water. Water came in and went out through small openings in it. By midsummer it
was no longer bare: grass grew on it, cut for manure or fodder, and often a row of beans.

An Edo land survey reckoned a bund between paddies at one shaku, about a foot, wide, and a field replanned in 1869 laid
it at two. At a dig of some of Japan's earliest paddies the bunds stood a few inches to a foot high, and the
larger ones along the canal ran 2.5 to 5 ft wide and seem also to have served as paths. How wide a bund built for walking
was in later times is not recorded.

Guesses:
- The width of a walking bund on the map, 2 to 5 ft: no source gives it, so it is held between the two-shaku bund and
  the one-ken (about 6 ft) farm road of a field replanned in 1869.
- The pile of earth at a crossing, 4 to 6 ft across, and that a crossing was the most worked point of a field: no
  source speaks of the junctions at all.
- That a hand-piled bund's corners wore round and its line wandered a foot or two, and that it never stepped sideways
  and carried on: reasoned from how piled earth behaves and what a bund cost to keep, not found written down.

Depiction: The map draws the bunds as one network of shared walls in a dark red-brown, the color of the fresh-plastered
mud of spring. By high summer, the season the map shows, a real bund was grassed and planted and all but vanished into
the green of the rice; the bunds are drawn anyway, because they carry the pattern of the plots. A bund between paddies
is drawn at its real width, about 1.5 ft, and one along a canal about 3 ft, inside the widths of the one early dig.
Earth is piled at each crossing, corners are rounded and lines wander, as a hand-piled bund's did. The openings that let
the water in and out are too small to draw, so a bund runs unbroken along every channel; the water crossed it all the
same. The path from the houses runs out onto the field's outer bund.

Name: bund
Covers: the stroke of every paddy plot and the piled junctions between them
