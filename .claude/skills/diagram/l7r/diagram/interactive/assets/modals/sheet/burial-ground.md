About: Where a village buried its dead in its shrine's yard, the burial ground was the village's graveyard, lying about the
hall. In Edo Japan each household was registered with a temple, which gave its members their funerals and memorial rites, and
temple graveyards spread as temples multiplied; one village temple in a Fukuoka farming town had its graveyard inside its
precinct. A real Shinto shrine was another matter: death was a defilement its priests were bound to avoid. In Rokugan the
country monk who keeps the village's shrine performs the funerary rites, so the shrine plays the part of the parish temple,
and its yard may hold the village's one burial ground.

The graves were small, the simplest at one excavated Edo temple graveyard mostly under 5 ft long, and at another temple's
ground space was so short that burials overlapped. A burial grave was marked with no more than a stone. In Rokugan,
families weed and clean their ancestors' graves at the spring and autumn festivals.

How large a village's ground was is not recorded; it filled as its inhabitants died, some 18 to 30 in a thousand a year in
three Edo farming villages.

Guesses:
- That a village's one ground lies in the shrine's yard or apart from it at even odds: both forms are recorded, but not how
  many villages took each.
- That a village whose shrine stands on a hill keeps its ground apart: no source says whether graves climbed a hill
  shrine's slope.

Depiction: Where a village's ground lies in the shrine's yard, the plan draws its graves in the yard around the hall and its
arches, within a short walk of the hall, never on the approach or under an arch.

Name: burial ground
Covers: the burial ground by the shrine, where the village has it there
