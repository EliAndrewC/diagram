About: A village was not always one cluster of houses. In the Edo period a settlement that split off from an older village
became its branch hamlet, the edago, which answered to the parent village for its taxes and its officials. In one district of
Kishu, on Japan's Pacific coast, the village taxed as one unit was mostly a single settlement on the plain and mostly several
in the mountains, more of them the deeper the valley. In Rokugan, a village is several clusters of farmhouses around larger
fields.

A village of two parts showed two groups of houses on two patches of dry ground, the fields between or around them. How far
apart the parts stood, and how many households each held, are not recorded.

How common such a village was is not recorded beyond the Kishu district, where it was commoner in the mountains.

Guesses:
- That a village may stand in two clusters on two patches of dry ground flanking its fields: no account describes the plan of
  a village in two parts, so the form is an extension of the attested lump of houses.
- How often a village comes out split: no count was found, so the share is set for the maps.

Depiction: The map seats the village in two clusters on two flanking patches of dry ground, apart enough to read as two. A
hamlet is never split, since it has too few houses for two groups to read apart.
