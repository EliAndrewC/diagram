About: The compound wall ringed a magistrate's whole compound, broken only at its gates; in Japan and in China alike a
magistrate worked and lived inside one walled enclosure. A wall was a mark of rank as well as a barrier: in the castle town
of Kanazawa, samurai of middle rank and above walled their land with earth, while foot soldiers were allowed no wall and
kept hedges.

An earth wall carried a small roof of its own, of tiles or boards, to keep its earth dry. It came in two forms. The lighter,
the neribei among them, was old roof tiles and small stones laid up in clay and plastered over, or earth plastered onto a
frame of posts and lath; it stood about 1 to 2 ft thick, and a surviving late-Edo neribei is 2 ft (0.6 m) across its base
and 5 ft (1.6 m) high. The heavy form, the rammed-earth tsuijibei, was packed down between boards an inch or two of earth
at a time, until it stood as much as 3 ft (1 m) thick and 10 ft (3 m) high.

How thick a magistrate's compound wall was is not recorded. Its length went with the compound: the smallest office on
record, a branch post at Joge, stood on a site of about 230 by 90 ft.

Guesses:
- That the compound's buildings backed onto the wall a foot or two off it, their eaves nearly touching, so the two roofs
  shed their rain apart and the wall stayed reachable for patching: no record describes it.

Depiction: The plan draws the wall 3 ft thick, the heavier of the two real forms, so its stroke reads; the lighter wall, at
a foot or two, would draw too thin a line. No building stands on the wall: where a building and the wall would meet, the
building gives way, and buildings back onto it with a foot or two between.

Name: compound wall
