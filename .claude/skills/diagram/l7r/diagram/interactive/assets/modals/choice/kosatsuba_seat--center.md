About: A notice board (kosatsuba) was the wooden frame on which the shogunate and the domains posted standing law - the ban on
Christians, the rules on coin, fire and packhorse charges - so that everyone would know it. The shogunate ordered boards set at
the entrances and centers of towns and villages, and every village of the Edo period, more than 60,000 of them, had a site
for one. A board stood where the traffic was heaviest, and a village's center was one of the commonest places: boards were
most often set where people passed, at the village center or at a crossroads. Nakatsugawa's board stood at a crossroads, some
66 ft (20 m) up the slope from its replica today, so that more passers-by would see it.

The boards measured at two post towns were frames about 10 ft high and 16 to 18 ft along the road, on a stone base inside a
fence, under a roof, the notices hung high in two tiers. A village's board could be built the same way, but none was
measured. The headman, who copied out the lord's circulars, had to read and write.

How many villages kept their board at the center, rather than at the way in or before an official's gate, is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That a village's board was the size of a post town's: no village board was measured.
- How often a settlement's board stands at its center, its way in or an official's frontage: no source counts them.

Depiction: The map seats the board last, once everything else stands, where it would meet the most passers-by: beside the
busiest way among the houses, a few feet off its edge and turned broadside to it. It is drawn as a roofed frame 16 ft long and
6 ft deep on a stone footing inside a fence.
