About: Fire-water tubs, tensuioke, "heaven-water tubs", held rainwater kept standing against fire. Wooden halls burned - the
Sado magistracy burned and was rebuilt five times, though Takayama's office never did - and the defense was water kept ready
where a fire might start. Edo's townspeople kept rainwater tubs at the front entrance and on the roof as a daily habit, with
soaked fire-beaters for falling sparks, and a town order required tubs of water kept ready against fire. The earth-walled
storehouses needed none: they were the buildings made to resist fire.

The tubs were fed from the gutters, and stood on the roofs, at the eaves and at the street corners; they became general, it
is said, after the Kansei era (1789-1801). A large tub at a street corner had hand buckets stacked on top of it, ready for
use. In the Qing, the Forbidden City in Beijing kept great water vats instead, 308 of them by its statutes, before its halls
and in its courtyards wherever a timber hall stood far from water.

How much a corner tub held is uncertain: about 6 koku, some 285 gallons (1,080 liters), by one encyclopedia, though its own
conversion gives a tenth of that.

Guesses:
- A tub at every major wooden building, and two at the kitchen, whose cooking fire burned all day: the habit and the town
  order are recorded, but not a rule for every building or a ranking of buildings by risk.
- A tub's size, about 2.5 ft across: no source gives a rain tub's width.

Depiction: The plan draws a tub at each major wooden building and two at the kitchen, and none at an earth-walled storehouse;
the tubs are drawn only on building plans, since a walled town or city map shows its fire defense as a watchtower.
