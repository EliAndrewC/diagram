About: A paddy field was rice, plot after plot, but not only rice: small ponds stood among the paddies to store water
for them. One form, seen mostly on the plains, was the dish pond (sara-ike), made by ringing a hollow or a stretch of low
wet ground with an embankment and digging out its bottom. Japan came to have over a hundred thousand ponds, more of them
built in the Edo period than in any other, together with the ditches of newly opened fields; on the dry Sanuki plain,
once the good sites ran out, even flat farmland was dug out for ponds.

The shallow dish ponds of the plains were ringed with reed, wild rice and cattail, with water chestnut and water lilies
over their surface. The embankment around one was kept clear of growth, to keep it strong.

The smallest ponds, about 33 ft on a side, were owned and kept by one person; the largest covered several hectares
(a hectare is about 2.5 acres). In Kagawa, the densest pond country in Japan, about half of its 12,000 ponds held under
1,000 tonnes of water (about 35,000 cubic ft), and before its modern canal the prefecture drew 70 percent of its farm
water from ponds. How often one field had a pond among its paddies is not recorded: the counts are of whole prefectures and
of the country.

Guesses:
- How often a field keeps a pond, a little over half the time where it has low plots: no count of ponds per field or per
  village was found, and the dense pond country of Kagawa bounds it only from above.

Depiction: The map sinks each pond wholly inside one low plot of paddy, with a fringe of reeds, never across a bund, as
a pocket of ground too low and wet for rice. It draws one on a field of paddy on a broad valley floor or in a polder, and
none among the fish ponds of dike-pond ground.
