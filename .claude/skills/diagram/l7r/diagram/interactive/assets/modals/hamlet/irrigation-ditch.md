About: An irrigation ditch carried water by gravity from its source, here the brook, to the paddies. A head race left
the brook at an intake on the bank nearest the fields and split at division points into smaller and smaller ditches,
each canal narrowing as its branches drew off their share. Since a canal watered only the ground below it, canals ran
along the high ground. The smallest ditches, which brought the water to the
paddies, were run by small groups of farmers and often counted as part of the paddies they served.

Before the early modern period most ditches were simply earth dug out and trodden firm. Water entered a paddy through a small gap in its bund, the inlet (mizuguchi), and in the old form went on
from one paddy to the next over the bunds rather than down a ditch to each; a ditch beside every paddy came only with
the land consolidation of the years around 1900. On flat ground each smaller ditch left the one that fed it at right
angles, a rule as old as the classical Chinese commentaries and Japan's ancient land grid.

A district's main canal ran from just under 6 ft to about 36 ft wide, by the size of the land it watered; a
distribution ditch about 3 ft; the ditch that watered a single paddy about a foot. At Toro, in the 3rd century, about 19 acres of paddy
were watered by a canal more than 1,200 ft long.

Guesses:
- How far the head race runs before it forks, set afresh for each map: no source gives a distance.
- The head race's angle off the brook, about 35 degrees: no source from before modern times gives one; that it leans
  downstream is read from the slanting weirs of the Edo period, which set their intake on the downstream side.
- That a village dug few ditches, so the network is drawn sparse: the accounts of water passing from paddy to paddy
  describe it without counting the ditches.

Depiction: [field_archetype=valley_paddy] The map lays the water out as a comb: the head race leaves the brook leaning
downstream and forks at the head of the paddies into two supply canals, one along each high flank, and delivery
ditches leave them at right angles and run down the slope between the plots.

Each ditch is drawn at its true width, the order of the widths kept - head race widest, then the canals, then the
delivery ditches - and narrows along its run as its water is drawn off, though at true size the narrowing is too gentle
for the eye to follow. The finest ditch drawn is held to a floor so it still shows. A stroke's width shows its rank, not
its water, so where a ditch forks the two branches together look wider than their trunk. The foot-wide ditch to a single
paddy, the inlets in the bunds and the water passing from plot to plot are too small to draw, so the bunds run unbroken
along every ditch, and the paddies stand back from a supply canal behind a bare bank. No gate, boards or settling basin
is drawn at the intake, for none is recorded at a village intake.
