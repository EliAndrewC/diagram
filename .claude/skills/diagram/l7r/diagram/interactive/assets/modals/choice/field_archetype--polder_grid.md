About: A polder was low, wet ground walled in by a dike and drained so it could be farmed. In China it was the weitian,
"polder field", made south of the Yangtze: wetland cut off by dikes from the rising and falling water around it. Japan had
its own, the waju, "inside the ring", on the Nobi plain along the Kiso, Nagara and Ibi rivers. The water outside rose and
fell with the seasons - on one great Chinese lake by up to about 42 ft (12.9 m) in a year - and a polder's floor lay below
it some or all of the time.

Its dike was a bank of heaped earth, planted with willows by the water and, where silk was made, mulberry along the crest.
Inside, the lower Yangtze polders were laid out on a planned grid of channels, like chessboards.
A polder drained through culverts in its dike, shut while the water outside was high and opened when it fell, and a chain
pump could throw water out in flood. In one Lake Tai village the houses stood near the water and on
the polder's raised margin, which served as its dike.

Polders ran from a few tens of mu (a few acres) to over a hundred thousand; in the Song the greatest were tens of li
square, "like a great city". Later they were cut into smaller, fish-scale polders. One Japanese waju had 35
drains against four intakes.

Guesses:
- That ten to twenty households share a small polder, behind a dike about 5 ft (1.5 m) high: no source gives either figure.
- That a polder's own village kept its dike, and stood on the dry ground just off it where the polder met a shore: no source
  says who kept a polder's dike, and houses are recorded on the raised margin itself.
- That reed marsh and mudflat lay outside the dike: no source says what lay outside a polder.

Depiction: The map encloses the polder with its dike all the way round, and water crosses it only at gated sluices, an inlet
high on the polder and an outfall low on it. The dike is drawn as a band of mottled earth that swells and pinches along its
length, up to about 40 ft across, its broadest stretches wider than a village's dike stood so that it reads as the bank
holding off the water. Willow runs along its water side and mulberry along its field side, set on the bank's faces, where a
Ming river manual set them on the ground off its foot, so they read as the dike's own planting. Inside the dike a ring canal
runs round its foot, a convention: a polder had channels inside its dike, but no one set ring. The paddies are laid on a
surveyed grid of canals drawn in long, nearly straight reaches that waver gently. Reeds fringe the flanks where the dike
holds water back.
