About: A polder was low, wet ground walled in by a dike and drained so it could be farmed. In China it was the weitian,
"polder field", made south of the Yangtze: wetland cut off by dikes from the rising and falling water around it. A Northern
Song statesman described the polders there as each tens of li square, like a great city, with channels inside and gates
through the dike, opened in drought and shut in flood. Japan had its own, the waju, "inside the ring", on the Nobi plain
along the Kiso, Nagara and Ibi rivers. The water outside rose and fell with the seasons - on one great Chinese lake by up to
about 42 ft (12.9 m) in a year - and a polder's floor lay below it some or all of the time.

Seen from above, the lower Yangtze polders looked like chessboards, laid out on a planned grid of channels. A polder drained
through culverts in its dike, shut while the water outside was high and opened when it fell, and a chain pump could throw
water out in flood. In one Lake Tai village the houses stood near the water, and along each polder's raised margin, which
served as its dike, ran a strip for mulberry 33 to 98 ft wide.

The great early polders were later cut into smaller ones, called fish-scale polders. One Japanese waju had 34 drainage
culverts and a water gate between 1789 and 1872, against four intakes.

Guesses:
- That ten to twenty households share a small polder, behind a dike about 5 ft (1.5 m) high: no source gives either figure.
- That a polder's own village kept its dike, and stood on the dry ground just off it where the polder met a shore: no source
  says who kept a polder's dike, and houses are recorded on the raised margin itself.
- That reed marsh and mudflat lay outside the dike: no source says what lay outside a polder.

Depiction: The map encloses the polder with its dike all the way round, and water crosses it only at gated sluices, an inlet
high on the polder and an outfall low on it. Inside the dike a ring canal runs round its foot, a convention, since no single
ring canal is recorded, and the paddies are laid on a surveyed grid of canals drawn in long, nearly straight reaches that
waver gently. Reeds fringe the flanks where the dike holds water back.
