About: Where a river leaves the hills it spreads a fan of gravel, and the middle of that fan was poor ground for paddy:
below its steep apex the river sinks underground and the water table lies low. In Japan the middle of a fan was often
left as coppice or scrub woodland, and was cleared for dry fields and orchards only from the end of the Edo period into
the Meiji era. The toe of the fan was another matter: there the water that sank into the gravels comes back up as a line
of springs, and villages and paddy have gathered along it since ancient times.

A visitor to such a settlement saw its houses and paddies strung along the foot of the fan, and above them a slope of
brush and coppice where the water could not reach. The dry fields kept to the narrow ground between the paddy and the
wild middle, so a hamlet at the toe had little dry ground to hand for its coarse grain.

Fans could be large: the Isawa fan, one of the largest in Japan, covers about 49,000 acres (20,000 ha). That a wild
middle was the usual case is a tendency, not a rule.

Guesses:
- That a fan's middle is left wild on about three maps in four: wild was the usual case, but no source gives a figure.
- That a fan's toe begins about two-thirds of the way down from its head: no source gives where the toe begins.
- [winter_crop=none] That a hamlet's dry fields are cleared up into the wild middle from the plots nearest the toe first:
  no source says from which end the clearing of a fan went.
