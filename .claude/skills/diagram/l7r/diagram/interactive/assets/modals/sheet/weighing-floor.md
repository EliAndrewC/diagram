About: Charcoal reached a town in straw bales, and a depot most likely weighed them when they changed hands; the
weighing floor is where that was done. A straw bale (tawara) had no standard size, not even for rice, so a stock of bales
could hardly be traded by counting them. Where bales were packed to a set weight by grade, a count of graded bales could
stand for a weight: in one district of Hokkaido, at a date not known, the best charcoal went at about 33 lb (15 kg) to the
bale, the next grade at about 83 lb and a lower one at about 66 lb.

The scale for such loads was the steelyard: a wooden or bamboo beam with a large hook at one end for the load and a weight
slid along the beam until the two balanced. Some were built for heavy loads - one Chinese wooden steelyard over two
hundred years old is about 7 ft long and weighs up to about 385 lb (175 kg) - and Japan used steelyards from the Edo
period, some for 16 kan, about 130 lb (60 kg), four of the best bales. The platform scale came only after 1868, in the
Meiji period.

What the floor itself looked like, and how large it was, is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That a dealer's or an office's bales were weighed on a floor of their own: no account says a charcoal dealer weighed
  the bales at sale; it is reckoned from the bale having no standard size.
- That the floor was roofed and stood on posts, about 16 by 14 ft: no account describes a weighing floor, so its form
  and size are those our maps give a charcoal yard's weighing floor, themselves a guess.

Depiction: The plan draws the weighing floor as a covered floor on posts beside the tally office; its steelyard and the
bales on it are each their own feature.
