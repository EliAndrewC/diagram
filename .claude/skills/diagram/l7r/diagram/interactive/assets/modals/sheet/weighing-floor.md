About: The weighing floor was an open, roofed floor where bales were weighed before the tally was written. A straw bale
(tawara) had no standard size, not even for rice, so a stock of bales could hardly be traded by counting them, and was
most likely weighed when it changed hands. Where bales were packed to a set weight by grade, a count of graded bales could
stand for a weight: in one district of Hokkaido, at an unrecorded date, the best charcoal went at about 33 lb (15 kg) to
the bale, the next grade at about 83 lb and a lower one at about 66 lb.

The scale was the steelyard: a wooden or bamboo beam with a large hook at one end for the load and a weight slid along the
beam until the two balanced. Some were built for heavy loads - one Chinese wooden steelyard over two hundred years old
is about 7 ft long and weighs up to about 385 lb (175 kg) - and Japan used steelyards from the Edo period, some for 16
kan, about 130 lb (60 kg), four of the best bales. The platform scale came only after 1868, in the Meiji period. What the
floor itself looked like, and how large it was, is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That a dealer's or an office's bales were weighed on a floor of their own: no account says a charcoal dealer weighed
  the bales at sale; it is reckoned from the bale having no standard size.

Depiction: The plan draws the weighing floor as a covered floor on posts beside the tally office; its steelyard and the
bales on it are each their own feature.
