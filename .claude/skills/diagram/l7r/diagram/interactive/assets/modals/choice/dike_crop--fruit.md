About: The fruit dike, 果基 (guoji, "fruit dike"), was the first planting on the pond dikes of the Pearl River delta: in the
middle of the Ming, in Nanhai and Shunde, fish ponds were ringed by banks of their own mud set with fruit trees. The mulberry
dike took its place in the late Ming and early Qing, but not everywhere: late in the 17th century, villages in Guangzhou's large counties still
gave up good fields to make dikes and planted them with fruit trees, lychee most, then tea and mulberry, then mandarin and
orange. Longan stood mostly beside the houses, but on the dikes as well.

A fruit dike was a bank of orchard trees above the water. Lychee and longan were planted along the embankments of Guangzhou
too, and some households gave up rice fields to plant them. Where a field was turned to orchard, each tree stood on a mound of
silt about 2 ft high against the floods.

How far apart the trees stood along a dike is not recorded. In a field turned to orchard a mu took twenty-odd lychee trees or
twice as many longan, one lychee to about 300 sq ft.

Guesses:
- That about one dike-pond hamlet in three plants fruit: no source counts the dikes of each kind, so mulberry, the dominant dike of the Qing, is weighted the commonest and fruit, the oldest, next.
- Trees about 18 ft apart along the bank: the one spacing found is for a field turned to orchard, about 17 ft between trees on a square, and none is given along a dike.
- A crown about 8 to 10 ft across: no source gives the crown of a lychee, longan or orange tree on a dike.

Depiction: The map plants every dike of the hamlet with fruit trees alone, as a single line of trees along the middle of each
bank. One crop to a hamlet is the map's choice, so each hamlet reads as one kind of dike-pond village: in the 17th century
fruit trees, tea and mulberry stood on the same villages' dikes.
