About: On many paddies rice in summer was followed by barley or wheat in winter, two crops a year (nimosaku). Once the rice
was reaped the water was let off and the paddy worked as a dry field for the winter grain. The practice is recorded from
the middle of the Heian period and was fairly widespread by the Kamakura period, when in 1264 the shogunate ordered the
provinces not to tax the winter barley. In the Edo period it spread further, above all around Kyoto and Osaka and along
the Inland Sea, where better seed, bought fertilizer and irrigation works came together.

Only a paddy that drained well could carry it: barley is weak to damp and cannot grow in a wet paddy. A second crop also
needed twice the manure of one, and in medieval times only places near densely settled districts could find it. Before
the Second World War the practice stopped at a line across central Japan, from the north of the Kanto plain to Toyama;
north of it cold, snow and wet paddies stood in the way.

Through the winter the drained paddies stood green with young barley, which headed in spring and ripened gold in May.
It was cut around June, and the rice went in close behind it: on the plains, where the crop before the rice left a short window,
transplanting was done in seven to ten days around the summer solstice. How much a winter barley crop yielded is not
recorded.

Guesses:
- How a settlement's drainage and the nearness of a town, whose manure the second crop needed, weigh against each other in
  whether its paddies carry a winter crop: both set the odds, but no source says how much each counts.
- That a drained paddy's winter barley spares an equal area of dry field for the hamlet's coarse grain: no source gives a
  winter barley crop's yield.

Depiction: The map is set in high summer, so this settlement's drained paddies are drawn under rice, and its barley is not
