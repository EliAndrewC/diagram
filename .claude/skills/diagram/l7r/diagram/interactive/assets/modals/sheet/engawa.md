About: The engawa was the veranda that ran along the outside of a Japanese house's rooms. In a house of the formal shoin style
it came in two layers: an inner veranda, the irikawa, inside the building line, a passage along the rooms; and an outer one, the
nure-en, on the edge, its floor usually a step lower. On the garden side a wide veranda served as a second space of the main
room, and late in the Edo period a veranda along two or three sides was also the way to the privy and the bath.

Along its edge the house was closed by its storm shutters, the amado: board doors sliding in a one-groove sill, shut at night and against wind and rain, and stowed by day in a box at the end of their run.

The great daimyo mansions of the early 1600s ringed their rooms with verandas on all four sides; the houses that took up the
veranda late in the period ran it along two or three. It was half a ken to one ken wide, about 3 to 6 ft, and could change from
one face to the next: at the Katsura villa one veranda is a ken wide on its east face and narrows to half a ken where it turns
the corner. A general width for the outer veranda is not recorded.

Guesses:
- That the garden face is drawn at the wider end of the range, where a plan does so: the one veranda that changes width, at
  Katsura, is wider on one face than the next, but no rule is recorded for which face was wider.

Depiction: The plan draws the veranda as a pale strip along the residence's garden face, and some plans carry it round one or
two further faces; none rings all four, the great mansions' form. It is drawn open, the shutters stowed, as a house stood by
day.

Name: engawa
Covers: the veranda strip along the residence's garden face
