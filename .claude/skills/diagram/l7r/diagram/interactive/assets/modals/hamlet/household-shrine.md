About: A household shrine housed the yashikigami, the guardian god of a household's own ground; the word names the shrine
too. In it a farming household enshrined the guardian of its land, its ancestral gods, or a share of the spirit of a god it
held in particular faith. In some places every house had one; in others only certain old families did.

Most were small shrines (hokora) of stone or of wood, some with a thatched roof, and almost never with a hall of the kind
an ordinary shrine had. Some households marked the god's place with a tree or a stone instead. The shrine stood in a corner
of the plot, and which corner varied from place to place: most often the northwest or the northeast, the northeast especially
in some accounts, and usually the southwest in one region. One house that was rebuilt moved its shrine from one corner to
another.

One measured stone shrine is about 16 in (40 cm) wide, deep and high, with a round stone inside held to be the god's own body.
In the one count from the twentieth century, of a town in the northeast, about one household in a hundred kept a shrine.

Guesses:
- How often each corner is drawn - the northwest 45 times in 100, the northeast 35 and the southwest 20: all three are
  recorded, but no count gives their shares, so they are ordered after the account that names the northwest first.

Depiction: The map draws the pattern in which only certain old families kept a shrine, so that one is notable where it
stands: a household shrine appears on three to eight homesteads in a hundred, and never more. It stands in the plot's
northwest, northeast or southwest corner. It is drawn vermilion, with a ridge line and a torii before its door, at 6 by 6 ft,
the size of a small shed, so that it can be seen and told apart from the sheds; a real one was a small box about 16 in on a
side, too small to make out at the map's scale.
