About: A row village's farm worked land that lay back from its house. Where the farms were strung along a road laid out
first, as on the planned new fields of the Edo period, each farm's land ran back from the road in a single strip as wide
as its frontage: the house lot, then the field, then woodland at the far end, which gave fodder, compost and fuel. Where
the row stood on the dike of land reclaimed from the sea, a farm's land lay near the house and fairly compact.

The strips that were measured, on the dry Musashino plateau, were dry field, never flooded, and worked like any dry
field in ridged rows; no row is recorded with its paddy in strips behind the houses. A village on a river plain held
paddy and dry field both, from about a fifth of its land in dry field to more than half, but whether a lowland row's dry
field lay behind each house, and which farms held it, is not recorded.

At Santome, a dry-field colony opened in the 1690s, each household's strip was about 240 ft wide and 2,240 ft deep,
about 12 acres; across the Musashino new fields a farm was given about 2.5 acres. A farm's frontage on the rows
measured ran from about 54 to 240 ft.

Guesses:
- That only the farms across the street from the paddy hold a strip behind them, and the farms between the street and
  the paddy hold none: no source says which farms of a lowland row held dry field.
- That the holding is dry field rather than paddy: the measured strips were dry field, but no lowland row's were measured.
- The strip's depth on a street laid out first, three lots: Santome's strips ran far deeper, but in a dry-field colony.
- The compact holding's depth on a row along the field's dry edge, one lot.

Depiction: Where a row's farms stand on both sides of its street, the map draws a holding behind each farm across the
street from the paddy; on a row with farms on one side only, none is drawn. Behind a street laid out first the holding is
a strip one lot wide, cut into plots about 150 ft deep, a size chosen for the map since the real plots' size is not
recorded. Behind a row that follows the field's dry edge, which stands for a levee or a fan's foot, the holding is compact
and near the house, as on a dike.
