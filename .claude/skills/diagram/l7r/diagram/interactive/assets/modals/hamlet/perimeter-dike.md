About: A polder's dike was a bank of earth heaped right round fields reclaimed from a lake, a river or a marsh, to hold the
water out: the ground inside lay below the water outside some or all of the time, and was drained through gates when the
water outside fell. In the dike-pond country of the Pearl River delta the dike was made of the mud dug out of the ponds.
In one Lake Tai village in the 1930s, each polder's raised margin was both its dike and its mulberry ground, with the
houses beside it.

The dikes whose planting is recorded were planted along their length: on one great Song polder, rows of mulberry flanked
the ridge and rows of willows ran along it, with reeds below. Willow roots bind a bank against the water, and willows
were cut for their shoots, woven into baskets, fish traps and fences. Willow and mulberry
stood bare in winter. Polders took the shapes the ground gave them, square, round, oblong, many-sided or irregular;
around Lake Tai the rectangular polder came only after 1978.

That great Song dike, rebuilt from 1061, was about five times as broad as it was high, broad at its foot and narrow at
its top. A river dike of early Edo Japan stood about 12 ft (3.6 m) high on an 18 ft (5.5 m) base. A village polder's own dike height and breadth are not recorded.

Guesses:
- That the dike's outer face followed the water's edge in gentle curves: polders took many shapes, but no account says
  how an outer dike ran along a shore.
- That a small polder's dike stood about 5 ft (1.5 m) high: no height for a small polder's dike was found, and the heights
  found, a great polder's and a domain's river dike, bound it only from above.

Depiction: The map draws the dike as a band of heaped earth all the way round the polder, 14 to 40 ft across and changing
in width along its length, mottled as mud patched year after year is. Where it runs broader than about 18 ft, the base of
the early Edo river dikes, it is drawn so on purpose, so that the dike holding the water off the whole polder reads as
the greater bank beside the crop dikes round the fish ponds. Two rows of trees run along it, willow on the water side and
mulberry on the field side; the maps set them on the bank's faces, where a Ming river manual planted them on the ground
off its foot, so they read as the dike's own planting. The willows are spaced about 8.5 ft apart and the mulberry about
4.4 ft, so their crowns read as touching trees rather than one hedge. The band and its rows are cut where a channel
crosses it, at a gate. The farmhouses keep their own windbreak groves besides, since the dike's low, bare trees did little
to shelter a village.

Name: perimeter dike
Covers: the earthwork band of `dikes[]` - the polder's enclosing dike, gapped at its sluices
