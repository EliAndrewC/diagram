Urban features: the research behind the state markers, trades and services

The findings behind the state markers, trades, wells, gate furniture and caste geography on these maps, the decisions they drove, and - where no generator draws the feature yet - the rules a map follows.

Every entry: what the research found, the decision it drove, and any deliberate departure from literal reality.

The notice board (kosatsuba) - siting is a TRAFFIC decision

Sources: ogose-kosatsuba, kosatsu-jawiki, adachi-kosatsu (the inline links below)

Every Edo town AND village kept the official edict board1 - standing shogunal/domain law,2 porter and packhorse rate tables,3 currency regulations (unsourced), ban edicts - and its siting was a TRAFFIC decision, not an administrative one: busy street corners, checkpoints, bridgeheads, the gate or center of the settlement,4 "in a prominent position alongside the main highway" (unsourced). Edo's principal board stood at Nihonbashi (the bridgehead where the five highways began) (unsourced: the page names Nihonbashi first among six great boards, without calling it "principal", and does not mention five highways),5 post towns' mid-shuku by the transport office - not at the government office (unsourced).

the ofuregaki circulars reached the peasantry through exactly this board (unsourced), posted where the settlement's one lane carries everyone - by the headman's frontage or the lane junction/entrance,6 since the headman (or in a hamlet the senior farmer answering to the village headman) received, copied, and relayed the circulars and was REQUIRED to be functionally literate for the job (unsourced). One reader per settlement makes the board work - notices were also read aloud by officials (unsourced) - so peasant literacy is no objection even at a 50-inhabitant hamlet; the board is also the state's standing presence in a place that may see a magistrate's deputy twice a year.

  • The bakufu set kosatsuba at points of heavy passage: barriers and ports, the foot of large bridges, and the entrances and centers of towns and villages75; in farming villages at the village center, the shrine precinct, or the place where villagers assembled (unsourced); also at bridgeheads and before the gate of the village officials' houses (Fuchu kosatsuba, Tokyo Jinjacho; Ogose town cultural property H26-04; 高札 - Wikipedia (ja); Adachi museum, 高札). Every attested site is ON the way - a verge, a gate front, a bridge foot - never a plot of open ground beside it. Decision: the board stands 6 ft from tread edge to board edge at the hamlet and village tiers; the 60 ft band stays only as the fallback when no roadside seat fits, and for towns and cities until their maps re-roll. The 60 ft figure is not a finding.

Every board is sited by the map's own probe, never hand-picked (the GM's ruling of 2026-07-27 on the two town boards that had been placed by hand: "fix both of them"). Both were wrong, and wrong the same way. Hoshizora's stood on the southwest arrival stretch by the theater and the flophouse; the case for it was that the packed merchant frontage upstream had no clear verge and the arrival stretch was the honest second best: 3 structures within 250 ft of it, against a peak of 30 elsewhere on the same map - a tenth of the traffic on offer. Hirameki's stood on the main street just inside the front gate; the case for it was that every arrival passes it: 10 against a peak of 29. Arrivals do pass it; the town does not, and the board is for the inhabitants rather than for the traffic coming through. Neither map lacked frontage. What both lacked was a seat that could hold the board and its caption, which is the next paragraph. Sited by probe they stand at 19 and at 29, Hirameki's on the busiest ground its own map has.

The caption is part of the seat, or the board walks out of town (GM 2026-07-27, from "the notice board in Ubame does not look well-placed; I'd expect it to be in a higher-traffic area"). The drawn marker is small and fits almost anywhere; its caption does not, and the busiest frontage is exactly where there is least room for one. A siter that hunts for ground large enough to hold both therefore walks away from the traffic by construction, and Ubame's board duly ended up across the bridge on the far bank from the whole town, about 120 ft past the east end of the shop rows: 8 structures within 250 ft against 23 on the high street. Nothing automated could see it, because the stretch of road beyond the last house is still a road. So the probe scores the caption instead of hunting for ground: a seat whose caption fits outranks any seat whose caption does not, and among those the busiest wins; a board with nowhere to put a caption is still placed, so the shortfall is reported rather than swallowed by a siter that quietly fails. Whether a feature stands on the traffic is a judgment call: there is no mathematical rule for "lots of foot traffic".

The board is the last thing placed, and it clears no ground at all (GM 2026-08-29). It is placed after every other feature on the map, the crop and the title included, and the reasoning is about the settlement rather than about the drawing: "where you put the notice board on the map does depend on what other features already exist ... the real humans that live in the society that decide where the notice board will go will look around at the things which already exist and then decide where to put the notice board. They may even decide to move a notice board which has already been placed." Every other feature either reserves ground or grows into it; the board does neither, so it is the one thing that should see the whole map before it chooses. Two consequences. The cleared woodland the board used to claim - about 55 ft of it, more than a well's or a shrine's - is retired, after an entrance seat on Kashikawa's windward fringe punched a 40 ft hole in the shelter belt that nothing replanted; the GM's ruling: "it would be very easy to put the notice board at the edge of the forest. We could even display it as being underneath the canopy because I think in many cases it would be ... humans would not need to clear any amount of space in order to put up a notice board at the side of a path." And the seat is tested against the map's own view rather than against the cloud of houses, which stood in for a crop that had not been computed yet and refused every seat outside the built ground - which is precisely where an entrance board belongs.

A board under a canopy is fine, and its label is why (GM 2026-08-29): "As long as there is a label attached to it and the label is visible - which it should be, because labels are always on the topmost layer of the way the image is rendered - then someone will always be able to see it, even on the PNG version of a map. The fact that the label is present tells someone looking at the map where the notice board is." Checked rather than assumed, across all five scripted hamlets: the caption is drawn above every tree crown on every map, and in the drawing itself the caption comes after the last crown. A crown cannot paint over a label. So "is the board findable under a tree" is settled - the label carries the read, the same convention that lets an oversized location marker stand for a board whose true 12 ft frame is too small to draw at city grain.

The board stands on a main way, and that is held by the placer rather than by a test after the fact (GM 2026-08-02, from Ubame again, where the caption-aware probe dutifully found the busiest clear verge and it was a side lane one block south of the high street, 49 legal ft off a way and 200 ft off the road the whole town walks: "The notice board should be along a main street/road on every map, never a side street... in order to be more noticed."). Every road is a main way by construction and so is a street the map declares as its gate-to-yamen avenue; where a map declares either, the probe samples only those verges, so a board on a side lane cannot be proposed. Two scope edges are deliberate. A map with no declared hierarchy - a lane web, a town whose streets are all unflagged - is exempt, because there is nothing "main" to stand on and the busiest-node scoring stands in for the judgment. And the punishment ground is not covered on purpose (GM 2026-08-02: "other map features like punishment grounds don't always need to be along a main road, but a notice board must be"): the ground is a display for locals who already know where justice is done, while the board must ambush the eye of everyone passing through.

The rule the map follows: every settlement carries a board, and it stands on the busiest way its own map has, squared to that way so the 7 by 3 ft face meets the traffic broadside rather than edge-on - within 30 degrees of some route segment in the siting band, which still presents about seven eighths of the face and clears the worst legitimate case in the pool, a board taking a road bend's bearing about 18 degrees off its nearest segment. At the hamlet and village tiers the board stands 6 ft from the tread edge; the 60 ft band is the fallback when no roadside seat fits, and the figure towns and cities still use. A CITY draws the SET and labels ONE: the principal board at the central market node plus a board on every main gate's approach, each within about 800 ft of its gate, the label going to whichever board has room for it, since an unlabeled board fits a tight gate verge that a labeled one cannot. The 30 degree tolerance and the 800 ft corridor are calibrations against the drawn maps, not figures from a record; the 60 ft band was a search radius wearing a rule's clothes and is not a finding either.

The justice works - why a county seat executes, and why the ground is outside

Sources: caishikou-enwiki, qiushen-zhwiki; suzugamori-jawiki, kozukappara-jawiki (the two highway-entrance grounds). The 棄市 term and the third Edo ground (Ōwada) were not confirmed in the pages read; the county-jail reading of the setting's own budget notes is setting-canon

  • The China/Japan split, and how it resolves. This is one of the few places the two grounding traditions genuinely disagree, so the reconciliation is load-bearing rather than decorative. Japan pushed executions OUT of the settlement for kegare (death pollution) - Edo's execution grounds (two are confirmed, Suzugamori and Kozukappara; a third, Ōwada, is unconfirmed)77 all sat at highway entrances outside the center,7 castle towns monopolized them, and village authority topped out at banishment. China executed in the marketplace (棄市 qishi, "abandon in the market"; Qing Beijing's Caishikou was the vegetable market at a crossroads outside Xuanwumen),8 and although a county magistrate could not CONFIRM a death sentence - capital cases climbed to the Board of Punishments and the emperor's autumn check-marking (秋審 qiushen9) - the confirmed sentence came back DOWN and was carried out at the county seat where the crime happened (unsourced: the page gives the four outcomes - confirmed, deferred, mitigable, kept to support parents - and nothing on where a confirmed sentence was carried out), because local deterrence was the point. So: China supplies the jurisdiction (the seat executes), Japan supplies the siting (past the built edge). That is the answer to the question every town on these maps is drawn from: what would a Chinese county seat administered by samurai do?
  • It explains a line of the setting's canon. The setting's own county budget funds "prisoner food, jail upkeep, court materials" at county level - a jail with no execution line - while "ceremonial executions" appear only in the domain and Imperial budgets. Read through the Chinese chain that is precisely right: the county jail is a holding pen for the condemned while the warrant travels, not a punishment in itself. (The building plans record the same shape: Edo detention was a holding function, not imprisonment-as-punishment.)
  • The governing variables. For the execution ground: the road and the direction pollution runs - NOT population and not the settlement's geometric edge. The ground exists to be seen by travelers arriving, so it hugs the busiest way out; kegare puts it downwind, downstream, and on the outcast side, which is why the ground is measured against the burakumin quarter (in the setting's canon, burakumin perform every execution that is not a samurai's). For the punishment ground: foot traffic.
  • Where the sizes come from. The hard anchor is Suzugamori: 74 x 16.2 m (~243 x 53 ft, about a third of an acre),10 serving Edo - a city of one million - for 220 years.11 Execution grounds are SMALL; the deterrent is the sight of the posts from the highway, not acreage. The tier ladder scales down from that by execution VOLUME, which is also what sets each tier's character: at ~1-3 executions per 100,000 per year, a county of ~7,000-8,000 inhabitants reaches the formal channel about once every 5-10 years (bandit sweeps add batches), a province sees on the order of one a year, a domain capital a handful. Hence the county ground is drawn weedy with EMPTY post sockets and no hoarding: a busy scaffold at county scale would assert something false about how often the Empire kills.
  • The beating is not here. The bamboo (笞 / 杖) was a COURT act administered inside the yamen courtyard before the magistrate's bench - which in our maps is the magistracy compound - while the 枷 jia (cangue) was the public part, worn one to six months with the offender's name, crime, and sentence inscribed on the boards for passers-by to read. So the street feature DISPLAYS; it does not try. It also draws no notice board, because the crime text rides on the cangue and the settlement already has a kosatsuba.
  • A deliberate departure from literal reality, disclosed. The 400 ft clearance between the execution ground and the burial ground is a map-legibility floor, not a historical measurement. In reality the executed went into a pit AT the ground (Kozukappara's burials were haphazard enough that a memorial hall was founded beside it in 1667 for that reason)12 while the community's dead went to temple graveyards elsewhere in the city entirely - usually a different road out of town, often a mile off. Our maps are a few thousand feet across and cannot hold that, so the separation is compressed and the RELATIVE ordering kept honest instead: the execution ground is always the further-out, more polluted of the two. Analogy to the project's existing pollution constant (the cremation ground and tanning yard demand 120 ft clear of dwellings, and two POLLUTED grounds need more separation than polluted-from-clean does) gives about 150 ft, and at 225 ft Nagahara's ground still read, in the rendered map, as part of the burial cluster beside it. 400 ft is derived from the picture rather than by analogy - at the coarsest scale we draw it is the distance at which the two are unmistakably two places.
  • Two rules, and why they are shaped as they are. (1) The ground stands ~120 real ft from a drawn ROAD but ~400 ft from a GATE: a road is a line the ground can hug (Suzugamori sat on the Tokaido),13 while a gate is a point standing in for the road that leaves it, and Edo's grounds were miles out along their highways. (2) The outcast-side rule tests DIRECTION only: radius is an artifact of where the wall happens to be, while the claim the research actually supports (downwind, downstream, the outcast side) is directional.
  • The boundary stone earns its place. 道祖神 dosojin / sae no kami stand at village boundaries, passes, and crossroads, and the etymology is the point: sae means "to block", and the deity's job is to stop evil, pestilence, and pollution from ENTERING. That converts "outside the settlement" from a vague spatial claim into a stated ritual boundary - the ground is not merely far from the houses, it is on the far side of the stone that keeps pollution out - which is why the stone stands outside the wall. A dosojin inside the rampart bounds nothing.

The pages read call Suzugamori and Kozukappara Edo's14 「the two great execution grounds」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「二大刑場」); the third (Ōwada) was not confirmed. The siting finding - past the built edge, on the highway - rests on the two.

The stone stands nearer the houses than the ground does, and a radial ordering could not say so (GM 2026-07-27). The between-ness rule compared two distances to the core's centroid, which orders the pair about one point - and a settlement is not a disc, so a ground tucked in beside a quarter on a different bearing satisfied it while lying inside the stone's line for most of the town: at Ubame, 86 of 118 dwellings stood nearer the killing ground than the stone that is supposed to bound it. The ordering is measured to the settlement's edge instead, and the edge is the rampart where there is one and the nearest dwelling where there is not - measuring a walled city to its nearest dwelling would let a lone hinterland farmstead stand for the town.

The rule the map follows: a seat of justice draws two grounds sited by opposite logics, and a settlement that is not a seat of justice draws neither. The PUNISHMENT ground - a cangue frame, a flogging post and a kneeling stone on about 30 by 12 ft of tamped earth - stands inside the settlement at the market or the magistracy frontage, within about 60 ft of a street and never in the roadbed, inside the rampart where there is one, and carries no notice board of its own because the crime rides on the cangue. The EXECUTION ground stands outside: past the boundary stone, within about 120 ft of a drawn road but about 400 ft out from a gate (a road is a line the ground can hug; a gate is a point standing in for the road that leaves it), 400 ft clear of the burial ground, off the farmland, and on the same side of town as the outcast quarter - a direction, never a radius. It is sized by tier: about 60 by 60 ft at a county seat, drawn weedy and unfenced with empty post sockets; about 100 by 60 ft at a provincial city, screened on three sides with the road side open; 150 to 250 by 50 to 80 ft at a capital. Its road side faces the road. The BOUNDARY stone stands on the road out, between the settlement and the ground: past the rampart where there is one, and 60 ft clear of the nearest dwelling's wall where there is not - not the 120 ft the ground owes, because that is a pollution separation and a boundary stone pollutes nothing; it marks where clean ground ends and all it must not do is stand among the houses. And the stone stands nearer the settlement's edge than the ground does. Every distance here is wall to wall, never center to center. The tier sizes are calibrated down from the one measured ground by execution volume rather than taken from a record, and the 60 ft around the stone is this project's own figure.

Trade works - which trades outgrow the shop glyph, and why

Sources: the pawnshop and kiln rest on their own entries (tontine-monastery-lending; "Kiln works") fukagawa-kiba-summary (the lumber yard as a 300,000 m2 waterfront works ordered out of Edo after 1657 - a guess, no readable page supports it); the brewery, dye works, oil press, bathhouse and farrier premises were searched on ja.wikipedia (酒蔵, 紺屋, 絞油 404, 銭湯, 馬沓) and none gives a footprint - they stay as reasoning

  • The GM's question, during the provincial-city audit: "are there trades whose real premises, with their attached works, yards, and outbuildings, are materially bigger than a shophouse, i.e. significant space our maps are not representing?"
  • The frame. Song/Ming China first, Edo Japan second. City maps already draw commerce as generic ~48x32 ft shop rowhouses plus merchant kura and dedicated inn/stables/flophouse glyphs, so the only question is which trades BREAK that glyph.
  • The shortlist - premises visibly bigger than a rowhouse at this scale. Sake/miso/soy brewery (sakagura; Chinese zaofang) is the big one: even a small county-seat brewer needed a vat hall of 5-6 ft fermentation tanks, a koji room, a rice-steaming area with masonry kamado and chimney, its own well, and a rice kura - a minimal hall runs 60-120 ft x 30-40 ft BEHIND a normal shopfront, 3-8x the shophouse, and surviving small-town compounds reach whole-block scale (one Nagano brewer at ~1,500 tsubo / ~53,000 sq ft). It was very often the largest commercial building in a small town; expect 1-2 per seat of ~3,000, sited in town on good well water because brewers were town elite. Dyer (kon-ya): modest building, large GROUND signature - the workshop's sunken indigo vats and boiling hearth fit a deep shophouse, but the DRYING does not (poles, racks, and rooftop platforms dominate the block in Hiroshige's Kanda Konya-cho print) and rinsing happened in open water (nunosarashi), so a town dyer needs a ~2,000-5,000 sq ft drying yard (bolts run 35-40 ft) or riverbank frontage. Lumber dealer (zaimokuya): stacked timber and floated logs need a ~3,000-8,000 sq ft open yard with a river landing, at the edge of town near the downstream gate or wharf.16 Oil presser (aburaya / youfang): the wedge-and-beam press is a massive timber machine (beam 15-30 ft) plus a seed-roasting stove and an ox-driven edge-runner mill on a ~20-25 ft circular track - a fire-conscious press barn ~40-60 x 25-30 ft toward the edge. Pawnshop (shichiya): an ordinary shop whose tell is STORAGE - pledges are bulky, so a prosperous broker kept 2-3 fireproof kura in a walled rear court,15 i.e. the existing kura glyph multiplied rather than a new building type. Bathhouse (sento): an ordinary streetfront building with a rear furnace and chimney, whose visible extra is a ~1,000-2,000 sq ft firewood stack yard; commercial baths are China-first correct, attested from the Song, so a city may keep 1-2.
  • The null results, which are equally load-bearing. The long tail genuinely fits the shop glyph - tofu maker, noodle and confection shops, apothecary, teahouse, cooper, tatami maker, carpenter's shop (his big work happens on site), rice dealer (treadle-pestle hulling in-shop, grain in a rear kura). And some trades belong OUTSIDE town entirely: tile, pottery, charcoal, and lime kilns are pushed to the periphery or to nearby villages by fire law and smoke, and tanneries and slaughter go downstream-riverside with the extramural burakumin quarter, never inside.
  • FARRIERY - why this setting has farriers where Edo Japan did not (GM 2026-07-25). Edo Japan did not shoe horses: it used woven straw horse-sandals (umagutsu / umazouri), and nailed iron shoeing arrived with Meiji. That is right about Japan and wrong about this Rokugan, for two reasons the GM's own notes already carried. (1) Iron. Japan's constraint was material, not ignorance - iron sand (satetsu) smelted with charcoal is a low-yield, fuel-hungry process, so iron went to blades, armor, and tools long before hooves. Rokugan is continental, with real ore bodies and whole iron districts already in the setting's canon (Tatarano, "bellows plain"; Kuroiwa; Ubame's charcoal-sited furnaces; the Reiji hill works; Crab iron for the Wall). Ore is abundant and iron is a normal industrial good, while good STEEL stays scarce and expensive - which buys horseshoes, plain low-carbon iron, without deflating a good blade. (2) Institutional mileage, more than paving. Hard surfaces do abrade hoof, but the sharper driver is annual miles per horse: the setting's own budget notes give ~5,000 waystations on ~50,000 miles of Imperial road at ~10-mile spacing - about 3x the density of the Tang yi network - plus 500-1,000 mounted couriers and mounted anti-banditry patrols. A courier horse working a fixed stage all year wears hoof at a rate no farm horse approaches, and straw sandals are consumables that wear out within a day's travel. Straw scales for a farmer walking an ox to the next village; it does not scale for institutionalized relay. Canon already assumed this: the setting's own budget notes staff busy trunk-route waystations with "a smith for shoeing horses and weapon maintenance," and the setting's canon introduces Moto Khuyag as a farrier who offers to reshoe the PCs' horses - a Rokugani-born one, who learned the trade at Kyuden Shinjo. Two arguments were considered and deliberately NOT relied on, because they do not survive checking: "later Chinese dynasties nailed iron shoes" (Chinese hoof practice ran mostly to trimming and strap-on or woven boots; nailed shoes are firmly a European development, and Rome's cursus publicus used strap-on hipposandals, not nails), and "the Mongol Yam proves it" (steppe horses on grass were famously ridden UNSHOD, which is a counterexample). The conclusion stands on iron price and relay mileage alone.
  • What it changes on the map, and what it does not. Even in Europe a farrier was a one-forge smithy with a bit of yard, and the big "farrier-forge-with-stable-yard" compound is a coaching-inn image that does not earn its footprint at city scale. So an ordinary town smith (kaji-ya) who also shoes still draws as a generic shop rowhouse - shoeing changes his REPERTOIRE, not his premises - and no new establishment joins the shop rows. What earns real ground is only the place horses CONCENTRATE: the gate caravan yard and the Imperial-road relay town, where the map draws a dedicated shed and apron. Two rules fall out of the physical facts. It is not attached to the stables: an open forge against a hay-and-timber stall range is a fire the yard does not survive, so real yards kept the smithy across the ground (anchored on the buildings research's ~6-8 ft wooden-service fire gap; the measure runs from the whole footprint, shed plus apron, which is deliberately conservative since the shed sits at the apron's far end). And the apron carries an ox-shoeing frame: Rokugan's draft animal is mostly the ox, a cloven hoof takes two plates per foot, and an ox cannot balance on three legs, so it must be slung in timber stocks - about 7 x 4 ft, drawn true-size like everything else. Sizes are the historical anchors with no legibility inflation: an 18-20 ft village smithy for the shed, and an apron a horse's length (~8 ft) plus room to lead one in and turn it. Straw survives everywhere iron is not worth it - peasant pack horses, oxen on soft paddy tracks, poor and mountain provinces - because it is free, not because iron is unknown; that is setting texture rather than a drawn feature, and it lives in the setting's own budget notes.
  • Frequency by scale, and the reasoning behind it. Rokugan's horse population is LOW relative to Europe - peasants work oxen, and horses are a samurai and Imperial animal - so farriery is not a mass trade and the count must not scale with population the way the bathhouse does. Provincial city: exactly one, at a gate caravan yard (not one per gate). Town: only on an Imperial road, which is why the rule keys on the Imperial road rather than on town scale alone - at ~10-mile waystation spacing plenty of towns simply are not on the network, and that non-uniformity is the point. Village and hamlet: never; the village smith trims hooves and tacks on straw with no dedicated premises. A county magistracy keeps only 2-4 horses per the buildings research, so it keeps no permanent forge either - the smith walks over from town.
  • What a city draws (GM 2026-07-24, "I'd like all of them"): brewery compound (vat hall + shopfront + rice kura + its own PRIVATE well; both cities keep one, and Tango's stands on the pond); dyer's drying yard (must sit ON water - both cities, since dyeing needs rinsing water rather than bulk transport, so a landlocked city keeps its dyers; Tango's rinses to the DRAINAGE side, never the field-supply channels); riverside lumber yard (a river port only - Nagahara, since timber is the one trade that genuinely needs water transport at scale, and the yard abuts the bank but never overlaps the water); oil-press barn; pawnshop; bathhouse (count rolled from population); kiln (outside the walls); farrier (2026-07-25). The tanning yard is drawn too and has its own entry below.

The rule the map follows: every provincial city keeps a brewery (the largest commercial building in the place - a vat hall, a shopfront, a rice storehouse and its own private well, sited on good water and excluded from every public-well count), a dye works whose rinsing and drying yard stands on water within about 120 ft of a stream, channel, canal, pond or moat and rinses to the drainage side rather than into a field-supply channel, an oil press toward the edge, a pawnshop whose tell is its walled two-storehouse pledge court, a bathhouse with a fenced fuel yard behind it, a kiln works strictly outside the walls, and a farrier standing on a gate caravan yard within 250 ft of a stables and at least 6 ft clear of every other footprint. Three works are declared rather than universal: a RIVER-PORT city also keeps a riverside lumber yard within about 180 ft of the bank, abutting the water but never overlapping it, since stacked timber stands on dry ground and a landlocked city has none; a town keeps a farrier only if it stands on an Imperial road, a relay town working courier and caravan stock hard enough to keep a forge where an ordinary market town does not; and a charcoal district or an iron district is drawn only where the map declares one. The tanning yard is the one work of this kind that is not city-only. The bathhouse COUNT rolls from population: one per full 2,000 inhabitants, plus a chance of one more equal to the remainder, floored at one - so a seat of 2,500 keeps one and rolls a quarter chance of a second, and a seat of 4,000 keeps exactly two. The 2,000 is Edo's own peak ratio, about 523 bathhouses for some 1.1 million residents in 1808 (this rests on general reading; no source is cited).83 Everything else a seat trades in - tofu, tea, medicine, cooperage, carpentry, rice dealing, and the ordinary smith, whose hoof work changes his repertoire rather than his footprint - stays inside the generic shop rows, and that null result is load-bearing: a works earns a glyph of its own only when its premises outgrow a rowhouse.

The rule the map follows: nuisance separations run on four rungs, and each rung is a different hazard rather than a different degree of the same one. About 6 ft between an attended open forge and anything that burns, which is the farrier's shed against hay and timber. 30 ft around an unattended charcoal stack, about one flame-height clear of a fully involved stack. 60 ft around a kiln body and around a refining forge, the attended-fire rung: a very large fire, but one being stoked and watched. And 120 ft between a nuisance carried on the air - the crematory, the tanning yard - and any dwelling not of the caste that works it. The rungs are reasoned against one another rather than measured from a record: only the 30 ft carries a derivation of its own, and reusing the 120 ft figure for charcoal was considered and rejected, because it defends against smell over distance and would push a charcoal yard off the cart route it exists to serve.

The log boom - a shore-fast holding pen, not sticks in the stream

Sources: nishikori-tsunaba, kiso-unzaihou, susquehanna-boom, stcroix-boom-nps, hudson-big-boom, kiba-koto, shangxinhe-gazetteer, timber-rafting-wiki (cited inline below)

  • The GM's reaction to the first drawn boom: "it just looks like a bunch of logs in the middle of the river. How did log booms really work? Did they block the whole river? I assume they were at least partially against the shore."
  • Two systems moved timber by water, and the full-river boom belongs to only one of them. Loose-log driving (Japanese kanagashi, one log at a time) ran the upper, unnavigable reaches; rafting ran the lower river.17 The one place a boom legitimately spans the whole water is the catch point where driving hands off to rafting: on the Kiso this was the Owari domain's tsunaba18 ("rope place") at Nishikori, sited exactly where the river leaves its gorge - a great rope stretched bank to bank, in use from the 1340s, catching ~300,000 logs a year in a season running autumn equinox to spring equinox, administered by a timber magistracy of 138 permanent officials (unsourced: the source page displays only a partial extract - the site's location and unrelated 1285/1345 Mino timber dates - not these figures). Rafts were assembled there (three-man crews) and floated on to the storage yards at Nagoya and the bay ports.19 A full-span catch boom is legitimate ONLY where the river is not a navigation route - upstream, at the gorge mouth, never at a port.
  • Where the river carries boats, the boom runs ALONG it - and the law said so. The great American boom works are the same furniture at maximum documented scale. The Susquehanna Boom at Williamsport ran seven miles, its crib line running diagonally across the river from the south bank near Duboistown to the north bank near Linden,78 strung from ~350-400 man-made islands (timber cribs filled with stone, 22 ft high),20 enclosing the holding pen between boom line and shore; it held up to 300 million board feet afloat at once - on the order of a million logs21 - and corralled ~40 million over its working life (unsourced: the only lifetime figure on the page is mill output, 5.5 billion board feet 1861-91). The St. Croix boom works divided the river lengthwise with piers, pilings and floating log fences into log channels and holding pens, a navigation channel maintained alongside and a hinged22 "sheer boom" at the head swinging open and closed to admit logs. Boom companies were statutorily barred from materially obstructing navigation, and spills into the fairway drew lawsuits and negligence rulings.23 Anchoring was always to fixed ground: bank abutments, stone-filled cribs, driven piles - the Hudson's Big Boom rode on hewn timbers bolted four-together raftwise, chained end to end over friction rollers to abutments on the banks.24 A boom is a floating fence; a fence anchored to nothing holds nothing.
  • At the destination city, timber sits in slack water, in bulk. Edo kept its stock at Kiba - a dedicated lumber district of canals and storage ponds where rafts were poled in and logs floated25 (water storage keeps timber sound and safe from fire - unsourced: the page gives fire risk as the reason the trade moved across the river, not as a property of water storage). Nanjing (China-first) kept its stock at Shangxinhe, a ~9 km side channel off the Yangtze (unsourced: no length is on the page) where timber and bamboo rafts moored along the banks in such mass that a permanent timber-merchant town grew around them,26 constantly sufficient through all seasons at its Qing peak. Nagoya used the Shirotori yards the same way.27 Nowhere is a city's working stock a scatter of loose logs in mid-channel: it is an enclosed pen against the bank, moored raft-mats lining a slack side-water, or off-river ponds.
  • The decisions it drove. The glyph is a PEN: chain anchored to the bank at both ends (mooring posts), running out around the held water on pile clusters, with the raft-mats drawn as a near-solid surface between chain and shore - the largest working thing on a timber city's water, per the glyph's own founding rationale. It never spans a navigated channel: drawn at about a third of the river's width and never more than two fifths, with the bank edge required to ride ON the shore line and the pen moored off the lumber yard it feeds. For Minami specifically: the Hayakawa is 120 ft wide and navigated (wharf, dock basin, cargo canal), so the city gets the shore-pen form; the romantic full-span tsunaba is off-map lore - the Fox catch-works at the gorge mouth upstream, where the loose drive becomes the rafts that arrive at the city.

Kiln works - a kiln is not a tile kiln, and the potters live at it

Sources: kawara-jawiki (commoners board-roofed, tile general only at the end of Edo - tile demand confined to the few); the potters living at the noborigama and the fire gap remain unsourced

  • Two GM questions: "would the tile kiln have housing close to it, i.e. would whoever works the kiln also live next to it?" and "why is it specifically a 'tile kiln' and not just a kiln? Are there potters in the city with their own non-tile kilns? Or are we not accounting for them as well?"
  • THE VOLUME REASONING RUNS THE WRONG WAY - it is a kiln, not a tile kiln. The GM's instinct ("I wouldn't have expected that to be such a huge difference as to warrant one getting a map glyph and the other not") is right, and if anything understates it. Tile demand is lumpy and project-driven. A fired clay roof lasts generations - Japanese kawara roofs are repaired in patches, not replaced (unsourced) - and in a county seat tile reaches only the temple halls, the gates, the governor's compound and a handful of rich merchants; the rest of the town is thatch and shingle.28 So the tile kiln has a huge year when a temple hall is reroofed and repair work otherwise. Pottery demand is continuous. Every household breaks and replaces bowls, pots and jars on a steady cycle, which is precisely why ceramics are the most reliable thing in an archaeological layer: the stuff never stops being consumed. The map's own features make it worse - the brewery needs fermentation vessels and big storage jars (kame), and a large storage jar is heavy and cheap per pound, so it is made near where it is used rather than freighted in. Per YEAR, a seat of this size burns more clay into vessels than into roof tiles.
  • So why one glyph rather than two. Because at county-seat scale it is genuinely one works. Coarse tile and coarse domestic ware share a clay source, an operator, and a low firing; a works fires mixed loads and there is no reason to draw the same establishment twice. What does NOT belong here is fine stoneware, which needs its own hotter and longer firing - that is a province's specialty (the Seto/Bizen/Jingdezhen pattern, a whole district known for one ware), not something every county seat keeps. A map that knows what its particular works fires says so in its caption; the default caption is just "kiln".
  • THE WORKERS LIVE AT THE KILN. Three independent reasons, any one of which would be enough. (1) A firing runs for days. An anagama or noborigama is stoked round the clock in shifts until the ware is done, then sealed and left to cool - nobody walks back into town at dusk in the middle of one. (2) The kiln stands at the CLAY, not at the customer. Siting follows the clay pit, the fuel supply, and a slope to build the chambers into, and those constraints pull digging, weathering, throwing, drying and firing out to one spot together. (3) It is how the trade was organized. CHINA FIRST: Song and Ming kiln districts were worked by registered kiln households living at their kilns, with Jingdezhen an entire city grown around its kilns. Japan corroborates rather than leads - Seto, Tokoname, Bizen, Imado on Edo's fringe, Awataguchi and Kiyomizu at Kyoto's edge are kiln VILLAGES and kiln quarters, not workshops somebody commutes to.
  • Banishment is about the CITY, not the operator. Fire law and smoke are a real constraint, and the firing does not happen inside the dense blocks. But that rule is about keeping an uncontrollable fire out of a packed timber town, and it says nothing whatever against the households whose trade it is. They stand off the kiln by the same fire gap anybody else would and no further. Getting this backwards - "banished" read as "banished including its people" - is the tannery's caste logic borrowed by a trade that does not carry it. Potters are not hinin. The tanning yard's siting rules turn on the burakumin quarter; the kiln's turn on clay, fuel and fire, and the two must not be conflated.
  • THE FORM. A low earthen mound is a CHARCOAL or lime kiln's shape, and would be indistinguishable from Ubame's charcoal district. A tile or pottery kiln in this culture is a long chambered tunnel built into a slope: firebox at the bottom, chambers climbing, chimney at the top. The chambered silhouette also carries the siting fact a mound would hide - the works wants rising ground. (No separate slope band is drawn behind the kiln: at city scale it reads as a stray tab off the kiln's shoulder rather than as ground, and the chambered form says it already.)
  • THE 60 FT FIRE GAP, and why it is not a new number. It joins the existing separation ladder at the rung the refining forge already occupies. A firing is a very large fire, but an ATTENDED one - being stoked in shifts is the whole reason it runs the way it does - so it sits with the live worked fire at 60 ft and not with the UNATTENDED self-heating charcoal stack at 30 ft, nor with the 120 ft nuisance figures, which defend against smell carried on air rather than against ignition. Duration does the work here that the forced blast does for the forge. The gap is measured from the KILN BODY rather than from the works' ground, because the works' own cottages stand inside that ground - a bounding-rect test could only ever measure the gap to somebody else's house.
  • The null result, which is what answers the GM's second question directly. The town's potters are NOT missing from the map. Only the FIRING is banished: a crockery dealer or a potter's shopfront inside the walls is an ordinary shophouse and stays in the generic shop rows with the tofu maker and the cooper, exactly as the null-results list above has it. The relationship worth remembering is that the ware is MADE at the works outside and SOLD by a dealer inside, which is the same shape as the charcoal district's "burned where the wood grows, tallied where it is sold".
  • Deliberate departures from literal reality. (a) One works per seat. A real kiln district could be several kilns and a dozen households; we draw one works with two or three cottages, because the map's job is to say "this is where the seat's pots and tiles come from" and a second identical works teaches a reader nothing. (b) The cottages count as the works' own, not as the settlement's housing stock. This is a bookkeeping decision, not a claim about the setting: every dwelling rule - public-well reach, ward classification, the burakumin standoff - is written about the settlement's own houses, and a satellite works' two cottages would be judged by rules that were never about them. The one place they must count as dwellings is the works' own well. (c) Sizes are true feet at the map's grain with no legibility inflation, pitched against the pool's other bulk works (the charcoal yard's 88 x 58, the brewery's 96 ft hall): a 46 ft kiln, a 32 x 18 ft throwing and drying shed, a 30 x 24 ft clay pit, and 28 x 18 ft cottages on a 140 x 120 ft ground.

The kiln glyph reads poorly at map zoom.

Tanning yards - hides come from fallen draft stock, and water is the gate

Sources: shiniuba-jawiki (danna-ba / kusaba), himeji-shironameshi-jawiki, northampton-tannery-1996 (how many pits the largest excavated tannery of a medieval town ran); the L7R horse stream is setting-canon

  • Why towns tan too, and this is not a city-only trade. The GM's opening guess was that most towns would have no tannery. The research says otherwise, because of where hides come from: NOT butchery but fallen draft stock. Under the Tokugawa status order the kawata held carcass rights over a defined service territory (danna-ba29) - when an ox or horse died it became that community's property automatically, with no compensation to the owner.30 So a county town's ~12 burakumin households (60 inhabitants at the town tier) work the whole county's dead animals, not just the town's. L7R canon supplies the same stream from the other end: the setting's canon has daimyo giving surplus horses to village headmen to distribute to farms as draft animals, and those horses "often slaughtered and eaten when the need for meat outweighs the capacity of the peasants to feed those horses."
  • Throughput is small, and scales with the territory served - which is what the pit count encodes. A county of ~6,800 inhabitants sheds on the order of a couple dozen carcasses a year, so the town yard is a seasonal works of ~4 pits, never an industry. A provincial city adds the daimyo's stable (several hundred horses per domain) and the armor/saddle/drum/bellows demand of its ~300 samurai, so it runs ~12. That ratio is why the city yard draws roughly 1.7x the town yard's area rather than 5x: pits, not prestige, set the size. For comparison, the largest excavated urban tannery at Northampton ran 36-37 pits31 - far past anything in this pool.

  • WATER is the real gate, not settlement size. Tanning is a water process: the Japanese shironameshi method stakes raw hides in the river for 「several days」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「数日間」)79 before the hair is knifed off, and every archaeologically-attested tannery sits on a watercourse at the settlement's edge. The caste's own name for itself was kawaramono, "riverbed people." Hence the rule: a town or city with a burakumin quarter and a watercourse keeps a yard; one with no running water keeps none, whatever its size.

  • WHY tannery effluent must not reach a paddy intake - and which part of it actually does the harm (GM question 2026-07-25). Tanning in general is famous for urine and dung: hides were limed OR soaked in stale urine to loosen the hair, then "bated" by kneading them in dog or pigeon dung. But the method these maps commit to is Japanese shironameshi, which uses none of it - no lime, no tannin, no dung, no urine.32 The hides soak in the river for several days34 (river bacteria do the unhairing), the hair is knifed off, and the hide is finished with salt and rapeseed oil alone. So the discharge is three things: several days of controlled putrefaction, SALT, and an oil film. The salt is what a rice paddy cannot take. Rice is the most salt-sensitive cereal, and it is more sensitive to the salinity of the FIELD WATER than of the soil - a yield threshold around 0.9 dS/m - so brine arriving down an irrigation ditch into a flooded paddy is the precise wrong input. The counterintuitive corner: the ORGANIC half of the effluent is arguably fertilizer (Rokugani farmers deliberately spread night soil), so putrefaction is the WEAK argument here. The rule stands on salt, the oil film, and the stench.

  • WHY A YARD MAY SIT ON A FIELD DRAIN AT ALL, AND WHY THAT IS THE CASTE'S NATURAL WATER (GM question 2026-08-08, of Hoshizora: "the tanning yard is next to a drainage ditch of a farm field - is that realistic as a source of water for a tannery?"). Hoshizora has no stream near its burakumin quarter (the valley watercourse runs across the far west), so its yard takes the northeast comb's drain. Three separate findings say that is right, and one says what it costs.

  • Water RIGHTS, not water quality, is the strongest argument - and it was the one nobody had written down. In both Japanese and Chinese irrigation communities an upstream tap was fiercely-held communal property, allocated by village agreement and litigated over for generations. A kawata quarter would never be granted a headwater offtake. The water an outcast community could actually hold is the water nobody upstream is fighting over: the tail of the system, below every paddy intake. So a yard on a field drain is not the map settling for second best when no stream is handy - it is the sociologically correct water for this caste, and it is the literal sense of kawaramono, "riverbed people": not people who happened to live by water, but people whose only claim was to the part of the watercourse nobody else wanted. Read that way, a ditch-fed yard is more attested than a yard on a good stream reach would be.
  • It is also the only siting that automatically satisfies the salt rule above. A drain is the one course on a map where every intake is upstream by construction, which is exactly what the two intake rules demand. The residual risk is real but off-map: paddy drainage is commonly RECAPTURED by lower fields in both traditions (a "drain" at one level is a "supply" at the next), so a ditch-fed yard is only honest where the drain reaches a genuine sink - off the frame, a marsh, or a drainage tameike - rather than another comb's head. Hoshizora's NE drain leaves the east edge of the map.
  • What it COSTS, which should not be elided: the method changes. A ditch-fed yard is not a cosmetic variant of a stream-fed one. Shironameshi stakes hides in a CURRENT for several days80 and lets river bacteria do the unhairing; a drain has no current, so a ditch-fed yard ponds its own soak behind a gated intake cut and is running pit-soaking, the method every other tanning tradition used, rather than the Japanese one these maps otherwise commit to. That is a downgrade, not an equivalence - it is why the glyph draws a sluice gate instead of staking frames, and it is the honest thing to say if a PC asks a Hoshizora tanner why his leather is not Ubame's.

  • And it makes the yard SEASONAL in a way a stream-fed yard is not. A paddy drain runs during the irrigation season and is near-dry from harvest to transplanting, with its heaviest flush at the pre-harvest drawdown. Carcasses arrive on the opposite schedule: draft stock dies most in late winter and early spring, when the fodder is gone and the animal is worn from the autumn hauling. The two do not have to be reconciled, because salted and dried raw hides keep for months and that storage is how the entire pre-modern hide trade worked - but it means a ditch-fed yard is a summer works whose pits stand empty half the year, while its winter is spent salting hides down and doing the clean in-town leather craft. Good adventure-hook texture, and it is why the small pit count is not the only thing keeping this from reading as an industry.

  • THE YARD FOLLOWS ITS BANK'S BEARING - and a ditch counts as a bank (GM 2026-08-08: "if one is next to a stream and the stream is at a 30 degree angle then the tannery itself is at that same angle. That should apply to ditches as well"). The rule: the pit rank and the staking frames share ONE edge of water, so a yard left square to the map on a slanted bank puts one corner in the water and strands the far end of the rank inland. A yard at a confluence may legitimately front either course, but when a yard fronts more than one course it is aligned to the reach it WORKS, never to a short incidental tail beside it.

  • WHERE THE QUARTER SITS RELATIVE TO ITS OWN WORKS - kegare has a DIRECTION, not a radius (GM question 2026-07-27: "does a tanning yard need burakumin housing close by? Or would the tanners just sleep at the yard?").

  • Distance between quarter and yard is not the measure. Across the pool it runs from 90 ft (Hirameki) to 2,587 ft (Tango), and the far ones are not defects. The GM's ruling: a walled city keeps its burakumin INSIDE, because the caste's in-town duties require it, while the noxious phase of the trade goes out to the water - so distance between the two is the NORMAL arrangement, not a failure of it. Measuring the same maps by BEARING from the settlement core separates them cleanly where distance cannot: Hirameki 0.6 deg, Minami 17.2, Hoshizora 28.5, Nagahara 56.0, Ubame 100, Tango 127. The rule is a direction test.

  • Why an in-city quarter is right. Japanese castle towns mostly had no circuit town wall at all, so "inside or outside the walls" is barely a Japanese question - the kawata quarter sat at the town's EDGE, typically past a river or on the road out. Edo is the clearest case: Asakusa Shinmachi, the Danzaemon compound, sat well within the city as a walled precinct of ~400 holding private residences, warehouses, workshops, a shrine, a courtroom and a prison, and its residents worked all over Edo. China-first agrees with fewer complications: no outcast caste at all, tanners and butchers are ordinary artisans, and the noxious WATER trades go to the extramural suburbs (guanxiang) below the water gate. Either way, quarter in-town and works downstream is the attested arrangement rather than a compromise.
  • The load-bearing detail: one caste, TWO work sites, and only one of them stinks. The soak/unhair/dry phase is riverbank work. Everything else the caste does - night soil for samurai and wealthy merchants, corpse removal, execution and prison service, and the actual leather CRAFT (sandals, drum heads, armor lacing, bellows) - is clean, quiet, and done at home or in town. So the quarter follows the in-town duties and the yard follows the water, and a daily walk between them is not a defect. It is also why no satellite hut stands at the pits: the historical unit is a corporate community under a headman, not scattered outbuildings, and a lone outcast house away from its quarter is not a thing that existed.
  • No, the tanners did not sleep at the works, and no watchman is needed. Nothing attests tanners bedding at the pits, and the setting does not need it: under shironameshi the exposed phase is several days81 of hides staked IN the river, which no watchman covers anyway. The security is not a person but kegare plus monopoly - a heimen who lifts a hide has handled death-pollution and has nowhere to sell it, because the burakumin hold the carcass right (danna-ba) and are the only legal channel for leather.33 Worth keeping straight for adventure hooks: stealing from a tanning yard is easy and nearly pointless, which is a more interesting fact than a guard post would have been.
  • And the smell argument runs the OTHER way. The 120 ft standoff exists to keep the stench off OTHER castes' houses, which is exactly why burakumin dwellings are already exempt from it. Distance-from-nuisance is a privilege of the clean castes; the quarter is inside the polluted zone by definition. Walking half a mile to escape your own trade's smell is a comfort a kawata household did not have. The exemption is deliberate and was paid for: counting their houses as something to stay away from inverts the institution, and it did - the first pass counted them, and returned zero viable sites for Hoshizora's yard until the exemption was added.
  • Why direction is the thing taboo actually systematizes. Kegare leaves a settlement ONE way. Edo stacked Asakusa, the Kozukappara execution ground and the Yoshiwara at the northeast kimon; Kyoto put its outcast communities on the riverbeds and the southern roads out. So the quarter, the execution ground and the tanning yard share a bearing, and the daily carcass haul never crosses the rest of the settlement - real castle towns kept designated carcass routes off the main streets for precisely that reason. The CREMATION ground is the deliberate exclusion: it is monk-run and follows the temple/funerary complex. Tested across the pool, that association is real where it matters (Hoshizora 130 ft from its monastery, Ubame 360 ft) and absent elsewhere (Tango 2,606 ft), so cremation is excluded from the bearing rule WITHOUT a positive "follows the monastery" rule being asserted in its place - the evidence supports the exclusion, not the converse claim.
  • The 90 deg threshold, and why it is not a fresh number. It is the "same half of the compass" test the execution ground already uses, applied to the other burakumin-run works rather than a fresh tolerance. The pool's loosest accepted map (Nagahara, 83.7 deg) sits inside it and the two defects sit outside.

A long walk to work is not a defect (GM ruling 2026-07-24). Tango's quarter is in-wall at the northwest and its yard is outside the southeast rim; Hoshizora's crematory is across town from its quarter. These are not to be "fixed" by moving quarters closer to their work: being made to walk is one of the indignities the caste is subject to even in a Rokugan kinder to its underclasses than the societies it draws on. There is deliberately no rule pairing a yard with its quarter by distance.

The two maps that waive the direction rule, and why each is allowed to. The first draft of the rule measured feet, and was wrong: Nagahara's yard stands about 1,390 ft from its quarter and is correct, and a metric rule would have condemned it. What the direction rule forbids is the yard facing the opposite way out of town, which sends the tanners' daily carcass haul back through the whole settlement - the traffic real castle towns routed around with designated carcass ways. Two maps stand outside it for reasons the map can state. TANGO because the Emperor lies southeast, and that status claim takes the quarter to the opposite side from its own works. UBAME because its land falls southwest while its only tannable water is the valley stream in the east, so the fall of the ground and the water genuinely disagree in that hill valley. A waived map no longer holds the rule honest, which is why both are recorded as waivers rather than quietly accommodated by loosening it. The cremation ground is excluded from the rule on purpose: it is monk-run and follows the funerary complex, which need not be the outcast side at all - Hoshizora's stands 130 ft from its monastery and almost exactly opposite the quarter, and that is a correct map. And the rule abstains on a settlement with no dwellings outside the quarter, where the core lands on the quarter itself and no bearing exists.

The rule the map follows: a town or city with a burakumin quarter AND a watercourse draws a tanning yard; one with no running water draws none, whatever its size. The yard stands (a) ON the water, its ground abutting a bank within about 20 ft, because a yard set back on dry ground could not work; (b) on DRY ground even so - the footprint stops at the bank and only the staking frames reach into the shallows, so a yard whose ground reads as a platform over the water is always a defect and never a design, and the test is the yard's true rotated rectangle against each course's real half-width, since a fixed brook-width tolerance cannot see a corner swallowed by a 120 ft river or a ditch threading under the rectangle between its corners; (c) strictly OUTSIDE the walls, with the kiln - the workers may live in-wall, the work may not; (d) DOWNSTREAM of everything the settlement draws from, which is two things at once: the yard's own course must end off the map or in a drain that does, never in a pond, a field, an irrigation ditch or the moat, and no sluice, weir or channel head on that course may lie below the yard, compared by how far each stands along the course itself, since what settles it is which of the two lies further upstream; (e) NEVER overlapping a field outline, dry plot or flower field, since a yard drawn on a field asserts ground that is at once cropped and ruined for cropping, though abutting one is fine; (f) SQUARE TO ITS BANK within 15 degrees, the yard's long axis running with the watercourse rather than with the map, measured against any course whose bank lies inside that same 20 ft reach and aligned to the reach it WORKS rather than to a short incidental tail beside it; and (g) on the QUARTER'S SIDE of the settlement, within 90 degrees of the quarter's bearing from the core - the same half-of-the-compass test the execution ground takes. It keeps 120 ft from every dwelling, wall to wall, except the burakumin dwellings that are exempt by design. The 15 degree tolerance is a calibration: a correct yard sits a few degrees off whichever segment it is measured against - the three drawn yards at 2.1, 3.8 and 7.2 degrees - while the failure it separates is an axis-aligned yard on a diagonal bank, which measured 22.9. The 20 ft reach and the 90 degrees are this project's own figures, the second borrowed rather than freshly invented.

The bell-and-drum tower - one per walled seat

Sources: pingyao-shilou-zhwiki (133.4 m2, three bays square), dongjing-menghualu-rujia (军巡铺 every 300 paces, 望火楼 with a hundred soldiers). Not found in any page read: a citywide count of Kaifeng's towers ("20+"), Kawagoe's base dimension (only its ~16 m height), the Dingbian / Xingcheng figures

What the research found: the "morning bell, evening drum" institution (dawn gate-opening, the dusk gate-closing that began the street curfew, the five night watches, alarm and ceremony) was part of the standard Ming county-seat kit alongside the yamen, the Confucian temple, and the City God temple - common approaching standard in walled northern seats. A county seat had ONE tower - a drum tower, a combined bell-and-drum tower, or a market tower straddling the main street; the paired gulou + zhonglou on an axis is CAPITAL grammar (Beijing, Xi'an), and Pingyao, a wealthy county seat, has exactly one (the ~60 ft Market Tower on South Street). County-tier form: Pingyao's Market Tower - the wealthy-county showpiece - is ATTESTED at 133.4 m^2 plan, ~38 ft square35; these towers dominated by HEIGHT (50-60 ft),36 not plan, and Kawagoe's famous time-bell tower served a town of tens of thousands from a <20 ft base. The 50-66 ft square examples (Dingbian 52 ft, Xingcheng 66 ft) are the tower-over-street-arch type - garrison/prefecture fabric, never a 3,000-person seat. The drawn band is therefore 30-45 ft: ~36 ft at city tier, ~30 ft at town tier - one rowhouse width, which is exactly how they read in plan. Siting: the central crossroads near (not inside) the yamen; poorer seats made do with a qiaolou - a drum tower over the yamen's own gate (a recognized cheaper variant, not drawn here). Critically, FIRE WATCH WAS A SEPARATE INSTITUTION in both reference cultures - Song Kaifeng ran 20+ dedicated wanghuolou fire-lookout towers with a standing brigade,37 and Edo split the licensed toki no kane time bell from the hinomi-yagura fire towers - so the fire towers do not cover the timekeeping/curfew niche. The decision it drove: every walled seat (provincial city or walled town) draws exactly ONE combined bell-and-drum tower at its main street crossing (a corner of the crossing, not straddling the street), ~36 ft at city tier and ~30 ft at town tier, visibly heavier-built than the skeletal fire towers; the capital tier may take the full paired gulou/zhonglou when it exists (and the street-arch platform type, 50-66 ft, is available as a garrison-city variant). An UNWALLED town is exempt: it has no gates to close, and its time signal is the monastery's bell - the Edo pattern, where most towns contracted a temple bell rather than building a tower - implied within the precinct, no glyph (Hoshizora). The tower follows the WALL, not the population. The caption reads "drum/bell" over "tower" (GM 2026-07-24): the drawn building is genuinely the combined zhonggulou - both instruments in one pavilion, and both are drawn - so the label says so. Audibility - one central tower covers the whole seat with enormous margin (GM sanity check 2026-07-24, researched). The geometry: Tango's farthest in-wall resident stands ~1,640 ft (500 m) from its tower and the farthest drawn gate-suburb structure ~790 m; Nagahara ~435 m / ~920 m. The attested anchors dwarf those distances: Edo's Kokucho time bell BILLED ~400-410 blocks of households "within earshot" (kane-yaku-sen, 4 mon per month per house - an effective fee radius of ~1.2-1.4 km in a far denser, noisier city, and a conservative figure, since only admitted hearers were billed); Basho casually heard the Ueno/Asakusa bells ~3-4 km away across the river; Beijing's single drum tower timed the watches for an inner city ~6.5 x 5.3 km. Acoustically, a large bell or great drum runs ~110-130 dB near the tower; at a 500-730 m city edge that arrives ~40 dB above a premodern NIGHT noise floor (~25-35 dBA, when the watches matter most) - loud, not merely detectable, everywhere inside the walls and suburbs, and the low frequencies diffract over one-story rooflines. One tower stops sufficing only past a ~2-3 km radius of daytime urban din, so even the future ~12,000-pop capital tier (~1 mi across) needs no second tower on audibility grounds: the historical scaling answers are Edo's multiplied fee-district bells vs the China-first pattern of ONE monumental tower whose watches ward night-watchmen relay through the streets with clappers and gongs - the capital tier should take the latter (grander tower, implied watchmen, still exactly one).

Stable yards - beaten earth, hitching rails, and watering by relay

Sources: qingming-shanghe-tu

Wells - the research, and the deliberate liberty

Sources: sphere-2004-water; the size for the reader: saijo-mizu-rekishikan, kotobank-idoyakata

What the research found: a pre-modern south-China rice village of ~70 households ran 1-3 communal drinking wells total, 2 typical - drinking water came mostly from SURFACE water (canal/pond/stream, stored in vats, settled, boiled), which is why schistosomiasis was endemic in the Yangtze rice belt for two millennia and why >70% of rural China still drank untreated surface water in the early 1980s; wells were expensive durable capital financed by per-family subscription and dug only as surface quality forced; the classical jingtian "8 families per well" is a Warring-States ideal nobody practiced. Japan corroborates from the other side: valley villages sited on springs/streams needed few or no communal wells, the famous dense pattern (~10-18 households per well, the idobata gossip circle) is URBAN nagaya tenement density, and per-household wells appear only on shallow-water-table plains. Capacity is never the constraint (one open well serves ~400 people by Sphere/UNICEF anchors,39 and a nucleated village is ~250m across so carrying distance never binds) - digging cost and morning queueing are.

The size, for the reader - the true well against the head we draw. The wellhead is a map drawing convention (GM ruling 2026-07-21: a location marker, not a to-scale footprint): at one foot per pixel the curb is drawn at a radius of 9.36 ft - about 19 ft across - under a well-house roof, so the well is visible at map scale and keeps about half a dwelling's width at every grain. What the record gives for the real thing: a hand-dug well's SHAFT is40 「A vertical shaft about 1 m in diameter, large enough for a person to get into, is dug, and the digging is carried on by human labor until the hole reaches the vein of groundwater (the aquifer).」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「人が入れる直径1ｍほどの縦穴を掘り、人力で地下水の水脈（帯水層）に到達するまで穴を掘りつづけていきます。」) - a vertical hole about 1 m across, big enough for a person to work in (Saijo City water-history museum); the roof over it is a 井戸屋形 (idoyakata, the well house),41 「A simple structure consisting of nothing more than posts set up beside the well with a roof provided on top of them.」 (Digital Daijisen) (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「井戸のそばに柱を立てて、その上に屋根を 設けただけの簡単な建物」) - posts beside the well with a roof on them and nothing more (Digital Daijisen via kotobank). Not found: a measured width for the curb frame (井桁 / 井戸枠, igeta / idowaku) itself - three pages define it and none sizes it42 - so the note says the shaft's meter and calls the curb a frame of that order; the map's "~3-4 ft" curb stays an unsourced estimate. Nor was the PREVALENCE of the well house read: the dictionaries define it, they do not say how common it was.

The rule the map follows: every rural settlement keeps communal wells - a town about one per 20 to 25 households, a village one per 8 to 26, a hamlet as richly as one per 2 to 20, which is the liberty above and not a finding; so a hamlet draws one or two, a village of 60 households six to nine, a town about sixteen. Ablution wells beside a shrine are not counted among them. A well stands AMONG the houses it serves and never out in the fields: at the hamlet and town grain of one foot per pixel a well must have a dwelling within about 95 ft, and the placement is seeded a little tighter than that. A household counts as watered by a well OR by the irrigation network it sits beside - a channel, pond, stream or moat within about 380 ft at that same grain - because a dispersed farm household drew from the water it already had; the count makes wells present, the reach makes sure no household is left dry. A shrine or temple set apart from the houses, its nearest dwelling more than about 150 ft off, keeps its OWN ablution well beside the hall, and specifically a well rather than a ditch or a pond, which are not ablution sources; the distance is measured to the hall's edge, so a large hall pushes its well outward, and a hilltop shrine is exempt because it draws from a spring and a well cannot be dug on a hill. The per-tier bands are the disclosed liberty; the reaches are calibrations against the drawn maps rather than figures from a record.

Commercial frontage and wealth-stratified housing

Sources: kyomachiya-jawiki, kyototuu-unagi, nagaya-jawiki

In premodern Japanese castle-towns (jokamachi) and Chinese cities the valuable street frontage is COMMERCE: shops and workshops line the high street, each facing the road, while dwellings sit behind them or in the block interior reached by alleys.4376 A merchant family lived over/behind its own shop44 (so merchant residences sit directly behind the storefronts, sharing their orientation). Housing was stratified by wealth - a few rich households in large homes or walled courtyard estates, most in modest/uniform dwellings,45 never one size - and the merchant quarter SPREADS (courtyard compounds) while the laborer warren packs dense and uniform.

How do you get through a packed commoner quarter - and why is the path not a street?

Sources: this project's own measurement of the drawn town and city maps; the character of a quarter's paths rests on general reading, and no source is cited for it

A town's warren with no lane, alley or street in it at all is a defect, walled or not - without one it hangs straight off the trunk road with no way into it, and reads as a scatter of identical boxes rather than as fabric. The CITIES carry ways through their quarters - Tango 6 alleys and 8 streets, Minami 9 and 7, Nagahara 3 and 10 - and walled Hirameki 2 streets, against zero lanes, zero alleys and zero streets on each of the two UNWALLED towns, Hoshizora and Ubame.

What such a quarter actually had was not a street. A dense commoner quarter is served by narrow TRODDEN FOOTPATHS between the house rows - packed earth, single-track, unpaved, paving being far beyond a quarter's means (this rests on general reading; no source is cited)84 - which is the same reasoning that gives a hamlet and a village their worn lanes. They are paths, not streets, and they are never drawn or labeled as streets.

And a footpath costs capacity. It takes a little housing out of the district, so the district is widened a little to make that back.

The rule the map follows: a packed commoner quarter of any size carries trodden footpaths between its house rows, one every few rows of the district's depth, drawn as worn single-track earth and captioned as paths if at all - never as streets, which a quarter could not afford to pave. A town's warren with no lane, alley or street in it at all is a defect, walled or not. How many rows apart is a map drawing convention chosen so the quarter reads as fabric at map scale, not a figure from a record.

Communal wells and the samurai exception

Commoner machi shared public wells in the block interiors (courtyard wells),46 roughly 1 per ~10-20 households - enough that no quarter lacks water, not one on every corner. The samurai/government quarter shows NO public wells: noble and official households drew from private wells inside their own walled compounds (a status + security distinction), so a communal well there would read wrong.

The rule the map follows: a city's commoner warren is sprinkled with public wells, one within a short walk of any home: no commoner dwelling inside the walls stands more than about 870 ft from a wellhead, and no single well is the nearest for more than 26 households. Servants are interleaved with the commoners and share their wells; a transient gate market outside the wall is not housing and is not counted; the samurai and government quarter draws no public wells at all, since those households drew from private wells inside their own walled compounds and gathering at the communal draw-point was a commoner institution. A wellhead sits in a block INTERIOR, clear of the lanes and off every building and compound, and the houses are packed around it afterward so it ends up enclosed. It is drawn as a location marker rather than at true size, and the marker scales with the map grain: mean well diameter against the median dwelling's larger dimension lands between 0.35 and 0.85 at every tier - a well is naturally smaller than a house, but not a dot. The 26 is a calibration and not a finding: coverage alone can look fine on average while the busiest wells in a dense warren are swamped, and assigning each household to its nearest well over-counts where two wells stand nearly equidistant, so the ceiling sits a little above the 10 to 20 households the record suggests. Charcoal yards: a tallied depot, a cooling ground, and a weighing floor

Sources: wagner-ming-iron, fao-charcoal-safety, tonya-enwiki, fires-in-edo-enwiki (the inline list below); economy-song-enwiki (Hartwell's 125,000 t by 1078; state charcoal supplies to iron households)

China first. Charcoal was an industrial input at state scale. Song iron-smelting households were government-regulated, with state support that explicitly included charcoal supplies50, and a large late-Ming iron enterprise - by Wagner's reading a firm running several works, which its source describes as one - is recorded with 200 charcoal producers alongside 200 furnace-tenders and 300 miners51 - the fuel workforce as large as the furnace workforce. Northern Song output reached roughly 125,000 tons of iron a year by 1078, about six times the ~800 CE level,52 and it ran on charcoal until it had deforested large parts of north China, after which coal largely displaced charcoal by the end of the 11th century.53 The decision this drove: a charcoal store is a supervised, tallied commodity depot, not a shop's back room - which is exactly the relationship the county magistracy's own plan already draws ("charcoal and bar iron, sealed here, never owned").

Japan corroborating. The ton'ya / toiya was the wholesaler-warehouseman of the Edo economy,54 and fire-resistant stores were built with thick plastered earthen walls82 precisely because urban timber burned. Edo's serious fires cluster in the dry, windy months from January into April, March worst of all - the charcoal-brazier season.55

Why the yard has an open cooling apron. Freshly produced charcoal absorbs oxygen from the air fast enough to heat itself to ignition; tightly packed masses of fines are the worst case56, and high-volatile charcoal can self-ignite below 150 °C (unsourced). The documented handling rule is to stand freshly discharged charcoal in the open, separate from previously cooled and conditioned stock, for at least 24 hours57; charcoal exposed to air for eight days is no longer considered hazardous (unsourced: not on the FAO chapter-5 page fetched). Water-spraying does not reliably prevent it.58 So the yard draws an open cooling apron set apart from the covered sheds, not merely sheds plus a weighing floor. That is the difference between a generic warehouse glyph and a charcoal yard.

The finding that justified a feature that would otherwise look like decoration. The charcoal hyō / tawara had no standard weight in the traditional Japanese system, unlike rice (a kan is 3.75 kg, but the charcoal bale itself was indeterminate).59 A commodity with no standard bale cannot be traded by count - it must be weighed at the point of sale. That is the weighing floor, and it is independently why the magistracy's hold on the trade is documentary rather than proprietary.

What determines it in reality: the commodity's physics and its measure - dryness (value), self-heating (separation and a cooling ground), and the absent standard bale (a weighing floor and a sealed tally). Not town size, and not wealth.

The 30 ft fire gap, derived. Reasoned against this project's existing separations rather than invented: ~6 ft for an attended forge against hay, 120 ft for putrefaction carried on air. The charcoal hazard is an unattended ignition in a large fuel mass, so it belongs between them, and 30 ft is about one flame-height clear of a fully-involved 10-12 ft stack - the usual rule of thumb for radiant ignition of adjacent timber. Rejected: reusing the 120 ft nuisance figure, which defends against smell over distance and would push the yard off the cart route it exists to serve.

Refining forges: smelting goes to the fuel, refining comes to the town

Sources: wagner-ming-iron, wagner-fining-puddling, tetsu-to-hagane-91 (the inline list below)

China first. Ming ironworks converted blast-furnace pig iron to wrought iron by fining, Chinese chao 炒, literally60 "stir-frying": an open fire under a forced blast, fuelled with charcoal, into which wood, charcoal and broken cast iron were charged and then stirred with an iron rod once semi-molten. Song Yingxing describes a rectangular hearth (tang 塘, "pool, basin") set a few chi from the blast-furnace outlet and a few cun lower, up against a short wall, with the men standing in a row on that wall, stirring with willow poles (which burned down 2-3 cun per cycle) while one of them spreads a sieved powder, wuchaoni - "filthy wet loam", probably nitre-bed material rather than a mineral additive.61 Those are two different things, and the authority on Chinese ferrous metallurgy says so: his chao hearth is an insulated pit under a blast, while Song Yingxing's basin is open, unfueled and unblown, which is why he calls it a very different process and a very difficult one to explain technically.62 The practice does run back to the Han, though the authority himself hedges it - the hearths found at Han ironworks sites are believed to be fining hearths, and the process seems to have been used that early.88 The one thing that cannot be supported is the site: the excavated smelting-and-fining works at Xuxiebian in Sichuan is named in neither of the two works of his that were read in full - one of them searched character by character in its Chinese translation - and neither names any excavated Sichuan ironworks at all.89

Japan corroborating. The tatara's chief product was pig iron (zuku), and the 17th century answered it with a two-stage refining process, the ōkaji 大鍛冶.63 Kera went first to the dōba to be crushed and sorted; the low-carbon fractions then went to the ōkajiba, which turned out flat bars called wari-tetsu 割鉄. At their 18th-19th century peak the ironworks of the Chugoku Mountains produced 80% of Japan's iron this way (unsourced: the Ohitayama page fetched gives only that the works used iron sand and charcoal from the Chugoku Mountains as raw materials). Two operations, two sites.

THE DISCLOSED DIVERGENCE. The Chinese arrangement puts the fining hearth a few feet from the blast-furnace outlet so the iron runs in still molten - one site. Rokugan's charcoal counties cannot do that: a kiln reduces roughly six parts wood to one of charcoal, so the kiln goes to the wood and the furnace follows the fuel into the hills, miles from the seat. So the design follows the Japanese two-site pattern - cold pig comes down and is re-melted at the town forge.

This is a resolution rather than a fudge, and the reason is economic. The Chinese single-site arrangement is viable exactly where ore, fuel and a workforce of hundreds can be concentrated at one state-supervised complex, which is what the 200/300/200 staffing describes. A county-scale trade strung along dispersed oak stands cannot concentrate the one input that matters most. Dispersed fuel forces two sites. Per the China-first rule, GM setting canon overrides the historical default where they differ - and here the canon and the Japanese practice agree.

What determines it in reality: economy and topography. Whether smelting and refining share a site is decided by whether fuel, ore and labor can be concentrated - one site where they can, two where the fuel is dispersed through hill forest, with the smelter at the fuel and the refinery in the valley settlement where the carts, the market and the tally are.

Rejected: drawing kilns or a tatara furnace on a town map at all. Canon and arithmetic both forbid it, and the Chinese 200-charcoal-producers figure shows the fuel workforce lives out at the stands, not in the seat.

A glyph that reads badly is not fixed by inflating it: the to-scale rule stands whatever the drawing costs in legibility.

Drawing a clan border

Sources: nanbu-date-mounds-enwiki, kuniezu-enwiki (the inline list below)

Linear, demarcated borders were not foreign to early modern Japan: domains were already building a territorial order with agreed boundaries and mutual exclusion, evidenced by boundary disputes, boundary markers and map-making.64 The worked example is the Nanbu-Date border mounds - a series of earth mounds marking the ~130 km boundary between Morioka (Nanbu) and Sendai (Date), agreed between the two domains in 1642 after more than fifty years of dispute, and growing smaller and more closely spaced over time as the zonal frontier became a line; such markers were common under the Tokugawa.65 Separately, the shogunate ordered every province to produce kuniezu 国絵図, provincial land maps66 with the boundaries drawn on them - the shogunate's own guideline for the Genroku survey ordered the parties to confirm the border against each other's maps, describe it in text, and depict it unambiguously on the final map - so putting the line on a map is itself the authentic act.

The Ubame magistracy's boundary pillars are drawn at ~1-1.5 ft of shaft (up to ~3 ft with a plinth) - an estimate, not a read figure; a GUESS.

The decision. Draw the LINE, not the mound. The period's physically large border markers were earthen mounds, which is a different thing and not what is drawn; the Ubame magistracy uses ~3 ft boundary pillars as location markers. A mound is a structure, which everything would then have to stay clear of - the opposite of what a frontier magistracy wants, since its whole design stands its east wall ON the line with a parley room built into that wall. So the border is drawn as a line with no footprint, which nothing need stand clear of.

What determines it in reality: jurisdiction, and the need to have agreed it in advance. A border exists where two authorities have negotiated and recorded it - which is why the magistracy's parley room, with the line across its floor and kneeling mats on each side, is the architectural expression of the same fact this drawn line is the cartographic expression of.

Wells in crop fields: two different objects, and only one of them is ours

The question (GM, 2026-07-26): our maps never draw a well inside a dry crop plot. "Should dry plots have wells? I could see a wheat field having a well in the middle of it if that's where the water is - but I'm not a farmer."

CHINA FIRST - a well in a dry field is real, and it is a whole system. The north-China plain is dryland farming on loess with 400-800 mm of annual rain87 concentrated in the summer monsoon,67 so crops are chronically drought-exposed. Groundwater lifting is the answer, and the gear is well documented: the jiegao (桔槔, the Chinese shadoof - an upright post, a hinged beam and a counterweight; where the operator stood is on no page read) and the lulu (辘轳, a windlass on a wooden stand over the well - wheel, axle, hand crank and ropes).68 During the Ming and Qing the lulu became the usual groundwater-lifting device of the north, and it was upgraded rather than replaced: horsepower instead of manpower, multiple containers at once, and deeper wells (how deep is not said, so tens of meters is a guess).69 So yes - a wheat field with a well in the middle of it is an entirely real picture, and the north-China field well is a substantial installation with lifting machinery over it.

JAPAN - dry fields leaned on rain and groundwater precisely BECAUSE they lost the canal fight. Hatake was the upland/dry allocation as against suiden paddy,70 and small independent farmers "had to do with rainfall or natural underground sources," settling for millet, hemp, barley, wheat and buckwheat (unsourced: this quoted phrase and crop list are not on the IDE page fetched). That was not a preference: in the later Tokugawa period new irrigation schemes for hata fields were proposed repeatedly and defeated, because downstream canal-holders' established water rights blocked any new upstream draw.71 The canals went to rice; the dry fields got the sky and the water table.

But the DOMESTIC well is a different object. The Japanese village well is a social institution before it is a water source - kabata spring-wells stood in houses and in the community and served drinking, cooking and washing, and were72 "cherished as places where water could be obtained from nature and where people gathered for a chat." Villages sited themselves along rivers where drawing water was easy (unsourced). And well COUNT was governed: the kabu-ido system capped the number of wells inside a ring-levee - a permit bought with a fee, a survey of the wells standing, a cap agreed between the upper and the lower villages and watched by patrolmen the villages provided for each other, with the local government expressly declining to interfere inside the levee. What it settled was not scarcity but its opposite: the upper villages' wells drained onto the lower villages' paddy.73

The decision

Our well glyph is the DOMESTIC draw-point, so it stays off the crop. The glyph we draw is a stone curb under an open-sided roof, and a well always stands within ~95 ft of a dwelling - it is the idobata, the neighborhood gathering point. A lone curb in the middle of a hatake plot is therefore wrong as drawn, and the rule that forbids it is right. The reason is not that field wells never existed; it is that our well means something else.

Two further reasons the north-China field well is the wrong analog here. First, an irrigation well is identified by its lifting gear - a windlass frame or a shadoof lever standing over it - and our glyph has none, so a reader could not tell the two apart. Second, our dry plots are the hem and margin around wet-rice combs in a monsoon valley, not the rain-starved loess dryland the system was built for; the water those plots need arrives as rain and as the field's own ditches.

An irrigation well would be a different thing. It would be a distinct glyph - the curb plus a windlass frame or shadoof - standing in the dry plot it serves rather than among the houses, in a dry upland county rather than a paddy valley.

One honest tension recorded, and it weighs less than it looks. Sixteen villages behind one ring levee on the Noubi Plain did cap the number of wells among them, by an agreement of 1812 that held until the 1930s, and they policed it themselves.74 Read closely, it bears on this project's deliberate liberty of drawing 2-3x the historical well count (see "Wells - the research, and the deliberate liberty") less than the bare statement suggests. The wells being capped are ARTESIAN irrigation wells, and the grievance is not that water was scarce but that their free flow waterlogged the paddy of the villages downhill - a draw-well in a house yard raises none of that. The case is also a local peculiarity rather than a norm, little known even in Japan, and it sits in the last half-century of the Edo period and the decades after it. The liberty stands, and it is still a liberty; what this does not show is a general historical pressure toward fewer wells.
