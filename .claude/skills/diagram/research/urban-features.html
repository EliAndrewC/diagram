The notice board (kosatsuba) - siting is a TRAFFIC decision

Grounds: town_has_kosatsuba, kosatsuba_by_the_road, city_has_kosatsuba, city_kosatsuba_per_gate, village_has_kosatsuba, hamlet_has_kosatsuba

Evidence: attested

Sources: fuchu-kosatsuba, ogose-kosatsuba, kosatsu-jawiki, adachi-kosatsu (READ 2026-08-26, feature 133 T13 - the inline links below)

Every Edo town AND village kept the official edict board1 - standing shogunal/domain law,2 porter and packhorse rate tables,3 currency regulations (unsourced at the check of 2026-09-06), ban edicts - and its siting was a TRAFFIC decision, not an administrative one: busy street corners, checkpoints, bridgeheads, the gate or center of the settlement,4 "in a prominent position alongside the main highway" (unsourced at the check of 2026-09-06). Edo's principal board stood at Nihonbashi (the bridgehead where the five highways began) (unsourced at the check of 2026-09-06: the page names Nihonbashi first among six great boards, without calling it "principal", and does not mention five highways),5 post towns' mid-shuku by the transport office - not at the government office (unsourced at the check of 2026-09-06).

the ofuregaki circulars reached the peasantry through exactly this board (unsourced at the check of 2026-09-06), posted where the settlement's one lane carries everyone - by the headman's frontage or the lane junction/entrance,6 since the headman (or in a hamlet the senior farmer answering to the village headman) received, copied, and relayed the circulars and was REQUIRED to be functionally literate for the job (unsourced at the check of 2026-09-06). One reader per settlement makes the board work - notices were also read aloud by officials (unsourced at the check of 2026-09-06) - so peasant literacy is no objection even at a 50-inhabitant hamlet; the board is also the state's standing presence in a place that may see a magistrate's deputy twice a year.

  • Roadside, not merely near the road - ACCURATE (researched 2026-08-26, feature 133 T13). The bakufu set kosatsuba "at points of heavy passage: barriers and ports, the foot of large bridges, and the entrances and centers of towns and villages"75; in farming villages "at the village center, the shrine precinct, or the place where villagers assembled" (unsourced at the check of 2026-09-06); also "at bridgeheads and before the gate of the village officials' houses" (Fuchu kosatsuba, Tokyo Jinjacho; Ogose town cultural property H26-04; 高札 - Wikipedia (ja); Adachi museum, 高札). Every attested site is ON the way - a verge, a gate front, a bridge foot - never a plot of open ground beside it. Decision: KOSATSUBA_VERGE_FT = 6 ft from tread edge to board edge at the hamlet and village tiers; the 60 ft band (2026-07-24) stays only as the fallback when no roadside seat fits, and for towns and cities until their maps re-roll. Deviation noted: the 60 ft figure was never a finding, it was the first placer's search radius wearing a rule's clothes.

The justice works - why a county seat executes, and why the ground is outside

Grounds: the punishment-ground and execution-ground vocabulary and their twelve checks (feature 015)

Evidence: attested, corroborated, setting-canon, liberty

Sources: caishikou-enwiki, qiushen-zhwiki (READ); suzugamori-jawiki, kozukappara-jawiki (READ: the two highway-entrance grounds). The 棄市 term and the third Edo ground (Ōwada) were not confirmed in the pages read; the county-jail reading of budgets.md is setting-canon

  • THE JUSTICE WORKS: WHY A COUNTY SEAT EXECUTES, AND WHY THE GROUND IS OUTSIDE (drives the punishment-ground and execution-ground vocabulary above and their twelve checks; feature 015, 2026-07-25).
  • The China/Japan split, and how it resolves. This is one of the few places the two grounding traditions genuinely disagree, so the reconciliation is load-bearing rather than decorative. Japan pushed executions OUT of the settlement for kegare (death pollution) - Edo's three grounds (corrected 2026-09-06, feature 194: the pages read confirm two, Suzugamori and Kozukappara; a third, Ōwada, is unconfirmed)77 all sat at highway entrances outside the center,7 castle towns monopolized them, and village authority topped out at banishment. China executed in the marketplace (棄市 qishi, "abandon in the market"; Qing Beijing's Caishikou was the vegetable market at a crossroads outside Xuanwumen),8 and although a county magistrate could not CONFIRM a death sentence - capital cases climbed to the Board of Punishments and the emperor's autumn check-marking (秋審 qiushen9) - the confirmed sentence came back DOWN and was carried out at the county seat where the crime happened (unsourced at the check of 2026-09-06: the page gives the four outcomes - confirmed, deferred, mitigable, kept to support parents - and nothing on where a confirmed sentence was carried out), because local deterrence was the point. So: China supplies the jurisdiction (the seat executes), Japan supplies the siting (past the built edge). That is exactly what this file's town doctrine already asks for - "what would a Chinese county seat administered by samurai do?"
  • It explains a canon line we had never used. budgets.md funds "prisoner food, jail upkeep, court materials" at county level - a jail with no execution line - while "ceremonial executions" appear only in the domain and Imperial budgets. Read through the Chinese chain that is precisely right: the county jail is a holding pen for the condemned while the warrant travels, not a punishment in itself. (SKILL.md already records the same shape for Mode A: Edo detention was a holding function, not imprisonment-as-punishment.)
  • The governing variables. For the execution ground: the road and the direction pollution runs - NOT population and not the settlement's geometric edge. The ground exists to be seen by travelers arriving, so it hugs the busiest way out; kegare puts it downwind, downstream, and on the outcast side, which is why execution_ground_on_the_outcast_side measures against the burakumin quarter (per l7r.md, burakumin perform every execution that is not a samurai's). For the punishment ground: foot traffic. A generator usually gets the existence of a thing right and its governing variable wrong; these two are the ones to protect.
  • Where the sizes come from. The hard anchor is Suzugamori: 74 x 16.2 m (~243 x 53 ft, about a third of an acre),10 serving Edo - a city of one million - for 220 years.11 Execution grounds are SMALL; the deterrent is the sight of the posts from the highway, not acreage. The tier ladder scales down from that by execution VOLUME, which is also what sets each tier's character: at ~1-3 executions per 100,000 per year, a county of ~7,000-8,000 inhabitants reaches the formal channel about once every 5-10 years (bandit sweeps add batches), a province sees on the order of one a year, a domain capital a handful. Hence the county ground is drawn weedy with EMPTY post sockets and no hoarding: a busy scaffold at county scale would assert something false about how often the Empire kills.
  • The beating is not here. The design originally put flogging at the street installation; the Chinese evidence moved it. The bamboo (笞 / 杖) was a COURT act administered inside the yamen courtyard before the magistrate's bench - which in our maps is the Mode A magistracy, unchanged by this feature - while the 枷 jia (cangue) was the public part, worn one to six months with the offender's name, crime, and sentence inscribed on the boards for passers-by to read. So the street feature DISPLAYS; it does not try. It also draws no notice board, because the crime text rides on the cangue and the settlement already has a kosatsuba.
  • A deliberate departure from literal reality, disclosed. EXECUTION_GROUND_DEAD_CLEAR_FT = 400 is a map-legibility floor, not a historical measurement. In reality the executed went into a pit AT the ground (Kozukappara's burials were haphazard enough that a memorial hall was founded beside it in 1667 for that reason)12 while the community's dead went to temple graveyards elsewhere in the city entirely - usually a different road out of town, often a mile off. Our maps are a few thousand feet across and cannot hold that, so the separation is compressed and the RELATIVE ordering kept honest instead: the execution ground is always the further-out, more polluted of the two. The number was first set at 150 ft by analogy to the project's existing pollution constant (the cremation ground and tanning yard demand 120 ft clear of dwellings, and two POLLUTED grounds need more separation than polluted-from-clean does). The Principle XII artifact review raised it to 400 - and that is the clearest example on record of why that gate exists: Nagahara's ground passed the 150 ft check at 225 ft and still read, in the rendered PNG, as part of the burial cluster beside it. 400 ft is derived from the picture rather than by analogy - at the coarsest grain we draw (3 ft/px, so 133 px) it is the distance at which the two are unmistakably two places.
  • Two rules that had to be loosened, and why that was right rather than convenient. (1) execution_ground_by_the_road measures ~120 real ft to a drawn ROAD but ~400 ft to a GATE: a road is a line the ground can hug (Suzugamori sat on the Tokaido),13 while a gate is a point standing in for the road that leaves it, and Edo's grounds were miles out along their highways. Measuring a gate at the road's tolerance made the rule unsatisfiable on Hirameki, which draws no extramural road at all. (2) execution_ground_on_the_outcast_side tests DIRECTION only. It began as "beyond the quarter as well", which was unsatisfiable on a walled town whose extramural outcast quarter already sits at the canvas corner - and radius is an artifact of where the wall happens to be, while the claim the research actually supports (downwind, downstream, the outcast side) is directional.
  • The boundary stone earns its place. 道祖神 dosojin / sae no kami stand at village boundaries, passes, and crossroads, and the etymology is the point: sae means "to block", and the deity's job is to stop evil, pestilence, and pollution from ENTERING. That converts "outside the settlement" from a vague spatial claim into a stated ritual boundary - the ground is not merely far from the houses, it is on the far side of the stone that keeps pollution out - which is why the check also demands the stone stand outside the wall. A dosojin inside the rampart bounds nothing.

Re-sourcing note (2026-08-28, feature 143): the pages read call Suzugamori and Kozukappara Edo's14 "二大刑場"; the third (Ōwada) is the entry's and was not confirmed. The siting finding - past the built edge, on the highway - rests on the two.

Trade works - which trades outgrow the shop glyph, and why

Grounds: s.brewery, s.dye_yard, s.lumber_yard, s.oil_press, s.pawnshop, s.bathhouse, s.kiln, s.farrier and their checks

Evidence: attested, corroborated, setting-canon

Sources: the 2026-07-24 pass recorded no pointers; not re-sourced on 2026-08-28 - the shortlist (brewery, dye yard, lumber yard, oil press, bathhouse, farrier) is the largest leftover of feature 143 and gets its own reader; the pawnshop and kiln rest on their own entries (tontine-monastery-lending; "Kiln works") fukagawa-kiba-summary (SUMMARY-ONLY: the lumber yard as a 300,000 m2 waterfront works ordered out of Edo after 1657); the brewery, dye works, oil press, bathhouse and farrier premises were searched on ja.wikipedia (酒蔵, 紺屋, 絞油 404, 銭湯, 馬沓) and none gives a footprint - they stay as reasoning

  • TRADE WORKS - which trades outgrow the generic shop glyph, and why (drives s.brewery, s.dye_yard, s.lumber_yard, s.oil_press, s.pawnshop, s.bathhouse/s.bathhouses, s.kiln, s.farrier and their checks; the DRAWN rules live under "Provincial cities" above. Research pass 2026-07-24, from the GM's question during the provincial-city audit - "are there trades whose real premises, with their attached works, yards, and outbuildings, are materially bigger than a shophouse, i.e. significant space our maps are not representing?" - plus the farriery reversal of 2026-07-25.)
  • Where this entry came from. It absorbs the former trade-footprint-research.md, a separate per-question research file. That file had already duplicated its own conclusions into the rules paragraph above, and the duplication is exactly what bit: when the GM reversed the horseshoe finding, the stale claim "there are NO FARRIERS" was living in THREE places (the research file, the rules paragraph, and a settlement.py comment) and every one had to be found and fixed. The standing convention that follows: the rule and its why belong in this file, stated once; a standalone research file is for a derivation too long to inline (flophouse-research.md) or a dated point-in-time audit (town-deep-audit.md), and it must never restate a rule the operative doc also states.
  • The frame. Song/Ming China first, Edo Japan second. City maps at 1 px = 3 ft already draw commerce as generic ~48x32 ft shop rowhouses plus merchant kura and dedicated inn/stables/flophouse glyphs, so the only question is which trades BREAK that glyph.
  • The shortlist - premises visibly bigger than a rowhouse at this scale. Sake/miso/soy brewery (sakagura; Chinese zaofang) is the big one: even a small county-seat brewer needed a vat hall of 5-6 ft fermentation tanks, a koji room, a rice-steaming area with masonry kamado and chimney, its own well, and a rice kura - a minimal hall runs 60-120 ft x 30-40 ft BEHIND a normal shopfront, 3-8x the shophouse, and surviving small-town compounds reach whole-block scale (one Nagano brewer at ~1,500 tsubo / ~53,000 sq ft). It was very often the largest commercial building in a small town; expect 1-2 per seat of ~3,000, sited in town on good well water because brewers were town elite. Dyer (kon-ya): modest building, large GROUND signature - the workshop's sunken indigo vats and boiling hearth fit a deep shophouse, but the DRYING does not (poles, racks, and rooftop platforms dominate the block in Hiroshige's Kanda Konya-cho print) and rinsing happened in open water (nunosarashi), so a town dyer needs a ~2,000-5,000 sq ft drying yard (bolts run 35-40 ft) or riverbank frontage. Lumber dealer (zaimokuya): stacked timber and floated logs need a ~3,000-8,000 sq ft open yard with a river landing, at the edge of town near the downstream gate or wharf.16 Oil presser (aburaya / youfang): the wedge-and-beam press is a massive timber machine (beam 15-30 ft) plus a seed-roasting stove and an ox-driven edge-runner mill on a ~20-25 ft circular track - a fire-conscious press barn ~40-60 x 25-30 ft toward the edge. Pawnshop (shichiya): an ordinary shop whose tell is STORAGE - pledges are bulky, so a prosperous broker kept 2-3 fireproof kura in a walled rear court,15 i.e. the existing kura glyph multiplied rather than a new building type. Bathhouse (sento): an ordinary streetfront building with a rear furnace and chimney, whose visible extra is a ~1,000-2,000 sq ft firewood stack yard; commercial baths are China-first correct, attested from the Song, so a city may keep 1-2.
  • The null results, which are equally load-bearing. The long tail genuinely fits the shop glyph - tofu maker, noodle and confection shops, apothecary, teahouse, cooper, tatami maker, carpenter's shop (his big work happens on site), rice dealer (treadle-pestle hulling in-shop, grain in a rear kura). And some trades belong OUTSIDE town entirely: tile, pottery, charcoal, and lime kilns are pushed to the periphery or to nearby villages by fire law and smoke, and tanneries and slaughter go downstream-riverside with the extramural burakumin quarter, never inside.
  • FARRIERY - the null result that was REVERSED (GM 2026-07-25). The 2026-07-24 pass recorded a flat "NO FARRIERS", on the correct observation that Edo Japan did not shoe horses: it used woven straw horse-sandals (umagutsu / umazouri), and nailed iron shoeing arrived with Meiji. That is right about Japan and wrong about this Rokugan, for two reasons the GM's own notes already carried. (1) Iron. Japan's constraint was material, not ignorance - iron sand (satetsu) smelted with charcoal is a low-yield, fuel-hungry process, so iron went to blades, armor, and tools long before hooves. Rokugan is continental, with real ore bodies and whole iron districts already in l7r.md (Tatarano, "bellows plain"; Kuroiwa; Ubame's charcoal-sited furnaces; the Reiji hill works; Crab iron for the Wall). Ore is abundant and iron is a normal industrial good, while good STEEL stays scarce and expensive - which buys horseshoes, plain low-carbon iron, without deflating a good blade. (2) Institutional mileage, more than paving. Hard surfaces do abrade hoof, but the sharper driver is annual miles per horse: budgets.md gives ~5,000 waystations on ~50,000 miles of Imperial road at ~10-mile spacing - about 3x the density of the Tang yi network - plus 500-1,000 mounted couriers and mounted anti-banditry patrols. A courier horse working a fixed stage all year wears hoof at a rate no farm horse approaches, and straw sandals are consumables that wear out within a day's travel. Straw scales for a farmer walking an ox to the next village; it does not scale for institutionalized relay. Canon already assumed this: budgets.md staffs busy trunk-route waystations with "a smith for shoeing horses and weapon maintenance," and l7r.md's Moto Khuyag is introduced as a farrier who offers to reshoe the PCs' horses - a Rokugani-born one, who learned the trade at Kyuden Shinjo. So this was a reconciliation, not a retcon; the diagram skill's null result was the thing out of step. Two arguments were considered and deliberately NOT relied on, because they do not survive checking: "later Chinese dynasties nailed iron shoes" (Chinese hoof practice ran mostly to trimming and strap-on or woven boots; nailed shoes are firmly a European development, and Rome's cursus publicus used strap-on hipposandals, not nails), and "the Mongol Yam proves it" (steppe horses on grass were famously ridden UNSHOD, which is a counterexample). The conclusion stands on iron price and relay mileage alone.
  • What that changes on the map, and what it does not. The SHAPE conclusion of the 2026-07-24 pass survives its own premise: even in Europe a farrier was a one-forge smithy with a bit of yard, and the big "farrier-forge-with-stable-yard" compound is a coaching-inn image that does not earn its footprint at 1 px = 3 ft. So an ordinary town smith (kaji-ya) who also shoes still draws as a generic shop rowhouse - shoeing changes his REPERTOIRE, not his premises - and no new establishment joins the shop rows. What earns real ground is only the place horses CONCENTRATE: the gate caravan yard and the Imperial-road relay town, where s.farrier draws a dedicated shed and apron. Two rules fall out of the physical facts. It is not attached to the stables: an open forge against a hay-and-timber stall range is a fire the yard does not survive, so real yards kept the smithy across the ground (farrier_keeps_fire_gap, anchored on buildings.html's ~6-8 ft wooden-service fire gap; the measure runs from the whole recorded footprint, shed plus apron, which is deliberately conservative since the shed sits at the apron's far end). And the apron carries an ox-shoeing frame: Rokugan's draft animal is mostly the ox, a cloven hoof takes two plates per foot, and an ox cannot balance on three legs, so it must be slung in timber stocks - about 7 x 4 ft, drawn true-size like everything else. Sizes are the historical anchors with no legibility inflation: an 18-20 ft village smithy for the shed, and an apron a horse's length (~8 ft) plus room to lead one in and turn it. Straw survives everywhere iron is not worth it - peasant pack horses, oxen on soft paddy tracks, poor and mountain provinces - because it is free, not because iron is unknown; that is setting texture rather than a drawn feature, and it lives in budgets.md.
  • Frequency by scale, and the reasoning behind it. Rokugan's horse population is LOW relative to Europe - peasants work oxen, and horses are a samurai and Imperial animal - so farriery is not a mass trade and the count must not scale with population the way the bathhouse does. Provincial city: exactly one, at a gate caravan yard (not one per gate). Town: only on an Imperial road, which is why meta(imperial_road=True) gates the check rather than town scale alone - at ~10-mile waystation spacing plenty of towns simply are not on the network, and that non-uniformity is the point. Village and hamlet: never; the village smith trims hooves and tacks on straw with no dedicated premises. A county magistracy (Mode A) keeps only 2-4 horses per buildings.html, so it keeps no permanent forge either - the smith walks over from town.
  • The implemented features (GM 2026-07-24, "I'd like all of them"): brewery compound (city_has_brewery, vat hall + shopfront + rice kura + its own PRIVATE well; both cities keep one, and Tango's stands on the pond); dyer's drying yard (city_has_dye_works, must sit ON water - both cities, since dyeing needs rinsing water rather than bulk transport, so a landlocked city keeps its dyers; Tango's rinses to the DRAINAGE side, never the field-supply channels); riverside lumber yard (city_river_port_has_lumber_yard, gated on meta(river_port=True) - Nagahara only, since timber is the one trade that genuinely needs water transport at scale, and the yard abuts the bank but never overlaps the water); oil-press barn (city_has_oil_press); pawnshop (city_has_pawnshop); bathhouse (city_has_bathhouse, count rolled from population); kiln (city_kiln_outside_walls); farrier (city_has_farrier / imperial_road_town_has_farrier, 2026-07-25). The tanning yard is drawn too and has its own entry below.

The log boom - a shore-fast holding pen, not sticks in the stream

Evidence: attested, analog (the American booms)

Sources: nishikori-tsunaba, kiso-unzaihou, susquehanna-boom, stcroix-boom-nps, hudson-big-boom, kiba-koto, shangxinhe-gazetteer, timber-rafting-wiki (cited inline below; read at the 2026-08-02 pass)

  • LOG BOOM - anchored to the bank, run along a navigated river, and FULL (drives s.log_boom's pen form and log_boom_moored_to_the_bank / log_boom_leaves_the_fairway / log_boom_serves_the_lumber_yard; the pre-fix Minami capture is frozen in pool/regressions/. Research pass 2026-08-02, from the GM's reaction to the first drawn boom - "it just looks like a bunch of logs in the middle of the river. How did log booms really work? Did they block the whole river? I assume they were at least partially against the shore.")
  • Two systems moved timber by water, and the full-river boom belongs to only one of them. Loose-log driving (Japanese kanagashi, one log at a time) ran the upper, unnavigable reaches; rafting ran the lower river.17 The one place a boom legitimately spans the whole water is the catch point where driving hands off to rafting: on the Kiso this was the Owari domain's tsunaba18 ("rope place") at Nishikori, sited exactly where the river leaves its gorge - a great rope stretched bank to bank, in use from the 1340s, catching ~300,000 logs a year in a season running autumn equinox to spring equinox, administered by a timber magistracy of 138 permanent officials (unsourced at the check of 2026-09-06: the source page displays only a partial extract - the site's location and unrelated 1285/1345 Mino timber dates - not these figures). Rafts were assembled there (three-man crews) and floated on to the storage yards at Nagoya and the bay ports.19 A full-span catch boom is legitimate ONLY where the river is not a navigation route - upstream, at the gorge mouth, never at a port.
  • Where the river carries boats, the boom runs ALONG it - and the law said so. The great American boom works are the same furniture at maximum documented scale. The Susquehanna Boom at Williamsport ran seven miles (corrected 2026-09-06, feature 194: the page gives the crib line running diagonally across the river, from the south bank near Duboistown to the north bank near Linden, not along one side)78, strung from ~350-400 man-made islands (timber cribs filled with stone, 22 ft high),20 enclosing the holding pen between boom line and shore; it held up to 300 million board feet afloat at once - on the order of a million logs21 - and corralled ~40 million over its working life (unsourced at the check of 2026-09-06: the only lifetime figure on the page is mill output, 5.5 billion board feet 1861-91). The St. Croix boom works divided the river lengthwise with piers, pilings and floating log fences into log channels and holding pens, a navigation channel maintained alongside and a hinged22 "sheer boom" at the head swinging open and closed to admit logs. Boom companies were statutorily barred from materially obstructing navigation, and spills into the fairway drew lawsuits and negligence rulings.23 Anchoring was always to fixed ground: bank abutments, stone-filled cribs, driven piles - the Hudson's Big Boom rode on hewn timbers bolted four-together raftwise, chained end to end over friction rollers to abutments on the banks.24 A boom is a floating fence; a fence anchored to nothing holds nothing.
  • At the destination city, timber sits in slack water, in bulk. Edo kept its stock at Kiba - a dedicated lumber district of canals and storage ponds where rafts were poled in and logs floated25 (water storage keeps timber sound and safe from fire - unsourced at the check of 2026-09-06: the page gives fire risk as the reason the trade moved across the river, not as a property of water storage). Nanjing (China-first) kept its stock at Shangxinhe, a ~9 km side channel off the Yangtze (unsourced at the check of 2026-09-06: no length is on the page) where timber and bamboo rafts moored along the banks in such mass that a permanent timber-merchant town grew around them,26 "constantly sufficient through all seasons" at its Qing peak. Nagoya used the Shirotori yards the same way.27 Nowhere is a city's working stock a scatter of loose logs in mid-channel: it is an enclosed pen against the bank, moored raft-mats lining a slack side-water, or off-river ponds.
  • The decisions it drove. The glyph is a PEN: chain anchored to the bank at both ends (mooring posts), running out around the held water on pile clusters, with the raft-mats drawn as a near-solid surface between chain and shore - the largest working thing on a timber city's water, per the glyph's own founding rationale. It never spans a navigated channel: drawn at ~1/3 of the river's width, checked at <=40% (log_boom_leaves_the_fairway), with the bank edge required to ride ON the shore line (log_boom_moored_to_the_bank) and the pen moored off the lumber yard it feeds (log_boom_serves_the_lumber_yard, association-family). For Minami specifically: the Hayakawa is 120 ft wide and navigated (wharf, dock basin, cargo canal), so the city gets the shore-pen form; the romantic full-span tsunaba is off-map lore - the Fox catch-works at the gorge mouth upstream, where the loose drive becomes the rafts that arrive at the city.

Kiln works - a kiln is not a tile kiln, and the potters live at it

Evidence: researched

Sources: the 2026-07-27 pass recorded no pointers; not re-sourced on 2026-08-28 - leftover (kawara demand, the noborigama and its potters' housing, the fire-gap) kawara-jawiki (READ: commoners board-roofed, tile general only at the end of Edo - tile demand confined to the few); the potters living at the noborigama and the fire gap remain unsourced

  • KILN WORKS - why the glyph is a WORKS with housing, and why the caption stopped saying "tile" (drives s.kiln, kiln_works_houses_its_workers, kiln_keeps_fire_gap, and the wording of city_kiln_outside_walls; the DRAWN rules live under "Provincial cities" in ../settlements/urban-features.md. Research pass 2026-07-27, from two GM questions: "would the tile kiln have housing close to it, i.e. would whoever works the kiln also live next to it?" and "why is it specifically a 'tile kiln' and not just a kiln? Are there potters in the city with their own non-tile kilns? Or are we not accounting for them as well?")
  • What was there before, and why it was thin. The kiln was drawn as a lone 28 x 18 ft earthen mound captioned "tile kiln", governed by exactly one rule (city_kiln_outside_walls) whose whole justification was "fire law + smoke". It appears in the 2026-07-24 trade-works pass only as a clause in the list of banished nuisance trades - it never got an entry of its own, and the "tile" in the caption was never argued for anywhere. Both GM questions land on that gap. This entry is the argument that was missing.
  • THE VOLUME REASONING RUNS THE WRONG WAY - it is a kiln, not a tile kiln. The GM's instinct ("I wouldn't have expected that to be such a huge difference as to warrant one getting a map glyph and the other not") is right, and if anything understates it. Tile demand is lumpy and project-driven. A fired clay roof lasts generations - Japanese kawara roofs are repaired in patches, not replaced (unsourced at the check of 2026-09-06) - and in a county seat tile reaches only the temple halls, the gates, the governor's compound and a handful of rich merchants; the rest of the town is thatch and shingle.28 So the tile kiln has a huge year when a temple hall is reroofed and repair work otherwise. Pottery demand is continuous. Every household breaks and replaces bowls, pots and jars on a steady cycle, which is precisely why ceramics are the most reliable thing in an archaeological layer: the stuff never stops being consumed. The map's own features make it worse - the brewery needs fermentation vessels and big storage jars (kame), and a large storage jar is heavy and cheap per pound, so it is made near where it is used rather than freighted in. Per YEAR, a seat of this size burns more clay into vessels than into roof tiles.
  • So why one glyph rather than two. Because at county-seat scale it is genuinely one works. Coarse tile and coarse domestic ware share a clay source, an operator, and a low firing; a works fires mixed loads and there is no reason to draw the same establishment twice. What does NOT belong here is fine stoneware, which needs its own hotter and longer firing - that is a province's specialty (the Seto/Bizen/Jingdezhen pattern, a whole district known for one ware), not something every county seat keeps. A map that knows what its particular works fires says so in s.kiln(..., label=...); the default caption is now just "kiln".
  • THE WORKERS LIVE AT THE KILN. Three independent reasons, any one of which would be enough. (1) A firing runs for days. An anagama or noborigama is stoked round the clock in shifts until the ware is done, then sealed and left to cool - nobody walks back into town at dusk in the middle of one. (2) The kiln stands at the CLAY, not at the customer. Siting follows the clay pit, the fuel supply, and a slope to build the chambers into, and those constraints pull digging, weathering, throwing, drying and firing out to one spot together. (3) It is how the trade was organized. CHINA FIRST: Song and Ming kiln districts were worked by registered kiln households living at their kilns, with Jingdezhen an entire city grown around its kilns. Japan corroborates rather than leads - Seto, Tokoname, Bizen, Imado on Edo's fringe, Awataguchi and Kiyomizu at Kyoto's edge are kiln VILLAGES and kiln quarters, not workshops somebody commutes to.
  • Banishment is about the CITY, not the operator. Fire law and smoke are a real constraint and city_kiln_outside_walls stays exactly as it was: the firing does not happen inside the dense blocks. But that rule is about keeping an uncontrollable fire out of a packed timber town, and it says nothing whatever against the households whose trade it is. They stand off the kiln by the same fire gap anybody else would and no further. Getting this backwards is what produced the lone-mound glyph: "banished" was read as "banished including its people", which is the tannery's caste logic borrowed by a trade that does not carry it. Potters are not hinin. The tanning yard's siting rules turn on the burakumin quarter; the kiln's turn on clay, fuel and fire, and the two must not be conflated.
  • THE FORM WAS WRONG TOO. A low earthen mound is a CHARCOAL or lime kiln's shape, and on the rendered map it read as one - which mattered more than it sounds, since Ubame's charcoal district is a live subject in this pool and the two would have been indistinguishable. A tile or pottery kiln in this culture is a long chambered tunnel built into a slope: firebox at the bottom, chambers climbing, chimney at the top. The chambered silhouette also carries the siting fact the mound hid - the works wants rising ground. (A separate slope band behind the kiln was drawn and then cut: at 1 px = 3 ft it read as a stray tab off the kiln's shoulder rather than as ground, and the chambered form says it already.)
  • THE 60 FT FIRE GAP, and why it is not a new number. It joins the existing separation ladder (see the head of the charcoal-district block in check_village.py) at the rung the refining forge already occupies. A firing is a very large fire, but an ATTENDED one - being stoked in shifts is the whole reason it runs the way it does - so it sits with the live worked fire at 60 ft and not with the UNATTENDED self-heating charcoal stack at 30 ft, nor with the 120 ft nuisance figures, which defend against smell carried on air rather than against ignition. Duration does the work here that the forced blast does for the forge. The gap is measured from the KILN BODY rather than from the works' ground, because the works' own cottages stand inside that ground - a bounding-rect test could only ever measure the gap to somebody else's house.
  • The null result, which is what answers the GM's second question directly. The town's potters are NOT missing from the map. Only the FIRING is banished: a crockery dealer or a potter's shopfront inside the walls is an ordinary shophouse and stays in the generic shop rows with the tofu maker and the cooper, exactly as the 2026-07-24 null-results list has it. The relationship worth remembering is that the ware is MADE at the works outside and SOLD by a dealer inside, which is the same shape as the charcoal district's "burned where the wood grows, tallied where it is sold".
  • Deliberate departures from literal reality. (a) One works per seat. A real kiln district could be several kilns and a dozen households; we draw one works with two or three cottages, because the map's job is to say "this is where the seat's pots and tiles come from" and a second identical works teaches a reader nothing. (b) The cottages are recorded INSIDE the kiln record (quarters), not in M["houses"]. This is a manifest decision, not a claim about the setting: every dwelling rule in the gate - public-well reach, ward classification, the burakumin standoff - is written about the settlement's own housing stock, and a satellite works' two cottages would be adjudicated by rules that were never about them. The one place they must count as dwellings is the works' own well, and wells_among_dwellings reads them explicitly for that. (c) Sizes are true feet at the map's grain with no legibility inflation, pitched against the pool's other bulk works (the charcoal yard's 88 x 58, the brewery's 96 ft hall): a 46 ft kiln, a 32 x 18 ft throwing and drying shed, a 30 x 24 ft clay pit, and 28 x 18 ft cottages on a 140 x 120 ft ground.

Tanning yards - hides come from fallen draft stock, and water is the gate

Grounds: settlement_has_tanning_yard, tanning_yard_on_water, pits sizing

Evidence: attested, setting-canon

Sources: shiniuba-jawiki (READ: danna-ba / kusaba), himeji-shironameshi-jawiki (READ), northampton-tannery-1996 (SUMMARY-ONLY); the L7R horse stream is setting-canon (l7r.md)

  • Why towns tan too, and this is not a city-only trade. The GM's opening guess was that most towns would have no tannery. The research says otherwise, because of where hides come from: NOT butchery but fallen draft stock. Under the Tokugawa status order the kawata held carcass rights over a defined service territory (danna-ba29) - when an ox or horse died it became that community's property automatically, with no compensation to the owner.30 So a county town's ~12 burakumin households (budgets.md town tier: 60 inhabitants) work the whole county's dead animals, not just the town's. L7R canon supplies the same stream from the other end: l7r.md has daimyo giving surplus horses to village headmen to distribute to farms as draft animals, and those horses "often slaughtered and eaten when the need for meat outweighs the capacity of the peasants to feed those horses."
  • Throughput is small, and scales with the territory served - which is what pits encodes. A county of ~6,800 inhabitants sheds on the order of a couple dozen carcasses a year, so the town yard is a seasonal works of ~4 pits, never an industry. A provincial city adds the daimyo's stable (several hundred horses per domain) and the armor/saddle/drum/bellows demand of its ~300 samurai, so it runs ~12. That ratio is why the city yard draws roughly 1.7x the town yard's area rather than 5x: pits, not prestige, set the size. For comparison, the largest excavated urban tannery at Northampton ran 36-37 pits31 - far past anything in this pool.

  • WATER is the real gate, not settlement size. Tanning is a water process: the Japanese shironameshi method stakes raw hides in the river for one week in summer and two in winter (corrected 2026-09-06, feature 194: the page gives 「数日間」 - several days - not a seasonal figure)79 before the hair is knifed off, and every archaeologically-attested tannery sits on a watercourse at the settlement's edge. The caste's own name for itself was kawaramono, "riverbed people." Hence the check's shape: a town or city with a burakumin quarter and a watercourse must have a yard; one with no running water keeps none whatever its size, and is exempt rather than failing.

  • WHY tannery effluent must not reach a paddy intake - and which part of it actually does the harm (GM question 2026-07-25). Tanning in general is famous for urine and dung: hides were limed OR soaked in stale urine to loosen the hair, then "bated" by kneading them in dog or pigeon dung. But the method these maps commit to is Japanese shironameshi, which uses none of it - no lime, no tannin, no dung, no urine.32 The hides soak in the river for 1-2 weeks (river bacteria do the unhairing), the hair is knifed off, and the hide is finished with salt and rapeseed oil alone. So the discharge is three things: two weeks of controlled putrefaction, SALT, and an oil film. The salt is what a rice paddy cannot take. Rice is the most salt-sensitive cereal, and it is more sensitive to the salinity of the FIELD WATER than of the soil - a yield threshold around 0.9 dS/m - so brine arriving down an irrigation ditch into a flooded paddy is the precise wrong input. Note the counterintuitive corner, so nobody "improves" the rule later by reasoning from rot: the ORGANIC half of the effluent is arguably fertilizer (Rokugani farmers deliberately spread night soil), so putrefaction is the WEAK argument here. The rule stands on salt, the oil film, and the stench.

  • WHY A YARD MAY SIT ON A FIELD DRAIN AT ALL, AND WHY THAT IS THE CASTE'S NATURAL WATER (GM question 2026-08-08, of Hoshizora: "the tanning yard is next to a drainage ditch of a farm field - is that realistic as a source of water for a tannery?"). Hoshizora has no stream near its burakumin quarter (the valley watercourse runs across the far west), so its yard takes the hoshizora-ne comb's drain. Three separate findings say that is right, and one says what it costs.

  • Water RIGHTS, not water quality, is the strongest argument - and it was the one nobody had written down. In both Japanese and Chinese irrigation communities an upstream tap was fiercely-held communal property, allocated by village agreement and litigated over for generations. A kawata quarter would never be granted a headwater offtake. The water an outcast community could actually hold is the water nobody upstream is fighting over: the tail of the system, below every paddy intake. So a yard on a field drain is not the map settling for second best when no stream is handy - it is the sociologically correct water for this caste, and it is the literal sense of kawaramono, "riverbed people": not people who happened to live by water, but people whose only claim was to the part of the watercourse nobody else wanted. Read that way, a ditch-fed yard is more attested than a yard on a good stream reach would be.
  • It is also the only siting that automatically satisfies the salt rule above. A drain is the one course on a map where every intake is upstream by construction, which is exactly what tanning_yard_below_every_intake and tanning_yard_discharges_to_nothing_drawn_from demand. The residual risk is real but off-map: paddy drainage is commonly RECAPTURED by lower fields in both traditions (a "drain" at one level is a "supply" at the next), so a ditch-fed yard is only honest where the drain reaches a genuine sink - off the frame, a marsh, or a drainage tameike - rather than another comb's head. Hoshizora's NE drain is tagged {"kind": "offmap"} and leaves the east edge; a future ditch-fed yard whose drain feeds anything drawn is a defect the two checks will catch on-map and nothing will catch off it.
  • What it COSTS, which should not be elided: the method changes. water="ditch" is not a cosmetic variant of water="stream". Shironameshi stakes hides in a CURRENT for one to two weeks (corrected 2026-09-06, feature 194: several days per the page)80 and lets river bacteria do the unhairing; a drain has no current, so a ditch-fed yard ponds its own soak behind a gated intake cut and is running pit-soaking, the method every other tanning tradition used, rather than the Japanese one these maps otherwise commit to. That is a downgrade, not an equivalence - it is why the glyph draws a sluice gate instead of staking frames, and it is the honest thing to say if a PC asks a Hoshizora tanner why his leather is not Ubame's.

  • And it makes the yard SEASONAL in a way a stream-fed yard is not. A paddy drain runs during the irrigation season and is near-dry from harvest to transplanting, with its heaviest flush at the pre-harvest drawdown. Carcasses arrive on the opposite schedule: draft stock dies most in late winter and early spring, when the fodder is gone and the animal is worn from the autumn hauling. The two do not have to be reconciled, because salted and dried raw hides keep for months and that storage is how the entire pre-modern hide trade worked - but it means a ditch-fed yard is a summer works whose pits stand empty half the year, while its winter is spent salting hides down and doing the clean in-town leather craft. Good adventure-hook texture, and it is why the small pits count is not the only thing keeping this from reading as an industry.

  • THE YARD FOLLOWS ITS BANK'S BEARING - and a ditch counts as a bank (GM 2026-08-08: "if one is next to a stream and the stream is at a 30 degree angle then the tannery itself is at that same angle. That should apply to ditches as well"). The rule itself is old (tanning_yard_square_to_its_water, and s.tanning_yard's docstring: the pit rank and the staking frames share ONE edge of water, so a yard left square to the map on a slanted bank puts one corner in the water and strands the far end of the rank inland). What 2026-08-08 added is the failure mode the rule's own tolerance clause admits. The check judges a yard against ANY course whose bank lies inside the ~20 ft on-water reach, which is right for a yard at a confluence - it may legitimately front either course - but it means a yard can be declared square to a SHORT INCIDENTAL tail while sitting badly askew to the long reach it works. Hoshizora was exactly that: rot=90 against the drain's 95 ft off-map sink stub at 83 deg, 47 deg off the 175 ft drain reach beside it, gate green and the rendered map plainly wrong. When a yard fronts more than one course, align it to the reach it WORKS, not merely to one the check will accept - and read the render, because this is precisely the class of defect a green gate cannot see.

  • WHERE THE QUARTER SITS RELATIVE TO ITS OWN WORKS - kegare has a DIRECTION, not a radius (GM question 2026-07-27: "does a tanning yard need burakumin housing close by? Or would the tanners just sleep at the yard?").

  • The first answer was wrong, and the way it was wrong is the lesson. Measuring yard-to-quarter DISTANCE across the pool produced a table running from 90 ft (Hirameki) to 2,587 ft (Tango) and an obvious-looking conclusion that the far ones were defects. They are not. The GM's objection was the correct one: a walled city keeps its burakumin INSIDE, because the caste's in-town duties require it, while the noxious phase of the trade goes out to the water - so distance between the two is the NORMAL arrangement, not a failure of it. Re-measuring the same maps by BEARING from the settlement core separated them cleanly where distance could not: Hirameki 0.6 deg, Minami 17.2, Hoshizora 28.5, Nagahara 56.0, Ubame 100, Tango 127. The rule was rewritten as a direction test. Generalizable: when a spatial rule produces a spread with no natural break, the quantity being measured is probably not the governing variable.

  • Why an in-city quarter is right. Japanese castle towns mostly had no circuit town wall at all, so "inside or outside the walls" is barely a Japanese question - the kawata quarter sat at the town's EDGE, typically past a river or on the road out. Edo is the clearest case: Asakusa Shinmachi, the Danzaemon compound, sat well within the city as a walled precinct of ~400 holding private residences, warehouses, workshops, a shrine, a courtroom and a prison, and its residents worked all over Edo. China-first agrees with fewer complications: no outcast caste at all, tanners and butchers are ordinary artisans, and the noxious WATER trades go to the extramural suburbs (guanxiang) below the water gate. Either way, quarter in-town and works downstream is the attested arrangement rather than a compromise.
  • The load-bearing detail: one caste, TWO work sites, and only one of them stinks. The soak/unhair/dry phase is riverbank work. Everything else the caste does - night soil for samurai and wealthy merchants, corpse removal, execution and prison service, and the actual leather CRAFT (sandals, drum heads, armor lacing, bellows) - is clean, quiet, and done at home or in town. So the quarter follows the in-town duties and the yard follows the water, and a daily walk between them is not a defect. This is also why the earlier idea of drawing a satellite hut or two AT the pits was dropped: the historical unit is a corporate community under a headman, not scattered outbuildings, and a lone outcast house away from its quarter is not a thing that existed.
  • No, the tanners did not sleep at the works, and no watchman is needed. Nothing attests tanners bedding at the pits, and the setting does not need it: under shironameshi the exposed phase is one to two weeks (corrected 2026-09-06, feature 194: several days per the page)81 of hides staked IN the river, which no watchman covers anyway. The security is not a person but kegare plus monopoly - a heimin who lifts a hide has handled death-pollution and has nowhere to sell it, because the burakumin hold the carcass right (danna-ba) and are the only legal channel for leather.33 Worth keeping straight for adventure hooks: stealing from a tanning yard is easy and nearly pointless, which is a more interesting fact than a guard post would have been.
  • And the smell argument runs the OTHER way. The 120 ft standoff (tanning_yard_clear_of_dwellings) exists to keep the stench off OTHER castes' houses, which is exactly why burakumin dwellings are already exempt from it. Distance-from-nuisance is a privilege of the clean castes; the quarter is inside the polluted zone by definition. Walking half a mile to escape your own trade's smell is a comfort a kawata household did not have.
  • Why direction is the thing taboo actually systematizes. Kegare leaves a settlement ONE way. Edo stacked Asakusa, the Kozukappara execution ground and the Yoshiwara at the northeast kimon; Kyoto put its outcast communities on the riverbeds and the southern roads out. So the quarter, the execution ground and the tanning yard share a bearing, and the daily carcass haul never crosses the rest of the settlement - real castle towns kept designated carcass routes off the main streets for precisely that reason. The CREMATION ground is the deliberate exclusion: it is monk-run and follows the temple/funerary complex. Tested across the pool, that association is real where it matters (Hoshizora 130 ft from its monastery, Ubame 360 ft) and absent elsewhere (Tango 2,606 ft), so cremation is excluded from the bearing rule WITHOUT a positive "follows the monastery" rule being asserted in its place - the evidence supports the exclusion, not the converse claim.
  • The 90 deg threshold, and why it is not a fresh number. It is the dot-product "same half of the compass" test execution_ground_on_the_outcast_side already used; the new check simply applies the existing rule to the other burakumin-run works rather than inventing a tolerance. The pool's loosest ACCEPTED map (Nagahara, 83.7 deg by the laborer-core measure) sits inside it and the two defects sit outside, so the line separates the corpus as it stands.

The bell-and-drum tower - one per walled seat

Grounds: s.drum_tower, walled_settlement_has_drum_tower

Evidence: attested, corroborated

Sources: pingyao-shilou-zhwiki (READ: 133.4 m2, three bays square), dongjing-menghualu-rujia (READ: 军巡铺 every 300 paces, 望火楼 with a hundred soldiers). Not found in any page read: a citywide count of Kaifeng's towers ("20+"), Kawagoe's base dimension (only its ~16 m height), the Dingbian / Xingcheng figures - leftover

What the research found: the "morning bell, evening drum" institution (dawn gate-opening, the dusk gate-closing that began the street curfew, the five night watches, alarm and ceremony) was part of the standard Ming county-seat kit alongside the yamen, the Confucian temple, and the City God temple - common approaching standard in walled northern seats. A county seat had ONE tower - a drum tower, a combined bell-and-drum tower, or a market tower straddling the main street; the paired gulou + zhonglou on an axis is CAPITAL grammar (Beijing, Xi'an), and Pingyao, a wealthy county seat, has exactly one (the ~60 ft Market Tower on South Street). County-tier form (footprint RE-VERIFIED 2026-07-24, second research pass after the GM's eye caught the first-draft 70 ft platform oversized): Pingyao's Market Tower - the wealthy-county showpiece - is ATTESTED at 133.4 m^2 plan, ~38 ft square35; these towers dominated by HEIGHT (50-60 ft),36 not plan, and Kawagoe's famous time-bell tower served a town of tens of thousands from a <20 ft base. The 50-66 ft square examples (Dingbian 52 ft, Xingcheng 66 ft) are the tower-over-street-arch type - garrison/prefecture fabric, never a 3,000-person seat - and the original 60-80 ft figure was contaminated by them. The drawn band is therefore 30-45 ft: ~36 ft at city tier, ~30 ft at town tier - one rowhouse width, which is exactly how they read in plan. Siting: the central crossroads near (not inside) the yamen; poorer seats made do with a qiaolou - a drum tower over the yamen's own gate (a recognized cheaper variant, not drawn here). Critically, FIRE WATCH WAS A SEPARATE INSTITUTION in both reference cultures - Song Kaifeng ran 20+ dedicated wanghuolou fire-lookout towers with a standing brigade,37 and Edo split the licensed toki no kane time bell from the hinomi-yagura fire towers - so the fire towers do not cover the timekeeping/curfew niche. The decision it drove: every walled seat (provincial city or walled town) draws exactly ONE combined bell-and-drum tower at its main street crossing (a corner of the crossing, not straddling the street), 70 ft at city tier, ~60 ft at town tier, visibly heavier-built than the skeletal fire towers; the capital tier may take the full paired gulou/zhonglou when it exists (and the street-arch platform type, 50-66 ft, is available as a garrison-city variant). An UNWALLED town is exempt: it has no gates to close, and its time signal is the monastery's bell - the Edo pattern, where most towns contracted a temple bell rather than building a tower - implied within the precinct, no glyph (Hoshizora). The tower follows the WALL, not the population. The caption reads "drum/bell" over "tower" (GM 2026-07-24): the drawn building is genuinely the combined zhonggulou - both instruments in one pavilion, and both are drawn - so the label says so. Audibility - one central tower covers the whole seat with enormous margin (GM sanity check 2026-07-24, researched). The geometry: Tango's farthest in-wall resident stands ~1,640 ft (500 m) from its tower and the farthest drawn gate-suburb structure ~790 m; Nagahara ~435 m / ~920 m. The attested anchors dwarf those distances: Edo's Kokucho time bell BILLED ~400-410 blocks of households "within earshot" (kane-yaku-sen, 4 mon per month per house - an effective fee radius of ~1.2-1.4 km in a far denser, noisier city, and a conservative figure, since only admitted hearers were billed); Basho casually heard the Ueno/Asakusa bells ~3-4 km away across the river; Beijing's single drum tower timed the watches for an inner city ~6.5 x 5.3 km. Acoustically, a large bell or great drum runs ~110-130 dB near the tower; at a 500-730 m city edge that arrives ~40 dB above a premodern NIGHT noise floor (~25-35 dBA, when the watches matter most) - loud, not merely detectable, everywhere inside the walls and suburbs, and the low frequencies diffract over one-story rooflines. One tower stops sufficing only past a ~2-3 km radius of daytime urban din, so even the future ~12,000-pop capital tier (~1 mi across) needs no second tower on audibility grounds: the historical scaling answers are Edo's multiplied fee-district bells vs the China-first pattern of ONE monumental tower whose watches ward night-watchmen relay through the streets with clappers and gongs - the capital tier should take the latter (grander tower, implied watchmen, still exactly one).

Stable yards - beaten earth, hitching rails, and watering by relay

Grounds: stables_have_yards, stable_troughs_beside_well, dung_heaps_clear_of_hitch_rails

Evidence: attested, reconstruction

Sources: qingming-shanghe-tu

  • What the research found (China-first, Song/Ming-Qing; Edo Japan secondary; sources incl. the Qingming Shanghe Tu 清明上河図 gate scene, the caravanserai court, the Chinese hitching post 拴马桩, the yizhan 驿站 / shukuba post-station). A city-gate cart/animal yard was beaten tamped earth - NO grass (trampled hard, and the animals were hay-fed at mangers, not grazed there) - and, crucially, NOT a fenced paddock (the least authentic option; a rail fence belongs to an inn's private back-yard or a walled caravanserai). Its edges are the wall + flanking buildings; its "in active use" signal is carts, animals tethered at hitching rails / picket lines, and littered ground (the Qingming convention: clustered carts and pack animals, no fence, no lawn).38 WELL-ATTESTED: the hitching post as a Chinese fixture, the Qingming gate scene, the caravanserai court. INFERRED (reasoned from those norms - no source describes an ordinary gate cart-yard's ground directly): the no-grass beaten-earth surface and the open-not-fenced edge.

What the research found: a working ox drinks ~10 gal/day (range 3-30 by heat/work), a pack horse 5-18, a water buffalo 40-60+ L and it barely sweats (it wants a WALLOW, not just a trough) - so a 20-40 head wagon-train arriving thirsty needs ~300-600 gallons, drunk in one or two big sessions. Animals are LED to the water in relays - you water at the well, not at the hitching rail. It is enough, and the reason is worth keeping because the naive fix (add troughs) does nothing. Edge capacity: each drawn trough is ~14 ft at ~2 ft of edge per drinking head, both sides of a freestanding trough usable - so one trough serves ~14 head at once (call it 7 where the wellhead crowds one side); the caravan ground's 3 troughs are ~20-40 simultaneous stations, a plain yard's 2 are ~14-28. Turnover: cattle drink ~1-2 gal/min, so a working ox's 5-8 gal session is over in ~5 minutes - even read one-sided, the caravan cluster waters ~20 head every ~6 minutes, and several dozen oxen clear in two or three relays (~20-30 min). That fits how a stage stop worked: animals were unyoked and rested for HOURS, so watering was a leisurely relay chore for a couple of teamsters, not a race - the London figure (one 9.5 ft trough, 1,800 horses/day) is the throughput-by-turnover anchor. The binding constraint is the WELL, not the trough edge: a hand bucket-draw sustains maybe 5-10 gal/min, so a train's 300-600 gallons is 1-2 hours of steady drawing NO MATTER how many troughs stand there. Extra troughs would not water anyone faster; they would just hold more standing water. What the troughs actually do is BUFFER - filled between arrivals so the first relay drinks instantly while the drawing continues - which is exactly why caravanserais centralized on one courtyard well/cistern instead of multiplying basins, and why coaching-inn yards typically show a SINGLE trough (our 2-3 is already the generous end).

Wells - the research, and the deliberate liberty

What the research found: a pre-modern south-China rice village of ~70 households ran 1-3 communal drinking wells total, 2 typical - drinking water came mostly from SURFACE water (canal/pond/stream, stored in vats, settled, boiled), which is why schistosomiasis was endemic in the Yangtze rice belt for two millennia and why >70% of rural China still drank untreated surface water in the early 1980s; wells were expensive durable capital financed by per-family subscription and dug only as surface quality forced; the classical jingtian "8 families per well" is a Warring-States ideal nobody practiced. Charcoal yards: a tallied depot, a cooling ground, and a weighing floor

Evidence: attested, corroborated

Sources: wagner-ming-iron, fao-charcoal-safety, tonya-enwiki, fires-in-edo-enwiki, sizes-koku (the inline list below). The Song output figures (125,000 tons by 1078; regulated smelting households) came from Grokipedia and are in the re-sourcing queue - to be re-read from en.wikipedia Economy of the Song dynasty and its references - leftover economy-song-enwiki (READ 2026-08-28: Hartwell's 125,000 t by 1078; state charcoal supplies to iron households) - the Grokipedia citation is replaced

China first. Charcoal was an industrial input at state scale. Song iron-smelting households were government-regulated, with state support that explicitly included charcoal supplies50, and a large Ming ironworks is recorded with 200 charcoal producers alongside 200 furnace-tenders and 300 miners51 - the fuel workforce as large as the furnace workforce. Northern Song output reached roughly 125,000 tons of iron a year by 1078, about six times the ~800 CE level,52 and it ran on charcoal until it had deforested large parts of north China, after which coal largely displaced charcoal by the end of the 11th century.53 The decision this drove: a charcoal store is a supervised, tallied commodity depot, not a shop's back room - which is exactly the relationship the Mode A magistracy sheet already draws ("charcoal and bar iron, sealed here, never owned").

Japan corroborating. The ton'ya / toiya was the wholesaler-warehouseman of the Edo economy,54 and fire-resistant stores were built with thick plastered earthen walls (corrected 2026-09-06, feature 194: the page gives "thick plastered earthen walls", not stone or mortar)82 precisely because urban timber burned. Edo's serious fires cluster in winter, when charcoal braziers were in use.55

The finding that CHANGED the design. Freshly produced charcoal absorbs oxygen from the air fast enough to heat itself to ignition; tightly packed masses of fines are the worst case56, and high-volatile charcoal can self-ignite below 150 °C (unsourced at the check of 2026-09-06). The documented handling rule is to stand freshly discharged charcoal in the open, separate from previously cooled and conditioned stock, for at least 24 hours57; charcoal exposed to air for eight days is no longer considered hazardous (unsourced at the check of 2026-09-06: not on the FAO chapter-5 page fetched). Water-spraying does not reliably prevent it.58 The first design was "sheds plus a weighing floor"; this says that is wrong, and the yard now draws an open cooling apron set apart from the covered sheds. That is the difference between a generic warehouse glyph and a charcoal yard.

The finding that justified a feature that would otherwise look like decoration. The charcoal hyō / tawara had no standard weight in the traditional Japanese system, unlike rice (a kan is 3.75 kg, but the charcoal bale itself was indeterminate).59 A commodity with no standard bale cannot be traded by count - it must be weighed at the point of sale. That is the weighing floor, and it is independently why the magistracy's hold on the trade is documentary rather than proprietary.

What determines it in reality: the commodity's physics and its measure - dryness (value), self-heating (separation and a cooling ground), and the absent standard bale (a weighing floor and a sealed tally). Not town size, and not wealth.

The 30 ft fire gap, derived. Reasoned against this project's existing separations rather than invented: ~6 ft for an attended forge against hay, 120 ft for putrefaction carried on air. The charcoal hazard is an unattended ignition in a large fuel mass, so it belongs between them, and 30 ft is about one flame-height clear of a fully-involved 10-12 ft stack - the usual rule of thumb for radiant ignition of adjacent timber. Rejected: reusing the 120 ft nuisance figure, which defends against smell over distance and would push the yard off the cart route it exists to serve.

Refining forges: smelting goes to the fuel, refining comes to the town

Evidence: attested, corroborated, liberty (the two-site divergence)

Sources: wagner-ming-iron, wagner-fining-puddling, xuxiebian-han-fining, tetsu-to-hagane-91 (the inline list below)

China first. Ming ironworks converted blast-furnace pig iron to wrought iron by fining, Chinese chao 炒, literally60 "stir-frying": an open fire under a forced blast, fuelled with charcoal, into which wood, charcoal and broken cast iron were charged and then stirred with an iron rod once semi-molten. Song Yingxing describes a rectangular hearth (tang) set a few chi from the blast-furnace outlet and a few cun lower, with workers standing on a protective wall above it, stirring with willow poles (which burned down 2-3 cun per cycle) while spreading a mineral additive (wuchaoni61). The practice runs back to the Eastern Han - the excavated smelting-and-fining site at Xuxiebian in Sichuan.62

Japan corroborating. The tatara's chief product was pig iron (zuku), and the 17th century answered it with a two-stage refining process, the ōkaji 大鍛冶.63 Kera went first to the dōba to be crushed and sorted; the low-carbon fractions then went to the ōkajiba, which turned out flat bars called wari-tetsu 割鉄. At their 18th-19th century peak the ironworks of the Chugoku Mountains produced 80% of Japan's iron this way (unsourced at the check of 2026-09-06: the Ohitayama page fetched gives only that the works used iron sand and charcoal from the Chugoku Mountains as raw materials). Two operations, two sites.

THE DISCLOSED DIVERGENCE. The Chinese arrangement puts the fining hearth a few feet from the blast-furnace outlet so the iron runs in still molten - one site. Rokugan's charcoal counties cannot do that: a kiln reduces roughly six parts wood to one of charcoal, so the kiln goes to the wood and the furnace follows the fuel into the hills, miles from the seat. So the design follows the Japanese two-site pattern - cold pig comes down and is re-melted at the town forge.

This is a resolution rather than a fudge, and the reason is economic. The Chinese single-site arrangement is viable exactly where ore, fuel and a workforce of hundreds can be concentrated at one state-supervised complex, which is what the 200/300/200 staffing describes. A county-scale trade strung along dispersed oak stands cannot concentrate the one input that matters most. Dispersed fuel forces two sites. Per the China-first rule, GM setting canon overrides the historical default where they differ - and here the canon and the Japanese practice agree.

What determines it in reality: economy and topography. Whether smelting and refining share a site is decided by whether fuel, ore and labor can be concentrated - one site where they can, two where the fuel is dispersed through hill forest, with the smelter at the fuel and the refinery in the valley settlement where the carts, the market and the tally are.

Rejected: drawing kilns or a tatara furnace on a town map at all. Canon and arithmetic both forbid it, and the Chinese 200-charcoal-producers figure shows the fuel workforce lives out at the stands, not in the seat.

Drawing a clan border

Evidence: attested

Sources: nanbu-date-mounds-enwiki, kuniezu-enwiki, mukoyama-linear-borders (the inline list below)

Linear, demarcated borders were not foreign to early modern Japan: domains were already building a territorial order with agreed boundaries and mutual exclusion, evidenced by boundary disputes, boundary markers and map-making.64 The worked example is the Nanbu-Date border mounds - a series of earth mounds marking the ~130 km boundary between Morioka (Nanbu) and Sendai (Date), re-confirmed by the shogunate in 1642; such markers were common under the Tokugawa.65 Separately, the shogunate ordered every province to produce kuniezu 国絵図, provincial land maps66 with the boundaries drawn on them (unsourced at the check of 2026-09-06: the page lists mountains, rivers, roads, landmarks, village names, rice yields and castle towns, not boundaries) - so putting the line on a map is itself the authentic act.

Pool-notes finding recorded here (feature 143, 2026-08-28): pool/magistracies/ubame-magistracy/ubame-magistracy.notes.md sizes its boundary pillars at ~1-1.5 ft shaft height (up to ~3 ft with a plinth) - a size-audit estimate, not a read figure; labeled GUESS. The notes are left as they are.

The decision. Draw the LINE, not the mound. The Mode A ubame-magistracy.notes.md had already reserved against the mound ("the physically large period border markers were earthen mounds, which is a different thing and not what is drawn") and used ~3 ft boundary pillars as location markers; the Nanbu-Date finding confirms that reservation. A mound is a structure, which everything would then have to stay clear of - the opposite of what a frontier magistracy wants, since its whole design stands its east wall ON the line with a parley room built into that wall. So M['borders'] carries a polyline with no footprint and is classified overlap-exempt.

What determines it in reality: jurisdiction, and the need to have agreed it in advance. A border exists where two authorities have negotiated and recorded it - which is why the Mode A parley room, with the line across its floor and kneeling mats on each side, is the architectural expression of the same fact this drawn line is the cartographic expression of.

Wells in crop fields: two different objects, and only one of them is ours

Evidence: attested, corroborated

Sources: irripro-jiegao-lulu, ide-japanese-experience, harie-kabata, kabu-ido-commons (the inline list below). The loess-plain rainfall and drought exposure came from Grokipedia (re-sourcing queue) - re-read from en.wikipedia Loess Plateau / Agriculture in China - leftover loess-plateau-enwiki (READ: 40-60% of rain in June-August; the 400-800 mm figure not on the page - labeled unsourced) - the Grokipedia citation is replaced

The question (GM, 2026-07-26): the overlap matrix forbids a well overlapping a dry crop plot. "Should dry plots have wells? I could see a wheat field having a well in the middle of it if that's where the water is - but I'm not a farmer."

CHINA FIRST - a well in a dry field is real, and it is a whole system. The north-China plain is dryland farming on loess with 400-800 mm of annual rain (the 400-800 mm figure unsourced at the check of 2026-09-06) concentrated in the summer monsoon,67 so crops are chronically drought-exposed. Groundwater lifting is the answer, and the gear is well documented: the jiegao (桔槔, a shadoof - pile, lever, rod and counterweight, the operator standing on beams at the well mouth) and the lulu (辘轳, a windlass on a support frame over the well).68 During the Ming and Qing the lulu became the usual groundwater-lifting device of the north, and it was upgraded rather than replaced: animal power instead of human, multiple containers at once, and wells driven to tens of meters.69 So yes - a wheat field with a well in the middle of it is an entirely real picture, and the north-China field well is a substantial installation with lifting machinery over it.

JAPAN - dry fields leaned on rain and groundwater precisely BECAUSE they lost the canal fight. Hatake was the upland/dry allocation as against suiden paddy,70 and small independent farmers "had to do with rainfall or natural underground sources," settling for millet, hemp, barley, wheat and buckwheat (unsourced at the check of 2026-09-06: this quoted phrase and crop list are not on the IDE page fetched). That was not a preference: in the later Tokugawa period new irrigation schemes for hata fields were proposed repeatedly and defeated, because downstream canal-holders' established water rights blocked any new upstream draw.71 The canals went to rice; the dry fields got the sky and the water table.

But the DOMESTIC well is a different object. The Japanese village well is a social institution before it is a water source - kabata spring-wells stood in houses and in the community and served drinking, cooking and washing, and were72 "cherished as places where water could be obtained from nature and where people gathered for a chat." Villages sited themselves along rivers where drawing water was easy (unsourced at the check of 2026-09-06). And well COUNT was governed: the kabu-ido system was a set of community rules regulating the number of wells per village, administered by the residents rather than by any authority, specifically to de-escalate conflict over groundwater.73

The decision

Our wells glyph is the DOMESTIC draw-point, so it stays off the crop. The glyph we draw is a stone curb under an open-sided roof, and the gate already demands a dwelling within ~95 px (wells_among_dwellings) - it is the idobata, the neighborhood gathering point. A lone curb in the middle of a hatake plot is therefore wrong as drawn, and the overlap matrix's GROUND x SOLID rule is right to forbid it. The reason is not that field wells never existed; it is that our well means something else.

Two further reasons the north-China field well is the wrong analog here. First, an irrigation well is identified by its lifting gear - a windlass frame or a shadoof lever standing over it - and our glyph has none, so a reader could not tell the two apart. Second, our dry plots are the hem and margin around wet-rice combs in a monsoon valley, not the rain-starved loess dryland the system was built for; the water those plots need arrives as rain and as the field's own ditches.

If we ever want one, it is a NEW FEATURE, not a relaxation. An irrigation well (s.field_well, say) would be a distinct glyph - the curb plus a windlass frame or shadoof - with its own siting rule (in the dry plot it serves, not among the houses) and its own scope (a dry upland county, not a paddy valley). That would be a genuinely good addition for a dryland district and should be built as one, rather than by letting the domestic well wander into the crop.

One honest tension recorded. The kabu-ido finding - villages capping their own well numbers to limit conflict74 - cuts mildly against this project's deliberate liberty of drawing 2-3x the historical well count (see "Wells - research + deliberate liberty"). The liberty stands, because it expresses the setting's thesis that Rokugan is unusually well-run; but it IS a liberty, and the historical direction of travel was restriction, not generosity.
