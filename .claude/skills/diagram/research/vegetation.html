Vegetation and terrain: the research behind the windbreak, commons and forest rules

The findings behind the windbreaks, groves, commons and woods on these maps, the decisions they drove, and - where no generator draws the feature yet - the rules a map follows.

The fengshui forest - real scale, and why ours is honest

Sources: forests-2020, hu-2011-fengshui-patches, afcd-ncsc-9-06, hk-herbarium-fsw, afcd-hkbio-8, yuan-liu-2009, park-2013-maeulsup, kweon-youn-2021, ma-2017-fengshui-carbon, coggins-minor-2018, fengshui-woodland-enwiki, zhongshan-fsw-2016, huangshan-daily-jiekou

  • Scale - the real numbers. How many: Fujian villages keep about two fengshui forests each (the province-wide figure the Forests 2020 paper reports from Xu, Qiu and Wang 2012)1 and two Buyi villages in Guizhou hold two parcels each,75 so the map's back grove plus water-mouth cluster is the attested count. How dense: stem density ~3,400 stems/ha (「3403 plants/ha」), basal area ~49 m²/ha in well-protected Pearl River Delta patches (one 1200 m² transect each; values the authors call consistent with well-developed evergreen broadleaved forests),2 and a basal area of 30.0-52.5 m²/ha in five Guangzhou fragments.74 How large - the back grove: patch area is highly variable. The one large measured sample readable is Hong Kong's territory-wide survey of 115 village woods with GIS-drawn boundaries - the wood 「seen today behind many villages, typically in a crescent shape」:76 median about 1 ha, mean 1.1 ha, half of them under a hectare, four in ten between one and two, one in ten at two or more, the range 0.06-5.3 ha69 (the Herbarium's summary: 「The average area of a fung shui wood is 1 hectare」, 600 m² to 6 ha).70 Across the delta in Zhongshan, Guangdong, a 2016 survey of the city's fengshui groves counts 66 of them in 66 natural villages totaling 269.85 ha - about 4.1 ha each on average, the record's own arithmetic over a total that the tail of large groves pulls up.79 The great lineage villages of Fujian are the tail: fengshui forests listed as 「A forest over three hectares」 and 「A forest over six hectares」 for two Youxi County villages, and Lingtou's over 20 ha in total, kept for over 800 years,77 a 22-hectare houlongshan grove in Nanjing County saved in 1958-59,3 and a 133 ha lineage forest at Letu.71 A TYPICAL village grove is nevertheless a small forest patch,5 and the ~1-2 ha back grove drawn here sits in the upper half of the measured band - a deliberate choice, recorded: the belt must read as a forest at two feet to the pixel. How large - the water-mouth cluster: no readable Chinese source measures one4 - the nearest analogue is the Korean village-entrance grove (동구숲), the analogue of the water-mouth grove (the Chinese grove type at the village's entrance, 「where the stream flows out of the village」 - Fujian's forests 「are mainly located near a villages’ entrances, ancestral halls, and temples」78): the most numerous type in the field survey (42 of the 100 groves surveyed), averaging 7,149 m² and standing ~730 m from the village center,72 while a 24-grove sample around Mt. Jiri averages 0.22 ha (range 0.025-0.63 ha).73 A Huangshan Daily piece on Jiekou (Shexian County, Anhui) describes that village's water-mouth grove as over a hundred mu - about 7 ha - with nearly a thousand ancient trees, the largest in East China: the one Chinese water-mouth figure found, an extreme rather than a type.80 So the ~0.1-0.5 ha water-mouth cluster is a GUESS that sits inside the analogues' spread, positioned by Coggins' topographic rule.7 How many trees: no readable source counts a Chinese grove's trees (note 6);6 the Korean groves hold 「less than 10 to more than 100」 each,73 and at the Pearl-delta density a 1-2 ha belt would carry some 3,400-6,800 stems of all sizes - so the 100-300 mature canopy trees over a dense bamboo/shrub understory drawn for the belt, and a few dozen big old trees at the water mouth, remain a GUESS bracketed by those figures. Per "relative sizes roughly honest": the back belt reads as a real small FOREST (clearly the largest vegetation feature, a wall of dozens-to-hundreds of crowns - the grove fills its polygon with overlapping dense clumps so a big belt does not read as a handful of lone trees), the water-mouth as a distinct smaller cluster, plus the bamboo/fruit scatter through the village. Hoshigaoka draws a ~1-2 ha embracing windward belt + a ~0.3 ha water-mouth + the leafy scatter.

  • Why the sizing is honest at this scale. At two feet to the pixel a ~1-2 ha grove is genuinely large relative to the ~11 ha built cluster (~10-18%), so the belt reads as the dominant feature without being cartoonishly oversized. What are the village's three groves - the windbreak belt, the water-mouth cluster and the dooryard copse?

Sources: coggins-minor-2018 (where each grove stands), forests-2020 (the entrance and water-tail groves); the GM's two corrections of 2026-07, and their ruling of the same month on a clump against a house wall; this project's measurement of the scripted hamlets

A nucleated village's greenery is not one wood but three, and each has its own place. The field survey of southern Chinese villages maps the groves at named topographic points rather than as a ring, so the map draws them the same way:81

  • The back-village belt (后龙林) - the big one: a dense crescent embracing the high windward back of the cluster, nestled against the houses and wrapping the flanks. Where the high side is the windward side, and under 背山面水 it usually is, the back grove is also the wind wall.
  • The water-mouth cluster (水口林) - a smaller, irregular stand of big old trees at the LOW entrance, where the stream and the track leave the valley, locking in the water and marking the way in.82
  • The dooryard copse - bamboo and fruit trees filling the open gaps THROUGHOUT the cluster, so the houses read as embedded in green rather than dusted with it.

Why one communal grove and not a grove per house. The per-farmstead belt is the DISPERSED settlement's answer; a nucleated cluster shelters itself and cannot fit a house-sized grove bundle onto every farm, so its windbreak is a single village-scale wood - which we take to be the dominant southern Chinese pattern, the grove protected by taboo, and the reason those stands survived as the most mature forest in the region (this rests on general reading; no source is cited).83

The village is LEAFY, not bare (the GM's correction). A southern village was not tan ground with a few tree blobs on it: houses stood among scattered bamboo and dooryard fruit - litchi, longan, persimmon, citrus, mulberry - and some villages were effectively ringed in bamboo (this rests on general reading; no source is cited).84 But the greenery is STRUCTURED. It does not line the main road and it does not shade the paddies or the drying yards: the field and downslope side stays open, which is the same crop-and-drying logic the farmstead grove follows.

Shape: organic and embracing, never a ruled wall (a second GM correction). A real grove hugs the terrain and wraps the settlement, so the belt is an irregular, terrain-following footprint filled with clumps, deepest in the middle and tapering into horns that wrap the corners; a rectangle and a square read as machine-planted. The raggedness of that crescent is a map drawing convention, drawn so the belt does not read as a planted line.

A clump stands against a house, never on it (the GM, 2026-07). Trees grow right up against a wall - directly next to is correct and common, and a few inches of canopy over the eaves is fine - but a clump drawn ON a roof, a threshing yard or a garden reads wrong, because the map loses the building it exists to show. The keep-out therefore reserves each house, yard, garden, byre and shed by its own circumscribing radius plus the clump's FULL drawn radius. A smaller reservation was tried and measured: reserving only about seven tenths of that radius fell roughly fifteen percent of a clump short, and a blob's corner clipped a small farmhouse on one map.

The belt is DEEP, and its outline stands back off the houses. Both rules come from one post-mortem: a hamlet whose belt outline ran through its northern house row had most of the clumps it could have grown thrown away as landing on a building, and sixteen farmhouses ended up sheltered by eleven crowns. So the outline is set BACK off the homesteads - the copse, not the belt, is what fills the inner gaps - and the belt is drawn 80-120 ft deep, which packs three or four rows of overlapping canopy; a 30 ft band holds one row and reads as scattered blobs rather than a wall. The belt's SIZE is the one-to-two-hectare figure of the entry above; the depth is what makes that size read as a wall.

The belt keeps out of the gardens' afternoon sun. A working belt about 10 m tall throws some 50 ft of shadow eastward in the late afternoon of the shoulder month, so no windbreak clump stands within 50 ft west or southwest of a yard's or a bed's west edge, and the belt's near face moves out by that lane wherever the wind puts the belt west of the houses. The geometry, and the 75 ft lane that was priced and declined with it, are in the homestead research. The lane binds the WINDBREAK's clumps only: the copse is the dooryard's persimmon and bamboo, which the record puts in the yard.

The rule the map follows: a nucleated hamlet or village draws three groves and no more - a windbreak belt on the windward, high side of the house cluster, embracing it and wrapping its flanks; a smaller water-mouth cluster at the low entrance where the stream or track leaves; and a loose copse of bamboo and fruit trees in the gaps between the houses. The field side, the drying yards and the main road stay open, and no grove clump stands on a house, a yard, a garden or a crop.

The rule the map follows: the windbreak belt is drawn 80-120 ft deep and its footprint is set back clear of the front rank of houses, so the clumps inside it are not thrown away for landing on roofs; the gaps left between the houses are the copse's to fill. A belt shallower than about 30 ft reads as a row of blobs and is not drawn.

Does a shelter belt wrap the settlement? No - it stands on one or two windward sides

Sources: coggins-minor-2018 (the separate patches at named topographic points), yashikirin-jawiki (the homestead grove on the north and west sides), irie-2020-igune (the grove formed on the north or the west and lacking the south or the east, and kept that way for centuries), sendai-igune-hozen (the city's encircling definition of the word), umca-windbreak-manual (what a gap does to the wind, and how a crossing that cannot be avoided is cut); the GM's rulings of 2026-08-29. No readable source was found for what the grove's open flank is used for, or for the Izumo hedge's full circuit before Meiji

The ruling that opened the question (the GM, 2026-08-29): "The actual ruling that I have on whether or not it is okay for a windbreak forest to have gaps is that we should just do whatever was historically true. If we believe that windbreak forests would have had such gaps based on our historical findings, then we can give them gaps. Otherwise, we should not have any gaps." The research pass that followed changed the shape of the rule rather than settling a number.

The question had been posed wrongly. It asked whether a belt is an unbroken wall or a wall with holes, and neither record describes a single continuous perimeter in the first place. In China, at village scale, the grove is a SYSTEM OF SEPARATE PATCHES: the survey maps them at named topographic points - the back or master-mountain grove, the water-head grove, the water-mouth grove downstream, and groves at the mountain gaps where the winds are strongest - with the paddies lying in the open ground between them, and that open ground is not narrow.85 In Japan, at farmstead scale, the grove is built on ONE OR TWO SIDES by design: the northeastern igune stands on the homestead's north and west, the winter-monsoon-facing sides,86 and a 2020 study of the igune states the rule with its reason: because the winter winds of the Sendai plain are mainly north-northwesterly and strong, the grove is formed on the north or the west side of the homestead plot and often LACKS the south or the east side.87 Groves built that way have been kept as windbreaks for several hundred years.95 That the open flank is the one the entrance, the garden and the yard take is on no page read.97 A city conservation page defines the WORD the other way, as trees planted so as to encircle the homestead,96 but that is a gloss on the term rather than a description of built form: the scholarly record gives the two-sided grove as the long-standing shape and the ring as the loose modern reading, which is the way round a pre-Meiji setting wants.

So the distinction that matters is between an ABSENT FLANK and a HOLE IN A PLANTED RUN, and only the second is a defect. A belt that does not wrap the settlement is correct and historical. A bare 40 ft run in the middle of the side the belt DOES occupy is attested nowhere, and shelter planting holds it to be actively harmful: a gap funnels the wind, and downwind of it the air often moves FASTER than it does over the open field.88 The attested remedy for a crossing that cannot be avoided is geometric rather than a screen across the opening: the opening is cut at an ANGLE to the prevailing wind, and a lane through a belt is put 100 to 500 ft from the belt's END rather than through the middle of its run.93 The physics of air through a constriction holds in any century; the angled crossing is a modern extension service's design rule, borrowed here because nothing read says how a Chinese or Japanese planter cut an access through a belt.

What that makes of the 30 ft threshold: a map drawing convention, not a finding. No source reached - Chinese or Japanese, historical or agronomic - gives a WIDTH for an opening in a shelter belt, neither for the open flank, nor for an access gate, nor for the water-mouth passage the stream and the road already run through. The 30 ft bar at which a bare run across the wind counts as a hole is ours, and is labeled ours. What IS sourced is the direction of the rule: within the planted run, close the holes.

A knob the record handed us, and the ruling that declined it. Both forms are reported of the Japanese homestead grove: the Izumo tsuijimatsu enclosed the entire circumference of the house before Meiji and was later reduced to a hook covering only the north and west sides as flood risk fell and building styles changed (this rests on general reading; no source is cited).89 Two supportable answers ordinarily become a per-settlement knob rather than a choice. The GM ruled for the hook and against the knob (2026-08-29): "I think that having a north and west hook is what we want because that is where things ended up, and Rokugan has been around for a long time. So I think they would have ended up in the same place and for the same reason. It also communicates that wind tends to blow from the northwest to the southeast, which is an interesting geographical fact about China and Japan, which we have decided is also true for Rokugan in most places when the local geography does not override the regional geography."

Two reasons, and the second is the one worth keeping. The first is that a long-settled country converges on the same answer for the same reason, so the END STATE is the one to draw. The second is that the belt's shape is communicative: a reader who sees belts on the north and west of most settlements is being told a real fact about the regional wind, and a per-settlement full-ring roll would scramble that signal for variety's sake. This is the rare case where two supportable answers are answered with a ruling instead of a knob - not because the second form is unsupported, but because the first carries information the map is trying to convey. The full-ring form stays recorded here and undrawn.

Nothing had to change for the ruling, and that was checked rather than assumed. The regional-against-local rule the GM describes is already how the maps are built - northwest by default, because at the scale of a province that is the right answer, while any one map's windward quarter is its upslope bearing, cold air draining downhill making the high side and the windward side the same fact. And the belts already draw as hooks. Measured over the scripted hamlets, the arc each belt subtends around its own cluster: 102 degrees, 146, 97, 136 and 129 - one side or two sides apiece, none past 200 degrees, none wrapping its settlement.

The rule the map follows: a shelter belt stands on the windward one or two sides of the settlement and is never carried round it. Its absent flanks are not gaps and need no explanation. Within the side it does occupy the planting is continuous: a bare run wider than about 30 ft measured across the wind is a hole and is filled, and where a lane or a track must cross the belt the planting resumes on both sides of the crossing rather than stopping for it.

Why does the belt run off the edge of the sheet, and why is the bare stretch there not a hole?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-08-29; this project's measurements of the scripted hamlets

A cropped map is read as a window, not as a world. The GM's ruling (2026-08-29): "I do not mind if a lot of the windbreak forest is off the edge of the map. So I am happy to make a ruling that as long as it is clear from the map and where the map is cropped, that there is or could be more windbreak forest that we are not seeing, then that is fine. That is completely in line with our map cropping conventions. And even though it sounds as if this might look a little different in situations in which there is discontinuity in the clumping of trees of a windbreak forest - that is still fine." A reader already understands that a feature meeting the frame continues past it; that is the convention every road, field and watercourse on these sheets relies on. So a belt whose planting runs off the sheet is sound and needs no repair, and a bare stretch beyond the frame is not a hole in anything the reader is shown.

Where the map is judged for holes, it is judged only where a reader can look. The belt's recorded footprint runs off the page as freely as its clumps do. Measured on the two maps that a widened reading first failed: on one, the footprint spans 747 ft along its own across-wind axis while the frame holds only the middle 537 ft of that, and the planting covers 596 ft that overruns the visible stretch at both ends; on the other, footprint 917 ft, frame 741 ft, planting 787 ft. On both, the planting covers every column a reader can see. The whole apparent gap was off-page, so the scan for holes is clipped to the framed view - the same rule the planting already follows, where a clump whose crown merely crosses the frame is kept and one with no visible ink is waste.

The other repair was tried first and measured. Extending the PLANTING out to the ends of the belt's footprint, so the end stretches were offered seats like any interior gap, bought one clump on one map, at the page edge. It was reverted, because a belt that appears to stop short is usually a belt whose short end is off the page: measure that before reaching for the planting.

Why the test is asymmetric on purpose, and what that costs. The window is clipped to the page; the PLANTING is not, so an off-page clump still fills an on-page column. An acceptance review read that as one-sided, which is a fair reading, so the symmetric form was built and rolled: credit a clump only where its crown reaches the view. It fails one hamlet at a 60 ft bare run on the frame's bottom edge, and the ground underneath settles it - that visible ground is a homestead. One farmhouse, two threshing yards, two kitchen gardens and a persimmon stand within 90 ft of it, and a yard's southern sun corridor and a garden's eastern one are keep-outs the belt's seating is right to respect. The symmetric form therefore demands canopy exactly where the generator is correct to refuse it. The reader loses nothing: the canopy in that gap is the copse, which is what a dooryard grove is for.

The limitation that was accepted, and the alternative that was priced. The guarantee is weaker than it looks - delete that hamlet's copse and the test still passes. Closing it would need the test to know the seating's own keep-outs, which is a second copy of the generator living inside the gate. Declined for one column at one page edge; it is reopened if a map ever ships a hole a reader can actually see.

What is still open. The second half of the 2026-08-29 ruling - "even though it sounds as if this might look a little different in situations in which there is discontinuity in the clumping of trees of a windbreak forest, that is still fine" - read as covering ON-PAGE holes would relax the rule exactly where it has earned its keep: it exists because a wellhead seated inside a belt suppressed the clumps around it and took a 40 ft band from six clumps to one, and because a lane web crossed a belt and removed some 45 ft of wall. Both of those are discontinuity in the clumping. The ruling and the rule's purpose meet head-on there, so the rule stands as written until the GM says otherwise. A measurement made at the same time is worth keeping beside it: one hamlet's three flagged bare runs were believed to come from its belt running off the sheet, and they do not - two lie wholly inside the drawn view and the third partly, all of them bare runs of 40-50 ft in belt the reader can see.

The rule the map follows: a large area feature near the frame - a shelter belt, a grazing ground, a wood, a forest - CROSSES the frame rather than sitting wholly inside it, so the reader sees that it continues. Nothing beyond the frame is drawn, and nothing beyond the frame is counted against the map: a belt is asked for unbroken planting only across the stretch a reader can see.

Why is the hillside past the grove open scrub rather than more forest?

Sources: none cited - the claim below rests on general reading of southern China's deforestation and of forest-rehabilitation writing, neither of which the record names by work

The grove is green because it is protected, and the hills around it are not. Southern China's hills were progressively stripped for firewood and timber over roughly a thousand years, so past the village's protected band the ground is cut over and open: a monotonous succession of hills either deforested or carrying thin open stands of masson pine and China fir, with severe erosion on the worst of them (this rests on general reading; no source is cited).90 The taboo on cutting the grove is exactly WHY it is lush while the slope behind it is bare, so drawing more forest out there would undo the point of the grove. How far that stripping went is a guess, pending a source; the direction of it - protected band, degraded hinterland - is what the map draws.

The land runs in an order, and the commons sits outside the grove. The sequence away from the houses is village, then back grove, then fuel-and-fodder commons, then whatever lies past the map - so the commons stands on the windward, high side and FURTHER out than the belt, never between the belt and the houses. That ordering is the fuel commons' alone: rough hill grazing, pasture and the managed woodland patches are general hinterland land and stand wherever the ground is marginal.

How it is drawn, and one form that failed. The commons is deliberately OPEN and SPARSE - coarse grass tufts, low brush and a few scraggly pines - so that it reads apart from the dense dark closed-canopy grove at a glance. It carries no solid fill and no hard outline: an early version drawn as a filled quad read as a literal rhombus lying on the hillside. Instead the scatter feathers out to nothing at its margin, so the ground has no boundary at all, and the band is drawn as a ragged strip paralleling the grove's back at a jittered width and UNDER the grove, so the canopy laps its inner edge and wood and scrub abut with no bare seam between them. The open degraded cover is the same guess as above, and the feathered edge and the absent outline are map drawing conventions.

The rule the map follows: the fuel-and-fodder commons is drawn beyond the windbreak on the high, windward side - village, grove, commons, off-map - and never between the grove and the houses. Rough grazing, pasture and woodland patches are not held to that order and clothe any dry marginal ground. No commons of any kind is drawn on the wet crop, and the dry margins are filled as one continuous band around the settlement rather than as isolated corner patches, because in a valley every edge of the map is marginal land of the same sequence.

Forest density and crown size

Sources: searched 2026-08-28: MDPI Forests 8(10):397 (a Castanopsis stand going 666 -> 404 stems/ha over 28 years) and the Kobe satoyama restoration paper have no readable page - the 500-800 stems/ha band and the 5-8 m crowns are a GUESS taken from their abstracts, which no readable page supports; a different Castanopsis study at 32-132 stems/ha (Indonesian, tourism-pressed) is not the same stand type

  • Density and crown size - the numbers and the why. A closed premodern hill wood (the mixed broadleaf/conifer cover of a settled valley's back slope, cut over for fuel and timber on a rotation) carries roughly 500-800 canopy stems per hectare (unsourced). 1 ha = 107,639 sq ft, so ~600 stems/ha is one canopy tree per ~180 sq ft: a mean spacing near 13 ft (unsourced). Canopy crowns in such a stand run ~5-8 m across (16-26 ft) (unsourced) with occasional wider emergents, so a mean crown radius of 8.5 ft is drawn, jittered 0.75-1.4x. Crowns of ~17 ft mean diameter on 13 ft centers OVERLAP, and that is the point - closure is what makes a wood a wood, so the packed look is honest rather than decorative. Same finding as the mulberry rows: at a to-scale grain, drawing real planted density honestly IS a dense mass of crowns, not sparse symbols spaced for the eye. Nothing is inflated for legibility - at one foot per pixel a crown is drawn 12-24 pixels across and it shrinks with the map at coarser grains, exactly like the buildings.

No canopy tree stands under another's crown

Sources: the GM's observation (2026-08) and the density entry above (the stems/ha band is a GUESS - no readable page supports it; see "Forest density and crown size"); the stand structure itself is not separately asserted

The GM, on the interactive map's highlight: "practically every tree might have a smaller tree underneath it ... I understand that real life trees can, in fact, overlap with each other ... but I don't see a single tree which is entirely subsumed within the branch structure of a different tree."

The rule. A crown is drawn only if its center is outside every crown already on the map AND no drawn crown's center lies inside it; two crowns closer than the sum of their radii may still overlap at the edge, because neighboring canopies do interlace. What is removed is the tree drawn wholly under a neighbor - in a real stand a suppressed understory stem, not part of the canopy layer the map draws. Measured after: Inashiro 787 crowns, 0 subsumed, 737 (94%) still touching a neighbor - the stand reads as closed canopy at ~one crown per 13 ft grid cell, which is the density entry's own figure: a canopy has one tree per crown, at the density that entry gives.

The crop margin - scrub stands 6 ft off every field edge

Sources: aze-standard, nougyoudoboku-keihan (the bund), PMC 7538448 via the paddy-setback entry on the homestead research (levees mowed periodically).15 No readable page was found for the Lake Biwa levee-flora study or for "cut several times a season" (the search returned only Biwa aquatic-weed work)

  • What was found. A paddy levee (keihan/aze; Chinese tian'geng) is a narrow earthen ridge - roughly 1-2 ft wide and under a foot tall,1110 up to ~3 ft (unsourced) where it doubles as a footpath (azemichi12). Levee structure studies (e.g. the Lake Biwa paddy-levee flora work) describe a flat trodden part plus a grassed face, and the levee grass was CUT several times a season1413 - fodder, thatch, green manure (unsourced) - as was the strip immediately beside any crop. Constant cutting is why woody scrub could not establish within about a scythe's swath (~1-2 m) of a field edge; the same ~1 m clean strip separating crop from boundary vegetation shows up as standing practice in traditional field-margin management (unsourced). The 6 m+ "conservation headlands" of modern European agri-environment schemes are a MODERN wildlife intervention, not the historical norm (unsourced), and East Asian land hunger kept margins at the narrow end of the range (unsourced).
  • The decision it drove. A crop margin of 6 ft - bund plus one cut swath (~1.8 m total). Scrub of every kind stands off every paddy and dry plot by this margin, and the tall glyphs (the scraggly pines and the woodland crowns) stand their own drawn reach back as well, so nothing leans over a crop.
  • Disclosed departures. (1) Grass-tuft blade TIPS may lean up to a few real feet past the margin line at coarse tiers - accepted deliberately: grass overhanging a bund is real, and the overhang is sub-pixel-to-invisible at render. (2) The reed marsh gets NO margin at all - wet ground genuinely starts at the polder dike, so reeds abutting a field's low bund is honest.

Scrub stays off open water - including the comb laterals' drawn width

Sources: not applicable - a rendering rule; nothing physical asserted

  • Vegetation never draws over open water, and every drawn watercourse counts at its drawn (tapering) width - the supply laterals run up to ~14 ft wide - so no grass tuft stands on a head race or crowds its banks.

The cut bank - scrub stands 6 ft off every irrigation channel's drawn edge

Sources: reasoning from the crop-margin entry (pmc7538448-levee: levees mown; bund upkeep) applied to the channel bank; no source states a bank margin in feet - the 6 ft is the crop margin's figure reused

  • What prompted it. The water's own edge and the 6 ft crop margin each kept scrub off their side, which left the 10-16 ft berm strips between the supply channels and the dry hem plots open to tufts mid-strip. The GM read the strips as scrub crowding the channels and ruled that the bank takes a margin too.
  • The decision. Scrub stands 6 real feet off the drawn water edge of every irrigation course. The reasoning is the crop margin's, applied to the bank: a supply channel's bank is maintained ground - walked for sluice operation and bund upkeep,16 its grass scythed for fodder on the same rotation as the field margins (unsourced)17 - so woody scrub never establishes within a swath of the water. 6 ft = one scythe swath, the same figure as the crop margin. Between them the crop margin and the bank margin close any berm strip up to ~12 ft of bare ground, which covers every hem berm the comb builds (Inashiro's run 3-9 ft).
  • Deliberate exclusions. (1) STREAMS take no margin - a natural brook bank is vegetated to the water's edge, and a sterile halo along a brook would be wrong; only the engineered courses are maintained ground. (2) The reed marsh keeps its no-margin rule from the crop-margin entry - reeds ARE the water fringe. (3) Grass-tuft blade TIPS keep their few-feet lean allowance, exactly as at crop edges.

The marsh margin: reed -> sedge/grass -> dry ground; woody at a reed edge is alder or willow, never pine - ACCURATE

Sources: mlit-vegetation-classes, hotes-wetland-diversity, lou-2016-floodplain-zones, gymnosperm-densiflora, packer-2017-phragmites, haneishi-2011-kushiro-alder (what the alder takes in a drying mire, and the standing water that holds it off); the managed-margin reasoning is marked unsourced in the entry

Grass alone grades into the reeds, and no pine or brush stands in the marsh; the managed-margin reasoning below is unsourced, and is marked as such where it is given.

What the record says. The textbook hydrosere - the zonation from open water to dry land that every lowland wetland shows in space - runs open water -> littoral -> reed swamp (Phragmites, ヨシ) -> sedge / wet meadow (Carex, Calamagrostis, スゲ) -> alluvial / swamp WOODLAND -> dry ground (Packer et al. 2017, Biological Flora of Phragmites australis).65 In Japan the woody stage is alder (Alnus japonica, ハンノキ林) and willow (タチヤナギ群落): the Kushiro Mire, mainly reed, is being invaded by alder as it dries - reed held about 87% of the mire in 1977 and alder about 45% by 200422 - and what resists the alder is STANDING water rather than a fluctuating table: the reed holds the points that are inundated the whole time, alder can hardly establish itself where the water stands 0.4 m or more above the ground, and it is around the rivers and the old channels, where the groundwater lies below the surface and swings widely, that it spreads94 (Haneishi, Nakatsugawa and Kudo 2011, on groundwater and the spread of the alder in the Kushiro Mire); a northern-Japan wetland classified into reed swamp, Carex lyngbyei marsh, and reed/Calamagrostis grassland with Spiraea shrubs23 (Otanoshike, Ecological Research 2004); the national river-vegetation classification names ヨシ群落, マコモ群落 and タチヤナギ群落 as the low-wetland communities24 and ハンノキ林 as the eutrophic-mire woodland2566 (MLIT river-environment vegetation classes; Hotes, wetland ecosystem diversity). Northeast-China floodplains show the same ladder: emergent Carex marsh streamside, meadow marsh at intermediate flooding, Calamagrostis wet meadow on the terrace26 (PLOS One 2016). Red pine (Pinus densiflora, アカマツ) - the scraggly pine our scrub draws - is a tree of ridges, rocky slopes and well-drained poor soil, drought-tolerant,28 absent from wet lowland (unsourced)27 (Gymnosperm Database; Mt. Takao museum).

The decision it drives. (1) Pines and dry brush inside the reeds: unsupported in every source - the hard exclusion stands as ACCURATE. (2) Grass grading into the reeds over the margin: ACCURATE - it is the sedge / wet-meadow zone of the hydrosere, drawn with the commons' blade glyph. (3) Woody plants DO stand at a reed margin in the natural state - but they are alder and willow, a wet woodland this map does not draw. Two supportable forms, so a KNOB, not a choice: a managed toe (reeds and sedge cut for thatch and fodder, the margin kept open - the form drawn here) versus an alder-willow carr along the toe (a distinct wet-woodland glyph, deliberately unlike the dry scrub and the fengshui grove). Unsourced, and labeled so: that Edo-period reed beds were mown (ヨシ刈り, 茅場) and that mowing is what kept the margin open - plausible, searched, not found.

The form the edge takes, and the two forms that failed on the way to it (the GM, 2026-08-26: "the marshland is not supposed to overlap with the scrubland rendering"). Dry-ground scrub - brush discs, blades, pines - had been scattering through the reeds in the same pattern as outside them; one hamlet carried 3,370 scrub bases inside its marshes, and four afterward, all of them on the band's own edge. The first repair cut the scatter dead at the marsh's boundary, which exposed a ruled line and a bare strip along the straight western edge of the toe, because the reeds themselves feather inset from that boundary. The second let every kind of scrub thin INTO the marsh across that feather; it closed the line, and left a seam of pines standing in the bog along some 1,600 ft of the northern edge, which the GM read as the overlap still being there: "it looks like it is still overlapping!" The form that holds is the third: grass alone grades into the reeds across the fade, a wild edge with no line in it, while brush and pines stop dead at the marsh's edge - because a bog's margin is sedge into reed, and woody cover stands on the dry ground above it. The reverse rule is not needed: the marsh is drawn after the scrub and its own reeds already keep off crops, water and buildings.

The rule the map follows: no dry-ground scrub is drawn inside a marsh. Grass thins into the reed edge and is gone beyond it; brush and pines stop at the marsh's edge, and every patch of rough ground a map lays down after the marsh keeps out of it the same way.

Does scrub stand under a village wood? No - the floor was worked clear; grass fringes the edge

Sources: satoyama-enwiki, takeuchi-2010-satoyama (the coppice as the source of fuel, fertilizer and fodder), jiao-2019-satoyama (litter gathered for the paddies, the oaks cut on a cycle, sunlight on the floor), uehara-2009-agris, waldrand-dewiki, pmc7898781-fukugi; no publicly readable source for the EUNIS mantle-and-fringe wording or the 2025 Okinawa forest-edge study (searched 2026-08-28: the E5.2 factsheet page was gone; the Forests article refused the request)

The GM: "Should scrubland overlap with forests? It seems like it shouldn't. Like, visually, it looks weird. but maybe what is being represented is more accurate than what I am imagining." It was not more accurate. Two findings, one from land use and one from vegetation structure:

  • A satoyama wood's floor was kept clear, on purpose. From Wikipedia "Satoyama": "During the Edo era, young and fallen leaves were gathered from community forests to use as fertilizer in wet rice paddy fields";29 "succession to dense and dark laurel forest is prevented by farmers that cut down these trees for firewood and charcoal every 15 to 20 years";30 satoyama declined with "the drastic shift ... from charcoal and firewood to oil and the change from compost to chemical fertilizer".31 The coppice was the village's source of fuel, fertilizer and fodder: forest litter (ochiba) and compost were gathered from it for the paddies,32 its deciduous oaks - konara and kunugi (Quercus serrata and Quercus acutissima) - were cut on a cycle for firewood and charcoal, every 20 to 40 years by one review against the 15 to 20 above,33 and the cutting and the litter-gathering together let sunlight reach the floor - so the floor's cover was herbs and flowers under a managed canopy, not brush.34 From Uehara et al. 2009, AGRIS: a managed coppice stand held 42 plant species against 23 in a stand left uncared for35 50 years,67 the neglected one poorer in herbs36 - the abandoned wood is the different one. So brush and young pine under the crowns of an inhabited village's grove is the form the read record does not show; the floor is litter-raked ground under a canopy cut every 15-20 years.
  • The edge is a gradient, and it is grass. From German Wikipedia "Waldrand": 「An ideal, fully grown forest edge is divided, from the outside inward, into a herb fringe (Krautsaum), a shrub belt (Strauchgürtel) and a forest mantle (Waldmantel)」 (translated from the German by this project; original: 「Ein idealer, ausgewachsener Waldrand gliedert sich von außen nach innen in Krautsaum, Strauchgürtel und Waldmantel」)37 - an ideal mature forest edge runs, outside to inside, herb fringe, shrub belt, forest mantle (the EUNIS habitat factsheet's own wording for the outer two layers has no publicly readable source - searched 2026-08-28: the E5.2 factsheet page was gone). On a worked wood the shrub belt is among what gets cut - the review above lists shrub cutting beside litter removal among a coppice's management techniques; the grass fringe is what remains, and it thins out under the first crowns.

Rule: brush and pines are kept out of every village grove; grass blades thin into it over the same fade the marsh edge uses and are gone beyond it; the crowns are the grove. Inashiro draws 1,263 grass blades, all within the fringe, and no brush or pines inside the belt. The coppice patches take the same rule - the coppice IS the worked wood, and the record above never supported treating the windbreak alone - so every woodland commons keeps scrub out too. The fade itself is a map drawing convention shared with the marsh - one ramp for every soft edge, so two edges never read differently for no reason. An abandoned-coppice shrub layer is a real second form for a DESERTED settlement; that an inhabited one never shows it is our inference from the managed-against-uncared contrast above, not a finding.

The homestead windbreak is the same kind of managed wood, from "Distribution and utilization of homestead windbreak Fukugi trees", PMC 7898781: "Homestead windbreaks are managed as part of a residence and are strips of trees planted and maintained to alter wind flow and microclimate38 ... designed based on Feng Shui concepts in the Ryukyu Kingdom, around 300 years ago". The three-layer terms above are the German record's; English Wikipedia "Woodland edge" does not carry them.39

How is a coppice lot bounded? By ridge, stream and path - never by a page axis

Sources: ijc-yamaguni, satoyama-enwiki, kichijoji-enwiki; the "bounded by ridge, stream and path" sentence is unsourced, of unknown provenance, and marked so in the entry; Totman The Green Archipelago and the Indiana DLC paper not read

The GM: "those coppice Patches. basically it looked like little squares ... I want to make sure that that is intentional and based on research rather than just happenstance because when we decided to draw patches of trees, we just kind of unthinkingly drew a square." It was happenstance.

What the record says. The village woods of the period were iriai commons - customary common-property land held by the village and governed by its own rules on who might cut, when, and how much40 (the usufruct the GM describes): firewood, forage and grass were the products (unsourced), coppiced on a cycle of every 15 to 20 years.68 From IJC, the Yamaguni district study: "each of the 11 villages in Yamaguni district has its own unique institutions for managing its customary common property forests",41 held by residents' associations with membership limited to qualifying residents (unsourced) - the management was a matter of RULES over a wood. UNSOURCED: the sentence that iriai boundaries were "described by ridge, stream and path",42 is NOT in that article and no source for it has been found; it stands as a claim of unknown provenance. What the read record does give: nothing in it describes a coppice lot laid out as a surveyed rectangle, and the coppice sits on the slope break above the paddy (the satoyama entries).43 So a managed wood is not evidence for a square - the GM's instinct was right.

The one rectilinear form, and why it is not drawn here. The shinden villages of the dry Musashino upland (Kichijōji, settled by migrants after the 1657 Edo fire)44 were laid out as long strip holdings45 - a household's house, dry fields and fuel wood (zokibayashi) in one strip (unsourced),46 some over 1,000 m long - so THERE the wood is a strip with straight sides. That is a settlement FORM (a planned dry-upland strip village with no paddy at all), not a shape knob for a hillside coppice: it would change the houses, the fields and the lanes together. A paddy-fan hamlet like Inashiro draws the hillside form.

Rule: a coppice lot is drawn as a ring whose edge wanders smoothly - because a wood's edge wanders rather than serrates - within the size the record allows. The wandering edge itself is a map drawing convention, and the strip form is recorded as a second form and not drawn here.

The Wikipedia "Kichijōji" page gives the 1000 m strip grants and the dry fields; a founding year and the 1664 survey are not in it.47

Bamboo: how common, where it stood, and how to show it

Sources: satoyama-enwiki, phyllostachys-enwiki, pmc5723622-bamboo-range, bamboo-growers-hardiness, tsuijimatsu; the Shirakawa farmstead grove (Kids Web Japan) has no readable page (searched 2026-08-27)

The GM: "is there supposed to be bamboo on the reference hamlet? Why or why not? how common was it for there to be bamboo in settlements such as this?" - and the rendering problem behind it: a culm is a few inches across, so at 1 px = 1 ft a bamboo stand has nothing to draw at true scale.

How common: ubiquitous below the frost line. From Wikipedia "Satoyama": satoyama "contains a mosaic of mixed forests, rice paddy fields, dry rice fields, grasslands, streams, ponds, and reservoirs for irrigation",48 and by the 1960s were used as "rice fields, plowed fields, shifting cultivation, grasslands, thatch fields, secondary forests for fuel, and giant bamboo forests".49 From Wikipedia "Phyllostachys bambusoides": madake's "long internodes and equally long fibres ... make it ideal for traditional basket-weaving and the production of fans";50 its sheaths wrapped food and covered geta;18 it made shakuhachi.19 From PMC 5723622, the moso/madake range study: bamboo "distribution remained south of 41°N, in areas that included plains and hilly regions of central Honshu, as well as the coasts of northern Honshu" (the paper's warming-scenario projection, not its present-day survey result);51 stands "were not found at study sites where ... mean annual minimum temperature was below -16.8°C",52 and "moso and madake bamboo distribution in northern Japan depends primarily on temperature",53 agreeing with prior work "that moso bamboo cannot withstand temperatures around or below -18 to -20°C".54 From the growers: madake "Minimum Temp: 5°F (-15°C), Hardiness Zone: 7"20 (completebamboo.com), with other readable growers giving -18 to -23 C55 - a spread, not a number; and no read source says new SHOOTS take frost, only that "colder weather will likely cause frost damage to leaves and canes"21 (bambubatu), so the record says only "frost-tender at the margin". Unread, and carrying no weight here: that a typical Edo farmstead kept "a grove where they could harvest bamboo" (Kids Web Japan - no readable page, searched 2026-08-27; no corroborating snippet found); the broader list of uses (Highlighting Japan). So: a lowland paddy hamlet in a temperate province has bamboo as a matter of course, and a cold upland one may have none - that half is sourced. THAT is the axis of variance the project wants: two attested forms, a knob per settlement.

Where it stood. Two places, both already in this record: (1) the homestead's own N/W strip - the "shady, always damp" side of the yashiki given to the kitchen drain and service sheds - is the bamboo strip (the homestead research); and the dooryard's "persimmon and bamboo" stand IN the sunlit yard; (2) the take-yabu (bamboo thicket) as its own stand at the village edge, harvested like a coppice. Bamboo was NOT scattered one culm at a time through a mixed wood: a stand is a clonal thicket, dense and monospecific, with a hard edge.

Why bamboo cannot be drawn as trees. Bamboo mixed at 20% through a cedar windbreak is not how bamboo grows (a stand, not a seasoning), and a glyph the size of one culm - about 6 ft across at true scale - reads as nothing at fit zoom, pale dots in a dark belt: a map drawn that way has bamboo and no reader can tell.

How to show it. Japan's own topographic maps solved exactly this: the legend of the GSI (the Geospatial Information Authority of Japan, the national mapping agency) has a distinct bamboo-grove symbol (竹林), separate from the broadleaf and conifer symbols,56 because a reader of a map must be able to tell the three apart at map scale.

As built (the GM, 2026-08-27: "make that change in the manner that you had previously proposed"). A bamboo stand is drawn as a polygon whose position and extent are to scale, marked with paired culm strokes and a leafy fork in pale yellow-green; bamboo is not mixed into the windbreak or the dooryard copse; whether a hamlet has bamboo at all, and where, varies by settlement - at the cluster's shady side (drawn as its NORTH, the side the house shades; the record's "N/W strip" read that way), as a thicket at the field margin's shady end, or both; a stand narrower than 14 ft on its short axis is not drawn, and no stand overlaps a paddy. The stand glyph is a map drawing convention - the marks are symbolic, the extent is not - and so are the stand sizes and the mark pitch; "north = the shady side" is a reading of the record's "N/W strip", recorded as a reading.

Household bamboo, per farmstead - which side, and how common (the GM's question, 2026-08-27). The GM asked whether a farmstead's own bamboo always stood to the north-west and whether every farmstead had one.

  • WHERE (ja.wikipedia "屋敷林"): on the Tonami plain 「on the south side there were storehouses and barns, and plants bearing fruit, such as figs, grapes and persimmons, and bamboo were planted」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「南側には蔵や納屋などがあり、無花果や葡萄、柿などの果実がなる植物や竹などが植えられていた」)57 - storehouses and barns on the SOUTH side, and there the fruit trees and the bamboo; and separately, 「at houses near rivers and in flood-prone areas, alder and root-spreading bamboo are planted as flood protection to keep the soil from washing away」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「河川の近い家や水害の多い地域では、防水用としてハンノキや、根を張る竹を植えて土壌の流出を防いでいる」) - by rivers and in flood-prone ground, bamboo (and alder) planted for its roots. From tsuijimatsu.com: the kainyo 「is centered on cedar, with ate (asunaro), zelkova, oaks, bamboo, persimmon, chestnut and others besides」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「はスギが中心で、ほかにアテ（アスナロ）・ケヤキ・カシ類・竹・柿・栗などで」)58 - cedar central, bamboo among the secondary species. A GUESS as far as the table goes: the grove as a whole faces the local harmful wind - north and west (Isawa, Iwate; Hikawa, Shimane), west (Iide, Yamagata), south and west (Tonami) - a regional table seen only in a search snippet, on no page that could be read.59 Not confirmed: the "N/W strip ... kitchen drain" sentence (the 1996 Tonami model homestead, the homestead research) - no readable source carries it. So the SIDE is two-formed at least (with the storehouses; at the wet edge; on the wind side) and is ROLLED per farmstead, weighted toward the back of the house and the shed's side (two supportable forms become a knob, here a per-house roll).
  • HOW COMMON (Visit Toyama): on the Tonami plain "each house has been surrounded by homestead woodland60 ... over 7,000 houses", with "cedar, Japanese zelkova, and bamboo ... used as materials for building new houses as well as materials for various everyday tools";61 and tsuijimatsu.com: 「bamboo was an important material for everyday life」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「竹は日常生活の資材として重要なものでした」).62 Not found: any share of farmsteads keeping bamboo, and any source distinguishing household bamboo from the communal take-yabu by number. So the presence rate is a GUESS - 0.6, "one of several secondary species" read as common but not universal, set like the shed's - labeled as such.
  • As built: a 22 x 16 ft strip stands at a farmstead that has one, most often behind the house or beside the shed; on Inashiro 3 of 15 farmsteads keep one.

On the GSI legend (ridgelineimages.com "Reading GSI Topographic Maps") the bamboo forest (竹林) symbol is listed as its own category beside broad-leaved (広葉樹林) and coniferous (針葉樹林) forest, and distinct from the bamboo-grass (笹地, sasa) symbol63.

How does a flat map show that the ground slopes?

Sources: none cited for the farming claims below - they rest on general reading and are marked in the text; the rejection of shaded relief is this project's own drawing decision

These maps are deliberately not topographic. There are no contour lines and no spot heights on them, and there is no hillshade. Slope is shown by its CONSEQUENCES instead, three of which stack on a rice village:

  • Pictorial hills. High ground is drawn as a shaded mound - tiers, hachures and a wooded cap - which is the old estate-map convention: a picture of a hill rather than survey lines.
  • The farming shows the slope. Water enters high and leaves low. On a genuinely steep slope the paddies become terraces, long narrow curved strips following the contour with a riser between them; on a gently tilted valley floor there are no visible terraces at all, only a flat patchwork whose level steps are imperceptible, so a gently tilted settlement is drawn without them or it misreads as mountainous (this rests on general reading; no source is cited).91
  • The water's direction is the honest gentle-slope cue. The source and the pond sit on the high side, the distributary ditches fan water in from there, and a drainage ditch leaves the low corner and runs off the map. Wet rice needs water in AND water out, so a drainage outlet is an expected feature in its own right, and it doubles as the downhill cue (this rests on general reading; no source is cited).92

What was rejected, and why. Directional shading and cast shadows to imply the gradient. On a plan map, shaded relief reads as a LIGHT SOURCE - the sun is in the northwest - rather than as rising ground: it fights the flat-plan convention the rest of the sheet is drawn in, and it misleads. Slope is carried by water, by steep-only terraces and by pictorial hills, never by shading. The absence of hillshade is a map drawing convention, and the pictorial mound is another.

The rule the map follows: no contour lines, no spot heights and no shaded relief are drawn at any tier. High ground is a pictorial mound; the fall of the land is read from the water - source and pond high, distributary ditches fanning downhill, a drainage ditch leaving the low corner off the map - and terraced paddies are drawn only where the slope is steep enough for a reader to expect risers.
