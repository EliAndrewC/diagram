How our maps draw the magistrate's manor on a town map

A town map draws its magistrate's compound as walls, a gate and an empty court - a simplification of the compound, not a picture of it shrunk to scale. On the Japanese model the manor stands at the town's edge, with its gate facing the town or the road it fronts; on the Chinese model it stands inside the wall on the main avenue. Where nothing else decides, the gate faces south.

  • The manor is drawn as a plain walled box, by convention.
    Walls, a gate and an empty court stand for the whole compound, a map drawing convention. Real seats were not one plan: jin'ya differed in what they held and in how strongly they were enclosed, so no one plan drawn small would be truer to them.
  • The box need not match the compound's own detailed plan.
    Its shape, its proportions and its size on the town map may all differ from the compound's own sheet, and a difference between the two is the convention working, not an error.
  • What the detailed plan draws outside the walls may be left off the town map.
    Notice boards at the gate, a bounty board, an approach road forking to a cart gate or a parley door need not appear.
  • The box is presumed to hold everything the detailed plan shows, drawn or not.
    Nothing it is presumed to contain is drawn a second time beside it.
  • A county seat draws no separate granary: its tax rice is stored inside the manor.
    That is the historical placement. An intendant's jin'ya was at once their residence, office, court and tax store, and the intendancy at Takayama kept its numbered earth-walled storehouses inside its own grounds. The exception is a rice-transit town, which keeps a storehouse row of its own.

Where the manor stands, and which way it faces, follow the model the town is drawn on.

  • On the Japanese model the manor stands at the edge of the town.
    Many Japanese seats were built beside a settlement already there, and the map reads that as a seat at the town's edge rather than in its middle.
  • On the Chinese model it stands inside the wall, on the main avenue.
    Neixiang's county yamen stands on East Main Street at the center of its town. What else such a town holds is at How our maps populate and furnish each kind of town.
  • The gate faces what the manor fronts: the town, or the road beside it.
    No one compass direction serves every manor, because the answer depends on where the town lies. Of the three drawn manors, one faces west to the hamlet below it, another south down its hill to the town, and the third fronts a diagonal road with its gate opening onto the roadbed. This is a calibration against those maps, not a finding.
  • Where nothing else decides it, the gate faces south.
    South is the formal orientation a Chinese local government office took by regulation and ritual propriety: the seat to the north facing south, the court in front and the sleeping quarters behind. The maps use it on both models, though the regulation is Chinese, not Japanese.
  • A manor fronting a road at an angle is turned to it.
    The whole compound is turned so that its front wall runs parallel to the roadbed.
  • When turning a manor pushes a caste's count out of range, the map's seed is re-rolled.
    Turning the compound is cheap to draw and expensive downstream: it changes the manor's footprint and so the ground it blocks, which reshuffles the seeded blocks of housing around it. The fix used is to re-roll the map's seed until the counts land back in range, and each of the dense towns and the drawn city carries a hand-picked seed for this reason. Hand-correcting the block counts instead chases its own tail - each correction perturbs the next block - and the map stops being the output of its own seed.
