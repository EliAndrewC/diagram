How our maps draw a town's wall

A county seat on the Chinese model is walled about two times in three, with a wall of earth 16 to 33 ft high, drawn 10 to 12 ft thick on an irregular line that hugs the built town. A seat on the Japanese model stands open, and only rarely rings itself with an earth bank and a ditch. Which model a seat follows is this setting's choice, not the history's; the shares within each model follow the history at Town walls, and which county seats had one (chengqiang).

  • A county seat on the Chinese model is walled about two times in three - a knob.
    That is the share of county seats walled by the late Ming.
  • A county seat on the Japanese model stands open, its magistrate's compound alone banked or moated.
    A jin'ya was often ringed with earth banks and a moat, mostly without high stone walls.
  • Only rarely is a town on the Japanese model ringed by an earth bank and a moat.
    That is a town that governs and defends itself, as the self-governing towns and the temple towns did.

The wall of a town on the Chinese model is drawn from the county walls the record measured.

  • How high is a town's wall?
    Rolled for each town between about 16 and 33 ft, a continuum from Shanghai's first wall to the top of the band most county walls kept. Pingyao's 12 m (~39 ft) stands above it.
  • The wall is drawn about 10 to 12 ft thick.
    Those are the two county walls measured: Zhenhai's 3.2 m (~10 ft) at the base and Pingyao's average of 3.5 m (~11 ft).
  • Its face is bare rammed earth or masonry over earth - a knob rolled per town.
    Both are attested: Pingyao's earth faced with masonry, Shanghai's rammed earth.
  • From above, the wall is a flat walk with a toothed outer edge and a plain inner one.
    That is the crenellated outer parapet and the plain inner one recorded at Xi'an. A masonry face may be drawn a shade lighter than bare earth, a drawing convention.
  • A town's wall has four to six gates.
    Shanghai's had six, Shou county's four. What stands at each gate follows What stands at a walled town's gate?
  • Does the wall carry towers?
    It may carry projecting towers about 164 ft apart and a tower at each corner, as Pingyao's does. That an ordinary county wall carried them at that spacing is a GUESS: no page we read says how many did.
  • The wall's length follows the town's size.
    How long it runs round is at How our maps size a town's built core and its ragged ends.

A wall costs, on this project's reasoning, by its length: the same earth, facing and upkeep run every foot of it, though no page we read prices one. So a town's wall is drawn to skirt what it must defend, kinking in to leave out a gully and bulging out round a quarter.

  • The wall's line is irregular.
    It has at least five sections, and their lengths are genuinely spread, not merely unequal: a coefficient of variation of a quarter or more. A spread test lets a wall hug a feature with several short sections while a near-regular outline still fails. Walls in hill country were irregular, drawn to take the heights around them inside.
  • The wall hugs the built town.
    No stretch of it longer than about 280 ft runs more than about 140 ft from the nearest building, feature or piece of terrain. The two thresholds are calibrated against the drawn walled town, not taken from a source.
  • Terrain may hold the wall off the town.
    A wall in hill country was drawn to take the high points around it inside, and on this project's reading it climbs or skirts a hill rather than leveling it.
  • A small open square inside the gate may hold the wall off the town.
    The square is a drawing convention, not a finding: nothing we read describes a square inside a town gate, and the nearest attested thing is the walled court of a barbican, at Did a town or city gate have a barbican?
  • A long empty margin along one face is fixed by pulling the face in.
    The face is moved to ride just outside the last buildings, and the cross street and the quarter behind it redrawn to match, rather than buildings scattered into the margin. The wall earns its length from the whole built area as a street earns its length from the buildings along it (How our maps lay out a town's streets).
  • The hugging rule is for towns, not cities.
    The walls of important cities often took in far more ground than was built, for growth and for timber and farmland in war. A city's wall follows Does the city wall close a full ring?
  • The wall need not close a full ring.
    It may be an open arc whose two ends run partway up the flanks of a steep hill, the slope closing each side in place of a blunt wall end, with the magistrate's compound on the summit.
  • Nothing is built on the wall.
    It carries a clear strip about 46 ft wide, so that even a large building's corner stays off it. A field may run up to the wall but never crosses it.
  • Only a walled town stands a fire watch tower.
    It stands clear of the wall, at How our maps draw fire watch towers and firefighting gear (hinomi yagura).

A town on the Japanese model that encloses itself is drawn with an earth bank instead of a wall.

  • The bank stands about 7 to 10 ft high, with a fence or wall along its top and its ditch outside it.
    That is the Japanese earth bank's height, 2 to 3 m (~7-10 ft), and its form on level ground.
  • The bank is drawn about 20 ft wide at its base.
    This is a GUESS: no page we read gives a small town's bank's base.
