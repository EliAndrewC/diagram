How our maps draw a town's wall

A county seat on the Chinese model is walled about two times in three, with a wall of earth 16 to 33 ft high, drawn 10 to 12 ft thick on an irregular line that hugs the built town. A seat on the Japanese model stands open, and only rarely rings itself with an earth bank and a ditch. Which model a seat follows is this setting's choice, not the history's; the shares within each model follow the history.

  • A county seat on the Chinese model is walled about two times in three - a knob.
    That is the share of county seats walled by the late Ming.
  • A county seat on the Japanese model stands open, its magistrate's compound alone banked or moated.
    In the early Edo period a jin'ya was often ringed with earth banks and a moat, mostly without high stone walls.
  • Only rarely is a town on the Japanese model ringed by an earth bank and a ditch.
    That is a town that governs and defends itself, as the self-governing towns such as Sakai and the temple towns of the Ikko sect did.

The wall of a town on the Chinese model is drawn from the county walls the record measured.

  • How high is a town's wall?
    Rolled for each town between about 16 and 33 ft, a continuum from Shanghai's first wall to the top of the band most county walls kept. Pingyao's 12 m (~39 ft) stands above it.
  • How thick is a town's wall?
    About 10 to 12 ft, from the two county walls measured: Zhenhai's 3.2 m (~10 ft) at the base and Pingyao's average of 3.5 m (~11 ft).
  • A town's wall is faced with bare rammed earth or with masonry over earth - a knob rolled per town.
    Both are attested: Pingyao's earth faced with masonry, Shanghai's rammed earth. No page we read gives the share of each, so the two are rolled evenly, an arbitrary share. A masonry face may be drawn a shade lighter than bare earth, a drawing convention.
  • What does the top of a town's wall look like from above?
    A flat walk with a toothed outer edge and a plain inner one, after the crenellated outer parapet and plain inner one recorded on the city wall at Xi'an.
  • How many gates does a town's wall have?
    Four to six: Shanghai's had six, Shou county's four. What stands at each gate follows What stands at a walled town's gate?
  • Does the wall carry towers?
    It may carry projecting towers about 164 ft apart and a tower at each corner, as Pingyao's does. That an ordinary county wall carried them at that spacing is a GUESS: no page we read says how many did.

A wall costs, on this project's reasoning, by its length: the same earth, facing and upkeep run every foot of it, though no page we read prices one. So a town's wall is drawn to skirt what it must defend, kinking in to leave out a gully and bulging out round a quarter. How long it runs round follows the town's size, at How our maps size a town's built core and its ragged ends.

  • The wall's line is irregular.
    It has at least five sections, and their lengths are genuinely spread, not merely unequal: a coefficient of variation of a quarter or more. Measuring the spread lets a wall bend round a feature in several short sections while ruling out an outline that is nearly regular.
  • The wall hugs the built town.
    No stretch of it longer than about 280 ft runs more than about 140 ft from the nearest building, feature or piece of terrain. The two thresholds are calibrated against the drawn walled town, not taken from a source.
  • Terrain may hold the wall off the town.
    Walls in hill country ran irregular, drawn to take the high points around them inside, and on this project's reading it climbs or skirts a hill rather than leveling it.
  • The wall need not close a full ring.
    It may be an open arc whose two ends run partway up the flanks of a steep hill, the slope closing each side in place of a blunt wall end, with the magistrate's compound on the summit. This is this project's decision, not a finding.
  • A small open square inside the gate may hold the wall off the town.
    The square is a drawing convention, not a finding: nothing we read describes a square inside a town gate, and the nearest attested thing is the walled court of a barbican, at Did a town or city gate have a barbican?
  • Where one side of the wall would run along a long stretch of empty ground, that side is drawn in to the buildings.
    That side is moved to ride just outside the last buildings, and the cross street and the quarter behind it redrawn to match, rather than buildings scattered into the margin. The wall earns its length from the whole built area as a street earns its length from the buildings along it (How our maps lay out a town's streets).
  • A town's wall hugs its built area; a city's need not.
    The walls of important cities often took in far more ground than was built, for growth and for timber and farmland in war. A city's wall follows Does the city wall close a full ring?
  • The wall runs in a clear strip about 46 ft wide that nothing is built on.
    The strip is wide enough that even a large building's corner stays off the wall. A field may run up to the wall but never crosses it.
  • A walled town's fire watch tower stands clear of the wall.
    Only a walled town has one; how it is drawn is at How our maps draw fire watch towers and firefighting gear (hinomi yagura).

A town on the Japanese model that encloses itself is drawn with an earth bank instead of a wall.

  • How high is the bank?
    About 7 to 10 ft, the Japanese earth bank's height of 2 to 3 m (~7-10 ft).
  • The bank carries a fence or wall along its top, and a ditch is dug with it.
    That is the Japanese earth bank's form on level ground.
  • The bank is drawn about 20 ft wide at its base.
    This is a GUESS: no page we read gives a small town's bank's base.
