How our maps lay out a town's farmsteads

A town's farmhouses are not a looser version of a hamlet's. They stand close together and use the same to-scale farmstead a village's farms do: the house, a threshing yard strictly to its south, a garden set where the sun reaches it, and a storehouse plot kept to its north. They stand outside the town's wall, ringing its paddies at about a village's density.

  • Each farmstead is tested against the fields by all the ground it occupies.
    Placing the house first and testing the fields by their centers is a 14 ft error in the wrong direction at a town's scale: a 44 ft town house passes such a test with a corner already sunk into the crop. Testing what the building actually covers against what the field actually covers is the only form of the rule that holds at this scale.
  • Does a farm on a town map keep a grove of its own?
    No. This is a GUESS: no page we read says whether the farms inside a village sheltered by a shared belt also kept groves of their own, and it is the same rule our maps give a nucleated hamlet. A leafy scatter of copse trees stands among the rows instead.
  • A town's shelter is one shared windbreak on its windward side.
    A belt planted for a whole settlement is attested well before modern times: the Ryukyu kingdom's village belts, Tarama's made in 1742, and the Tsugaru domain's planting of 1682 on the side the sea wind came from. Tarama's belt rings its settlement; our maps set the belt on the windward side only, as Tsugaru's stood, and that is this project's choice.
  • The farmsteads stand outside the wall, around the paddies.
    A farmer builds close to the ground they work, and our maps keep a town's fields outside its wall. That is a deliberate departure from the history: the Chinese walled city counted farmland among the ordinary uses of the ground inside it, and Suzhou's wall still enclosed farmland in the twentieth century.
