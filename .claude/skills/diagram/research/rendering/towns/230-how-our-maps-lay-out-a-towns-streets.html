How our maps lay out a town's streets

A town on our maps is drawn as blocks fronting streets, never as a scatter of houses. Japan's post towns and market towns grew along the road that ran through them, so an unwalled town takes one of three street-town forms. A Chinese walled town such as Leping was laid out on planned streets, so a walled town gets a main avenue to the magistrate's compound (yamen) and cross streets. Every street the map draws is there to serve the buildings that front it.

  • A town's plan is a knob of four forms: three street-town forms for an unwalled town, and a planned form for a walled one.
    The forms are distinct in the record, so each is drawn and none stands for all:
    • a street town built along both sides of its road;
    • a one-sided street town, where the site is squeezed between slope and water;
    • a street town with back streets and blocks behind its main street, for a town that is its district's market center;
    • a walled town laid out with a main avenue and cross streets.
    Which street form an unwalled town takes is chosen at random for each town from the map's seed.
  • How often does each street form come up?
    The plain street town is the commonest, because the Japanese reference work says almost all the Edo period's post towns, market towns and temple-gate towns took it. The weights among the three forms are a GUESS.
  • In an unwalled town, the road running through it is its spine.
    The buildings line the road, and the blocks are what stands along it. How many cross streets a town runs off its road, and how the road bends at the town's ends, is at How many cross streets does a town have, and how does its road come in?
  • A walled town is laid out on one main avenue, from its gate to the magistrate's compound, with one or two market cross streets.
    Its streets are drawn first, before any building is placed. The avenue is about 26 ft wide and carries on through the gate, so that the market outside fronts it too. The cross streets are about 20 ft wide and carve the interior into blocks. What a town's streets measured in history is at How wide is a town's street, its side lane and its back alley?
  • Did an ordinary county seat's main avenue run from its gate to the magistrate's compound?
    We draw it so, as a GUESS. A planned grid is attested for a capital, streets joining the gates for a walled city, and a south-facing yamen for a county seat, but no page we read puts an avenue from the gate to the yamen in an ordinary county seat.
  • Businesses line the street two deep, each turned to face it, and housing fills the blocks behind.
    The two rows nearest a street are businesses facing it. The housing behind them is turned toward its own nearest street, and a spot in a block's interior that would face no street at all is left empty.
  • The poor live behind the street front, on back lanes of their own.
    Wide frontage on a Japanese town street took great wealth, and the poor rented row houses let by the shop owners on the lots behind. So a town's laborers are given lanes off the avenue, and their dwellings front a street without standing on the main commercial frontage.

Once the buildings stand, the map checks every street against them.

  • Our maps draw a street only where there are buildings for it to serve.
    Where a path is not paved it is worn into the ground by the traffic it carries, as the shortcut to where walkers are actually going, and Beijing's hutong lanes were formed by the rows of courtyard houses along them. So a planned street line that never gets built up is not drawn: it is an undeveloped block.
  • Inside the walls, no stretch of street longer than about 130 ft goes without a building that fronts it.
    The distance is a convention of ours.
  • A building counts only for the street it was turned to face.
    It does not count for a street it merely stands near. Crediting any building near a lane would let a building that stands nearer a cross street, while facing another, keep a dead-ending lane alive.
  • A fence or wall between a building and a street cuts it off from that street.
    Laborers housed on the far side of a samurai ward's fence serve their own side of it. They cannot reach a government avenue beyond the fence, however near they stand, so they do not line it, and an avenue with only them beside it is empty.
  • An empty lane is fixed by giving it houses before it is cut.
    If there are dwellings the lane could serve, or households the town's population count has not yet placed, the lane is routed to them or they are moved onto it, the lower-status dwellings first. Only when the population is already fully housed is the lane trimmed back to its last building, or removed.
