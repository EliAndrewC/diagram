How our maps lay out a town's streets

A town on our maps is drawn as blocks fronting streets, never as a scatter of houses. Its plan is one of four forms rolled per town, and every street it draws is there to serve the buildings that front it.

  • A town's plan is a knob of four forms, rolled per town from the map's seed.
    The forms are distinct in the record, so each is drawn and none stands for all:
    • a street town built along both sides of its road;
    • a one-sided street town, where the site is squeezed between slope and water;
    • a street town with back streets and blocks behind its main street, for a seat that is its district's market center;
    • a walled town laid out with a main avenue and cross streets.
  • An unwalled town takes one of the three street forms, and a walled town the fourth.
    The weights among the three street forms are a GUESS. That the plain street town is the commonest of them follows the Japanese reference works, which say almost all small towns took it.
  • In an unwalled town, the road running through it is its spine.
    The buildings line the road, and the blocks are what stands along it. How many cross streets a town runs off its road, and how the road bends at the town's ends, is at How many cross streets does a town have, and how does its road come in?
  • A walled town has its streets drawn first, before any building is placed.
    One main avenue about 26 ft wide runs inward from the gate to the magistrate's compound, carried on through the gate so that the market outside fronts the same avenue. One or two market cross streets about 20 ft wide carve the interior into blocks. What a town's streets measured in history is at How wide is a town's street, its side lane and its back alley?
  • The avenue's run from the gate to the magistrate's compound is a GUESS.
    A planned grid is attested for a capital, and streets joining the gates and a south-facing yamen for a county seat, but no page we read puts an avenue from the gate to the yamen in an ordinary county seat.
  • Businesses line the street two deep, each turned to face it, and housing fills the blocks behind.
    Each building in a block's interior is turned toward its nearest street, and any spot that would front no street is left empty.

Every street the map keeps has buildings to serve.

  • A street exists for the buildings it serves.
    Where a path is not paved it is worn into the ground by the traffic it carries, as the shortcut to where walkers are actually going, and Beijing's hutong lanes were formed by the rows of courtyard houses along them. So a planned street line that never gets built up is not drawn: it is an undeveloped block.
  • Inside the walls, no stretch of street longer than about 130 ft goes without a building that fronts it.
    The building must front that street - the one it was turned to face, not merely one it stands near.
  • A building with no street is allowed; a street with no building is not.
    Wide frontage on a Japanese town street took great wealth, and the poor rented row houses let by the shop owners on the lots behind. A house behind the street front is history; a street fronted by nothing is not.
  • An empty lane is fixed by giving it houses before it is cut.
    If there are dwellings the lane could serve, or households the census still owes, the lane is routed to them or they are moved onto it, the lower-status dwellings readily lining the lesser back lanes, as the back tenements of a Japanese town did. Only when the population is already fully housed is the lane trimmed back to its last building, or removed.
  • Frontage counts only to the street a building faces, and a fence or a wall between them breaks it.
    Laborers housed on the far side of a samurai ward's fence serve their own side of it. They cannot reach a government avenue beyond the fence, however near they stand, so they do not line it, and an avenue with only them beside it is empty.
  • A looser rule, crediting any building near a lane, fails.
    A building that fronts the cross street it stands nearer to would then keep a dead-ending lane alive.
  • A town's laborers get back lanes of their own.
    Their quarters are given lanes off the avenue, so their dwellings front a street without standing on the main commercial frontage.
