How our maps draw gardens behind a town house (tsubo-niwa)

On our maps a town's gardens follow the house, not a chance drawn town by town. A merchant's house keeps a small open court (tsubo-niwa), some 6 to 12 ft across, inside the house, and a planted garden behind it, as Kyoto's town houses did. A farming household keeps vegetable beds, and a back-tenement row keeps none.

The rule the map follows:
  • A merchant's town house shows a small open court inside the house, near the back.
    It is about 6 by 6 to 6 by 12 ft, the one to two tsubo of a Kyoto town house's court.
  • The plot behind a merchant's house is drawn as a planted garden, not vegetable beds.
    It fills what is left of the plot behind the house. Many Kyoto town houses kept a planted back garden; the shrubs and a tree or two our maps draw in it are a GUESS at its planting.
  • Did a provincial town's merchant houses keep the same court and back garden as Kyoto's?
    No page we read says how common the court garden was in a provincial market town. Our maps give them both, as Kyoto's town houses had them: a GUESS.
  • A farming household's plot in town is drawn as a vegetable garden.
    A farming household's lot often ended in its vegetable garden (How a town is zoned: shops on the street, housing behind).
  • A back-tenement row gets no garden.
    A back-tenement unit was a single room on a shared lane (Laborers' housing: back-lot tenements (uradana) and the master laborer's house).
