How our maps populate and furnish each kind of town

A town map draws every household of its census that keeps a house of its own, one building to each, and furnishes the town by its kind: a market county seat on the Japanese or the Chinese model, or a post town strung out along its road. The census is the setting's own, about 238 households in a county seat; the street plans, the houses and the magistrate's compound follow the history on the research page.

  • Every household that keeps a house of its own is drawn, one building to each.
    The setting's counts are documented, not invented, so a map places the actual count of each caste rather than a token sample of each.
  • No peasant headman is drawn in a town.
    The setting's notes give the village a headman's household and name none for the town, whose working samurai are the magistrate and their staff; read from those tables, the state is present in the town in person, as the magistrate.
  • A town's houses come in more than one size.
    The setting's wealth tables put a minority of merchants in the very rich and rich brackets and a few master laborers above the rest of their caste.

Three drawing conventions stand between the census and what is drawn. Each follows one reasoning: a map draws a household where it stands, and a household that lives inside someone else's walls is presumed to be there rather than drawn twice.

  • Of a town's 13 servant households, only the five or so miscellaneous ones are drawn as houses of their own.
    The other eight are presumed to live inside the samurai and merchant compounds they serve, as a merchant's apprentices lived in the shop and a samurai household's private servants, the komono, lived in and did its odd jobs, so they are not drawn.
  • A shop stands for no household.
    A shop is drawn as business premises, counted apart from the merchant dwellings: whoever sleeps in it is already counted with its merchant, so adding one changes no population figure.
  • A town's samurai show as five to ten houses, its platoon of about fifteen living inside the magistrate's manor.
    The town's working platoon is presumed barracked inside the magistrate's manor and not drawn; a small lord's seat could hold its retainers' official houses inside its own bank and moat, beside the residence and the offices.

The rule the map follows for a county seat:

  • About 24 merchant houses, 29 laborer dwellings, 5 free-standing servant households, 12 burakumin dwellings, 5 to 10 samurai houses and about 156 farmhouses. Shops are extra and count against no household.
  • The farmhouses remain the largest single group on the map, which caps the laborers, small and large together, below them.
  • Four or five of the 24 merchants live in a large house, and two or three of the 29 laborers in a master's; the rest live in small or standard dwellings. The large houses stand just behind the street frontage, in gaps left among the packed rows.

A county seat on the Japanese model and one on the Chinese model are furnished differently, after the contrast on the research page.

  • A town on the Japanese model has no town wall.
    A Japanese county seat was usually unwalled; the history and its exceptions are at Town walls, and which county seats had one (chengqiang)
  • A town on the Japanese model draws its commoners' houses as rows of townhouses facing the street.
    A Japanese county town was in many cases one or two rows of townhouses along a highway or a river on strip lots, and at a post station on a busy road each main house filled the whole width of its lot; the map gives the county town that close-packed front too.
  • In a town on the Japanese model, the magistrate's compound is banked or moated, not stone-walled, with rows of samurai houses around it.
    A small lord's seat could have earthworks and moats but no high stone walls, and samurai houses lined up in the town that grew around a jin'ya. Where the compound stands in its town and which way its gate faces follow How our maps draw the magistrate's manor on a town map

A county seat on the Chinese model turns inward, behind walls.

  • A town on the Chinese model usually draws a town wall.
    A Chinese county seat was usually walled, and our maps wall one about two times in three, at How our maps draw a town's wall; the history and its exceptions are at Town walls, and which county seats had one (chengqiang)
  • In a town on the Chinese model, the wealthier houses are inward-facing courtyard compounds behind blank outer walls.
    A large siheyuan was walled round from outside with high windowless walls.
  • In a town on the Chinese model, the magistrate's compound stands on the main avenue.
    This is a GUESS: no page we read says a county seat's main avenue ran from its gate to its yamen. What the planned Chinese town does show is at Town plans: the street town (gaison), the planned grid and the castle town.

A post town is laid out differently from either kind of county seat: it lives by its road, and the map strings it out along it.

The rule the map follows for a post town:

  • The town is a strip along its road rather than a cluster around a square.
  • An ordinary post town on a busy trunk road holds about 300 to 600 houses and 30 to 60 inns, the size of an ordinary post town on the Tokaido.
  • The relay office (toiyaba) and the lords' inn (honjin) stand at about the middle of the strip, ordinary inns and teahouses on either side of them, other shops beyond.
  • The road through it is 12 to 24 ft wide, with a water channel along it.
  • The lots are strips of nearly one depth, of differing frontage, running back from the road, most ending in a vegetable garden.
  • The road bends, or turns through a squared kink, at each entrance.
  • The street front is continuous on a trunk road and broken by front yards on a lesser one, a choice made at random for each town (a knob).
