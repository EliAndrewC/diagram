How our maps place a town's inns and lodging houses

A town on our maps keeps two grades of lodging: one caravan inn on the road its traffic uses, and a cheap lodging house (a flophouse) where travelers arrive. A provincial city keeps more: inns at its gates, and a cheap lodging house inside its walls and outside every gate.

  • Every county seat on our maps stands on a trade route, so every town keeps a caravan inn.
    The town is drawn on a through road, the road its inn serves.
  • A town keeps one inn.
    The count is set against the towns our maps draw, not taken from a source: no page we read counts the inns of a county town off the relay.
  • A town is not a post station, so the post stations' dozens of inns are a ceiling, not a target.
    In this setting the relay horses belong to the Imperial waystation (What was the Imperial waystation on the ground, and where does it stand?), not to a street of inns.
  • The inn takes one of two forms, rolled for each town: a single-story wagon inn or a two-story post-station inn.
    The record holds two analogues that disagree on the one thing a map can show, the inn's height: the north-Chinese wagon inn is single-story, and a surviving Tokaido inn of about 1836 is two stories, its upper floor the guest rooms. Neither is the better analogue, so the form is a knob, rolled from each map's seed with no bias between the two, because nothing we read says how common either was; a map may pin one.
    • The wagon inn's single story is a GUESS for an inn before modern times.
      The 1780 account of the north-Chinese inns gives no height, so the single story is carried back from the later account.
  • A wagon inn keeps its stable within about 150 ft, with open ground beside the stable.
    The north-Chinese inn kept a long stable and a broad yard for the carts, so no more than four dwellings stand within about 75 ft of the stable. The distances are calibrated against the drawn towns.
  • The maps draw no grooms' lean-to and no well at the inn.
    Only a 2026 newspaper's account of the wagon inn shows them, and this project draws nothing that only modern sources show.
  • A post-station inn is drawn without a stable or a cart yard behind it.
    No page we read shows either at a Japanese inn before 1868; the one picture of a large stable behind a travelers' inn is a sentence from a novel of 1913. The stable and the yard belong to the wagon inn, whose source before modern times shows them.
  • A post-station inn is a house on the street, not a compound, most of them at the small end of 18 to 33 ft wide.
    At Mishima a large inn had 5 ken or more of frontage and a small one 3 ken or less, small inns were the commonest, and one Hiratsuka inn was 5.5 ken (about 33 ft) wide. The inn's own plan, and the largest our maps draw, are at How our maps draw an inn.
  • The inn fronts the road it serves.
    It stands within about 115 ft of the roadbed with no building between it and the road, its entrance turned to the nearest point of the road and its long side running along it, so a diagonal road gets a tilted inn. This is a convention of our maps: a caravan pulls up to the road it is already on, so an inn behind two ranks of shops is an inn no wagon can reach.
  • An unwalled town sites its inn along the road where the caravans pass; a walled town keeps it inside the wall.
    The caravans come in through the gate, so a walled town's inn and stable stand inside the rampart, fronting the main street.

The flophouse is the town's other lodging, for the poorest travelers and for the market crowd.

  • Every town draws one market-day flophouse unless it declares otherwise, and a busy hub draws more.
    It serves the poor of the road and, by this project's reading, a GUESS, the market-goers from the far edge of a market's reach who come in the night before.
  • The flophouse is drawn as a plain thatched house with one shared room.
    It is drawn like the one in an Edo print, a thatched house marked by its name on the paper doors, with no awning or shop front, so it reads as nothing like a shophouse.
  • It is not drawn as a barn with a long row of doorways.
    No source of any period shows that building, or a house bedding dozens, and this project draws no form that no source before modern times shows.
  • How big is a flophouse?
    No bigger than a large house. This is a GUESS: no page we read measures one.
  • A walled town's flophouse stands in the gate market outside its gate.
    It is where travelers arrive, at How our maps draw gate markets outside a town (guanxiang): the gate shuts at dusk, and a peasant arriving late cannot get in. That a county town's gate shut at dusk is this project's extension of the night closing recorded for the Tang and Ming capitals, a GUESS, since no page we read says so.
  • An unwalled town's flophouse stands along the road at the town's edge.
    So the kichin-yado stood at the edge of a post town.

A provincial city keeps more cheap lodging, and its count is a stylization.

  • A provincial city draws one cheap lodging house inside its walls, and one outside every gate.
    The one outside stands within about 1,560 ft of its gate. Inns did gather outside a city's busy gates, but how many cheap houses a city held is on no page we read, so the symmetric count is a GUESS, a deliberate stylization on the generous side of a range nobody can bound.
  • Why does a city draw so many?
    The setting is more attentive to its peasantry than its historical models, and the count is drawn so the provision reads at a glance. This project declined two alternatives: putting the cheap lodging at the busiest gate alone, and drawing a separate cart-inn compound with an animal yard.
  • The one inside the walls stands in a humble quarter.
    It stands in the laborers' quarter, or the farming quarter where a city has one; never within about 330 ft of the temple, merchant or samurai neighborhoods, and never within about 450 ft of the burakumin quarter.
  • At a gate where the caravans stop, the cheap house stands beside the inn and the stable.
    This is the one exception to its standing alone: there it belongs with the gate's caravan cluster.
  • How much lodging does a city keep that the map does not draw?
    A highway seat of about 3,000 inhabitants that is not a post station would run something like eight to fifteen lodging houses all told, by this project's GUESS: the two to four inns and two to four cheap houses the map draws as buildings of their own, and five to eight small family inns that were not separate buildings at all. These would be ordinary shophouses whose back rooms took lodgers, the household living on the meal trade, a field and porterage with lodging as the extra, and on the night a lord's procession stopped the whole town became the billet. So several of the shop rows on the road and at the gates are understood to sleep travelers, and nothing on the map counts them.
