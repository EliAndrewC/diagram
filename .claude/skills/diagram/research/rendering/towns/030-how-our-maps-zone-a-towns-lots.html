How our maps zone a town's lots

A town map sorts its lots in bands back from the road: the storefronts on the road, the merchant families' houses in a tight row directly behind them, about 100 ft back, then, across a gap of a few dozen feet, the laborers' houses, and off at one edge the burakumin quarter. A town whose shops and houses stood mixed together would read as a village that happens to have shops, not as a town whose commerce is its street. Every house faces its street, with its garden behind it.

  • Shops hold the road front, and no merchant home stands among them.
    The street front of a town was its commerce, and the poorer households lived behind it, on the back lots. Housing is set back at least 92 ft from the main street.
  • A merchant's shop and house are drawn as two buildings, a deliberate deviation.
    A Kyoto townhouse was one building, the shop at the street and the dwelling behind it along the passage. Our maps draw the merchant's house as a second building directly behind the shop, our choice so that the street front shows shops alone.
  • Each merchant house stands directly behind its own shop, 15 to 74 ft back, and parallel to it.
    It lies within the stretch of road its shop fronts, one building deeper, so the pair reads as one unit.
  • Where the road runs at an angle, the shops and the houses behind them turn with it.
    Every town house faces its street, so the storefronts tilt to match an angled road, and each merchant house tilts to the same angle to stay parallel to its shop. A house turned end for end still counts as parallel.
  • The laborers' houses begin at least 35 ft deeper than the deepest merchant house.
    The gap is this project's own: the sources sort the back lots below the street front by wealth, but give no gap between the bands. On one unwalled town drawn with these rules, the merchant houses sit 106 to 116 ft from the road and the laborers begin at 156 ft.
  • The burakumin quarter is its own group at an edge, no part of the street front.
    Outcaste settlements stood in many cases at a village's edge, beside a river or on ground unsuited to farming. No page we read places one in a town's plan, so the edge siting is this project's reading of the village evidence.
  • Every town house faces its street.
    Its door is on the street side whether its ridge runs along the street or its gable is turned to it, because the two forms are named for which face holds the main entrance.
  • Behind each house lies its own garden plot.
    A post town's lots were strips of nearly one depth, their backs often a vegetable garden.
  • Houses stand one deep along their street.
    A post town's lots were strips of nearly one depth. A second row appears only on a back lane, a guess of ours, and follows the city's rule for that lane, at which way a city house's door faces.
  • A walled town is banded street by street.
    Our maps lay a walled town out on a main avenue and cross streets rather than one road, so its bands are read back from each street rather than by depth from a single main road. How its streets are laid, and the walled town they are drawn from, is at How our maps lay out a town's streets.
