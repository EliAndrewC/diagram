How our maps draw the townsmen's office and guild halls (machi-kaisho, huiguan)

A town map draws no separate office for the town's commoners and no guild hall. Where a headman keeps the town's papers, the office is their own house on the main street, drawn as a house.

  • Our county town takes the castle town as its nearest counterpart.
    A county town is its magistrate's seat. In some castle towns the commoners' officials worked at the town magistrate's office and no ward office was set up, and in Edo the ward headman's house served as the office. So a town map draws no ward office of its own; that is this project's decision.
  • The headman's house doubles as the office, on the main street.
    Setting it on the main street is this project's choice, after Edo's three town elders, whose residences doubled as their office on corner lots facing Honcho street. It is drawn as a house, with nothing to mark it as an office.
  • A ward office of its own belongs to a self-governing or great commercial city.
    Those are the towns that kept one in every ward, and a county town is neither.
  • No guild hall is drawn.
    A huiguan was the hall of its members, people from one home region or merchants of one trade, and no page we read places one in a county town.
