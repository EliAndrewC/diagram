How our maps site a town's theater stage

Every town on our maps draws one theater stage, unless the town declares otherwise: a roofed stage facing an open viewing ground, beside a temple's or shrine's hall. That follows the shrine-ground plays of Edo-period Japan, put on in a precinct with nothing roofed but the stage. No town draws a permanent roofed playhouse; how our maps draw those is at How our maps draw roofed playhouses.

  • The stage stands within about 260 ft of the nearest temple or shrine hall.
    The distance is set against the towns our maps draw, not taken from a source.
  • Its open viewing side faces that hall.
    A stage turned away from the hall would put its audience outside the precinct, with its back to the hall that owns the ground. That the audience stood between the stage and the hall is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • A walled town keeps its stage inside the walls, unless it declares otherwise.
    A town may declare its stage outside the walls, as it may declare no stage at all.
  • The stage is drawn as a roofed stage facing open ground, never as a seating bowl.
    Only the stage of a shrine-ground play was roofed, so the ground before it is drawn open. A Chinese-style town's stage is drawn the same way, a GUESS: the record has no readable page on the stage of a Chinese temple.
