How our maps draw a town's gate market

A walled town on our maps draws a small gate market outside its gate: a few shops strung along the approach road, with an inn among them where the gate opens onto a through road. Its size follows the gate's traffic, not the town's population, and it is drawn whole inside the town's map.

  • Every walled town draws a gate market, unless it is a real exception.
    By the nineteenth century few walled Chinese cities lacked a suburb outside at least one gate, so only a purely military fort, or a town whose gate is depopulated or suppressed, draws none.
  • How many premises does a gate market hold, and how far out does it run?
    Typically four to eight permanent premises, never fewer than three, along the approach road within about 420 ft of the gate. The numbers are set against the towns our maps draw, and are a GUESS: no page we read counts a county town's gate strip or says how far it ran.
  • Its premises are shops selling to the villagers, at every gate that carries a road.
    The ground outside a gate drew markets and businesses serving the country people, and inns only outside the gates that opened onto long-distance routes, so an inn stands among the shops only where the gate opens onto a through road.
  • The gate on the town's main through road has the larger strip.
    That is the gate the traffic uses: the suburb that grew largest lay in the direction of the main traffic.
  • The strip fronts the same avenue as the main street inside the gate.
    The approach road its shops line is the road that runs on through the gate as the town's main street.
  • A town's gate market sits whole inside its map.
    Like the farmland, the gate market is a slice of something larger. A city's map is cropped, so its strip shows that by running off the edge; a town's map is the whole view and keeps its buildings clear of the edge, so the approach road running off the edge says by itself that there is more beyond.
  • A larger gate market does not change the town's population.
    The premises beyond the merchants' dwellings are shops, which house nobody.
  • The town's laborers and servants are never moved outside the wall to make room.
    That would invert the real pattern, in which the wall is what the commoners live inside: a Tang city's residents lived in walled wards within it.
