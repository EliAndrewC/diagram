How our maps draw and place the waystation and its stable

No map yet draws a waystation, a relay office or a relay stable. Where one does, these are its rules. Which towns have a waystation is set by the setting's spacing of about ten miles, not by the history.

  • The waystation is a walled compound about 262 ft square on the Imperial road.
    Its hall, barracks and stables stand inside, as seven or more buildings stood inside the excavated ancient relay station at Koinumaru, walled about 80 m (~262 ft) a side. A compound that size fits the complex the setting lists.
  • Where the waystation stands is a knob: its own compound on the road, or at a county seat beside the magistrate's compound.
    Both forms are attested: some ancient stations were compounds of their own, and a good many were run by the district office or kept in the station master's house. The magistrate's compound is at How our maps draw the magistrate's manor on a town map.
  • A Chinese walled post town such as Jimingyi is not the waystation's model.
    Jimingyi was a town about 2,330 m (~7,644 ft) around with a garrison of more than 300 at its height, a fortress with a town inside it, where the setting's waystation is a compound with a staff of 15 to 20.
  • No map draws a relay office in a town or a city besides the waystation.
    In this setting the relay horses are the waystation's; whether a town or a city also kept a relay office of its own is not something the history can decide.
  • Where a map draws a relay office (toiyaba), it is a house on the street, not a yard.
    In the Edo period, a post station's relay office was part of the headman's house or a building of its own.
  • Where a post station's relay horses stand, beyond the waystation's own, is a knob: dispersed through the householders' own stables, or gathered in one horse compound.
    The Japanese post station's horses were requisitioned from its residents, and no page we read places a stable or a horse yard at its relay office, so this project reads them as kept at home, a GUESS. Jimingyi kept its post horses in one horse compound on its back street.
  • Should a map draw a city's relay quarter (tenma-cho), it stands near the main gate of its lord's castle.
    A castle town's relay quarter was placed, as a rule, close to the castle's main gate.

The stable is drawn from the only premodern stable we found both dated and measured.

  • How wide is a stall?
    About 8 to 9 ft, in a stable about 19 ft deep: the stall of Hikone castle's stable of about 1688-1703, in the Edo period, by this project's division of its wings among its horses. It is a castle lord's stable, not an inn's or a relay's.
  • The maps do not size a stall from an undated farmhouse stable of about 10 ft square.
    The encyclopedia that gives the Kinki farm stable as about 3 m (~10 ft) square names no period for it, and could as well describe a house of the Meiji era, after 1868, as one of the Edo period, so the maps size a stall from the dated castle stable instead.
  • How long is the waystation's stable?
    About 65 to 90 ft of stalls, for its 8 to 10 horses at 8 to 9 ft a stall. The length is a GUESS built on the castle stable's stalls: no page we read measures a relay stable.
  • The stable yard's beaten earth, hitching rails and litter are a GUESS.
    No page we read describes the ground of a stable yard; how the maps draw it is at How our maps draw stable yards and their troughs.
