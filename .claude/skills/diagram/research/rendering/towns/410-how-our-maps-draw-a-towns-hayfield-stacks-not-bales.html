How our maps draw a town's hayfield: stacks, not bales

A town's hayfield is drawn as open cut grass with no fence around it, because the record shows fodder cut on common ground and carried to the stable, and no page we read shows a hayfield fenced or a pasture penned. Hay stored in bulk is drawn as the record keeps it, as stacks standing in the open; the maps draw no hay barn and no bale.

  • The maps draw no barn in a town's hayfield.
    The large free-standing barn, and its 84 by 56 ft footprint, rest on nothing in the record: no page we read describes a hay barn standing in a field.
  • A big store of hay is drawn as stacks and thatched storehouses together inside a wall.
    That is the form of the one fodder depot read, in a Ming novel: stacks all round, thatched storehouses and halls, and an earth wall with a gate.
  • The buildings a town's horses need are outer stables with their sheds, near the town.
    A farmhouse's outer stable stood by itself with a shed built on, so the maps put the horses' buildings near the town rather than out in the grass.
  • The maps draw no hay bales.
    The rectangular bale is what a nineteenth-century baling machine makes, a modern form.
  • The maps draw no fence or dashed line around the hayfield.
    No page we read says a fodder ground was marked off by anything built, so its edge follows the lie of the land - a stream, a slope, the foot of a hill.
  • Whether a town's hayfield carries haystacks is a knob, rolled per town.
    Both forms are attested: the Chinese record keeps hay in stacks in the open, while the Japanese record shows fodder cut on shared ground and carried to the stable, with no page read showing it stacked in the field. So a town either carries no stored hay or carries a few round haystacks standing in the open grass.
  • How many stacks, and how big?
    A GUESS, drawn to read as stacks rather than as buildings. The one premodern measure read, a stack a zhang high inside a fence five or six paces around or across, is the sixth century's and is not converted here.
  • The fodder meadow lies past the town's fields, on the side away from the road.
    It stands beyond the fields on the town's hill or river side, as a village's meadow does, as a band of open grass meeting the edge of the map, with no fence, no pen and no grazing animals. Its width and the line of its inner edge are a GUESS, set to read as a meadow beside the fields rather than as a size: no page we read gives a road town's fodder ground's size.
  • The caption says hayfield or fodder meadow, not grazing.
    The record found cutting and not grazing, so "grazing" in a caption would be a GUESS.

The rule the map follows: a town's hayfield is a band of open grass past its fields on the side away from the road, meeting the edge of the map, with no fenced or dashed outline, no rectangular bales, no barns, no pen and no grazing animals; its edge follows the lie of the land, and its width and inner edge are a GUESS. Per town, rolled from the seed, it either carries no stored hay or carries a few round haystacks standing in the open grass, a GUESS at their number and size, drawn to read as stacks rather than as buildings. Its caption reads hayfield or fodder meadow.
