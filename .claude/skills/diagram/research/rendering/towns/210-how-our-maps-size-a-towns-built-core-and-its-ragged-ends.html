How our maps size a town's built core and its ragged ends

A town map sizes its built core from the lengths and house counts of Edo-period post towns and from the Ming and Qing grades of city walls, scaled by this setting's count of households. The results are calibrations, not measurements of a town of this size: a road town's strip of houses runs about 800 to 1,300 ft along its road, and a walled town's wall no more than about 6,600 ft round.

  • How long is a road town's strip?
    About 800 to 1,300 ft for the households that do not farm, and about 2,400 to 3,800 ft where the farmhouses stand on the road as well. A county seat here holds about 238 households, about 80 of them not farming (see Towns: the county seat and the post town, and who lives in them (machi)). The small stations put about 21 houses on every 100 m (~328 ft) of road, both sides counted, and busy Itabashi about 33. At the small stations' rate the 80 would line about 390 m (~1,280 ft) of road and all 238 about 1,150 m (~3,773 ft); at Itabashi's, about 240 m (~787 ft) and 720 m (~2,362 ft). That this setting's town strings along its road at a post station's rate is this project's decision.
  • The rates are houses per length of road, not lot widths.
    Each count is of a station's houses, and whether every one of them fronted the road is on no page we read.
  • The scale rests on the Edo-period lengths, not on the preserved townscapes.
    The preserved post towns of today are of the same order, but their extents are what survives, not what the period recorded.
  • How long is a walled town's wall?
    Toward the small end of the smallest class, under 2 km (~6,562 ft) round, where most county seats stood. This is a GUESS: no source found ties a town's household count to the length of its wall.
  • A town's street front is packed, and the ground behind it is gardens.
    On a busy road each main house filled the whole frontage of its lot, and the back of the lot was in many cases a vegetable garden, so the ground behind each row is its lots' gardens, not open waste.
  • How much of a town's ground is under roofs?
    No figure sets it, and the maps use no houses-to-the-acre figure, since the record gives none; what the roofs cover follows from the frontage and the gardens behind, and is a GUESS.

The rule the map follows for a town's core:

  • An unwalled road town's built strip - the houses of its households that do not farm, lining both sides of the road - runs about 800 to 1,300 ft along the road, and about 2,400 to 3,800 ft where its farmhouses stand on the road as well.
  • Its depth is two rows of lots and the road between them.
  • The street front is packed at about 32 ft of street to a house in a post town of two or three hundred houses, down to about 20 ft on a busy great road.
  • The ground behind each row is its lots' gardens.
  • A walled town's wall runs no more than about 6,600 ft round.

At its ends a town does not stop at a line. A long, narrow settlement facing a highway and living by its traffic is the street village, and the maps give a town's ends that form along its roads.

The rule the map follows for a town's ends:

  • An unwalled town's built edge does not stop square at its last houses where a road leaves it.
  • Along each road that carries traffic, houses run on out as a ribbon one lot deep on either side, thinning with distance.
  • The road carrying the most traffic has the longest ribbon.
  • On a walled town the ribbon outside the gate is the gate market, at What is in a town's gate market, and how far out along the road does it run?

The ribbon's length, its depth of one lot, its thinning and its being longest on the busiest road are each a GUESS: no page we read gives a length for the strip or a rate at which its houses thinned.
