How our maps draw lodgings for lords and officials on the road (honjin)

No map yet draws a lord's lodging. Where one does, these are its rules.

  • A lodging for lords and officials is a gated house of one story.
    A honjin was single-story in principle, with an imposing gate and a wide entrance, and the gate and the entrance are what set it apart from the inns on the same street.
  • It is the grandest house in its street.
    That it outranks every inn and shop beside it is this project's reading of its gate, its entrance and its raised audience room, a GUESS: no page we read compares it with its neighbors.
  • Its site is at most about 50,795 sq ft.
    That is the site of Kusatsu's honjin, among the largest that survive. A second-rank lodging, a waki-honjin, is drawn smaller, since it was smaller, but how much smaller no page we read says.
  • Where in the street does the lodging stand?
    The record does not say, so no place in the street is preferred.
  • A waystation that hosts such guests may keep a lodging court of its own.
    Busy trunk-road waystations occasionally host ceremonial Imperial travelers, and the Chinese station at Jimingyi kept an official lodging court three courtyards deep.
