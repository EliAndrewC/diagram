How our maps draw a town's street front from above

A town map draws its street front by three choices, each rolled for the town from its seed: how wide each merchant's frontage is, by wealth; whether the houses touch along the street or stand apart; and what the roofs are made of. Which of the two traditions the town follows, the Japanese or the Chinese, governs all three; that choice is at How our maps choose between the courtyard house and the Japanese house.

A map seen from overhead shows the roof, not the stories. A second story does not change a house's footprint, so it changes nothing drawn in plan, while the roof's material does: thatch, board and tile read differently in color and texture.

  • A merchant's frontage is set by wealth, in three bands.
    On the Japanese model a small shop is about 10 to 13 ft wide, an ordinary one about 26 to 33 ft and a rich house 65 ft or more, as in Kyoto's three bands, each about 33 ft deep at the street, the depth of a city's front shop, with its lot running on behind it.
  • On the Chinese model, the ordinary shop is about 41 to 49 ft wide, with a courtyard behind.
    That is the five-bay shop of one Chinese county seat.
  • How many shops fall in each band?
    A GUESS: no page we read counts a town's merchants by band.
  • The shop symbol, 48 by 32 ft, is drawn by convention.
    Its depth is a city front shop's, about five ken. Its width is at the top of the Chinese five-bay range, and one and a half to nearly twice an ordinary Japanese shopkeeper's four or five ken; only Kyoto's large houses and Edo's great stores are wider. So the drawn shop sits in the rich band on the Japanese model and the ordinary band on the Chinese one. How the symbol is drawn, and a shop's size at real scale, is at How our maps draw shops and trades in towns and villages.

Whether the houses touch is a choice rolled for each town.

  • A busy town builds wall to wall.
    A town on a great road or at a market builds each house across the full width of its lot, touching its neighbors along the street, as a busy highway's post towns did.
  • A quiet town sets its houses apart.
    A town on a side road or in the hills sets its houses back behind a front yard, with a gap between one and the next, farmhouse fashion.
  • A town on the Chinese model is close-set.
    Chinese houses stood close, with fire gables between them.
  • How often does a town take each Japanese form?
    The weight between the two is a GUESS, since no page we read counts them.
  • The joint between two touching shops is drawn by a convention.
    It is the city's, at Machiya row density: a commercial street is a continuous built edge.

The roofs are rolled for each town among three forms.

  • Tile, in a town on the Chinese model or a western-style Japanese town.
    Western Japan roofed its town houses in tile, and a Chinese county seat's old houses were roofed in small gray tile.
  • Board weighted with stones, in an eastern-style or hill town.
    Eastern Japan roofed in board, weighted with stones.
  • Thatch, in a quiet town that is half a village.
    A half-farming station such as Ouchi lined its road with thatched houses.
  • How do a map's towns divide among the three roofs?
    The weights are a GUESS, and so is giving a rich merchant's house tile in a board-roofed town: no page we read counts a small town's roofs or gives that merchant's.
  • A storehouse stands at the back of its lot, never on the street.
    Kyoto set its storehouses at the back. Doing the same in a small town is a GUESS, since no page we read says where a small town's storehouse stood.
  • In a great city, the street-front house itself may be plastered like a storehouse.
    Late Edo's were.
