How our maps draw a town's street front (machinami)

A town map draws its street front by three choices: how wide each merchant's frontage is, by wealth, from about 10 ft for a small shop to 65 ft or more for a rich house; whether the houses touch along the street or stand apart; and whether the roofs are tile, board or thatch. Each is chosen at random for the town, and the same town always gets the same choices. A shop is drawn as a symbol 48 by 32 ft. Which of the two traditions the town follows, the Japanese or the Chinese, governs all three; that choice is at How our maps choose between the courtyard house and the Japanese house.

A map seen from overhead shows the roof, not the stories. A second story does not change the ground a house covers, so it changes nothing in a drawing from above, while the roof's material does: thatch, board and tile read differently in color and texture.

  • On the Japanese model, a merchant's frontage is set by wealth, in three bands.
    A small shop is about 10 to 13 ft wide, an ordinary one about 26 to 33 ft and a rich house 65 ft or more, as in Kyoto's three bands.
  • How wide is an ordinary shop on the Chinese model?
    About 41 to 49 ft, with a courtyard behind: the width of the five-bay shop of one Chinese county seat.
  • How many shops fall in each band is our own guess.
    A GUESS: no page we read counts a town's merchants by band.
  • The shop symbol, 48 by 32 ft, is drawn by convention.
    Its depth is that of the shop room at the front of an Edo townhouse lot, about five ken (~30-33 ft), with the lot running on behind it. Its width is at the top of the Chinese five-bay range, and one and a half to nearly twice an ordinary Japanese shopkeeper's four or five ken; only the large houses of 10 ken or more, in Kyoto as in Edo, are wider. So on the Japanese model the drawn shop is wider than any ordinary shop, though short of the rich band's 65 ft, and on the Chinese model it is an ordinary shop. How the symbol is drawn, and a shop's size at real scale, is at How our maps draw shops and trades in towns and villages.

Whether the houses touch is chosen at random for each town.

  • A busy town builds wall to wall.
    A town on a great road or at a market builds each house across the full width of its lot, touching its neighbors along the street, as a busy highway's post towns did.
  • A quiet town sets its houses apart.
    A town on a side road or in the hills sets its houses back behind a front yard, with a gap between one and the next, farmhouse fashion.
  • How often a Japanese-model town takes each form is our own guess.
    The weight between the two is a GUESS, since no page we read counts them.
  • A town on the Chinese model is close-set.
    Traditional Chinese wooden houses stood close along narrow lanes, and in the Hui style horse-head gable walls rose between house and house.
  • The joint between two touching shops is drawn by a convention.
    It is the city's, at Machiya row density: a commercial street is a continuous built edge.

Each town's roofs are chosen at random among three forms.

  • A town on the Chinese model, or a Japanese town built as in western Japan, is roofed in tile.
    Western Japan roofed its town houses in tile, and a Chinese county seat's old houses were roofed in small gray tile.
  • A Japanese town built as in eastern Japan, or a hill town, is roofed in board weighted with stones.
    Eastern Japan roofed in board, weighted with stones.
  • A quiet town that is half a village is roofed in thatch.
    A half-farming post town such as Ouchi lined its road with thatched houses.
  • How a map's towns divide among the three roofs is our own guess.
    The weights are a GUESS: no page we read counts a small town's roofs.
  • A rich merchant's house is roofed in tile, even in a town roofed in board.
    A GUESS: no page we read gives the roof of a rich merchant's house in a board-roofed town.

Two last choices are not about the roof: where a storehouse stands, and whether a great city's house fronts are plastered.

  • Where does a town's storehouse stand?
    Our maps put it at the back of its lot, never on the street. Kyoto set its storehouses at the back. Doing the same in a small town is a GUESS, since no page we read says where a small town's storehouse stood.
  • A great city's plastered house front is not drawn.
    Late in the Edo period, plastering the street-front house like a storehouse spread through the city of Edo, but from above only the roof shows, so our maps draw the house as any other.
