How our maps fill a town's edge with wood and grass

A town's margin beyond its fields is never drawn bare. The ground there was worked, so our maps clothe it in one or both of the two covers the history shows: a cut-over broadleaf wood and a cut grass ground.

  • Which cover a town's margin carries, and how much of each, is a knob rolled per town.
    The share is a GUESS: no page we read says how much of a town's margin was wood and how much grass. Today the wood and the grass ground are drawn by separate passes, and the roll between them is not yet made.
  • The wood is drawn young and low.
    Many thin stems, not a closed canopy of old trees: such woods were cut and left to sprout from the stumps, so they stood as thin, low trees in clumps, a form attested to at least the Muromachi period in the kunugi woods of the upper Ina River.
  • The grass ground is drawn open, with no fence and no animals.
    It was ground where thatch and fodder were cut, and our maps draw it as a cutting ground, not a pasture.
