How our maps fill a town's edge with wood and grass

A town's margin beyond its fields is never drawn bare. The ground there was worked, so our maps clothe it in the two covers the history shows: a cut-over broadleaf wood and a cut grass ground.

  • Which cover a town's margin carries, and how much of each, is meant to be a knob set at random for each town.
    The share is a GUESS: no page we read says how much of a town's margin was wood and how much grass.
  • The wood is drawn young and low.
    Many thin stems, not a closed canopy of old trees: such woods were cut and left to sprout from the stumps, so they stood as thin, low trees in clumps, and the kunugi woods of the upper Ina River were cut for charcoal from at least the Muromachi period.
  • The grass ground is drawn open, with no fence and no animals.
    It was ground where thatch and fodder were cut, and our maps draw it as a cutting ground, not a pasture.
  • Our maps draw no marsh below a town's fields.
    A village often left the wet ground below its fields undrained, but the more a settlement had invested in draining its land, the less of that ground was left, and a town had invested the most; that gradient is this record's reconstruction from attested pieces, set out at No toe marsh at town/city scale - the drainage-investment gradient.
