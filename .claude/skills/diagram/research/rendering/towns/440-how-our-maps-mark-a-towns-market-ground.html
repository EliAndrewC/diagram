How our maps mark a town's market ground

A map shows a town on an ordinary day, between market days, so it draws no stalls and nothing taken from how often the market meets. What it marks is the ground the market meets on, in one of the two places the record finds: a clear strip along the main street with the market god's stone at its end, or open ground before a temple's or shrine's gate. A provincial city keeps the same market ground and adds, at each gate, room for the caravans.

  • Where a town's market meets is a knob.
    Two places are attested for a town's periodic market: along the main street, as in the market towns of Edo-period Kanto, and before the gate of a temple or shrine, as the medieval market met and the Chinese temple fair did. So each town rolls one from its map's seed.
  • On the street, the house fronts stand back by a clear strip about 6 to 12 ft deep.
    That is the one to two ken of the Kanto market ground, kept between the street and the house lots for putting up stalls on market day. It runs along the stretch of street where the market meets.
  • A market god's stone stands at one end of the stretch, at a crossroads or a bridge foot.
    Those are among the places the stone is recorded at, and its body was most often a round natural stone. It is drawn as a small round mark; its size is a GUESS, since no page we read measures one.
  • Before a gate, the ground in front of the temple's or shrine's gate is kept open.
    The medieval market met mainly before temple and shrine gates.
  • No stalls are drawn.
    No page we read says what a stall was built of, or whether anything of it stood between market days, so the map shows only the ground, a GUESS that nothing stood.
  • A town draws no walled market.
    The walled markets the record finds are a capital's, and the Song dynasty ended the system they belonged to.
  • A town draws no festival ground of its own.
    A festival uses the shrine and temple precincts and the route of the god's procession, and an otabisho, where one is drawn at all, is a small shrine or a house. No page we read describes a square or festival ground a town kept open.
  • The rest of what a town keeps for the market crowd is drawn under other rules.
    The lodging for those who stay over is at The market-day flophouse - who actually stays over, and the stage the troupes play on, beside a temple, at Where does a town's theater stage stand, and which way does it open?

The rule the map follows: a town rolls where its market meets.

  • On the street, the house fronts along the stretch where it meets stand back from the street by a clear strip one to two ken deep, about 6 to 12 ft, and a market-god stone stands at one end of the stretch, at a crossroads or at a bridge foot, drawn as a small round mark whose size is a guess.
  • Before a gate, the ground in front of the temple's or shrine's gate is kept open.
  • Nothing of the stalls is drawn.

A provincial city is a bigger version of the market center a town already is, so the town's civic furniture carries up to it rather than changing in kind: fireproof storehouses behind the merchant houses, market-day lodging, a theater stage in a temple precinct. What a city adds is scale at the gate.

  • Three town rules do not carry up to a city.
    A city has no farmer plurality and no farmers living inside it; it has temples rather than a monastery with a clan patron; and its wall is a regular defensive ring rather than the irregular run a town's may take. This is this project's own reading of the town tier's sections; no source we read states it for a city.
  • A city draws at least five merchant storehouses.
    That floor is low against the record's count, at How many fireproof storehouses stand behind a street, and where on the lot?: two great merchant houses alone hold eight.
  • Just inside each city gate stands a caravan cluster.
    A cheap lodging house, a prominent inn and a large stables stand within about 1,020 ft of the gate, with open ground around the stables for tying up and penning: no more than four dwellings within about 225 ft of it. The fencing itself is not drawn.
  • Is a city's gate cluster supported by the record?
    Only in part. That a town inn can absorb a caravan into a small stable yard and a city cannot is this project's own contrast, and the evidence cuts against it. The post stations that carried the road's traffic were themselves towns, a hundred horses kept standing at one was not always enough, and the answer the sources record is not a bigger yard but the neighboring villages, which supplied the men and horses that ran short. So the crowding at the gate is real and documented; that a city meets it worse than a town is not.
  • A city's theater stage has a wider viewing ground than a town's, about 555 ft across.
    It stands beside a temple, as a town's does.
  • The city's counts and distances are calibrated, not sourced.
    They are set against the three drawn provincial cities rather than derived from a source.
