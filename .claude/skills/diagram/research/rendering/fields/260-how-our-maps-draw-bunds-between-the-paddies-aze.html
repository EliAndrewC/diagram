How our maps draw bunds between the paddies (aze)

Our maps draw the bunds between paddies as one connected network of shared walls, each about 1.5 ft wide, its real width, in a dark red-brown line. The walls meet at T-junctions, bend and wander as a hand-piled bund does, and never step sideways; the path from a hamlet to its fields runs onto them. Some bunds carry a row of soybeans, drawn as dark green beads.

  • The dividing bund is drawn about 1.5 ft wide, at its real size.
    That is within the one to two shaku read: the Edo survey's reckoning of one shaku and the two-shaku bunds of the field replanned in 1869. It sits inside the widths of the early Yayoi bunds dug up at Hattori as well. The line never thins below half a pixel, so on a city map it still stands for about 1.5 ft.
  • The bund's height is not drawn.
    Nothing on a map depends on it. Where a map's explanation gives a height, it is a few inches, the height of the early bunds read, not the foot of today's standard bund, which is attested only in modern times.
  • A walking bund is allowed 2 to 5 ft, a GUESS.
    No page we read gives its width, so it is held between the two-shaku bund and the one-ken (about 6 ft) farm road of the field replanned in 1869. How close a farmhouse stands to the bund is at How close does a farmhouse stand to the paddy?

Two paddies side by side share one bund on our maps, never two.

  • The bunds are one connected network, meeting at T-junctions.
    No free-standing ring of bund is drawn inside a field, and no bare strip is left between two paddies: a strip of watered rice land is worth more than any dry field, and no farmer left it idle between two walls. A gap too narrow to plant is joined to the paddy it shares the most bund with, so the two walls become one.
  • A plot that laps a little over its neighbor is left alone.
    A plot drawn over part of its neighbor paints out the bund it covers, so the pair still reads as one shared wall; only a plot nearly inside another is a fault.
  • Ground more than 24 ft wide between two paddies is not a doubled bund.
    Wider than that, it is bare floor, and the rule that the fan of paddies leaves no gap governs it instead.
  • A pocket too thin to bund is joined to its neighbor, not planted.
    A needle-thin basin could not be leveled or bunded at any reasonable cost, so it takes the same thickness rule as the toe of a fan of paddies.

A bund on our maps may bend, but it never hops sideways and carries on.

  • A bund runs on, bends, or ends at a T-junction.
    A basin may be a trapezoid and a wall may bend, but a wall that runs, steps a few feet sideways and carries on parallel to itself is not drawn. On this project's reading, a real boundary that stepped had a ditch, a path, a rock or an inherited holding at its corner, and at the scale our maps are drawn those things are drawn themselves, so a step with nothing at its corner is no boundary at all - a GUESS.
  • A long sideways offset makes an L-shaped plot, not a step.
    Past a certain length the offset is a limb of the plot, the honest odd shape a patchwork of old paddies has.
  • The maps draw only the wavering fabric, never a plain ruled on the jori grid.
    A western plain whose fields descend from the jori division was ruled, and our maps do not yet draw that second form; see How our maps draw rice paddies and their plots (suiden).

No bund on our maps is ruled straight or square at its corners, and how the earth is drawn at a corner depends on whether the bund there is shared.

  • Where plots are held apart, their corners give up earth; where the bund is shared, the crossings gain it.
    Where the plots are separate shapes with a real gap between them - a polder, a block of ponds - each plot's corners are rounded off and the bund, the space between, widens there. Where a field is divided by shared bunds, the basins must tile without a gap, so the dividing lines stay and earth is piled into the crossings: a junction becomes a lumpy node of bund, and the four basin corners read rounded because the earth has taken them. A polder's plots share no corner, so nothing is piled at a crossing there.
  • How large is a junction's pile of earth?
    Four to six feet across, a GUESS: no page we read gives a size. It is drawn at true size, never thinner than the map's line so it never vanishes, and never inflated past the node itself, since a dot sized only to be legible would be a fake fifteen-foot earthwork at every crossing.
  • Each bund line wanders on its own.
    Rounding the corners alone reads as a circle at each crossing, less natural than before; what reads as stamped is a lattice warped as one piece, every line keeping its neighbors' shape. Fragmented tenure did not look like that: each bund was laid by eye by a different household in a different decade (Polder parcels were a private tenure patchwork). So each row and column line of a polder takes its own gentle bow, up to about a tenth of the grid's spacing, still meeting its neighbors at the shared crossings; the bow fades to nothing at both ends and is kept off the four boundary lines, so the block's edge, its dike and its ring canal do not move, and the ditches on the column lines meander with them.
  • In a valley fan, no two rows of paddies carry the same shape.
    A point drifts downhill as well as along its row, by an amount that depends on where it sits along the row and on which row it is. The drift fades to nothing at a sector's first and last rows, which the water pins: the head row sits on the supply canal and takes water through cuts in its own bund, and the last row sits on the drain (Where does the water go once it has watered the paddies?).
  • Some corners are drawn broad, some all but square.
    A corner's rounding is drawn from a wide, long-tailed spread, because on this project's reading corners really do differ that much: the one on the walked side wears to a broad sweep while the one behind a neighbor's bund keeps its angle for years - a GUESS. The arc itself is roughened, since a mathematically clean curve reads as stamped.
  • The earth at a shared crossing is piled corner by corner.
    Each basin corner meeting at a crossing gets its own fillet of earth, with its own reach, and roughly a quarter are left bare, so a crossing can be piled heavily on one side and untouched on the other.

The bund's color, and its showing at all in summer, are choices made so the map reads.

  • The bund is drawn in a dark, red-leaning mud brown.
    A pale tan would depict 4-5 ft of bare earth - a walking bund on every boundary - and reads as an orange lattice over the green. The dark brown was chosen from a ladder of five colors rendered on one village, over a lighter chestnut that stays more plainly brown at hairline width: a line that dark blends toward black against the rice green at map view, and that weight was preferred.
  • The pale tan is kept for earthworks of real width.
    Perimeter dikes, the house pads on dike tops and the banks of dike ponds are filled in it, where the ground really is bare earth several feet across. A terrace lip takes the dark color but a wider stroke, because a terrace's near-vertical face has real width seen from above, where a flat bund does not.
  • The bund shows in high summer by a map drawing convention.
    The dark color is true of spring, when the ridge is fresh wet plaster. By high summer, the season the paddies are drawn in, a real bund carries mown grass and a row of beans, and on this project's reading the green bund then all but disappears into the green field; no page we read says so. The network is drawn anyway, because it is what carries the plots' structure to the eye: the honest summer view, green on green, would erase the field's whole fabric.

The rule the map follows: the field path runs from the hamlet to the paddy's outer bund and joins it, however short that leaves the path; it never ends in open ground short of the bund and never passes through a gap in it, since no page we read tells of either. Where no path is left to draw, the hamlet's nearest lane runs on to the bund. That the path joins the bund at its point nearest the hamlet is a GUESS. How a lane runs across the dry fields is at Where could a village lane run?

The soybeans on the bunds are drawn by a convention of their own.

  • Each bean plant is drawn as a round bead about 3 ft across, in a single row on the bund.
    The beans grew in a row along the bund, and a stretch of bund that carries them shows at least two beads, because a single bead does not read as a row of anything. The real plant is a knee-to-waist-high bush, 50 to 125 cm (~20-49 in) tall; the bead says only where it stands, and since no page we read gives a plant's spread, the bead's width is not compared to one.
  • The beads are a deep pine green, darker than the plant.
    Real soybean leaves are medium green. The darker green was chosen from a rendered ladder of three candidates, because the old olive sat between the rice green and the bund brown and vanished at map zoom. The two others on the ladder were declined with it: a hunter green that grayed out, and a forest green that read as black against the near-black bund.
