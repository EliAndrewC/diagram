How our maps draw rice paddies and their plots (suiden)

Our maps draw a settlement's rice land as a patchwork of small, irregular paddies, each basin at its real size in feet, some 40 to 60 ft across, and never as the tidy grid of rectangles that modern land consolidation laid out. No basin narrows to a point sharper than 25 degrees, none is a scrap under a quarter of the basin its field was cut to, and none is shaped like an arrowhead.

  • The patchwork is drawn everywhere, even on the western plains.
    The plains of western Japan were laid out on the ancient jori grid, so the patchwork drawn there is a deviation.
  • A planned reclamation's fields may be drawn in equal strips, never in a checkerboard.
    In the planned reclamation road villages of the Kanto plain, each new-field house's land was laid out in a strip of equal area behind it, and no page we read shows an Edo reclamation's paddies in a grid.

The rule the map follows: a paddy is drawn at a few se or less, never at the one-tan block of Meiji or the larger blocks after it.

  • A basin is about 39 ft square on a hamlet map, 47 ft on a village map and 58 ft across on a town map.
    That is one leveled, diked basin: about 1,500 sq ft (0.03 acre) on a hamlet map and 0.05 acre on a village map. It is worked out in real feet at whatever scale the sheet is drawn to, so it does not grow or shrink with the sheet.
  • Some of our maps, the hand-authored ones drawn before the comb fields, divide a field only as far as the parcel.
    They draw plots of about 0.10 acre on the village maps among them and about 0.19 acre on the rest. The larger plot is a parcel, one of the scattered fields a holding was made of; the smaller is the leveled basin. Neither is enlarged to make the map easier to read.
  • Why is a basin smaller than a parcel?
    On this project's reading a parcel on any slope is split into several basins, because each must be level to hold its water at an even depth - a GUESS.
  • The maps allow a household about nine plots of 0.02 to 0.25 acre.
    Both are this project's own figures, a GUESS: no page we read gives either.
  • On a valley hamlet's map the paddies are drawn larger, about 2.8 se (0.07 acre).
    That is a third larger than the usual basin and half the parcel recorded for Kami-Baba village in its 1684 land survey, and they are drawn as irregular as the rest.
  • Why is a town's basin about 58 ft across?
    On a town map a basin is about 58 ft across, on rows 52 to 72 ft apart, about 0.08 acre (~3,600 sq ft). The size is a calibration against what the town's canals can feed, set just under the smallest paddy lot in cultivation in the 1602 register of Iwamuro village; it lies inside the 0.02-0.25 acre band, and no average plot of before modern times was found to set it by. Nor is it the one-tan (quarter-acre) block that Meiji land consolidation laid out.
  • A coarser town grain starved the canals.
    At about 66 ft across, the rule that an offtake stand at least two plot-widths along its canal put the least spacing between offtakes above the length of the short canals a town has room for, and the network stopped delivering water to its paddies.
  • Town paddies are drawn without their small ponds, rocks and grave islands.
    At town scale and above, a pocket pond, a rock or a grave island in the paddy stops reading as a small feature and reads as a pond and an ossuary standing in the middle of the rice, so they are left off. This is a drawing convention, not a claim that town paddies had none.
  • The soybeans are drawn as a food crop.
    The soy on our maps, about 6% of their crops, grows on the dry fields and along the bunds, as the azemame were sown on the bunds and harvested with the rice; it is not drawn as the paddy's fertilizer.

A basin's shape follows from what a paddy is: a level, diked, puddled basin holding standing water at an even depth.

  • A fan of paddies narrows toward its drain, and its bunds converge with it.
    That is how our maps build a cascade fan, and the convergence is kept. It is the drawn form, not the alluvial landform, which fans outward from its apex.
  • A narrow basin is allowed.
    A terrace bed is usually 2 to 6 m (~7-20 ft) wide, and the smallest paddy at Shiroyone Senmaida is half a meter (~20 in) square. So the maps set no least width for a basin; that would have been the obvious rule, and a wrong one.
  • No basin tapers to a point sharper than 25 degrees.
    At a point of 7.5 degrees a basin is 5 ft wide 40 ft back from its tip and 2.6 ft wide at 20 ft, while its bund is about 1.5 ft of puddled mud on each side, so its last yards are two bunds with no floor between them: nothing that can be leveled, hold a depth or be planted. At 25 degrees the same wedge is 17 ft wide 40 ft back, a workable strip. The maps refuse a point sharper than 25 degrees.
  • A point the maps refuse is cut off into a headland along the drain, or taken into the basin beside it.
    The odd corner is taken into the basin beside it rather than walled off on its own. A strip that would draw every basin it touches out to a needle point stays bare, with the fan's floor drawn under it. That a real fan did the same is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • The maps set no least area in acres for a basin.
    Such a floor was considered and declined. The paddies at Senmaida average about 18 m2 (~194 sq ft), and the smallest basin on our four sample hamlet maps, 240 sq ft, is already larger than that; a floor in acres would condemn the most famous paddies in Japan.
  • Is a scrap on the valley floor worth its own bund, as a tiny terrace bed is?
    No. On a terrace the wall already exists: the riser is built for the slope, and the alternative to a tiny bed is no paddy at all. On a valley floor the bund is the whole structure, puddled mud re-plastered every year before the paddy is flooded, and the alternative to a scrap is not no rice but a bigger basin next door. This is this project's reasoning.
  • No basin is smaller than a quarter of the design cell its fan was cut to.
    A quarter of the cell's area is half its size each way, so anything smaller is a fragment of a cell, not a small one.
  • Why a quarter of the design cell?
    A quarter of the cell is where the bund's cost doubles. With a 1.5 ft bund, half charged to each side, the bund takes 8.1% of the ground a hamlet's 38.6 ft cell encloses, and 16.2% at 19.2 ft; a quarter of the cell is the square at which that share has doubled. The point barely moves with scale - 0.248 of the cell at 38.6 ft, 0.256 at the village's 47 ft - so the figure is not tuned to one map.
  • The size rule touches under 2% of basins and changes no acreage.
    Of the 2,829 basins our four sample hamlet maps would draw without it, 1.63% fall under a quarter of their cell: small parcels of 240-370 sq ft against a 1,488 sq ft cell, not absurd ones. Each is taken into the basin it shares the most bund with, so the planted acreage, the field's outline and the count of households are unchanged; Inashiro keeps its 20.5 and 19.5 acres and its 15 households.
  • A basin may have any number of sides.
    A rule that every basin have four sides was considered and declined: it would bring back the tidy grid of modern consolidation. A triangular basin is legitimate, and several stand on every map; a basin is refused for its size, not its number of sides, and a clipped corner at the fan's edge is the shape a fragment naturally takes.
  • The maps refuse a basin shaped like an arrowhead.
    A basin may taper, and a basin may be concave, as one wrapped round its neighbor is; one that does both - a point sharper than 40 degrees and a notch deep enough to bring its solidity under 0.90 - is an arrowhead, and the maps refuse it. Of the 2,777 basins on our four sample hamlet maps, 71 had the point alone, 49 the notch alone and 13 both. The thresholds are round on purpose, not fitted to any one basin.
