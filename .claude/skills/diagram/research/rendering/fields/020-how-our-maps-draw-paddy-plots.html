How our maps draw paddy plots

Our maps draw a settlement's rice land as a patchwork of small, irregular paddies, each basin at its real size in feet, and never as the grid of equal rectangles that modern land consolidation laid out. The rules below set how big a basin is on each kind of map, and which shapes a basin may not take: none tapers to a point, none is a scrap under a quarter of the basin its field was cut to, and none is shaped like an arrowhead.

  • The patchwork is drawn everywhere, even on the western plains.
    The plains of western Japan were laid out on the ancient jori grid, so the patchwork drawn there is a deviation, until the jori grid, recorded as a future option at Where does a field's water come from?, is built.
  • A planned reclamation may run its fields in equal strips, never in a checkerboard.
    Edo new-field villages were laid out with each house's land in a strip of equal area behind it, and no page we read shows an Edo reclamation's paddies in a grid.

The rule the map follows: a planned reclamation's fields may run in equal strips from the road or canal that founded them; the paddies are never drawn as a checkerboard of equal rectangles unless the ground is jori ground; a paddy is drawn at the size of a few se or less, never at the one-tan block of Meiji or the larger blocks after it.

  • How big is a basin on a hamlet or village map?
    About 0.05 acre, a 47 ft square, for one leveled, diked basin. It is worked out in real feet at whatever scale the sheet is drawn to, so a basin is the same real size on a hamlet map and on a city map.
  • Some older maps divide a field down to the parcel instead.
    They draw a grain of about 0.10 acre on the village maps and about 0.19 acre on the rest. The coarser grain is a parcel, one of the scattered fields a holding was made of; the finer is the leveled basin. Neither is enlarged to make the map easier to read.
  • Why is a basin smaller than a parcel?
    On this project's reading a parcel on any slope is split into several basins, because each must be level to hold its water at an even depth - a GUESS.
  • What size and number of plots do the maps allow a household?
    Plots of 0.02 to 0.25 acre, about nine to a household. Both are this project's own figures, a GUESS: no page we read gives either.
  • A valley hamlet's larger paddies are about 2.8 se (0.07 acre).
    That is a third larger than the usual basin and half the Kami-Baba parcel of 1684, a size the registers of the 1600s hold, and they are drawn as irregular as the rest.
  • How big is a basin on a town map?
    About 58 ft across, on rows 52 to 72 ft apart, about 0.08 acre (~3,600 sq ft). The size is a calibration against what the town's canals can feed, set just under the smallest paddy lot in cultivation in the 1602 register of Iwamuro village; it lies inside the 0.02-0.25 acre band, and no average plot of before modern times was found to set it by. The planned quarter-acre plot is not its anchor.
  • A coarser town grain starved the canals.
    At about 66 ft across, the rule that an offtake stand at least two plot-widths along its canal put the least spacing between offtakes above the length of the short canals a town has room for, and the network stopped delivering water to its paddies.
  • Town paddies are drawn without their small ponds, rocks and grave islands.
    At town scale and above, a pocket pond, a rock or a grave island in the paddy stops reading as a small feature and reads as a pond and an ossuary standing in the middle of the rice, so they are left off. This is a drawing convention, not a claim that town paddies had none.
  • The soybeans are drawn as a food crop.
    The soy on our maps, about 6% of their crops, grows on the dry fields and along the bunds, as the azemame were sown on the bunds and harvested with the rice; it is not drawn as the paddy's fertilizer.

A basin's shape follows from what a paddy is: a level, diked, puddled basin holding standing water at an even depth.

  • A fan of paddies narrows toward its drain, and its bunds converge with it.
    That is how our maps build a cascade fan, and the convergence is kept. It is the drawn form, not the alluvial landform, which fans outward from its apex.
  • A narrow basin is allowed.
    Real terraced paddies are tiny - at Shiroyone Senmaida about 18 m2 (~194 sq ft) each, the smallest half a meter (~20 in) square - though a terrace bed is usually 2 to 6 m (~7-20 ft) wide. So the maps set no least width for a basin; that would have been the obvious rule, and a wrong one.
  • No basin tapers to a point.
    At a point of 7.5 degrees a basin is 5 ft wide 40 ft back from its tip and 2.6 ft wide at 20 ft, while its bund is about 1.5 ft of puddled mud on each side, so its last yards are two bunds with no floor between them: nothing that can be leveled, hold a depth or be planted. At 25 degrees the same wedge is 17 ft wide 40 ft back, a workable strip. The maps refuse a point sharper than 25 degrees, and one sharper than 15 degrees is a fault.
  • What becomes of a point the maps refuse?
    It is cut off into a headland along the drain, or the odd corner is taken into the basin beside it rather than walled off on its own. A strip that would needle every basin it touches stays bare, with the fan's floor drawn under it, as the slivers dropped for thinness always have. That a real fan did the same is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • The maps set no least area in acres for a basin.
    Such a floor was considered and declined. The paddies at Senmaida average about 18 m2 (~194 sq ft), and the smallest basin on our scripted hamlets, 240 sq ft, is already larger than that; a floor in acres would condemn the most famous paddies in Japan.
  • A scrap on the valley floor is still not worth its own bund.
    On a terrace the wall already exists: the riser is built for the slope, and the alternative to a tiny bed is no paddy at all. On a valley floor the bund is the whole structure, puddled mud re-plastered every year before the paddy is flooded, and the alternative to a scrap is not no rice but a bigger basin next door. This is this project's reasoning.
  • How small may a basin be?
    A quarter of the design cell its fan was cut to; a basin under a fifth of one is a fault. A quarter of the cell's area is half its size each way, so anything smaller is a fragment of a cell, not a small one.
  • Why a quarter?
    It is also where the bund's cost doubles. With a 1.5 ft bund, half charged to each side, the bund takes 8.1% of the ground a hamlet's 38.6 ft cell encloses, and 16.2% at 19.2 ft; a quarter of the cell is the square at which that share has doubled. The point barely moves with scale - 0.248 of the cell at 38.6 ft, 0.256 at the village's 47 ft - so the figure is not tuned to one map.
  • The size rule changes no acreage, but it moves the houses.
    Of the 2,829 basins on the four scripted hamlets, 1.63% sat under a quarter of their cell and 0.46% under a fifth: small parcels of 240-370 sq ft against a 1,488 sq ft cell, not absurd ones. Each is taken into the basin it shares the most bund with, so the planted acreage, the field's outline and the count of households are unchanged; Inashiro keeps its 20.5 and 19.5 acres and its 15 households. The houses around the fields do move, since the count of drawn plots changed.
  • A basin may have any number of sides.
    A rule that every basin have four sides was considered and declined: it would bring back the tidy grid of modern consolidation. A triangular basin is legitimate, and several stand on every map; what was wrong with the scraps was their size, and a clipped corner at the fan's edge is the shape a fragment naturally takes.
  • No basin is shaped like an arrowhead.
    A basin may taper, and a basin may be concave, as one wrapped round its neighbor is; one that does both - a point sharper than 40 degrees and a notch deep enough to bring its solidity under 0.90 - is an arrowhead, and the maps refuse it. Of the 2,777 carved basins on the four scripted hamlets, 71 had the point alone, 49 the notch alone and 13 both, few enough to read one by one. The thresholds are round on purpose; fitted to the basin that prompted the rule (39 degrees and 0.88) they would have caught 11.
