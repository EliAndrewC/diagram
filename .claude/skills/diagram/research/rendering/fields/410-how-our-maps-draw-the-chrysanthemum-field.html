How our maps draw the chrysanthemum field

One map draws a chrysanthemum field: Hirameki, a walled county seat, keeps one inside its west rampart, and no public street is cut through it. No other town grows flower plots in its ring of farmland.

  • The field is the setting's, not history's.
    In the setting the chrysanthemum is the flower of the ninth month's festival, when "Gardens, temples, shrines, and fine homes display fine specimens of the seasonal flowers". The field is Hirameki's own, standing for the Emperor in a town that changed hands in war.
  • An Imperial field in a provincial town is a deliberate deviation.
    No page we read describes a planted field of chrysanthemums kept for a ruler or a court, so the field departs from the history on purpose. It stands inside the wall at the cost of dwellings a planned town of the same size would house, which is why Hirameki is excused from housing its declared population (What every settlement map is told before it is drawn, and when it may break a rule).
  • A town's ring of farmland grows no flower plots.
    Every market case read is on the edge of a great city, Edo, and none is of the farmland around an ordinary provincial town; nothing read describes flowers grown for a shrine or a temple in a plot of their own. So the one flower field drawn is the Imperial one.
  • How big is the field drawn?
    About 220 by 340 ft, some 1.7 acres. That size is a GUESS: no page we read gives the size of a historical flower field to set it against.
