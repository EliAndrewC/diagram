How our maps draw dry fields and their furrows

Our maps draw the dry fields as a hem of small rectangular plots along the supply canal at the field's high edge, just above it, where the paddy water stops: the higher, drier ground the dry crops took (Dry fields and their crops). Each plot is barley, millet, buckwheat or soybean, drawn with furrows, and the plots beside the canal stand square to it.

  • No grain plot is drawn beside a house.
    The plot a household worked by its house was its kitchen bed, and no page we read puts its grain or beans there, so the dry crops go only in the hem.
  • Whether a fan's dry middle stays wild is a knob.
    An alluvial fan's middle was often left wild until late, but old heartlands cleared theirs early, so each map rolls it from its seed: where the middle stays wild the hem keeps to the fan's toe and leaves the middle to scrub, and where it is cleared the whole canal is hemmed. Wild is weighted three to one, a GUESS, since the record calls it the usual case without a figure; the toe is taken to begin two-thirds of the way down the fall from the fan's head, also a GUESS.
  • How much of each crop a map grows is rolled from its seed.
    The mix of the four crops is a GUESS at their proportions.

The crops are drawn in the colors of their own season, which is not the season of the rest of the map.

  • Each crop is drawn at its ripest color.
    Barley tan-gold, millet ochre, buckwheat pale with reddish stems, soybean green.
  • Which season should the dry plots show?
    That is left open. The map is drawn in high summer, the season the paddy stands flooded and green (How our maps draw bunds between the paddies). In high summer barley is already cut, harvested around June, so a barley plot is stubble or bare; the millets are tall, green and still heading; soybean is a low green bush; buckwheat may be in flower, or its autumn sowing bare ground. The ripest colors put May's barley and October's millet beside July's paddy. Whether to keep one season across the sheet, or each crop at its most recognizable color, is not yet decided.
  • The maps do not draw the rotation of rice, barley and soybean.
    It is today's practice, and no page we read dates it earlier.

Every dry plot is drawn with furrows, and which way they run is set tract by tract.

  • The furrowed texture is accurate; its spacing is a GUESS.
    A premodern dry field was worked in ridged rows, but no page we read gives how far apart they stood in a Japanese field.
  • Neighboring plots form tracts that share one row direction.
    A tract is two to four strips of plots running upslope from the canal. Each tract runs along the contour or down to its outfall, a right angle apart, turned up to about 17 degrees for its own ground, and never straight down a steep slope; within a tract each plot turns about 3 degrees either way. The land set the direction, tract by tract, in the one classical passage on it. The tract's size and the turns are GUESSES.
  • Tracts are set apart by at least 20 degrees, by a drawing convention.
    The seams between tracts, not every plot boundary, are what read the family strips apart, and a seam must turn far enough to read as one: two rows within about 6 degrees read as one direction.
  • On steep ground every tract runs the contour.
    There the land of every tract calls for it, so no seam is required. That rows on steep or terraced dry fields ran the contour is this record's inference, a GUESS.
  • Why not run every plot's rows along the contour?
    Contour ridging is one supportable option on the gentle ground these maps draw, where its advantages are greatest, not a rule that stops at the valley margin. But run everywhere it makes every plot's rows identical, and neighboring family strips cannot be told apart.

The hem's plots take their shape from the canal they front.

  • A dry plot beside a canal stands square to it.
    The plots are laid end to end along the canal's own curving line, and each is a rectangle square to the canal where it stands, so neighbors abut continuously along the canal, nothing is sheared or stepped, and only the outer, upslope edge is ragged. The strips run near-horizontal along one map's canal and near-vertical along another's, and that is the point, not an inconsistency.
  • Why take the canal's frame rather than the paddy's?
    Built in the paddy's frame, columns down the fall and rows on the contour, the plots would come out sheared into parallelograms, since the canals feeding the paddy on these maps commonly run diagonally to the fall. A modern Chinese design guideline makes the canal grid and the field grid one grid, perpendicular grade to grade on flat ground, and has a field canal water along the field's own fall, for irrigated dry land as for paddy; it never says which grid is the parent. That a plot takes its frame from the canal, not the rice, is this project's choice.
  • The frame is Edo work, but not its place at a paddy's edge.
    Strips squared to the canals that watered them are attested on the Musashino plateau, so the frame is labeled accurate. There each strip was a whole holding behind a house, not a plot beside rice, so setting the frame at a paddy's edge is this project's own.
  • A plot is not given a softened lean.
    Keeping the paddy's frame and only softening the parallelograms' lean was priced and declined: the plots take their orientation from the canal.
  • The plots keep to the dry side of the canal, behind a bare bank.
    Both edges of a plot lie upslope of the canal, behind a narrow bank (the bank left beside a supply canal). Where a map carries the paddy of more than one canal, a plot that would lap onto a neighboring paddy's rice is dropped, since dry crops on flooded ground contradict the reason the hem is where it is.
  • How big is a drawn dry plot?
    Across a map, the plots average under 0.25 acre, calibrated to the survey registers: seven single dry fields of 1678 averaging about 0.12 acre, and five of 1591 about 0.16. The village map's strips come to 0.16-0.17 acre, near the 1591 average, and the hamlet's garden-scale strips about 0.04, near the smallest field of 1678. The plots are tiled in real feet at every scale, roughly 92 ft across and 72 ft down a row on the village map, so a dry plot never dwarfs the rice basins beside it.

The rice paddy beside the dry plots shows no rows at all.

  • The paddy is drawn with an unruled scatter of shoots.
    Before the Meiji period rice was transplanted in "disorderly planting", not evenly spaced, so no paddy is drawn in ruled rows.
  • The scatter is a sample of the planting, not each plant.
    At today's spacing of about 30 cm (~12 in) a hill, one dot a plant on a map drawn at one foot to the pixel would be one dot a pixel, a solid fill, not a texture. So any scatter that can be drawn samples the planting, and how coarse a sample is a drawing choice, not a finding.
  • The rice among a polder's fish ponds takes a denser sample.
    There the crop has to read distinctly against the water, so the shoots stand about 4.5 ft apart, alternate rows half-offset, each moved up to about 1.5 ft and roughly a tenth dropped, so they land irregularly with no ruled row or column.
  • Whether the ordinary paddies take the denser sample is left undecided.
    The comb maps keep the sparse scatter. Nothing in the record decides between the two, both are honest samples of the same irregular planting, and the change would move the surface of every comb map at once.
