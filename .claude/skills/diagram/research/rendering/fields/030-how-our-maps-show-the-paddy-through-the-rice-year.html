How our maps show the paddy through the rice year

Our maps show the paddies at one moment of the rice year: high summer, the basins flooded and the rice green. The seasonal things that come and go on the same ground - the midsummer drain, the winter crop, the straw ricks, the spring rape - are set aside for seasonal maps of their own, which are not yet drawn. Nothing drawn depends on the water's depth.

  • Every rice basin on a map is drawn the same green.
    A district transplanted within days, on a calendar set by the crop before the rice, so at any moment its basins stood largely at one stage of growth; the spread that early and late varieties give is left out, since no page we read gives its size. The bund network and the footpath dots carry the field's structure instead of a rainbow of stages.
  • A different color on cultivated ground means a different ground.
    A blue-green basin is one of the low plots on the drain collector, pooling before the outfall (at The wettest plots are their own kind of ground); a gold one is early ripening, where a map uses it at all; and a plot on the upslope margin drawn in its own color, with its own furrow direction, is a dry crop. So uniform green is the one rice crop at the one stage, and a different color is never decoration.
  • The paddies are drawn flooded, one moment of a season that runs from flooded to cracked and back.
    That season is old: a Chinese farm manual of 544 already drains the paddy to firm the roots and again before the rice ripens. The depths the record gives for it are modern extension guidance, and the map's pop-up for a paddy says so rather than offering them as history.
  • Nothing drawn depends on the depth.
    The drawn bund is a width, not a height (at How our maps draw bunds between the paddies (aze)), and the flooded basin is a fill.
  • A midsummer map would drain some settlements' paddies: a knob, not built.
    A map set in midsummer would draw cracked mud where this one draws water. A settlement whose water is plentiful could have its paddies drained and cracking; one short of water keeps its paddies flooded, as terraced paddies on unreliable water do not drain today.
  • The winter crop is a knob for the seasonal maps: some settlements' paddies carry one, others lie bare.
    Both forms are attested, so the choice would be rolled per settlement from its seed. A double-cropped settlement's dry paddies are green with barley over the winter, while its wet paddies, which never dry, cannot be; a single-cropped settlement's paddies are left as reaped ground. The drainage and the nearness of a town's manure decide the roll, and how the two weigh against each other is a GUESS. A summer map draws neither, so nothing on today's maps changes.
  • No rape flowers stand beside the rice.
    Rape is sown on the paddy after the rice and cut in spring, and comes off as the rice goes in, so the two are never both standing: a map that mixed them at any share of its plots would show two seasons at once. The plots a map may recolor beside its rice hold only uses that last all year - a dike-pond, a lotus paddy, a hill tea garden. Rape belongs to a seasonal map, where the whole field turns yellow against bare stubble.
  • No straw rick is drawn.
    The rick stood on the reaped paddy and its bunds from the autumn, so a summer map has none. That a rick on the paddy floor was gone before the paddy was flooded is reasoning, and whether one on a bund still stood at midsummer no page we read says, so leaving it off rests on setting the seasonal features aside, not on a finding that it was gone. A seasonal map from late autumn through the winter would draw ricks on the reaped paddies and their bunds; on a paddy sown to winter barley a rick on the floor would stand in the barley's way, so there the bund is the likelier place - a GUESS.
