How our maps draw the farmland around a town or a city

Our maps draw the farmland around a town or a city with the same irrigation works as a village's: a sluice tapped on a real stream, a head race forking into supply canals, flooded paddies stepping downhill, a dry hem on the upslope margin, a drain, and farmhouses around each field. The settlement sits among those fields, and the open ground between them reads as pasture and commons, and as paddy country carrying on past the edge of the map.

A town map shows a sample: the far countryside is taken to lie off the sheet, and the fields near the town need not cover every foot of it.

  • The land nearest a town is drawn full, not left to scrub.
    The fields nearest a castle town got the richest manure, so a few paddies in a sea of scrub up to the gate would draw that gradient upside down.
  • Where does the maps' unworked land lie?
    At the far margins, toward the edge of the map. That the fallow lay far out is a GUESS: the account of the manure ladder around Kanazawa says nothing of fallow.
  • Two cheaper fillers are not used.
    A quilt of dry fields and kitchen plots, and gridded paddies with no waterworks of their own, would each draw the land near a town as something other than irrigated fields, which this project chose not to do.
  • On a town map at least one field runs off the edge.
    A field cut by the margin says there is more farmland beyond, and a town that shows only enclosed fields claims to show all of its countryside, which no town sheet can. A field running off the edge is exempt from the rules that want a field ringed with farmhouses and tied to a water source, because its houses and its water are off the map too.
  • The land near a town or city reads packed.
    Within about 800 ft of a walled city's rampart at least 12 percent of the ground is farmed, and around a town at least 28 percent. The sample is taken in the map's own space, so widening or narrowing the frame cannot change the answer. Both floors are calibrations against our own drawn maps, not figures from a source: a bare city ring measured about 7 percent, and a town with five fields about 31.
  • What density of farmland do the maps aim at?
    A dense wet-rice plain near a city, roughly 70 to 75 percent paddy, the rest bunds, ditches, lanes and farmsteads, with about 1.5 to 2 farming households for each hectare (~0.6-0.8 per acre) of paddy. That is a GUESS from general reading: no page we read gives either figure.
  • On a city map the head of each field is planted with dry crops.
    The ground along the supply canals, and the triangle between the two arms where the head race forks, sits at or above its own canals, so their water cannot be brought onto it by gravity. It is the well-drained high ground that dry crops take, and barley, millet and buckwheat are drawn there beside the head race.
  • No more than a fifth of the ground along a city field's main channels is left bare.
    The ceiling is a calibration against the drawn maps, which ran about 25 percent bare before the heads were planted and 13 to 16 percent after, not a figure from a source.

A city is ringed by the fields that feed it.

  • A city is ringed with farmland until the ground refuses it.
    Cities grew up to serve the countryside around them, and a single token field on one flank would draw that the wrong way round. So fields and farmhouses are added around a city until there is no more room, at least four fields across at least three sides; fewer means the ring was not attempted, not that the ground ran out. The three provincial cities drawn carry eleven fields, seven and six.
  • Each field is a whole water chain, not a colored patch.
    It has a tap that sits on the water, following the watercourse's own line; a head race that bends rather than running ruler-straight; a gate at each end; an offtake from a moat swept downstream with the moat's current; a stated source and a stated drain, the drain carried clear off the map rather than stopping in mid-air; and the farmsteads that work it.
  • A farmstead stands above its field's drain.
    In a wet lowland the settlements sat on the well-drained rise along a river, and the low, poorly drained ground behind it was made paddy. The maps carry that principle to a feature those accounts do not name, the strip just below a dug drain, and take it for the wettest ground in the valley. A farmstead's seat is measured against the drain's line rather than the field's center, since a seat can be above the middle of a field and still below the drain where it bends. The provincial cities never needed that, because their drains run off the map; the capital, whose drain runs on the sheet, did.
  • A farmstead may stand hard against a strip of dry crop, but never on it.
    The dry crops at a field's head are ground to be farmed, not a seat for a house.
  • How large is a field around a city?
    Its paddy strips run about 78 ft across, its stepped rows 39 to 57 ft, its supply canals 180 to 780 ft long, and its fan falls 270 to 570 ft; the dry hem across its head is 140 to 265 ft deep. A field is worth watering from the moat past about eleven and a half acres. These are calibrations against the three drawn cities, chosen so that a field at a city's fringe stays the size of the field it replaces, not figures from a source; the hem's depth is set field by field, because the pooled value is deeper than some ground allows.
  • Every field outside a city's wall has farmhouses around it, even where it runs off the map.
    What is counted is the part a reader can see: along the field's boundary inside the map, about seven farmhouses for every 3,000 ft. Village fields run between four and nineteen by the same measure, about twelve on average, so seven is a floor rather than a target. Because most of a fringe field lies off the map, the ring is drawn densely enough that enough houses land on the visible edge, and those that fall off it are not drawn.
  • The farmhouses around a city's fields are not counted in its population.
    The farmers who work a city's land live in the surrounding villages and are counted there. On a town map the same houses are part of the county's farmers and do count, the one place the two sizes of settlement differ here.
