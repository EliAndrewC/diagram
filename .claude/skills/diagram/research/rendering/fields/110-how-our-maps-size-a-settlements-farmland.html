How our maps size a settlement's farmland

Our maps size a village's paddy by the mouths it feeds: 0.8 to 1.0 tan (~0.20-0.25 acre) of paddy for each inhabitant, so a village of 350 draws 280 to 350 tan, or 69 to 86 acres. How large a single field is drawn is set by water, terrain and that population, never by whether the settlement is a hamlet, a village or a town.

  • A village of 350 draws 69 to 86 acres of paddy.
    Kikuta's paddy comes to about 79 acres in about 600 plots. The dry fields are drawn beyond that, at the margins.
  • How much paddy does a farm household work on our maps?
    About 1 to 2.5 acres, the band the maps are measured against. At the setting's household of about five (The five sizes of settlement), 0.8 to 1.0 tan an inhabitant comes to 4 or 5 tan, about 1 to 1.2 acres, and an eyewitness account of Japanese farming puts all the land one family worked nearer 2.5 acres.
  • The smallest field drawn with its own waterworks is about 7 to 20 acres.
    A comb fan is shared waterworks - a weir, a head race, a fork into supply canals, tapering ditches to the plots and a drain - and small works were run by one farmer or a few. At 1 to 2.5 acres a household, even a handful of households working together needs 3 to 8 ha (~7-20 acres), the size of a hamlet's paddy. Ground of 2 acres or so is one household's holding, watered by a single ditch, and this project draws no weir and fork for it. So a hamlet is drawn as a small tract of paddy with its farmhouses around it.
  • A field's size is set by water, terrain and mouths fed, not by the settlement's class.
    A market town sits in the middle of farmed countryside, so a town's edge may carry anything from a hamlet's small fan, where the ground that can be watered is short, through a village's 50 to 90 acres, to open farmland running off the map. A small field is drawn only where the map shows why - hay country, forest, slope - as the Kiso valley's farmland was held to its narrow flats by the mountains.

The size of a field is ruled by whether it runs off the map or is enclosed on it.

  • A field enclosed on the map is at least 8 acres.
    A field that does not run off the map reads as a complete system, so it is never smaller, and it is that small only on a map that shows why there is no more ground to water: hayfields, grazing and forest, and a thin ring of farms near the town.
  • A town's ordinary small end is about 20 acres.
    It is drawn as a modest enclosed field plus fields running off the map, since a real town sits amid continuous farmland.
  • A village's paddy stays sized to its population, at about 50 to 90 acres.
    It is reckoned from the inhabitants the village feeds, as above.
  • A city's fields run off the map.
    The cut edge says the field goes on, so the small acreage on the sheet is honest. A field running off the map is exempt from the 8-acre floor, which bites exactly where a field reads as whole.
  • A town's other farmers work the fields off the map.
    An enclosed field's ring of farmhouses reads as 1 to 2.5 acres a household for the farms around it, and the rest of a town's farmers are taken to work the fields beyond the sheet, which is why every town map shows a field running off its edge (How our maps draw the farmland around a town or a city).

A field watered from a pond is drawn with a pond to match.

  • A reservoir pond is sized by the paddy it waters.
    Its area is kept in proportion to the area it waters, as a Song-dynasty farming manual of 1149 sized a high field's pond at two or three parts in every ten (How big a pond did a field need before modern times?). No page we read, of any period, gives how much a pond stored for each hectare it watered.
  • How big is Hoshigaoka's pond?
    About 1.5 ha (~3.7 acres) of water for 31.8 ha (~79 acres) of paddy, about a twentieth of the land, with a stream feeding it as well. Holding a stream-fed pond below the Song manual's measure is a GUESS.
