How our maps draw dry fields and their crops (hatake)

Our maps draw the dry fields as a hem of small rectangular plots just upslope of the supply canal that runs along the paddy's high edge, where the paddy water stops: the higher, drier ground the dry crops took. Each plot is barley, millet, buckwheat or soybean, drawn with furrows, each about a sixth of an acre on the village map, and the plots beside the canal stand square to it.

  • No grain plot is drawn beside a house.
    The plot a household worked by its house was its kitchen bed, and no page we read puts its grain or beans there, so the dry crops go only in the hem.
  • Each map decides at random whether an alluvial fan's dry middle is left wild.
    An alluvial fan's middle was often left wild until late, but old heartlands cleared theirs early, so a knob decides it for each map from the map's seed: where the middle stays wild the hem keeps to the fan's toe and leaves the middle to scrub, and where it is cleared the whole canal is hemmed.
  • How often is a fan's middle left wild?
    On three maps in four, a GUESS: the record calls a wild middle the usual case but gives no figure.
  • A fan's toe is taken to begin two-thirds of the way down the slope from its head.
    That point is a GUESS.

Where a fan's middle stays wild, the hem along the toe is far too short to grow a hamlet's coarse grain, and the winter-crop roll (How our maps show the paddy through the rice year) decides where that grain grows (Where a farming hamlet grew its coarse grain).

  • A hamlet whose paddy carries barley over the winter grows its grain there, and its dry fields stay on the toe.
    Barley followed the rice on a paddy that drained, never on a wet one, so only the drained plots count. A drained paddy's winter crop is counted acre for acre against the dry field it spares, a GUESS: no page we read gives a winter barley crop's yield.
  • A hamlet whose paddy lies bare over the winter clears its grain fields up into the fan's wild middle.
    The dry plots climb from the toe into the middle, plot by plot, out as far from the canal as the ground runs, until they hold the grain the hamlet needs: the middle is cleared as far as the grain required and no further. A fan's middle, once cleared, held wheat-and-barley fields among its uses. The plots nearest the toe are taken first, a GUESS: no page we read says from which end the clearing went.
  • How much dry field does a hamlet's grain need?
    About 0.85 acre a household, beside its 1.3 acres of paddy: the lower of the two assessed rates the research gives, the earlier Edo one, in which a middle-grade dry field yields 1 koku to the tan against the paddy's 1 koku 3 to. It is a GUESS resting on attested rates.
  • The roll offers only the forms the hamlet's ground can feed.
    A bare winter paddy is offered only where the toe and the whole wild middle together can hold the whole need. On a fan whose middle is already cleared, the cleared ground is drawn only as the strip of dry fields along the fan's upper edge, which cannot hold the whole need, so such a hamlet is always drawn growing its barley on its paddy.
  • No grain is drawn in the island fields inside the paddy or on a levee.
    The island fields are recorded with cotton, mulberry and figs, not grain, and the levees' dry fields belong to the lowland below a fan, not to its toe (Where a farming hamlet grew its coarse grain); nothing we read puts a toe hamlet's grain in either.

The crops are drawn in the colors of their own season, which is not the season of the rest of the map.

  • Each crop is drawn at its ripest color.
    Barley tan-gold, millet ochre, buckwheat pale with reddish stems, soybean green.
  • Each map picks its mix of the four crops at random.
    The mix of the four crops is a GUESS at their proportions.
  • Which season should the dry plots show?
    That is left open. The map is drawn in high summer, the season the paddy stands flooded and green (How our maps draw bunds between the paddies). In high summer barley is already cut, harvested around June, so a barley plot is stubble or bare; the millets stand tall and unripe, not harvested until autumn; soybean has set its pods, its top leaves near 80 cm (~3 ft) by late August in the one modern field found; buckwheat may be in flower, or its autumn sowing bare ground. The ripest colors put May's barley and October's millet beside July's paddy. Whether to keep one season across the sheet, or each crop at its most recognizable color, is not yet decided.
  • The maps do not draw the rotation of rice, barley and soybean.
    It is today's practice, and no page we read dates it earlier.

Every dry plot is drawn with furrows, and which way they run is set tract by tract.

  • Drawing the dry plots in furrowed rows is accurate.
    A premodern dry field was worked in ridged rows.
  • The furrows' spacing is a GUESS.
    No page we read gives how far apart the ridged rows stood in a Japanese field.
  • Neighboring plots form tracts that share one row direction.
    A tract is two to four strips of plots running upslope from the canal. Its rows run either along the contour or down to its outfall, the two a right angle apart, turned up to about 17 degrees to suit its own ground. Within a tract each plot turns about 3 degrees either way. In a classical passage, in an ancient Chinese chronicle, the rows ran whichever way suited the land, not one way across a whole realm. The tract's size and the turns are GUESSES.
  • Tracts are set apart by at least 20 degrees, by a drawing convention.
    The seams between tracts, not every plot boundary, are what set neighboring strips apart, and a seam must turn far enough to read as one: two rows within about 6 degrees read as one direction.
  • On steep ground every tract runs the contour.
    There the land of every tract calls for it, so no seam is required. That rows on steep or terraced dry fields ran the contour is this record's inference, a GUESS.
  • Why not run every plot's rows along the contour?
    Contour ridging is one sound option on the gentle ground these maps draw, where its advantages are greatest, but not the only one. Run everywhere, it makes every plot's rows identical, and neighboring strips cannot be told apart.

The hem's plots take their shape from the canal they front.

  • A dry plot beside a canal stands square to it.
    The plots are laid end to end along the canal's own curving line, and each is a rectangle square to the canal where it stands, so neighbors abut continuously along the canal, nothing is sheared or stepped, and only the outer, upslope edge is ragged. So the strips run near-horizontal along one map's canal and near-vertical along another's.
  • Why square the plots to the canal rather than to the paddy's slope?
    Laid out square to the paddy's slope, in columns down it and rows along the contour, the plots would come out sheared into parallelograms, since the canals feeding the paddy on these maps commonly run diagonally to the fall. A modern Chinese design guideline makes the canal grid and the field grid one grid, perpendicular grade to grade on flat ground, and has a field canal water along the field's own fall, for irrigated dry land as for paddy; it never says which grid is the parent. That a plot is squared to the canal, not the rice, is this project's choice; keeping the paddy's layout and only softening how far the plots lean was considered and declined.
  • Were strips squared to their canals Edo-period work?
    On the Musashino plateau, on this record's reading, yes: there the row villages formed along the canals that watered them, with long, narrow strips of dry field behind the houses, so the strips stood square to the canal. The page gives the line of the villages, not of the strips, so the frame is a reconstruction. There the strips lay behind the houses, not beside rice, so setting them at a paddy's edge is this project's own.
  • The plots keep to the dry side of the canal, behind a bare bank.
    Both edges of a plot lie upslope of the canal, behind a narrow bank (the bank left beside a supply canal).
  • A plot that would lap onto another canal's rice is dropped.
    Where a map carries the paddy of more than one canal, dry crops on flooded ground would contradict the reason the hem is where it is.
  • How big is a drawn dry plot?
    Across a map, the plots average under 0.25 acre, calibrated to the survey registers: seven single dry fields of 1678, in the Edo period, averaging about 0.12 acre, and five of 1591, just before it, about 0.16. The village map's strips come to 0.16-0.17 acre, near the 1591 average, and the hamlet's garden-scale strips about 0.04, near the smallest field of 1678. The plots are tiled in real feet at every scale, roughly 92 ft across and 72 ft down a row on the village map, so a dry plot never dwarfs the rice basins beside it.

The rice paddy beside the dry plots shows no rows at all.

  • The paddy is drawn with an unruled scatter of shoots.
    Until row planting came in during the Meiji period, rice was transplanted in "disorderly planting", not evenly spaced, so no paddy is drawn in ruled rows.
  • The scatter is a sample of the planting, not each plant.
    At today's spacing of about 30 cm (~12 in) between clumps of seedlings, one dot a plant on a map drawn at one foot to the pixel would be one dot a pixel, a solid fill, not a texture. So any scatter that can be drawn samples the planting, and how coarse a sample is a drawing choice, not a finding.
  • The rice among a polder's fish ponds takes a denser sample.
    There the crop has to read distinctly against the water, so the shoots stand about 4.5 ft apart, alternate rows half-offset, each moved up to about 1.5 ft and roughly a tenth dropped, so they land irregularly with no ruled row or column.
  • Whether the ordinary paddies take the denser sample is left undecided.
    The comb maps keep the sparse scatter. Nothing in the record decides between the two, both are honest samples of the same irregular planting, and the change would move the surface of every comb map at once.
