How our maps place paddies left to rest (kataarashi)

A settlement's paddy takes one of two forms the record attests, and each settlement rolls one of them from its map's seed, a knob. Settled paddy is cropped every year and no plot rests: the form of the nucleated village on stable ground, and of the early modern village after the land surveys. Unsettled paddy rests a few plots this year, scattered among the cropped plots and grazed: the form of the early medieval estate, where water or soil could not carry every plot every year.

The empire our maps are set in sides with the settled form. Its canon holds that a wet-rice paddy, once established, can be cropped continuously for centuries without depleting the soil, and that what limits its rice is labor, not soil. Its unworked rice land is ground a village lacks the hands to work, not a plot resting inside the paddy the village does work. The canon does not rule out a plot left dry for want of water.

The rule the map follows:
  • Most settlements roll the settled form and draw no resting plot.
    Four in five do. That the settled form is the commoner is this project's reading of the setting's nucleated villages and its canon; the weight between the two forms is a GUESS.
  • A settlement that rolls the unsettled form rests two to four whole plots in each field.
    Each is a complete basin inside its own bunds, never a patch within a basin. How many rest is a liberty the map takes, and two to four is a GUESS. The estate of 1102, with nearly a third of its paddy resting, is the upper bound of the record, not a hamlet's norm.
  • The resting plots are scattered, never side by side.
    No two share a bund - a drawing convention for resting land mixed in among the land under crop.
  • A resting plot is drawn as grass with tufts.
    It was grazed in common while it rested.
  • The resting plots favor the far end of the water's run.
    A plot's chance of resting grows with how far down the fall of the field it lies. That the plots short of water rested is our reading of "short water", a GUESS, since no page we read says which plots of a village's paddy rested.
