How our maps draw dike-ponds (sangji yutang)

Our maps draw a block of dike-ponds as ponds of uneven shape, each ringed by a bank of its own mud planted with rows of low mulberry bushes, with canals running between the banks. About six parts in ten of each pond's parcel is water. The sluice gates drawn are the polder's own, in its outer dike, not one in every pond's bank. How the block's ponds are laid out as a mosaic, and where its sluices stand, is at How our maps draw polders (weitian, waju).

  • A pond's parcel is drawn about six parts in ten water.
    Each pond is set 23 ft in from the edge of its parcel, and the planted bank fills the ring between, leaving about six parts in ten of the parcel water. That share is this project's GUESS, read from the two figures Qu Dajun gave in 1678 for Jiujiang, a dike-pond township of the Pearl River delta, which are the oldest found. Every split of water and dike written as a number is modern (Dike-ponds: fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes (sangji yutang)). Across the whole block the share of water is lower, once the canal corridors between the parcels count.
  • The maps give a pond's parcel more water than dike, one of two recorded readings, and say so on the map.
    The sources give the split of water and dike both ways round, six parts dike to four of pond as well as six of water to four of dike, and seven of water to three of dike besides (Dike-ponds: fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes (sangji yutang)). Kuwabata, the dike-pond hamlet, follows the water-heavy reading, a deliberate deviation disclosed on every pond and bank of its map: "a regional reading; the classic prescription is also recorded as six parts dike to four parts pond".
  • How wide is a pond's bank drawn?
    23 ft, inside the modern figures for a pond's dike. No width of a pond dike from before modern times was found, so the drawn bank rests on modern pages alone: dikes 6 to 10 m (~20-33 ft) wide (Dike-ponds: fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes (sangji yutang)), the bank drawn 23 ft.
  • The bank is drawn as planted ground: mottled earth under rows of mulberry bushes.
    Like the polder's own dike (How our maps draw a polder's dike and its trees), the bank is mottled earth, and along it run rows of low bushes, one row to every 5.5 ft of the bank's width, the bushes about 4.4 ft apart in the row: about one bush to every 24 sq ft.
  • Each bush is drawn a few feet across, never enlarged into a tree symbol.
    The crowns are drawn 4.4 to 7 ft across, a dot of a few pixels. No page we read gives a bush's crown width, so the width is this project's GUESS. Bushes that could each be told apart as tree symbols would have to be drawn three to five times their size, and the maps do not do that.
  • The bushes are spaced at the one planting density dated before modern times, so they touch in stretches without fusing into one hedge.
    At the density of the Pearl River delta's root-cut planting, about a bush to the square foot and given with no date, the crowns would merge into one solid band of green (Dike-ponds: fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes (sangji yutang)). The maps take instead the late-Qing Yangtze delta's mid-trunk planting, about 300 trees a mu, one to about 24 sq ft, the only figure dated before modern times (Mulberry and other crops on pond dikes (sangji, guoji)). At that spacing the crowns run together in stretches, with bank earth showing through the gaps, which reads as bushes.
  • No berries are drawn.
    Whether a bush stripped of its leaves to feed silkworms bore fruit is on no page we read, and a mulberry's fruit would be smaller than a pixel at this scale, so the crown's color carries the read.
  • A bush's center always stands on the bank, never over a canal.
    The mulberry was planted on the dike as low bushes (Dike-ponds: fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes (sangji yutang)), so a crown whose center fell over a canal would be a bush growing in a dug channel. A crown's edge may lean over the water: the canals are drawn beneath the crowns, so a leaf edge may cover part of a canal, but a canal never cuts across a crown. A rule that kept the whole crown clear of the water would pull the bushes back from the edge of the bank, which the maps do not do.
  • A canal may graze the edge of a bank, on purpose.
    The canal is drawn along the foot of the dike, where earth meets water, so a bent canal may graze a parcel's edge by a foot and a half or so: the foot of the bank, not a canal cut through the dike. Widening every bank by 5 ft to cover the parcel line was weighed and set aside, because it put banks over the wavering canals, so the bank stays on the true parcel line.
  • The bank is a ring, not a disk under the pond.
    A planted bank is drawn as the band between its outer edge and the water's own outline, the pond showing through the middle, as the polder's dike is drawn. So pointing at a bank on the interactive map lights the bank alone, not the ponds inside it. It is a map drawing convention. Three other ways were weighed and set aside: a change to the page's styling rules alone, which cannot cut a hole in a filled shape; drawing the pond above the lit bank, at a measured cost of a fifth of a second on every hover; and a second outline for the bank's outer edge, which doubles a dike's ink for a fringe nobody sees.
  • The maps draw no sluice through a pond's own dike.
    A sluice for each pond is found only in a modern training manual, and no page we read has a pond fed at its high side and drained at its low side (Dike-ponds: fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes (sangji yutang)). So the sluices drawn are the polder's own, through its outer dike.
  • The canals are drawn as the waterways the ponds lie among, not as field ditches.
    The ponds lay among the polder's creeks and canals, which its sluices watered and drained (Dike-ponds: fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes (sangji yutang)), so the maps draw the canals as that network, not as a rice field's irrigation ditches.
