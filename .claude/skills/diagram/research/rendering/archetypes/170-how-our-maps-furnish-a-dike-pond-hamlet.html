How our maps furnish a dike-pond hamlet

Our maps give a dike-pond hamlet what the record finds on one: pig sties on the pond banks, nursery ponds in a hamlet that raises fry, mulberry, fruit or tea on the dikes, and, in about half the hamlets, the night soil kept in jars sunk in the ground. They leave off what the record does not place there: what is found only in modern times (sheep huts, cane and banana dikes), what one village's geography gave it (a creek with boats), what was done elsewhere (silkworms reared inside the house, ducks herded in the rice fields), and what no page we read names (a hut on a pond bank).

  • The houses stand on the dry side of the ponds, beside the planted dikes.
    As Fei's houses stood beside the mulberry on each plot's raised margin, the hamlet's houses stand on its dry flank, its planted dikes beside them; how the ponds and their dikes are drawn is at How our maps draw dike-ponds (sangji yutang).
  • The sluices drawn are the polder's own.
    They stand in its outer dike, not one in every pond's bank (How our maps draw polders (weitian, waju)).
  • Each hamlet plants its dikes with one crop: mulberry, fruit or tea.
    Half the hamlets plant mulberry, a third fruit and a sixth tea, a GUESS (Mulberry and other crops on pond dikes (sangji, guoji)).
  • Pig sties stand on the pond banks nearest the houses.
    A quarter to half of the households keep a sty, rolled per hamlet, a GUESS (Which animals did a dike-pond village keep at its ponds?).
  • Nursery ponds are drawn only in a hamlet that raises fry for sale, about one hamlet in four.
    Most hamlets bought their fry and have no nursery ponds; how a fry village's ponds are chosen and drawn is at How our maps draw fish fry and nursery ponds (yumiao).

Around each house, the farmstead.

  • Every farmstead keeps a yard before the house, as a rice hamlet keeps its threshing yard.
    Threshing was a rice task, so where a hamlet's ground holds no rice, its yard is best read as a work yard, where cocoons were sorted and leaves handled. That reading is this project's GUESS, and the yard is drawn the same either way.
  • How much ground does each household farm?
    1.3 ac, the rice hamlet's figure, a GUESS: no page we read gives the ground a silk-and-fish household farmed.
  • A hamlet keeps its manure either in heaps or in sunk jars, each at even odds.
    Both forms are recorded: the rice hamlet's heap (Where did the manure heap stand, and what went into it?) and the silk village's earthenware jar half buried in the ground. No source says how many villages used each, so each hamlet, rice or silk, rolls one with even odds, a GUESS.
  • A manure jar stands behind its house by the privy, or out by a field or a road.
    Both places are recorded, in George Staunton's 1797 account of the British embassy to China and in Fei Hsiao-tung's Peasant Life in China, his 1930s study of a silk village by Lake Tai.
  • How many households keep their jar out by the fields?
    From about 2 in 100 to 85 in 100, rolled per hamlet: the span a 1959 survey of three Japanese villages found for their field pits (Where did the privy stand, and where was its night soil kept?). Carrying Japan's field pit to the silk village's jar is this project's own step, a GUESS; the field-head pits Wang Zhen describes for south China were lined with brick, not jars. The field or road is looked for within 160 ft of the house, also a GUESS.
  • Manure jars are drawn as mouths 3.5 ft across, flush with the ground.
    No passage we read gives a manure jar's size, so 3.5 ft is a GUESS. Each is drawn as a dark disc inside a pale rim. Staunton's jars were buried to the rim and Fei's half buried; seen from above, as the map sees them, both are the same mouth in the ground, a map drawing convention.

What the record does not place on a silk-and-fish hamlet is left off.

  • No silkworm house or reeling shed is drawn.
    The silkworms were reared and the silk reeled inside the house.
  • No sheep hut is drawn.
    Sheep huts are recorded by the Lake Tai houses only in the 1930s, after sheep raising had grown important there in the ten years before.
  • No duck pen is drawn.
    The ducks were herded in the rice fields, not penned at the ponds (Which animals did a dike-pond village keep at its ponds?).
  • No hut is drawn on a pond bank.
    No page we read names one.
  • No cane, banana or vegetable dikes are drawn.
    They are found only in modern accounts (Mulberry and other crops on pond dikes (sangji, guoji)).
  • No creek or boat landing is drawn.
    The hamlet as drawn has a header reservoir and a ring canal, but no water a boat could travel. Fei's village stood along its streams, but that was one village's geography, and not every silk-and-fish hamlet need have had a navigable creek. The hamlet's lane leads off the map to its market, or to whatever water carried its goods there, and the map leaves that unseen. A creek along the hamlet's landward side, with boats and a landing, was weighed and set aside.
  • No shrine to the silkworm goddess is drawn on a hamlet.
    No page we read says whether a hamlet kept one or where such a shrine stood. A hamlet, as these maps draw it, has no shrine; one would belong on a village's map.
  • Neither straw ricks nor the small ponds a rice hamlet digs in its fields are drawn.
    Both belong to a hamlet that grows rice.
