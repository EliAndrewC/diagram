How our maps draw rice land on a slope

A map draws rice on a slope in one of two forms, and only where its ground calls for one: terraces on hill ground, and a long valley paddy in a confined valley. A map with neither draws the ordinary valley fan. In both forms the field is divided into small cells, so it reads as a row or a chain of small paddies, as the history has it, rather than as one long sheet. No scripted map draws either form yet; only hand-drawn maps use them.

The rule the map follows, on hill ground:

  • Rice is drawn as stacked terrace bands along the contour.
    They step down the slope from the catchment above, with a supply channel running down one flank and cascading from step to step into a drain at the foot.
  • A map that draws terraces carries at least eight retaining banks across the slope.
    The count is a drawing convention; no page we read gives a number of steps.
  • Every step is divided along the contour into small cells.
    Each cell is the size of a leveled basin the record gives, so a step reads as a row of small paddies rather than one continuous band.

The rule the map follows, in a confined valley:

  • Rice is drawn as one long narrow field meandering down the valley floor.
    Its outline stays markedly longer than it is wide, as a valley paddy is long and narrow.
  • The brook runs down the field's center.
    No page we read says where the stream ran through a valley paddy; the middle is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • Cross banks step the field down the fall, and it is split across its width into cells of the same size.
    So it reads as a chain of small paddies rather than a single sheet.

The paddy's size, and the wall that holds each bench, follow the slope the map draws.

  • How big is a terrace paddy on our maps?
    It is tied to the slope, a calibrated liberty: on a steep slope the benches are narrow and the paddies small, down to a few tens of square meters (a few hundred square feet), as at Shiroyone; on a gentle one the benches are deep and a paddy reaches a few hundred square meters (a few thousand square feet), as at Obasute.
  • The wall stands higher where the ground is steeper.
    That is the direction the history gives, so the wall's height moves opposite to the paddy's size.
  • The wall's form is a knob, rolled settlement by settlement.
    A near-vertical stone face, as western Japan built, or a sloping earth bank that takes up more of the slope, as the east built. They are two attested forms, not degrees of one, so the map rolls between them rather than choosing.
