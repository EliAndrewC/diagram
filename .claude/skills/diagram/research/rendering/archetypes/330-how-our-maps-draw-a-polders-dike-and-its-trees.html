How our maps draw a polder's dike and its trees

Our maps draw a polder's dike as a band of heaped earth all the way round the polder, 14 to 40 ft across and changing in width along its length, its outer face curving with the water's edge. Two rows of trees run along it, willow on the water side and mulberry on the field side, and the farmhouses keep their own windbreak groves besides.

  • How high and how broad is a village polder's dike?
    A dike's height and breadth were a matter of degree, set by the size of the polder and the water it faced, so this project treats them as a calibrated liberty rather than a choice between forms. What was read gives bounds, not a village's own figure: a broad foot several times the height, narrowing to a crown a person can walk, and a village's dike below a domain's river dike of about 3.6 m (~12 ft) high on a foot of about 5.5 m (~18 ft) (Polder dikes: what they were made of, how big, and what grew on them). The maps take a small polder's dike to stand about 1.5 m (~5 ft) high, a GUESS that sits inside those bounds (Polders: fields diked against the fluctuating water (weitian, waju)).
  • Is the drawn band inside those bounds?
    Not everywhere. The band is drawn 14 to 40 ft across, and its broadest stretches are wider than the 18 ft base of the Echizen river dikes, which in this project's reading a village's dike stood below. The drawn breadth has not yet been reconciled with that bound.
  • The band changes in width and is mottled, never a ruled line.
    A dike of heaped mud, raised and repaired with pond mud year after year (Polder dikes: what they were made of, how big, and what grew on them), is drawn thicker in some stretches and pinched in others, and mottled with patches of earth of different ages, as repairs are.
  • The dike's outer face curves with the water's edge, while its inner face follows the polder's grid.
    That a polder's outer dike followed the water's edge is this project's GUESS. A dead-straight rectangular polder is a modern form, and this project draws nothing that is only modern.
  • Two rows of trees run along the dike, one on each face of the bank.
    The willow row stands about three quarters of the way out from the field side, toward the water, and the mulberry row about a quarter of the way, on the field side. So the distance between the rows follows the width of the bank, and the maps draw to no figure for it. The rows of a Ming river manual stood on the ground off the dike's foot rather than on its faces; the maps set them on the bank, where they can be seen as the dike's own planting.
  • The willows are drawn farther apart in the row than the nearest recorded planting.
    The willows are drawn about 8.5 ft apart, looser than the 5 chi (~5.5 ft) of the Willow Palisade's statute, and the mulberry about 4.4 ft apart, so their crowns read as a run of touching trees rather than one fused hedge, as on the dike-pond's banks (How thickly was dike mulberry planted, and how wide did a bush grow?).
  • Where a channel crosses the dike, the band is cut, and the rows stop with it.
    The water runs through a gap in the earthwork, not over its top.
  • The maps draw both the dike's trees and the farmhouses' groves.
    Neither stands in for the other: the trees on the dike, cut low and bare in winter, do little to shelter a village from the wind, and the homestead groves are a different planting for that job (Polder dikes: what they were made of, how big, and what grew on them).
