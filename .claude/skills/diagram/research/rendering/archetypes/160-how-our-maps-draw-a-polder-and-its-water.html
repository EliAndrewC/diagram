How our maps draw a polder and its water

Every polder on our maps is enclosed by its dike all the way round, and water crosses the dike only at gated sluices: an inlet high on the block and an outfall low on it. A large polder had many openings, drains outnumbering intakes; how many is a calibrated liberty set by the polder's size, and a village polder is drawn with two, which in this project's reading, a GUESS, a small polder may well have had.

The rule the map follows, for where the polder and its village sit:

  • Both polders in the pool are drawn against the landward shore.
    The wild header pond that feeds the block stands to the northwest, reed marsh lies along the low south side where the outfall drains, and the village stands on natural dry ground to the east.
  • The polder's west flank carries a reed fringe.
    So the map shows that the west dike holds water back, as every flank of a polder but the landward one does.
  • The village stands on the dry ground at the dike's shoulder.
    Houses on a polder's raised margin, which was also its dike, are recorded; that a margin polder's village stood instead on the landward dry ground a few steps off is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • A village sits hard against its own polder.
    In this project's reading, a GUESS, a polder's community kept its own dike, so its houses press up to its block.
  • Dike-top housing is a knob no map uses yet.
    A polder with water on every flank and no shore to build on would seat its farmhouses in single file on the dike crest, the recorded form of houses on the margin. The generator can roll it, but no settlement in the pool does.
  • The pool's polder villages fall inside the guessed household range.
    Enokida's 16 households sit inside the ten to twenty households this project guesses for a small polder (Polders: fields diked against the fluctuating water).

The rule the map follows, for the water inside:

  • A ring canal runs round the inside foot of the dike, on the field side.
    From outside in, a map draws the wild water, the dike, the ring canal (the weineihe), the field ditches and the paddies, and no channel runs outside the dike. Channels inside the dike are recorded; a single ring at the dike's foot is not, so its place is a drawing convention.
  • The canals run straight in their long reaches, but gently wavy, with rounded corners.
    A dug canal wavers with the ground and with repair, and the finer field ditches are drawn crookeder still. The line is a drawing convention.
  • The horita is a knob no generator draws yet.
    A waterlogged polder may roll the horita in place of ordinary paddy: long raised strips of rice with water channels between, recorded for Japanese polders from the eighteenth century.
  • The chain pump is not drawn.
    Nothing we read describes a fixed mark it left on the plan.

A rice polder and a dike-pond block lay out their insides differently.

  • A rice polder is drawn on a surveyed grid.
    The lower Yangtze polders were laid out on a planned lattice of channels, so the pool's rice polder keeps the grid.
  • A dike-pond block is drawn as a mosaic, never as a uniform grid of ponds.
    The grid is recorded for the canals and the mosaic for the ponds; a uniform chessboard of ponds is found only today, as an aerial view and as a modern plan, and this project draws nothing that is only modern. So the layout of the ponds is not a choice between two recorded forms: there is the one.
  • The mosaic bends the lattice; it never breaks the ponds.
    The interior lattice is pushed out of line by a smooth drift that fades to nothing at the fixed outer edge. Neighboring crossing points move together, so the lines stay continuous but bend, the interior ditches become meandering creeks, and the parcels skew to trapezoids while each pond stays four-sided, as the record has them.
  • How strongly does the lattice bend?
    A calibrated liberty, set on the Kuwabata map, which was drawn with it.
