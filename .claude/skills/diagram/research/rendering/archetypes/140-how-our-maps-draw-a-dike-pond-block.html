How our maps draw a dike-pond block

Our maps draw a dike-pond block as ponds of uneven shape, each ringed by a bank of its own mud planted with rows of low mulberry bushes, with canals running between the banks. About six parts in ten of each pond's parcel is water. The sluice gates drawn are the polder's own, in its outer dike, not one in every pond's bank. How the block's ponds are laid out as a mosaic, and where its sluices stand, is at How our maps draw polders (weitian, waju).

  • A pond's parcel is drawn about six parts in ten water.
    Each pond is set 23 ft in from the edge of its parcel, and the planted bank fills the ring between. That leaves about six parts in ten of the parcel water, the reading this project takes from Qu Dajun's two figures for Jiujiang, the oldest found; every split written as a number is modern (Dike-ponds: fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes (sangji yutang)). Across the whole block the share of water is lower, once the canal corridors between the parcels count.
  • The maps take the water-heavy order of the split, and say so on the map.
    The sources give the split both ways round, six parts dike to four of pond as well as six of water to four of dike, and a seven-to-three variant besides. Kuwabata, the dike-pond hamlet, follows the water-heavy reading, a deliberate deviation disclosed on every pond and bank of its map: "a regional reading; the classic prescription is also recorded as six parts dike to four parts pond".
  • The bank's width and the pond's size rest on modern pages alone.
    No width of a pond dike and no ordinary size of a pond from before modern times was found, so the drawn bank, and the ponds it rings, stand inside the modern figures: dikes 6 to 10 m (~20-33 ft) wide, the bank drawn 23 ft.
  • The bank is drawn as planted ground: mottled earth under rows of mulberry bushes.
    Like the polder's own dike (How our maps draw a polder's dike and its trees), the bank is mottled earth, and along it run rows of low bushes, one row to every 5.5 ft of the bank's width, the bushes about 4.4 ft apart in the row: about one bush to every 23 sq ft.
  • Each bush is drawn a few feet across, never enlarged into a tree symbol.
    The crowns are drawn 4.4 to 7 ft across, a dot of a few pixels. No page we read gives a bush's crown width, so the width is this project's GUESS. Bushes that could each be told apart as tree symbols would have to be drawn three to five times their size, and the maps do not do that.
  • The bushes are spaced to touch in stretches, not to fuse into one hedge.
    At the density of the Pearl River delta's root-cut planting, about a bush to the square foot, the crowns would merge into one solid band of green (Dike-ponds: fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes (sangji yutang)). The maps space them so they run together in stretches, with bank earth showing through the gaps, which reads as bushes. That is a map drawing convention in the density only; the spacing taken is the sparser older planting at How thickly was dike mulberry planted, and how wide did a bush grow?
  • No berries are drawn.
    Whether a bush stripped for its leaf bore fruit is on no page we read, and a mulberry's fruit would be smaller than a pixel at this scale, so the crown's color carries the read.
  • A bush's center always stands on the bank, never over a canal.
    The planting is bushes on the dike, not trees arching over water, so a crown whose center fell over a canal would be a bush growing in a dug channel. A crown's edge may lean over the water: the canals are drawn beneath the crowns, so a leaf edge may cover part of a canal, but a canal never cuts across a crown. A rule that kept the whole crown clear of the water would pull the bushes back from the edge of the bank, which they did not do.
  • A canal may graze the edge of a bank, on purpose.
    The canal is drawn along the foot of the dike, where earth meets water, so a bent ditch may graze a parcel's edge by a foot and a half or so: the foot of the bank, not a canal cut through the dike. Widening every bank by 5 ft to cover the parcel line put banks over the wavering ditches in 72 places, up to 6.5 ft deep, so the bank stays on the true parcel line.
  • The bank is a ring, not a disk under the pond.
    A planted bank is drawn as the band between its outer edge and the water's own outline, the pond showing through the middle, as the polder's dike is drawn. So pointing at a bank on the interactive map lights the bank alone, not the ponds inside it. It is a map drawing convention. Three other ways were weighed and set aside: a stylesheet fix, which cannot cut a hole in a filled shape; drawing the pond above the lit bank, at a measured cost of a fifth of a second on every hover; and a second outline for the bank's outer edge, which doubles a dike's ink for a fringe nobody sees.
  • The maps draw no sluice through a pond's own dike.
    A sluice for each pond is found only in a modern training manual, and a pond fed at its high side and drained at its low side on no page we read (Dike-ponds: fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes (sangji yutang)). So the sluices drawn are the polder's own, through its outer dike, and the canals are drawn as the creeks and canals the ponds lie among and exchange water with, not as a rice field's irrigation ditches.
