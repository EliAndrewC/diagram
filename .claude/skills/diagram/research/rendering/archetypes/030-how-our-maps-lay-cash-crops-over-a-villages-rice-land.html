How our maps lay cash crops over a village's rice land

A village map may carry one cash crop over its rice, chosen settlement by settlement: dike-ponds, lotus or tea, or none. Each takes only the ground its crop needed in the history - the low, wet plots for ponds and lotus, the dry hillside above the rice for tea - and a map without that ground draws no cash crop at all, rather than scattering one across ordinary rice.

  • The ground decides which plots a cash crop may take; a separate share decides how many of them it does.
    The share is of the eligible ground, never of the whole field. As a share of the whole field it would decide nothing, because the eligible ground is always the smaller, so every settlement would convert all of its low ground. A share well short of everything is what the history shows: Longshan's ponds in 1581 were 18% of its hill, dry land and ponds, and by Lake Tai mulberry stood on the polder's margin while rice stayed the main crop.
  • The low ground a cash crop may take is the bottom two levels of the valley floor - a calibrated liberty.
    No page we read gives how wide a valley's wet bottom ran, and the estimate of a lotus-growing village's share, a few percent to about 10-15% of its field, is itself an interpolation. Taking only the single row of plots along the drain put lotus at about 2% of the field: inside that range, but so sparse that villages stopped looking different from one another, which is what the cash crop is drawn for. So the maps take the bottom two levels, the upper part of the range, for a reason that is not historical - so the map reads - and say so here.
  • A lotus field is drawn larger than any premodern page records, as a map drawing convention.
    Lotus fields and lotus-growing villages both existed before modern times (Cash crops on rice land), so the maps keep the lotus field; drawing it larger than the record supports, so that it shows, is a convention, not a modern form. The upper part of the band fits a village in a lotus district, where rice was given up for lotus on low or reclaimed ground, better than an ordinary village with a few deep plots; the one share read is modern, Iwakuni's lotus at about six times its rice in 2015.
  • Converted plots are set in patches rather than scattered evenly - a GUESS the map cannot show.
    Conversion is taken to have spread plot by plot from one household to its neighbors, though no page we read says how it spread, so the maps place converted plots in patches. On the map the eligible ground is always a thin, continuous strip along the wet bottom, and any part of a strip counts as a patch by a measure that compares the gaps between plots with the strip's length - an even scatter of the same number scores as well. So the patches are asserted from the history, not shown by the drawing.

Each crop then has its own rule.

  • Dike-ponds take a scatter of a village's low plots.
    A scatter among the rice is how the system usually stood, so the dike-pond cash crop converts some of the low plots and leaves the rest in rice.
  • The whole-landscape dike-pond village is the same system carried to its end.
    Where the ponds took nearly everything - 75% of the cultivated land at Longshanbao in the Qianlong reign, and mulberry ponds with no fish ponds at Longjiang and Longshan - the ponds had engulfed ordinary ground too, so the mulberry-dike-fishpond field archetype is not held to the low-ground rule. The two are two scales of one system, not duplicates. How a dike-pond block is drawn is at The 6:4 water-to-dike ratio, and coppiced mulberry.
  • Lotus is drawn as deep-water lotus, on low ground only.
    Deep-water lotus grows in more than 40 cm (~16 in) of water, which only the wettest, lowest plots hold, so a lotus field never stands on high ground.
  • Shallow-water lotus in ordinary paddy is not drawn.
    It was grown in turn with rice in the same field, a household's choice from year to year that the maps do not model, and drawn on ordinary paddy it would look like an even random scatter across the rice.
  • Tea is drawn as rows of bushes on the dry ground above the highest irrigation ditch.
    Tea grew on the lower hillside and never on the low land, so its lower edge is the highest ditch that waters a paddy, and a map draws tea only where it has a hillside or terraced margin to hold it.
  • Tea is not drawn on contour terraces.
    The terraces of a slope are drawn for its paddy, not its tea: neat contour terraces of tea are taken to be modern, a GUESS.
  • Bund tea, the second form of tea, is not drawn yet.
    Tea had two forms: the hillside garden, which takes plots, and in Japan a single row along the field banks, which takes none. With two attested forms, the tea a village carries is a knob between them; only the hillside garden is built.
  • On a rice polder, mulberry belongs on the dike crest, not in the paddies.
    The record of the Wanchun polder has its mulberry in rows along the ridge of the dike, so a polder that is not a dike-pond village takes no mulberry into its paddies. Why its dikes were planted is at Why dikes were planted, and what the row spacing rests on.
