How our maps draw reservoir ponds, their reeds and their embankments

Every reservoir pond on our maps has a fringe of reeds standing on wet ground at its shore, a bare, dry embankment beyond them, one outlet, and an area sized to the paddy it waters. None of these is a drawing convention: each is how the record describes a pond.

  • A pond's reeds stand on ground drawn wet.
    The shallow shore is where the tall water plants root, so the pale wet tint that marks a waterside bed is drawn under a pond's reeds too, even where the fringe is narrow.
  • Reeds and the wet tint are kept off the embankment.
    A pond's bank is dry, firm ground, kept clear of growth for its strength, so no reed or tint is drawn on it, nor on any raised rim around a pond.
  • The bank is bare for the older reason, not today's.
    Our maps draw only what is attested before modern times, so the bank stands for a bank turfed or trodden firm by cattle, not for the mowing and burning of today's ponds, which the older record does not show.
  • A bank with a few trees on it is not drawn yet.
    The Song-dynasty manual plants a pond's bank sparsely with mulberry and cudrania, a second form attested beside the bare bank. Our maps draw every bank bare and do not yet vary between the two - a shortfall, not a finding that banks were bare.
  • No reed harvest is drawn at a pond.
    The pond's reed stands uncut, with no cut bed, drying racks or stacks, because nothing we read puts a harvest at a pond's margin. It is left out because it is not attested, not because it is known to have been absent.
  • Each pond has one outlet, and its channel forks below it.
    A pond draws its water off through one intake, so the maps draw one outlet and one main channel, which divides at junctions downstream as the large canal systems branch. That the pond's channel forks the same way is this project's reading.

A field watered from a pond is drawn with a pond to match.

  • A reservoir pond is sized by the paddy it waters.
    Its area is kept in proportion to the area it waters, as the Song manual of 1149 gave a high field's pond two or three parts in every ten. A pond that is its fields' only water is drawn to that measure. No figure for how much a pond stores for each acre it waters is used, because no page we read, of any period, gives one.
  • A pond that a stream keeps topping up is drawn smaller.
    Such a pond need hold less of the season's water itself - this project's reasoning, not a finding - so it is held below the Song measure. How far below is a GUESS: no page we read gives how much a feeding stream saves.
  • The pond on our map of Hoshigaoka is about a twentieth of the paddy it waters, because a stream feeds the paddy as well.
    About 1.5 ha (~3.7 acres) of water for 31.8 ha (~79 acres) of paddy.
