How our maps keep buildings off wet ground

On a hamlet or village map, a drawn marsh is ground nothing is built on: houses, wells, byres, shrines and graves all keep off it, and no farmhouse stands on the wet low ground below a field's drain. Houses and graves keep to dry ground because the record shows them doing so before modern times; a shrine and a well keep off it by this project's choice. On a town or city map, which draws no marsh below its fields, the rule is a strip of about 33 ft either side of a channel on which nothing is built.

  • Nothing sacred or funerary stands on a marsh.
    Graves went to high ground in China by about 239 BC, and a Japanese rule of 1884 sites a new graveyard on high, dry ground. For a shrine's hall no page we read says the same, and keeping it off the marsh is this project's decision. No shrine, temple hall, graveyard or mausoleum stands on marsh by any part of its footprint: a clipped corner is as wrong as a center.
  • A shrine or a graveyard with nowhere dry inside the settlement goes outside it.
    It is placed by hand on the spur or the high ground beyond the houses.
  • A pond's reed fringe does not count as marsh.
    It is a water margin wherever the pond lies, not a valley bog, so the keep-out does not reach it.
  • A well keeps off the bog because the houses it serves do.
    No rule from before modern times keeps a well off wet ground (Ground too wet to build on). A well is drawn among the houses it serves, and the houses keep to dry ground, so the well does too; that is this project's reasoning, not a finding.
  • A well is tested against the ground below the fields' lowest point, not against the drawn marsh.
    The marsh's upper edge reaches about 90 ft above the foot of the fields, so the reeds tuck under the field (How our maps draw marshes and wetlands (shitchi)), and that strip is farmed and built ground with no reeds on it. Testing a well against the drawn marsh would refuse every well standing there.
  • The well's test binds hamlet and village maps only.
    Towns and cities draw no marsh below their fields.
  • No farmhouse stands on the wet low ground below a field's drain.
    Below the drain that collects a field's water lies reed marsh, low paddy reclaimed from it, or the drainage pond itself. It is not always marsh, but it is the low ground where drainage channels are laid, and such ground drains poorly, so no farmhouse sits there. On a river plain the old settlements likewise stood on the dry levees, with the back marsh farmed as paddy.
  • "Below the drain" is not the same as "low ground".
    The maps' ground is a tilted plane, so its height depends only on how far along the fall a point lies; the edge of the wet ground is a contour, not the ditch. The drawn drain is not a contour: it descends along its length to reach its pond, more steeply than a real one would, a map drawing convention (How our maps draw marshes and wetlands (shitchi)). Measured on the drawn maps, a drain's ends differ in height by 269 ft on Akagahara, 340 ft on Kikuta and 352 ft on Hoshigaoka.
  • So a farmhouse is judged only within the drain's own span across the slope.
    The ground judged is what lies downslope of the drain along the fall, between the drain's two ends. A farm off to the side, past the drain's ends, is a homestead on a dry flank and stands there legitimately.
  • The rule binds dispersed settlements, not a nucleated cluster.
    Each farm of a dispersed settlement is sited on its own and must stand on dry ground on its own; a nucleated cluster is placed as one block under its own rule. A tight cluster beside a diagonal drain reads as "downslope of the drain" without standing on any wet ground at all: unscoped, the rule would flag Ueda, whose cluster on its west margin sits southwest of a drain running northwest to southeast. The scope was measured, not assumed.
The rule the map follows:
  • Hamlet and village maps: no sacred or funerary ground stands on marsh.
    No shrine, temple hall, graveyard or mausoleum, by any part of its footprint. A pond's reed fringe is not marsh for this. One with nowhere dry inside the settlement goes onto the spur or the high ground outside it.
  • Hamlet and village maps: no dwelling stands on the wet ground below a field's drain.
    The ground judged lies downslope of the drain along the declared fall and within the drain's own span across the slope; a farmstead past the drain's ends, on a dry flank, stands legitimately. The rule applies where farmsteads are strewn one by one, not to a nucleated cluster placed as one block, and needs both a declared fall and a drawn drain to mean anything.
  • Town and city maps: nothing is built within about 33 ft of a channel's centerline.
    The width keeps a farmhouse's whole footprint off the water, not merely its center - a hamlet farmhouse's half-diagonal is about 26 ft - a calibration; at a coarser scale the strip is whatever keeps the largest neighboring footprint clear. The test is on the footprint: a corner clipping a channel is a failure.
