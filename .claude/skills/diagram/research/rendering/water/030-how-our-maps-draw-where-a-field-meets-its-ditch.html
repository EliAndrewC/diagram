How our maps draw where a field meets its ditch

Our maps stop drawing ditches at the lateral, about 3 ft wide, and draw a paddy's bund unbroken along every channel it borders. Below the last ditch drawn, the water passes from paddy to paddy through openings in the bunds, as it did before modern times, and neither the openings nor the flow between plots is drawn: both are smaller than a map can show.

  • The finest ditch drawn is the lateral, and it ends blunt.
    A ditch does not taper away to a point. It ends at about 3 ft, with a blunt end, because a real terminal channel has a width of its own and hands its water on to an inlet and the paddies below. The narrowing along its run is what makes that ending read, the stroke arriving at its last width by dwindling (How our maps draw a channel narrowing along its run).
  • The field ditch below it is not drawn.
    The finest channel of the old ladders is about a foot across or less: a hairline on a hamlet map and invisible on a city's. Drawing one grade finer would put the stroke below the 1.5 ft floor that keeps a channel visible on a hamlet map, and collapse the order of widths the maps keep (How our maps draw channel widths).
  • A column of paddies runs on several to about ten plots past where its ditch stops.
    So a ditch ending in the middle of a field is the handoff to the flow from plot to plot, not a line left unfinished. What is modern is a ditch reaching every plot, and the maps do not draw one (How our maps lay out irrigation canals).
  • The inlets and outlets are never drawn.
    An opening of at most 50 cm (~20 in) is about 1.6 ft: a gap of a pixel or two in the bund on the finest map and half a pixel on the coarsest, which does not read as an opening at any scale. A legible one would be drawn larger than life on the most numerous feature of the map, one to a paddy on a map with hundreds. This is a map drawing convention.
  • The bund is drawn unbroken along every channel's bank.
    A reader is expected to understand that water crosses it through openings too small to draw, as they understand that a drawn farmhouse has a door. The unbroken bund is not a claim that the paddies are unwatered.
  • How wide are the bunds drawn?
    About 1.5 ft between plots or along a delivery ditch, and about 3 ft along the head race or a canal - a large bund, inside the 80-150 cm (~3-5 ft) of the large bunds along the canal at the Hattori site. The 3 ft is a calibration against that one excavated site, not a measured Edo figure: no width was found for an Edo bund along a canal.
  • The 1.5 ft bund lies inside the older widths.
    The Hattori site's small bunds were 20-60 cm (~8 in to 2 ft), and an Edo survey reckoned a bund at 1 shaku, about 1 ft, so a narrower bund, if one is ever wanted, stays inside the range from before modern times too.
  • Dry plots stand behind a bare bank 5 ft wide beside a supply canal.
    A strip of walkable earth beside a canal is as old as the early Yayoi paddies and the classical Chinese field layout, so the bank stays. The width was first set from a modern Chinese design guideline's least bank top; the drawn 5 ft is at the top of the Hattori site's 80-150 cm (~3-5 ft). How the bank is measured from the water is at How our maps draw channel widths.
  • A paddy's lowest bund runs with the drain.
    The field's edge on the drain side is a single line running along the ditch, and the bunds that run down the slope between plots meet it end-on.
  • The bottom row of plots follows the drain at both of its ends.
    A plot laid on the contour, while the drain runs at up to about nineteen degrees to it, would drift some 9 ft against the ditch across a 26 ft plot, starting above the drain and ending below it. Where that row holds most of the plots along the edge - 14 of 31 on one map's western spread of paddy - the field's edge reads as a sawtooth, brown ticks poking into bare ground. So each plot of that row samples the drain at both of its ends, and runs both of its edges parallel to the ditch between them.
  • The bund keeps clear of a drain that widens downstream.
    A flat set-back cannot see the drain widening, and a correctly angled bund would still sit inside the ditch's stroke over the lower half of its run. So the set-back is half the ditch's drawn width at that point plus half a bund, and the two meet instead of overlapping.
  • A straight bund is lifted clear of a wandering drain.
    The drain's line wanders on purpose, about 6 ft at a bend every 120-170 ft, so its stretches differ by a few degrees, and a paddy as our maps draw it has straight sides that cannot follow. A bund pinned only at its ends would cut the corner at every bend and land in the water, so each bund is lifted to clear the highest point of the bank across its own span. A farmer laying a straight bund against a crooked ditch would do the same - this project's reasoning, a GUESS. Accepted: a bund can sit a few degrees off its own stretch of drain - about nine on one map - while clearing it everywhere.
