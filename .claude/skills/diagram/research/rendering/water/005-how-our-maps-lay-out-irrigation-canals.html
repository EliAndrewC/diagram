How our maps lay out irrigation canals

Our maps lay a settlement's water network out first and cut the paddies between its lines. The default layout is a comb: supply canals along the high margins of the fields, delivery ditches leaving them at right angles and running down the slope, and one drain gathering the water along the lowest line. Two other layouts are recorded for the ground that calls for them - a fan of canals on an alluvial fan, which the maps can draw, and the ancient jori grid, which they do not draw yet. The history behind each is at Irrigation canals and how they are laid out (yosuiro).

  • The comb follows the Edo reclamation at Minuma.
    Supply along the raised edges and the drain down the low middle is how the Minuma reservoir was reclaimed in 1728, so the comb's supply canals along the high edge and its one collector on the low line are a form from before modern times, and stay as drawn.
  • Each grade of canal leaves the one that feeds it at right angles on flat ground.
    That is the rule of the classical Chinese commentaries, the Lake Tai polders and Japan's jori grid, not only of a modern standard, so the comb's right angles between grades stay as drawn.
  • The comb is the default by choice, with rice China in mind.
    Village pond systems watered much of China's rice land - in the 1950s some 8.3 million of them served 39% of its irrigated area - and the setting's population follows the China of the Song and Ming dynasties, so a village-scale network fed from its own source is the layout a map draws unless its ground calls for another.
  • No ditch runs to every plot.
    A supply ditch giving every plot its own inlet, with its own outlet to the drain, is the standard of modern land consolidation, and no source read shows it earlier, so the maps do not draw one. Below the last ditch drawn, water passes from plot to plot, the older form.
  • A canal is drawn narrowing as its branches leave it.
    The main canals gradually decrease in size as they are tapped by branch canals, so every drawn canal tapers from its head toward its tail. How much it narrows is at A channel taper is a square root, not a straight line.
  • The smallest ditch drawn is the edge of the plot it serves.
    The smallest ditches were often counted as part of the paddies they watered, so the last ditch the map draws runs along the boundary of its plot rather than through open ground.
  • A pond drawn at the foot of a field ends its drainage.
    Where a map draws a pond below the fields to gather the water that leaves them, that siting is how the map ends the drain, a convention: the pond's bank and outlet there are not drawn from a surveyed example.

Where the head race reaches the fields it forks, and the comb carries its supply along both edges.

  • The head race forks into two supply canals, one on each flank.
    A canal waters only the ground below it, and the comb plants paddy on both sides of the division point (bunsuiguchi), so the drawn network shows both arms of the fork, as the two edge canals at Minuma did. Every hamlet gives the second canal an offtake, which draws the second arm partway down its edge, tapering to a thread.
  • How much of each flank does the drawn supply reach?
    On every flank with more than about 150 ft of paddy across the slope from the fork, the drawn main or branch supply reaches at least 80 ft or 30% of that flank. Measured, every hamlet's short flank carries about 170-200 ft of drawn supply against about 250-260 ft of paddy.
  • A supply arm ends in one of two ways.
    Where the dug arm runs past its last offtake, its tail tapers to a thread and dies at the edge of the crop (Inashiro, Kashikawa, Mizuguchi); where it ends at its offtake, the canal hands its whole flow to the delivery ditch and stops at the junction (Sawada). What the maps never draw is a canal at its full working width cut off in the middle of an edge.
  • A single arm is right where the fields lie on one side of their supply.
    A strip of paddy along a contour canal, a flight of terraces and a ring of polder need no second arm, and record no fork, so the rule does not touch them; nor does a fan whose far flank is a sliver under the 150 ft line.
  • Inside a settlement's own fields, the fork is drawn as a fork and nothing more.
    How water was shared at a split between villages, and what stood there, is at How was a ditch's water split between villages, and what shows at the split?

Two layouts besides the comb are recorded for the ground that calls for them.

  • The fan: canals radiating from the mouth of a valley.
    Where the land fans out below the pond, the maps can draw gently descending canals spreading from the valley mouth over the fan. It is an option, not the default, and its radiating plan is a GUESS, as the history section says.
  • The jori grid is recorded for later and not drawn.
    A long-settled plains village in the setting's old heartland provinces, such as the Crane and Phoenix cores, would on this project's reading show semi-regular gridded paddies. Until the grid is built, the maps draw the irregular patchwork everywhere, a deviation on such ground (How our maps draw rice paddies and their plots (suiden)); the patchwork is right for villages whose fields follow the terrain, like Kikuta.
