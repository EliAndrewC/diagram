How our maps draw a channel narrowing along its run

A supply ditch on our maps narrows along its length as it hands its water to the fields it passes, as a real main canal narrows where its branch canals tap it, and a drain widens as it gathers theirs. Both change width as a square root, not in a straight line, so a ditch keeps its working width while it still has most of its water. At real size the change is too gentle for the eye to follow along a stroke, and the maps keep real size rather than draw every channel wider than it was.

  • A channel's width follows the square-root taper law along its length.
    The square of the width falls by the same amount every foot, so the width falls slowly at first and fast at the end. On a delivery ditch running 8.0 to 3.0 ft, drawn here about three times its real size so the curve's shape shows:

  • The drawn channel and the bunds beside it take their width from the same rule.
    Everywhere the maps need a channel's width at a point - the ink, and the room the bunds keep from its bank - one shared rule gives it, so no second formula can lay a bund inside the water.
  • A width that narrowed in a straight line would misstate the water and look wrong.
    Narrowing the width evenly quietly says the water a channel carries is in proportion to its width, when in an earth channel the width grows only as the square root of the water. It also looks wrong: the stroke thins imperceptibly along its whole length and then stops dead at a still-substantial width. Under the square root the ditch keeps its working width while it has most of its water, then dwindles hard.
  • A drain widens the same way in reverse.
    From 3.0 to 12.0 ft it is 8.75 rather than 7.50 at mid-run, so a drain gains body in its first stretch.
  • The taper law barely changes a channel whose two ends are close in width.
    It bites only where a stroke really sheds, or gathers, most of what it carries; a shallow taper is drawn almost as a straight line.
  • Can a reader see a ditch narrow at true size?
    Hardly: what the eye follows is the rate of narrowing, and at true size it is tiny. A delivery ditch sheds one foot of width over five hundred, and the supply canal along the upper edge of the fields three over fifteen hundred. The two ends of a canal differ plainly side by side, but only the short canal along the far edge visibly narrows along its length.

  • Our maps draw every channel at its real width, even though the narrowing then barely shows.
    A taper the eye can follow would need every channel drawn wider than it was, not a different law, so real size and a visible taper pull against each other. Drawing every channel one and a half times its width (a delivery ditch 3.75 to 1.8 ft, its tail scaled from its real 1.2 ft end; 0.39 ft lost per 100 ft) or twice it (5.0 to 2.4 ft, 0.52) was weighed, and real size kept, so that every channel stays the width it really was.
