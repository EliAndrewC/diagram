How our maps draw flood defenses: ring dikes and refuge mounds (waju and mizuka)

Villages on Japan's low river plains lived with floods, and our maps draw the three ways they built against them. They set their houses on the natural levee, the highest ground by the river. On the lowest plains, from the seventeenth century, some ringed the village and its fields with a dike. And a few wealthy farmsteads kept a storehouse on a raised mound to flee to, with a mound in common or a raised shrine for everyone else.

  • A flood-plain village seats its houses on the natural levee, the highest ground by the river.
    That is where the villages of the Kiso plain stood from early on, so every flood-plain map does it, with no choice made at random.
  • Only on the lowest plains may a village be ringed by a dike, and which ones is a knob, chosen at random.
    The ring dike is a regional form of the lowest, most flood-prone plains, and a late one: such dikes came in the seventeenth century, and before that and elsewhere levees were built only where they were needed. They survive along the Kiso, the Shinano, the Arakawa, the Tone and the Yodo. The record gives no share of ringed villages, so the odds are arbitrary.
  • A ringed village draws its water from wells as well as through an intake at the upstream head of its dike.
    A waju generally had its intake at the head of the ring and its drain at the tail, and an intake through the dike so often caused a breach that wells came into use beside it.
  • Which farmsteads carry a refuge storehouse?
    The richest: the headman's and one or two more. Only fairly wealthy houses had one, and no source counts how many, so the number is a GUESS.
  • A farmstead's refuge storehouse stands to the northwest of its house.
    The mizuya stood northwest of a south-facing house, on a stone base a step higher.
  • How high above the house is the refuge's mound?
    Its fill raised it some tens of centimeters (under a foot to about 3 ft) to 3 m (~10 ft) above the house, so the map raises it 1 to 10 ft.
  • Every flood-plain village also has one refuge in common, a mound or its shrine on raised ground, for the households with none of their own.
    The life-saving platforms were raised by the community or a landlord for those who had no mizuya, and a raised shrine could serve instead. Both forms are attested, so which one a village has is a knob, chosen from the seed.

The rule the map follows: a flood-plain village seats its houses on the natural levee, the highest ground by the river. On the lowest plains the map chooses from its seed whether a ring dike is drawn round the village and its fields; a ringed village draws its water from wells as well as through an intake at the upstream head of the dike. The richest farmsteads (a guess at the share: the headman's and one or two more) carry a mizuya: a storehouse on a stone-faced mound 1 to 10 ft higher than the house, to its northwest; every flood-plain village also has one communal refuge, a mound or its shrine on raised ground, the form chosen from the seed.
