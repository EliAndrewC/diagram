How our maps draw moat water

Every moated city on our maps shows where its moat's water comes from and where it goes. A city's moat is about 66 ft wide and is fed from off the sheet by a watercourse nearly as wide; a closed ring that is fed has an outfall as wide again, leaving from its far side; a city on a river opens its moat onto the river; and the fields around a city draw water from the moat or drain into it. The castle's inner moat is still water joined to nothing.

  • A city's moat is about 66 ft wide.
    That is a provincial seat's moat, inside the range the measured ditches of the great cities give (How wide and deep is a city's moat?). It is a closed ring pushed outward from the rampart, with every point of the wall inside it.
  • At least one watercourse reaches the moat from a map edge, and it is within a quarter of the moat's width.
    A trickle could not keep a moat full, so the feeder is drawn at the moat's own width rather than as a thread: a stream half a moat wide reads as a moat that must be nearly empty. That a feeder carries something like what its moat holds is a GUESS. A stream may end at the moat instead of running on off the far edge.
  • Moat water is drawn as solid color with a lighter sheen down its middle, never with a dashed centerline.
    A dash is this map's mark for a road's lane, so a dash on water would borrow from the wrong vocabulary - a map drawing convention.

A moat's current is recorded, not worked out from its shape. These rules bind the towns and cities no generator draws yet.

  • A moat records where its water comes in and where it goes out, not a direction.
    A ring has no upstream end, so "downstream of a moated city" is reasoned from those two points. An open moat cut from a river takes them from its two junctions with the river; a closed ring declares them, because the ring is dug before its feeder and its outfall exist.
  • Every rule about a moat's current reads those two points.
    None works a direction out again from the moat's shape alongside them: two sources of truth for one fact is how the two drift apart.
  • A closed ring moat that is fed also drains.
    A second off-map stream leaves the ring on the face opposite the feeder, so the current flushes the ring corner to corner, as Beijing's moats took their water in at the northwest and let it out at the southeast. It leaves by the low, downstream rim - on Tango, to the southeast - and is drawn as wide as the feeder, because the water that comes in has to go out. This binds the cities no generator draws yet.
  • Irrigation taps are no substitute for the outfall.
    They are a seasonal second way out: a 21 ft summer tap cannot balance a 66 ft feeder that runs all year.
  • A moat with no feeder needs no outfall, and a moat cut from a river already has one.
    A moat filled by springs or rain has no inflow to shed. An open moat cut from a river takes from it upstream and returns to it downstream.
  • A city on a river opens its moat onto the river.
    Minami's and Nagahara's moats back onto their rivers, so the moat's two ends where they meet the river are the connection, and nothing more is drawn.
  • A ring standing off its river is joined to it by two channels as wide as the moat.
    Shiro Daika's ring stands about 600 ft off its river bank, so a 66 ft sluiced feeder and a 66 ft drain join it to the river, as Tango's 66 ft feeder and outfall join its ring, and the moat reads as flowing through.
  • The feeder taps its river upstream, not where the river comes closest.
    On Shiro Daika the land falls from northeast to southwest, so water let in at the ring's low southeast corner, where the river comes closest, could not climb the east arc, and the whole north of the ring would be a dead arm. The feeder taps the river's upper reach, below the aqueduct's intake, and runs steadily down the map - every stretch west and south - into the ring's east arc.
  • The drain rejoins the river below its bend, swept downstream.
    It leaves the ring's southeast arc onto ground that the land's fall puts below the arc.
  • The towpath crosses the drain's mouth on a plank deck.
    A towpath was bridged where a side channel cut across it, as on British canals, the one tradition read on the point.
  • Tango's moat is a stream turned into a ring and sent on its way.
    That arrangement has a history: Edo dug a stretch of its outer moat by moving a river's course, and part of Beijing's spring water ran on through the city and its palace moat into the inner city's south moat.
  • No trace of the stream's old bed is drawn through the city.
    Cities build over even their live channels - Japan's urban streams were culverted, and Beijing's moats covered, in modern times - and that an abandoned course is gone under centuries of building is this project's reading, a GUESS. Its absence is realistic, not an omission.
  • Irrigation taps may leave either side of a ring.
    The ring splits its feeder's flow into two arcs that rejoin at the outfall, so a tap on either side draws with the current.

Where a channel meets the moat, its angle shows which way the water runs.

  • A channel meets the moat swept with the current.
    An offtake leaves pointing downstream, and a drain arrives pointing downstream: its heading where it meets the ring carries a downstream part. On the page, a tap that meets the ring square says nothing about which way the water runs. What hydraulics says of the angle at which channels part and join is at Which way water flows, and how channels bend and join, and how our maps draw those junctions at How our maps draw bends, junctions and the run of the water.
  • Every offtake on Tango and Nagahara leaves the ring at 47 to 48 degrees, and every drain arrives at 14 to 60 degrees.
    These are measured on the two drawn provincial cities, after their square taps were regraded.
  • A square tap is regraded at its moat end only.
    Its end on the moat walks along the rim - upstream for an offtake, downstream for an arriving drain - until its mouth is swept, measured by distance along the rim; the sluice does not move, so neither does the field it feeds.
  • Whether a field lies upstream of its tap is judged against a current snapped to the compass.
    An irrigation offtake must not leave the ring from a point back upstream of the ground it waters. Measuring the ring's direction exactly at each tap was built, and is the physically correct picture, but an offtake leaves the ring roughly at a right angle, so its share along that exact direction is near arbitrary: it flagged one of Tango's taps and two of Nagahara's while sparing their neighbors, on nothing more than which side of the tap the field happened to sit. The coarse test asks the real question, whether the field lies back upstream. A map drawing convention: a simplification chosen because the precise measurement answers a different question.

Around a moated city, the fields draw water from the moat or drain into it.

  • A moated city waters at least three of its large fields from the moat.
    Each tap stands about 90 ft outside the moat's rim, with a short stub bridging rim to gate, and the channel it feeds ends inside the field, not near it.
  • A tap stands upstream of the field it serves.
    So the water runs downstream into the field. A channel whose field lay upstream of its tap would read as water running out of the field into the moat, backwards.
  • A field upstream of the moat drains into it instead.
    Such a field takes its water from high ground off the sheet, or from the moat's source stream, and discharges into the moat below it, gated at the outfall and swept with the current; or it runs off the sheet with its water implied. The count of three moat-fed fields is then made up from a field downstream. Which way the water runs between a field and the moat is this project's choice, following the slope, not something a source sets; that moats both drained and watered the ground around them is attested.
  • A field's collecting ditch may empty back into the moat.
    Or it runs off the edge of the cropped view.
  • A channel's mouth is laid onto the moat's open water.
    Its end is snapped onto the water's edge so that it covers the rim, rather than drawing its bed as a colored line across the water - a map drawing convention, the same one a channel meeting a pond gets.
  • Channels are drawn with a bend, as a hairline, and farmsteads keep about 100 ft from them.
    No channel is ruler-straight. The hairline's width on the sheet is a drawing convention, not a measured ditch. The ground about 100 ft either side of each channel is kept clear of farmsteads.
  • A field inside the walls drains to the moat underground.
    It records the moat as where its water goes, but its ditch stops short of the patrol road: the runoff drops into an implied underground stone conduit that passes beneath the ring road, the rampart and the moat. No ditch is ever drawn running through a city wall; the water gate is underground.
  • The capital's north shelf stays unfarmed, for want of room.
    About 360 ft lie between the rampart and the edge of the sheet, and a field system of the kind the maps draw cannot fit there at any edge: a field's run downhill from its head channel, plus the strip that channel and its offtakes need along the top, is deeper than the shelf. The current does not rule it out - a field there could drain into the moat like any other upstream field - the room does.

The castle's own moat is another matter.

  • A castle's inner moat is still water joined to nothing.
    It holds groundwater and rain, and no channel is drawn to feed or drain it. Still, standing water is how such a moat looked, not a lapse of the map.
