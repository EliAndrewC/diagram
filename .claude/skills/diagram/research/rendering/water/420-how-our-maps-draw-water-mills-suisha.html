How our maps draw water mills (suisha)

A village or hamlet whose stream has fall - enough drop to turn a wheel - may have a water mill, rolled for each settlement; a town draws no mill of its own.

  • A village mill is a knob.
    The record shows two forms: none, each household pounding its own rice, which leaves nothing on the map; or one water mill on the stream, owned by the headman or a wealthy farmer. So whether a settlement has one is rolled, and only where a stream with fall passes. The proportion is a GUESS.
  • Where does a drawn mill stand?
    On the stream bank below the settlement's intake, near the headman's house where the stream allows, its wheel in a short channel (a mill race) led off the stream. The seat and the race are a GUESS: no page we read says where a mill stood against a village's intake.
  • How large is a drawn mill-house?
    About 15 by 20 ft, a GUESS: no page we read gives a village mill-house's size.
  • A town draws no mill of its own.
    Its rice was polished in its shops, and its water mills stood on the streams of the villages outside it, as round Edo.

The rule the map follows: a village or hamlet whose stream has fall rolls from its seed whether it has a water mill (the proportion a guess); if it does, one mill stands on the stream bank below the settlement's intake, its wheel in a short channel (a mill race) led off the stream, the mill-house about 15 by 20 ft, near the headman's house where the stream allows - the seat, the race and the size all guesses. A town draws no mill of its own: its milling is in its shops and yards, and on the streams of the villages outside it.
