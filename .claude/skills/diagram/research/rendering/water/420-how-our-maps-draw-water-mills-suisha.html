How our maps draw water mills (suisha)

A village or hamlet whose stream has fall - enough drop to turn a wheel - may have a water mill, decided at random for each settlement; a town draws no mill of its own.

  • On our maps, some villages have a water mill and some have none.
    The record shows two forms: none, a household pounding its own rice in its own mortar, which leaves nothing on the map; or a water mill on the stream, owned by the headman or a wealthy farmer (and sometimes more than one: the Oguruma and Shinguruma stood about 200 m (~656 ft) apart on one stream). So whether a settlement has one is a knob, decided at random, and only where a stream with fall passes. The share of villages with a mill is not yet set; it will be a GUESS.
  • Where does a drawn mill stand?
    On the stream bank below the settlement's intake, near the headman's house where the stream allows, its wheel in a short channel (a mill race) led off the stream. The seat and the race are a GUESS: no page we read says where a mill stood against a village's intake.
  • We draw a mill-house at about 15 by 20 ft.
    The size is a GUESS: no page we read gives a village mill-house's size.
  • A town draws no mill of its own.
    Its rice was polished - pounded free of its bran, the work a village mill did - in its shops, and its water mills stood on the streams of the villages outside it, as round Edo.

The rule the map follows: a village or hamlet whose stream has fall rolls from its seed whether it has a water mill (the proportion a guess); if it does, one mill stands on the stream bank below the settlement's intake, its wheel in a short channel (a mill race) led off the stream, the mill-house about 15 by 20 ft, near the headman's house where the stream allows - the seat, the race and the size all guesses. A town draws no mill of its own: its milling is in its shops and yards, and on the streams of the villages outside it.
