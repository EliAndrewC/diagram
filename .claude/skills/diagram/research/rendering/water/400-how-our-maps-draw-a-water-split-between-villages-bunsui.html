How our maps draw a water split between villages (bunsui)

A fork inside one settlement's own fields is drawn as the fork and nothing else, because the water there was shared by rule and by turn, which leave nothing to draw. Where a ditch forks between two settlements, the map rolls one of two forms the record attests: an open fork, or a division flume across it.

  • No work is drawn at a fork inside one settlement's fields.
    Inside a village the smallest ditches were kept by the small groups of farmers they served, and under rotation the head of the village directed the water to every paddy. Turns and rules leave nothing on the ground.
  • A split between two settlements is a knob.
    Where a map shows a neighbor's ditch leaving its own, the form is rolled for each settlement: an open fork whose mouths were cut, staked or sandbagged to agreed widths, or a division flume across the fork, as at Kuji. The open fork is taken as the likelier; the proportion is a GUESS.
  • An open fork's cut, stake or sandbags are not drawn.
    None of them is big enough to draw at a hamlet's scale, so an open fork looks like any other fork.
  • How large is a division flume on our maps?
    A box across the fork about as wide as the ditch and about 6 ft long along it. It is a GUESS: no page we read gives the size of a flume.
  • What is a division flume drawn as?
    Timber, a GUESS: no page we read gives what the Kuji flume was built of.
  • The round concrete divider is never drawn.
    The first was built in 1914, long after the period these maps are set in.
