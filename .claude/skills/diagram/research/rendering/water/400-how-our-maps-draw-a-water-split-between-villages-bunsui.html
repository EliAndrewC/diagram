How our maps draw a water split between villages (bunsui)

A fork inside one settlement's own fields is drawn as the fork and nothing else. Where a ditch forks between two settlements, the map picks one of two forms the record attests, at random: an open fork, or a division flume across it.

  • No structure is drawn at a fork inside one settlement's fields.
    Inside a village the smallest ditches were kept by the small groups of farmers they served, and under rotation the head of the village directed the water to every paddy. Turns and rules leave nothing on the ground.
  • A split between two settlements is drawn in one of two recorded forms.
    Where a map shows a neighbor's ditch leaving its own, the record gives two forms: an open fork whose mouths were staked or sandbagged to agreed widths, or a division flume across the fork, as at Kuji. Which one a settlement gets is a knob.
  • Which form is the commoner on our maps?
    The open fork. The record shows the staked or sandbagged mouth in a general reference and on a Nagano canal, and the flume at one named place, Kuji; no page counts them, so the open fork as the commoner, and the share each form gets, are our GUESS.
  • The stakes and sandbags that set an open fork's shares are not drawn.
    None of them is big enough to draw at a hamlet's scale, so an open fork looks like any other fork.
  • A division flume is drawn as a box across the fork, about as wide as the ditch and about 6 ft long.
    The size is a GUESS: no page we read gives the size of a flume.
  • A division flume is drawn in timber.
    This is a GUESS: no page we read gives what the Kuji flume was built of.
  • The round concrete divider is never drawn.
    It splits a canal's flow in exact shares, and the first was built in 1914, long after the period these maps are set in.

The rule the map follows: no division work is drawn at a fork inside one settlement's fields. Where a ditch forks between two settlements, the map picks the form at random from its seed: an open fork (the commoner, a guess at the share) or a division flume (timber, a guess), a box across the fork about as wide as the ditch and about 6 ft long along it (a guess). The round divider is never drawn.
