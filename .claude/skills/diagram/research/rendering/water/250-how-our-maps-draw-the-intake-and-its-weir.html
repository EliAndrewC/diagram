How our maps draw the intake and its weir

On our maps a hamlet's brook comes down off the high ground, passes the head of its fan of paddies on one side, and runs on down that flank. At the fan's head, on the fan's bank, is the intake, and the head race leaves it at an acute angle pointing downstream and runs to the point where it forks. Some hamlets have a weir across the brook at the intake and some take their water off the bare bank, and a weir is built in one of four ways.

  • The brook runs on past the intake.
    A brook is tapped, not swallowed: the old closures mostly crossed half the river, and the water divides on the dug canal below the intake. So the fan of paddies is cut away from the brook - its supply canal on the brook's side stops short, leaving the stream its margin - and the brook runs on down that flank to leave the map or to take in the field's drain (where the water goes once it has watered the paddies).
  • The maps never draw a brook taken whole by its ditch.
    A diversion weir may by definition take all of a stream, but no source we read shows a village's ditch doing so, so the maps draw only the form the record shows. The maps roll between two forms only where the record shows a thing done two ways, and this one is shown done one way.
  • The intake stands at the head of the fan, on the fan's bank.
    A one-bank intake on the side of the land it serves is the rule on steep rivers, and a treatise of 1902 set the head works where the river leaves the mountains, which is where a hamlet's brook meets the head of its fan.
  • The head race leaves the bank at an acute angle, pointing downstream.
    A mountain brook's water is clean, and an offtake on clean water may leave at an angle, where one on silt-laden water takes a right angle. The angle is the one the maps give any offtake leaving its parent (Canal junction angles - an offtake leaves pointing downstream), itself a GUESS, no source giving a figure for it.
  • How long is the head race?
    As long as the ground makes it: the race runs from the intake to the fork at the fan's head, and the intake's distance above the fork is chosen afresh for each map within the short distances the record allows. The number is a GUESS, no source giving one; the shape is derived.
  • No settling basin is drawn.
    A basin at the head of the canal is modern practice, and whether a village dug one is not recorded.
  • Whether a hamlet has a weir is a knob, rolled with an even chance.
    Which form a hamlet built followed from its brook's level through the irrigation season: a brook that kept its level took a bare mouth, and one whose level fell took a weir. The maps give a brook no level or season, so they cannot follow that condition, and each hamlet rolls one form or the other. No source counts the two, so the even chance is a GUESS.
  • On a hamlet without a weir, the head race opens out of the brook's bank.
    The bare intake is an opening, so the ditch begins at the bank's edge and is not laid over the stream. No gate or boards are drawn: none is recorded at a village intake, and at a two-foot opening either would be smaller than the map can show.
  • A weir runs diagonally upstream from the intake mouth.
    That is how the old oblique weirs ran. The form is taken from the modern engineering histories' descriptions, since we read no period drawing of a village weir.
  • A weir is drawn across the whole brook.
    Half-river closures were the common old form, but at a brook 7 ft wide a half-bar would be a pixel or two, so the full closure is a map drawing convention, chosen so the weir can be seen.
  • A weir is drawn so that it does not read as a footbridge.
    The stone-packed crib is drawn in stone gray with its baulks ticked across it and a lip along its upstream face, so that it reads as a thing that holds water back rather than as one of the timber planks that carry a lane over a ditch.
  • A weir is built in one of four ways, rolled with an even chance.
    A weir on small water was built of what lay to hand, in more than one way, so its form is a knob, and each form is drawn at its own thickness. No source counts the forms, so the even chance among them is a GUESS.
    • A fence of stakes woven with brushwood, drawn about 1.5 ft thick.
      A fence is as thick as its row of stakes, so it is drawn wider than that, so that it can be seen - a convention. It is woven with brushwood or bamboo, the weave the premodern record names, not with the reed found only in a present-day weir; a fence of brushwood draws the same as one of reed.
    • A frame of stakes and logs packed with clay, drawn 5 ft thick.
      It is built like the weir excavated at the Kodera site. The 5 ft is a GUESS, since no village figure was found.
    • A crib of timber packed with stone, drawn 5 ft thick.
      Up to the late sixteenth century, intake works were mostly small ones of timber frames packed with stone. The 5 ft is a GUESS for the same reason.
    • A course of stone-filled baskets, drawn about 2 ft thick.
      That is a basket's read diameter. A course of them across a brook at all is a GUESS: the only gabions we found damming water were on large rivers.

The rule the map follows: a weir hamlet's weir takes one of four forms, rolled per settlement with an even chance - a fence of stakes woven with brushwood, a frame of stakes and logs packed with clay, a crib of timber packed with stone, or a course of stone-filled baskets (this last a guess for small water) - drawn about 1.5 ft thick for the fence, about 2 ft for the baskets, and 5 ft for the frame and the crib.
