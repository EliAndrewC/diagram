How our maps draw marshes and wetlands (shitchi)

On a hamlet or a village map, a reed marsh lies on the undrained low ground below the fields of the alluvial fan, where the water that sank into the fan comes back up. Paddy let go on such ground turns back into wetland, so the marsh is drawn where the fields stop. It is as wide as the fields above it, with 90 ft more on each side, its upper edge follows the foot of the fields, and nothing is built on it. A town or a city map draws no such marsh. A walled town may instead carry a strip of marsh hugging its walls.

  • The marsh is as wide as the fields above it, with 90 ft more on each side.
    The springs at a fan's foot come out along its toe, and slope wetlands are found on the edge of permeable fans, so the wet ground follows the fan, not the valley. The band takes its width across the slope from the cultivated ground - the fan's plots and the dry fields round them - and adds 90 ft each side, stopping at the map's edge, so the wet ground reads as spreading a little past the watered fields. The 90 ft is not a new number: the marsh's upper edge already reaches 90 ft up past the foot of the fields, where no reed is drawn because that ground is paddy.
  • Every hamlet and village map leaves dry ground for a road out downhill.
    The marsh lies across the foot of the fields, which is the way a road leaving downhill must pass. With the marsh no wider than the fields, dry ground is left at both ends of it; on Ikegami's and Akagahara's maps the roads run south on it. Moritono's ground falls west, where a straight track would run through the wet ground, so its road is turned aside onto dry ground. Where a lane may run is at How our maps draw village lanes
  • The marsh runs straight across the slope as a whole, not in the arc of a real fan's toe.
    A fan is laid down in a fan shape with the valley mouth as its apex, so its spring line curves round its foot. This project draws a band along the contour instead, a deliberate deviation: its reeds are scattered and fade to nothing at the margin, and its ends bow outward downhill as seepage spreads, so the straight run does not read as an edge.
  • The marsh's upper edge follows the foot of the fields, point by point.
    Across the slope, at short steps, the edge is set 90 ft above the lowest field near that point, and smoothed, so the reeds begin at the fan's foot all along it and no reed is drawn on a field.
  • No strip of reeds is laid along the collector drain.
    A modern drainage standard lays a field's interceptor drain along the contours, but no source from before modern times puts a strip of reeds along a drain, so the maps lay none. Where the marsh's edge runs beside the collector, it is because both follow the foot of the fields.
  • The collector drain is drawn falling far more steeply than a real one.
    A drain is drawn steep so its flow reads, a map drawing convention. A real lowland collector falls perhaps 0.1%, this project's estimate and a GUESS, since no page we read gives one. The marsh is not bent to the drawn grade.
  • On the maps drawn by hand rather than by the program, the marsh may reach up among the fields.
    These maps lay out their marsh as a generous region that overlaps the fields, and reeds fill it up to each field's low edge, since no reed is drawn on a paddy. On Kikuta's map the marsh reaches above the whole collector drain, so ground at rice height is drawn as wet.
  • Town and city maps draw no marsh below their fields.
    At Edo an inlet was filled to make land for the city, and the villages round a city manured its vegetables with its night soil, and at Osaka merchants made new fields on the old riverbed and the shallows of the bay shore; on this project's reading of the difference between village and city, a city's edge kept little wet ground. The split is by the map's scale.

Wet ground kept for war is drawn differently. The Song dynasty's official history records a proposal to give China's northern frontier a chain of shallow lakes and dikes against cavalry, and in Japan Bitchu Takamatsu castle's surrounding wetland served it as a moat.

The rule the map follows: on town and city maps (none of which has one yet), placed by hand:
  • A defensive marsh is placed by hand outside a town's walls, hugging them.
    It begins at the moat's outer bank or the foot of the wall and stays within about 180 ft of the wall or moat line, because a bog standing off from the fortification defends nothing. The 180 ft is a GUESS.
  • A defensive marsh is drawn only on a map that has a wall or a moat.
    With no fortification to guard, it has no purpose.
  • A defensive marsh follows the walls wherever they run, not the low ground.
    The rule that puts the marsh on low ground does not apply to it.
  • A road across a defensive marsh is a causeway, and keeps a bare tread.
    A causeway keeps the approaches few and narrow, which is what this project takes such a marsh to be for.
  • Nothing can be built on a defensive marsh, as on the marsh below the fields.
    Shrines, temples and graves are kept off it too.
