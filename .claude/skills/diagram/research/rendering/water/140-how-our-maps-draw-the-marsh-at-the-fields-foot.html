How our maps draw the marsh at the fields' foot

On a hamlet or a village map, a reed marsh lies on the undrained low ground below the fields, where draining stopped and the ground stayed wet. It is as wide as the fields above it, with 90 ft more on each side, and its upper edge follows the foot of the fields. A town or a city map draws no such marsh. A walled town may instead carry a belt of marsh hugging its walls.

  • The marsh is as wide as the fields above it, with a shoulder of 90 ft on each side.
    The springs at a fan's foot come out along its toe, and slope wetlands are found on the edge of permeable fans, so the wet ground follows the fan, not the valley. The band takes its width across the slope from the cultivated ground - the fan's plots and the dry fields round them - and adds 90 ft each side for the seepage spreading a little past the watered ground, stopping at the map's edge. The 90 ft is the same distance the band already overlaps the fields uphill, not a new number.
  • Every hamlet and village keeps a dry way out downhill.
    With the marsh no wider than the fields, dry ground is left at both ends of it. On Ikegami's and Akagahara's maps the roads run south over ground dry for their whole length; Moritono's, whose ground falls west and whose straight track would run down the wet side, keeps its turned course.   • The marsh is drawn as a straight band across the slope, not the arc of a real fan's toe.
    A fan is laid down in a fan shape with the valley mouth as its apex, so its spring line curves round its foot. This project draws a band along the contour instead, a deliberate deviation: its reeds are scattered and fade to nothing at the margin, and its ends bow outward downhill as seepage spreads, so the straight run does not read as an edge.
  • The marsh's upper edge follows the foot of the fields, point by point.
    Across the slope, at short steps, the edge is set a shoulder above the lowest field near that point, and smoothed, so the reeds begin at the fan's foot all along it and no reed is drawn on a field.
  • The edge is set by the fields, never by the drain.
    A modern drainage standard lays a field's interceptor drain along the contours, but no source from before modern times puts a strip of reeds along a drain, so the maps lay none. Where the marsh's edge runs beside the collector, it is because both follow the foot of the fields.
  • The collector is drawn falling far more steeply than a real one, and the marsh does not follow its grade.
    A drain is drawn steep so its flow reads, a map drawing convention. A real lowland collector falls perhaps 0.1%, this project's estimate and a GUESS, since no page we read gives one, so the marsh keeps to the ground's own contours rather than following the drawn drain.
  • A hand-drawn map's marsh may reach above its fields.
    Some older maps draw their marsh by hand and claim a generous region that overlaps the fields; the reeds then fill up to each field's true low edge, because no reed is drawn on a paddy. Kikuta's marsh reaches above its whole collector, claiming ground at rice height as wet, which shows only because no reed stands on a paddy. A drawn edge is judged by what the ground is, not by which method drew it.
  • Town and city maps draw no marsh below their fields.
    Around towns and cities the record finds wet ground drained or filled and the farmland worked hard, so on this project's reading of the difference between village and city, only hamlet and village maps carry the marsh. The split is by the map's scale, because that is how the difference is stated.

Wet ground kept for war is drawn differently: a frontier could be given a belt of meres and dikes against cavalry, and Bitchu Takamatsu castle's surrounding wetland served it as a moat.

The rule the map follows: on town and city maps, where no generator draws the settlement yet and no drawn map carries one,
  • A defensive belt of marsh is placed by hand outside the walls, hugging them.
    It begins at the moat's outer bank or the foot of the wall and stays within about 180 ft of the wall or moat line, because a bog standing off from the fortification defends nothing. The 180 ft is a GUESS.
  • It is drawn only on a map that has a wall or a moat.
    With no fortification to guard, a belt of marsh has no purpose.
  • It follows the walls wherever they run, not the low ground.
    The rule that puts the marsh on low ground does not apply to it.
  • A road across it is a causeway, and keeps a bare tread.
    Few and narrow approaches are the belt's whole purpose.
  • It cannot be built on, just like the marsh below the fields.
    Shrines, temples and graves are kept off it too.
