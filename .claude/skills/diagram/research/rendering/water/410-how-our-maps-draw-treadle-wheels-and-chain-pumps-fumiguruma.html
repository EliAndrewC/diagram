How our maps draw treadle wheels and chain pumps (fumiguruma)

Our maps water every field by gravity, from a ditch that leaves a stream above it, so a field needs nothing to lift its water and none is drawn. Only where a settlement's fields stand above the water they draw from is a lifting work drawn, and then only sometimes.

  • No lifting device is drawn at a gravity-fed field.
    The comb of ditches the maps draw is fed from above, so its water runs down to the paddies on its own.
  • A treadle wheel is not drawn, because it was a tool, not a fixture.
    The Japanese devices were carried out to a paddy's edge and home again; the treadle wheel was not fixed in place. A map does not draw a tool.
  • Two lifting works were fixtures, and could be drawn.
    Both stand only where a settlement's fields lie above their water: a chain-pump station at the lip of a polder or a low terrace, and, on a river bank, a noria in the stream with its trough to the fields.
  • A polder is the likeliest such ground, though in Japan even there the wheel was carried.
    The treadle wheel spread in the reclaimed lowlands downstream, where new channels were hard to dig.
  • Where fields stand above their water, the form is a knob.
    The two traditions differ, so the form is rolled for each settlement: the Japanese form, the carried wheel, which leaves nothing to draw, or the Chinese form, a fixed pump or a noria. The proportion between them is a GUESS.
  • How many lifting works does such a settlement get?
    One per feeding ditch, at the ditch's lip - a GUESS: no page we read counts a village's devices.
  • How long is a drawn chain pump?
    About 10 to 21 ft up the bank: the Ming encyclopedia gives a pump one to two zhang long, and the conversion takes the zhang at about 3.2 m (~10 ft), a figure not cited here.

The rule the map follows: no lifting device is drawn at a gravity-fed field. Where a settlement's fields stand above the water they draw from, the map rolls the form from its seed: nothing drawn (the carried wheel), or one fixed lifting work per feeding ditch at the ditch's lip - a chain pump, about 10 to 21 ft long up the bank, or, beside a river with current, a noria. The proportion between the forms, and one work per ditch, are guesses.
