How our maps draw torii and the shrine approach

On our maps every shrine's approach runs up to its hall under an avenue of one, three or seven torii, the number rolled by the size of the settlement. The arches are laid 12 ft apart, the first where the approach enters the shrine's ground and the innermost 12 ft off the hall's face. A row of arches at an ordinary shrine is a deliberate deviation: history has one only in modern times.

  • Our maps raise arches at any shrine, a deliberate deviation.
    Before modern times a row of donated arches is attested only at a great Inari shrine; at a village shrine, or any ordinary shrine, the early dated arches are single gates, and a row is attested only in modern times. The arch itself is historical: a single stone arch at a shrine's approach is dated by its inscription to 1679, and another is thought older still. This setting mixes periods, so it uses a real form more widely than history did.
  • How many arches does a shrine get?
    One, three or seven, rolled from the map's seed by the size of the settlement (a knob):
    settlementone archthreeseven
    village60%30%10%
    town30%60%10%
    city30%40%30%
    capital10%60%30%
    History has most shrines with one arch, a ranked few with two or three, and rows of dozens or more at the Inari shrines. The maps keep to one, three and seven because those numbers carry power in this setting, seven most of all - a deliberate deviation. The larger the settlement, the likelier a long avenue - this project's reading of an arch as a gift, a larger place having more givers. The seven arches at Hoshigaoka are that map's own.
  • The count follows patronage, not the room in front of the shrine.
    An avenue could be sized to the space before it, on the reasoning that a shrine with a long approach would fill it. It is not: an arch was a gift, so the count records what worshippers gave, and sizing by space would rule out three arches, which a town rolls most of the time. What the ground does govern is the avenue's line, which the map's author draws to fit the ground it crosses.
  • The arches are laid 12 ft apart, on every map.
    That is two ken, the building measure of about 6 ft, and the middle of a band of 10 to 13 ft, which the one small rural row whose spacing can be estimated, at about 10 to 14 ft, bounds. The figure is a GUESS inside that band. The same pitch holds at every scale and in every size of settlement.
  • Why not closer, or wider?
    At 10 ft, at three feet to the pixel, the arches would stand barely three pixels apart, too close to read as separate. Nothing favored 13 ft over 12. A real donated row, about 1.3 m (~4 ft) an arch over a whole climb, is packed tighter than arches can be drawn apart at these scales; that limit is the drawing's, not a finding about how gates were spaced.
  • A few great named sites are drawn with a row of donated arches.
    A row like Fushimi Inari's, around 800 arches on its main path, is not how an ordinary approach in this setting works. It belongs to a handful of named great sites - Shinden Togashi, the Temple of Amaterasu, the Ki Rin Shrine and others of their rank - and a map of one of them is drawn as an explicit outlier, exempt from the 12 ft pitch.
  • Ranked gates, hundreds of feet apart, are not drawn.
    A ranked shrine's first and second gates can stand about 300 m (~984 ft) apart, which puts the pair off the map at every settlement scale.

Where the avenue begins and ends is set by the shrine's ground and its hall.

  • The first arch stands where the approach enters the shrine's ground.
    The first arch stands at the approach to a shrine's grounds (accurate), so on our maps it stands at the edge of the shrine's wood (Village shrines: where they stand, and how big their grounds are).
  • The innermost arch stands one pitch, 12 ft, off the hall's nearest face.
    An avenue can be perfectly spaced and still stand yards from the shrine it serves - three arches up a street beyond another building, or with a label and two rows of houses between it and its hall - and then it reads as marks near some other building, not as that shrine's approach. So the whole run is moved, keeping its direction, its curve and its spacing, until its innermost arch stands 12 ft off the nearest face of the hall, measured to the building's outline rather than its center, so that a run drawn off to one side slides onto that flank. No page gives the distance from a village shrine's innermost arch to its hall, so this is a map drawing convention.
  • A hall's label never covers an arch.
    A hall's label and its approach both want the ground at the hall's face. The label tries three places in a fixed order - the side the map's author asked for, that same side moved past the far end of the avenue, then the opposite side - and takes the first that touches no arch. Moving past the avenue is needed because a graveyard may sit on purpose behind a temple, so flipping the label off the approach would only bury it in the graves.

A compound shrine - a small shrine inside a household's or an office's grounds - has a smaller ground and its own rules.

  • How far before its hall does a compound shrine's arch stand?
    About 5 ft where the plan is tight, up to 12 ft - the distance of a village shrine's innermost arch from its hall - where there is room. The arch marks the edge of the shrine's ground, and a compound shrine's ground is small; no page gives a distance from any torii to its hall, so the figure is a GUESS.
  • Two shrines on one ground share one arch or have one each - a knob.
    Both are attested: a precinct's arch stands before everything inside it, subsidiary shrines included, and a subsidiary shrine may have an arch of its own. Each map rolls one from its seed.
  • A compound shrine may have no arch at all.
    A household shrine that is carefully kept "may even have a torii", so an arch is a mark of care, not a fixture, and a map may leave it out.
