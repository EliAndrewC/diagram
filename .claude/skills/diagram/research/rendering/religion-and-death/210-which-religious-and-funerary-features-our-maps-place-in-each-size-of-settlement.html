Which religious and funerary features our maps place in each size of settlement

Each size of settlement on our maps carries its own set of religious and funerary features, following the setting's ladder of clergy. A hamlet draws no shrine or temple, a village one shrine kept by its country monk, a town its monasteries and a city its temples, and the dead of each are buried and burned in grounds of its own or its village's.

The table below sets out, feature by feature, what each size draws and where the history behind it is. A cell that departs from that history says so, and one resting on no finding is labeled a GUESS.

  • A hamlet has no religious building of its own.
    It shares its village district's, just as it has no headman. Modern counts give many small communities a shrine, but they reflect a wealthier, even post-industrial society, so this project draws a hamlet without one; it keeps only the rare household shrine (Did a hamlet have a shrine of its own?).
  • A village's shrine is kept by its country monk.
    The monk is the one tax-free religious figure of the district, and their dwelling may double as the shrine itself.
  • A town's monasteries are each dedicated to a patron Fortune of the clan whose holdings include it.
    A preceptor of each Order serves in its town monastery.
  • A city keeps full temples.
    Its temple neighborhood is dotted with small wayside shrines, placed as the bullets below the table say.
  • An arch fronts any shrine, monastery or temple that a way runs straight up to.
    The rule holds at every size of settlement.
  • Monks perform the funerary rites.
    They do so in the countryside, and many of them in the cities, so a town and a village each have a cremation ground.
  • The country monk lives in the main village and serves its whole district.
    A district is the village and its hamlets, usually about half a dozen, and within it the village alone keeps the shrine, the headman's house and the cremation ground; its hamlets have none of them.

A city's small wayside shrines are placed as the history places them, at thresholds.

  • Three wayside shrines by a cluster of temples is a floor, not a pattern to repeat.
    More placed at meaningful spots elsewhere in the city read truer than one more shrine crowded by the halls.
  • A wayside shrine stands at a threshold.
    The deity of the way was enshrined at spatial and social boundaries - a village's entrance and exit, a mountain pass, a crossroads, the foot of a bridge - and a small shrine could stand by a well, as a dragon-god shrine does near a named well at Mikurahana. That gives the gate, the crossing, the bridge and the well.
  • A landing is a threshold too.
    It stands between water and land, so this project also places a shrine at the wharf, fittingly to the Fortune of fishermen and honest commerce. With the bridge, the gate and the public well, these are the places where the city's life turns.

The rule the map follows: each size draws what its column of the table names, sized and sited as the section each cell names specifies.

  • A hamlet draws its wayside stones and, on its knob, a burial ground of its own at its edge, and no shrine, arch or cremation ground.
  • A village draws one shrine with its arches, one burial ground, one cremation ground, its wayside stones and, on its knob, its wayside halls.
  • A town draws its monasteries - two by default, one for each patron Fortune of the clan that holds it - with one or two graveyards in their precincts and, if it is walled, a common graveyard outside the walls as well, a cremation ground at its edge and a street shrine for each block.
  • A city draws:
    • its temples - two major complexes by default, more only under a declared exception;
    • two to four graveyards, one in every temple precinct that has not opted out;
    • at least three small wayside shrines by a cluster of two or more temples (a lone precinct among houses needs none);
    • the clan mausoleum inside the walls;
    • and the cremation ground with the pauper ossuary outside them, a funerary complex beyond a gate.
