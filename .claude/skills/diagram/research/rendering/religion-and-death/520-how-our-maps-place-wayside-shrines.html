How our maps place wayside shrines

Our maps set wayside stones where the history puts them, at the places a road enters a settlement's houses, and give a town a small street shrine for each of its blocks. The halls some regions built beside their roads are drawn in some villages and not others.

  • A group of one to three stones stands beside the way where each road or lane enters a hamlet or a village.
    Most dosojin stand at a settlement's entrance, and at one entrance two stand in a row with other stone towers, so a map draws a group rather than a single stone.
  • A crossroads, the foot of a bridge or the village's well may take one more.
    The deity of the way was enshrined at crossroads and bridges' feet as well as at a village's entrance, and a small shrine could keep a well.
  • Each stone is drawn about 4 ft across, a map drawing convention.
    Real stones ran from palm-sized to nearly 2 m (~7 ft) tall.
  • Does a village keep wayside halls as well?
    Each village's seed decides - a knob, because the halls were common in one province and rare in others. The even odds are a GUESS.
  • A village that keeps wayside halls has one for every 20 to 30 of its households.
    Each is an open hall about 6 to 18 ft square, at a crossroads or beside the road between settlements, and never away from the houses and roads.
  • A town keeps a small street shrine, a jizo's or an Inari's, for each of its blocks.
    Towns in the Kinki region kept a jizo for each block, and Inari shrines were met everywhere in Edo. That the count holds outside the Kinki is a GUESS: from the Kanto eastward the dosojin is said to have done the street jizo's work.

A city's wayside shrines, at its gates, crossings, bridges and landing and beside its temples, are placed as How our maps place shrines, temples and graves in each size of settlement says.
