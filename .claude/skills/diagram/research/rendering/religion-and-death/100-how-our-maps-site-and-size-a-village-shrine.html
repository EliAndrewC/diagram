How our maps site and size a village shrine

A village on our maps has one shrine. It stands at the village's water mouth, inside its own wood, fronted by an arch facing the village, and it is drawn small: a modest hall, about 60 by 48 ft, on a precinct inside the band the registers give, about 150 to 650 tsubo (the history).

  • The village's shrine stands at its water mouth.
    On our maps the village's entrance is its shuikou: the point where the water leaves and the track sets off for the road, and where the water-mouth grove already grows (the village's groves). Temples at a Chinese village's water mouth or entrance are attested, but that the earth god's shrine took that site as a rule is this project's reading, a GUESS. The Japanese form of the same idea is the guardian shrine in its wood, within the village or at its edge.
  • The hall and its arch carry no label.
    A vermilion hall with an arch in front of it reads as a shrine on sight, as the rows of markers beside it read as a graveyard, so neither is labeled - a map drawing convention. A village site is never called a temple; where the word is used of one, it means a shrine.
  • How big is the drawn hall?
    About 60 by 48 ft, some 275 m² (~2,960 sq ft), two farmhouses' worth under one roof. It is the country shrine in its one-roof form, the villagers' hall and the monk's home in one building. The one such building attested, the hall and priest's quarters of Kaie-ji, a Zen temple in the city of Sakai, measures 25.2 m (~83 ft) by 8.0 to 13.0 m (~26-43 ft), some 200 to 330 m² (~2,153-3,552 sq ft), a form its own heritage listing calls unusual. The drawn hall lies inside that band (accurate; the country shrine's halls, the monk's dwelling).
  • How large may a showcase hall be?
    A showcase primary hall runs to about 490 m² (~5,274 sq ft), and 600 m² (~6,458 sq ft) is the ceiling, because the monastery and temple tier of 1,000 m² (~10,764 sq ft) and up belongs to towns and cities. These three figures are this project's own calibration: no source read gives a Japanese village shrine's hall a ceiling.
  • A village shrine's own halls, without a dwelling, would be far smaller.
    Hie Shrine in Kurihara's worship hall comes to about 18 by 12 ft (a village shrine's halls); the drawn hall is larger because it holds the monk's home.
  • The hall stands beside a lane, never on one.
    A building sits off the way. The arch is the deliberate exception: a gateway straddles the approach, and the way runs under it.
  • A shrine's water is drawn beside the lane.
    Its stone basin stands beside the approach (what else stood in the precinct), never on the hall and never under an arch.
  • The hall and its arches stand in a cleared opening in the wood.
    They are drawn clear of the canopy, not under it - a map drawing convention. The bare ground is a small clearing at the hall and the sanctuary, not a yard.
  • The precinct is drawn at about 150 to 650 tsubo.
    The band is the registers' figures as found, and they can err either way against an Edo precinct; Edo sites and exempt lands span the same band, so it is not a modern-only figure (accurate as a range).
  • A wood stands in the precinct, covering some or nearly all of it.
    The rest is open ground. That the unbuilt ground was mostly wood is a GUESS; where the wood stands and its outline are at the shape of a shrine's wood, and why no fence bounds it at a shrine's enclosure.
  • Hoshigaoka's shrine precinct is about 860 tsubo.
    Measured from its well to its outermost arch, it lies above the typical band and above the largest Saitama village shrine, inside Tochigi's range of 142 to over 2,700 tsubo, above every Edo site figure found and inside the Edo exempt-land figures, which may include fields. The range is accurate; the precinct's place in it is a GUESS.
  • A hamlet draws no shrine of its own.
    The record shows both a part of a village with a shrine of its own and one with none, which would ordinarily make it a knob. But the counts that show small communities with their own shrines are modern, and reflect a wealthier, even post-industrial society, so this project draws a hamlet without one; it keeps only the household shrine that some of its homesteads have, which is rare. There is no knob (what each size of settlement draws).
