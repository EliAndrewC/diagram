How our maps draw shrine woods and fences (chinju no mori, tamagaki)

A village shrine on our maps stands in its own wood, as a village shrine did, with no fence, wall or hedge round its precinct and none round its sanctuary. Its first gateway (torii), where the approach path enters (How our maps draw shrine gateways and the approach to the hall (torii, sando)), and its wood mark its ground; how large the wood is drawn is at How our maps site and size a village shrine. Which sides of the hall the wood takes is picked at random for each settlement from the forms the ground allows.

  • Our maps leave a village shrine's precinct open, with no fence, wall or hedge round it.
    No dated fence round a whole village precinct was found, and the fences found at ordinary shrines ring the sanctuary alone or line the approach, so the precinct is drawn open (accurate).
  • No fence is drawn round the sanctuary, however rich the parish.
    Before 1868 such a fence is known only where the shogunate or a lord built the shrine, and at an ordinary shrine only from 1918 and 1928; none round a village shrine's sanctuary dated before 1868 was found, so the maps do not draw one there (accurate). What else a richer parish gave its shrine is at How our maps draw a village shrine's precinct: halls, basin, sacred tree and offerings (keidai).
  • Which sides of the hall the wood takes is a knob of four forms.
    The forms are behind, behind and at the sides, at the sides, and all around. They are accurate as forms: a modern survey of shrine woods on the slopes of one city found each tied to where the hall sits, and behind and at the sides is also a naturalist's general description, while behind only and at the sides only rest on that one modern city survey alone.
  • The slope a hall stands on decides which forms are possible.
    A hall at the foot of its slope takes behind, or behind and at the sides. A hall at the top of its slope, with the approach climbing to it, takes behind and at the sides, or at the sides. A hall on a rise, or on flat paddy ground, is wooded all around.
  • A hall midway on an even slope may take any of the slope's three forms.
    With no break of slope at it, it is in neither of the survey's classes, so it takes the forms of both - behind, behind and at the sides, or at the sides. This is a GUESS.
  • Where two or three forms are possible, each settlement's is picked at random, each equally likely.
    The only counts of the forms are the city survey's percentages, which do not come out as whole counts of its 27 shrines, so treating the forms as equally likely is a GUESS.
  • Where the wood meets scrub or slope, its edge is drawn ragged.
    It follows the outline of the trees' crowns, as the edge of a surviving patch of old forest would, never a ruled line. Ruled edges belong to what is surveyed or built, as at How our maps draw the swept ground around shrines and graves. The ragged edge is a GUESS.
  • Where a wood in the paddy plain meets its fields, its foot is a second knob.
    Its edge along the fields is either straight, following the field boundary, as a modern photograph shows, or ragged along its crowns, as a surviving patch of old forest would be. Nothing shows either to be the rule, so each settlement's is picked at random, each equally likely, a GUESS.
  • How far a flank reaches along the approach, and how many trees stand in it, are calibrated liberty.
    No source read gives how far a shrine wood's flank reaches or how many trees stand in it, nor splits a precinct into buildings, wood and open ground, so both are set within what the wood's form allows: the wood stands on part of the precinct, and the rest is open ground.
  • Single trees may stand along the approach outside the wood.
    A naturalist describes single trees, often held especially sacred, planted in front of a shrine's buildings or along its approach; the shrine's sacred tree on our maps is one of them, at How our maps draw a village shrine's precinct: halls, basin, sacred tree and offerings (keidai).
  • On our map of Hoshigaoka, the random pick put the wood behind the hall and at its sides.
    The hall there stands midway on the back-slope - the ground rises behind it toward the village and falls before it down the approach, with no break of slope at it - so its possible forms are behind, behind and at the sides, or at the sides. The wood stands behind the hall and down both its flanks to ragged tips beside the forecourt. The wood meets no paddy there: its ground is the scrub of the back-slope.
