How our maps place a city's temples and its temple quarter (teramachi)

Our maps give a city two major temple complexes by default, though a real city kept many temples, and draw more only where a city is declared an exception. The default is a choice for play: a few named temples a player can visit. Where a map draws several temples together, they follow the forms the history shows: a temple quarter at the town's edge, its precincts along a street in one of two attested forms, with small shrines among them. A clan capital differs: its two great temples flank its central axis of government buildings, and its modest ones stand at its edge.

  • A city draws two major temple complexes by default.
    That is a choice for play, not a claim about history: a few named temples a player can visit read better than a dozen anonymous ones. How far their clergy outnumber a real temple's is at the setting's liberty.
  • A city draws more than two temples only under a declared exception.
    Minami, the Fox seat of 2,600 inhabitants, is one: it draws eight modest precincts. That moves it toward the history rather than away from it, though eight sits just below the smallest temple quarter found, Takayama's of over ten temples and shrines.
  • A temple quarter meets its street in one of two forms, a knob.
    Both are attested at Kanazawa, so the form is rolled for each quarter from the map's seed. In the walled row, the precincts stand side by side along a wide straight street, each with its wall and gate on the street. In the gate-land row, town houses on narrow deep lots line the street, and each temple stands behind them, reached by an approach from a gate set back between them.
  • How many precincts stand to a block?
    Two or three. An ordinary precinct is about 35,000 sq ft (What does a temple precinct hold?), and the Edo-period blocks surviving in Tokyo's Yanaka and Negishi-Shitaya average about 79,000 to 97,000 sq ft. The count is this project's arithmetic from those sizes.

The rule the map follows: a temple quarter stands at the town's edge; its precincts are ordinary precincts of about 35,000 sq ft, two or three to a block, sorted by sect, their main halls facing the street and their graveyards at the back; the quarter also carries town houses, on the street itself in the gate-land row.

A clan capital places its temples by its own rules, since it keeps both a pair of great temples and a larger number of modest ones.

  • A capital's two great temples flank its government axis.
    The two sovereign temples, Benten's and Jurojin's, are the capital's great complexes, institutions of the domain set at equal distances either side of the capital's central axis of government buildings.
  • A capital has several temple neighborhoods and no single temple quarter.
    Each great temple grows its own gate-front neighborhood (monzen) with the capital's streets, so there is one temple neighborhood to each great temple.
  • A capital's modest temples stand at its edge, just inside the wall.
    The edge is where many castle towns set their temple quarters. Defense is the reason usually given, but it is a reading rather than a settled fact.
  • Why does a capital put its modest temples at its edge, when a provincial city with only its two default temples keeps them among its streets?
    A capital has enough temples to spend them on its edge. A provincial city's few serve the life of its streets, so they stand in a lane among them, as Nagahara's temple neighborhood does. That contrast is this project's reading.
  • A capital's modest temples stand in one of three forms, a knob.
    One group against one stretch of wall, a line along one side, or several smaller groups along it, rolled from the map's seed: these are the forms the castle towns show, and none rings a whole town's edge, so a line of temples along the whole inner face of the wall would be more than the history shows.
  • Each modest temple of a capital reads as the patron temple of the noble house whose estate stands beside it.
    The Temple of Hotei beside the Tokiwa Estate is Tokiwa's temple, kept by that lineage out of a family tie to the Fortune or its monks, and the others pair likewise where the map already sets them side by side: Bishamon's temple near Hazama, Daikoku's near Anzu. That follows the Edo period, when a great house kept its own family temple (bodaiji), as the Tokugawa kept Kan'eiji and Zojoji. That the lineages keep theirs in the capital's rim is the setting's; that real retainers kept theirs in a castle town's temple quarter no page read confirms.

Among clustered temples, a city also draws small shrines.

  • Where two or more temples cluster within about 1,200 ft, the map sets at least three small wayside shrines among them.
    They stand clear of the temples, the wall and the streets, and are placed before the houses are packed, so the rows of houses flow around them. Small shrines several to a neighborhood are attested, but not in the ground between a quarter's temples: placing them there is a GUESS. More belong at a city's thresholds, and every small shrine is drawn in one glyph (How our maps place shrines, temples and graves in each size of settlement).
  • A lone temple among houses gathers no small shrines.
    A single temple is not a temple neighborhood: the city's warrior-fortune temple in the middle of the samurai quarter is drawn as a building, not a precinct.
