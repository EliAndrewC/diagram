How our maps draw cremation grounds and bone mounds

This setting cremates its dead and inters their bones, so every settlement from a village up burns its dead somewhere on the map. A village draws a cremation ground of its own, beside its burial ground or at its edge; a town draws one at its edge; a city draws one outside its walls, with the pauper bone mound beside it. Each is an open, cleared ground around a fire bed, 30 to 80 ft across in a village or town and up to about 180 ft square in a city, drawn at its real size.

  • Monks perform the funeral rites, with burakumin assisting them.
    In this setting the burakumin are the morticians, and the monks stand outside the caste order, so handling the dead does not pollute them. Monks perform the rites in the countryside, and many of them in the cities.
  • A village and a town each have a cremation ground; a hamlet has none.
    Cremating settlements in Japan commonly burned their own dead at their own ground (Cremation grounds and bone mounds (kasoba)), so each village and town draws one. A hamlet's dead are burned at its village's. The village is the seat of its district: its country monk lives there and serves its hamlets, usually about half a dozen, and within the district the village alone keeps the shrine, the headman's house and the cremation ground.

A village's cremation ground has two seats, both drawn.

  • Where does a village's cremation ground stand?
    It is a knob: each village's seed chooses beside the village's burial ground, wherever that lies, or on its own at the village's edge. The ground beside the burial ground is attested. The seat on its own is a GUESS, borrowed from the sanmai where a Wakasa hamlet buried its dead, at the hamlet's edge; no page we read puts a cremation ground there. The odds between the two are a GUESS: even.
  • A cremation ground on its own stands downstream of the houses, just beyond the last of them.
    It keeps 120 ft clear of houses and wells and a strip of bank, about 6 ft, from the water, and stands as near the houses as that allows. No distance from the houses is drawn beyond that, since none from before modern times was found.
  • Either way, its fire bed stays off the approach to the shrine.
    Nobody should pass the pyre on the way to worship - this project's reasoning.
  • The six stone jizo stand at the burial ground's entrance, not at a cremation ground on its own.
    How they are drawn is at How our maps draw village burial grounds (bochi).
  • A village draws no bone mound.
    No village ossuary from before modern times was found (Cremation grounds and bone mounds (kasoba)).

A town's and a city's cremation ground stands apart from the town.

  • A town draws its cremation ground at its edge, clear of the dwellings; a city draws it outside the walls, beyond a gate.
    That smoke, fire risk and pollution put it there is this project's reading; the margin itself is attested, for the dead were burned in open country away from where people lived.
  • How far from the dwellings does a city's cremation ground stand?
    About 390 ft, a GUESS. The only distance on record, the 1884 standard's 120 ken (~709 ft), is modern.
  • The cremation ground is kept off the trunk road.
    It is reached by a minor funeral path, not by the high street. That is this project's reasoning from the same smoke and pollution; no page we read describes such a path. Minor town streets do not trigger the rule, only the trunk road.
  • A cremation ground never stands between a temple and the road.
    Nobody should walk past the pyre to reach worship, so it stands behind or beside a hall, never on its approach - this project's reasoning. Where a monastery or temple stands within about 1,200 ft of it, the ground stands at least as far from that road as the hall does, less about 120 ft for "beside it, a little away".
  • A temple's own graveyard may face the road.
    It stands beside the temple, wherever the temple is; only the cremation ground is kept off the road and the approach. This is this project's reading, on no page we read.
  • The cremation ground adjoins a burial ground outside the walls.
    The permanent fire bed lay beside a burial ground; that the body is burned and its bones interred next door, making the two one funerary complex, is this project's reading. The burial ground stands within about 210 ft of it, edge to edge. A graveyard inside the walls does not count as its burial ground.
  • No fire clearance is drawn around a pyre.
    No rule before modern times sets one, and the recorded removals of cremation grounds were for their smoke and stench, not fire (Cremation grounds and bone mounds (kasoba)). The only figure for a fire of this size is a present-day code's 50 ft for a bonfire, and the maps take no distance from it. A pyre stands in its yard with no clearance ring, and at a moated city a wet moat ends the fire risk at its outer face. How far outside the wall the funerary ground sits is at How far outside the wall does the funerary ground sit?; its least distance past the moat is a map drawing convention, so that the ground reads as outside, not a fire setback.

The fire bed and its ground are drawn from the Edo forms.

  • The fire bed is about a coffin's length and a little wider, and most grounds leave it open.
    Nearly every ground was open to the sky until the end of the Edo period, so a roofed bed is the minority on the maps, the share a GUESS. A permanent bed may be roofed on four posts a few feet out from the bed, about 10 to 12 ft square, or, in snow country, set in a walled hut of about four to six tatami, roughly 8 to 10 ft square. No raised stone pyre platform and no hut for the officiants are drawn: neither is attested.
  • How large is a village's or a town's cremation ground drawn?
    Its cleared ground around the fire bed is 30 to 80 ft across, a GUESS with no premodern figure behind it. The recorded sizes of village grounds are all from 1910 and later, so the maps do not calibrate to them.
  • A city's cremation ground is sized to Edo's at Yoyogi, up to about 180 ft square.
    Yoyogi's 900 tsubo is the one extent on record from before modern times.

A city also draws the pauper bone mound and the ruling clan's mausoleum.

  • The pauper ossuary is a low earthen mound with one weathered stupa, outside the walls by the cremation ground.
    The mound form is attested, at a Shinagawa temple. Its place by the cremation ground is this project's own, a GUESS. It is a city feature, so a town's common burial ground takes the same unclaimed dead itself.
  • How large is a bone mound drawn?
    About 22 ft across, and anything outside 8 to 32 ft is wrong. The band is a GUESS: no page we read gives a pauper bone mound a size. The mound keeps a small floor so it never vanishes at a map's scale, a map drawing convention.
  • The clan mausoleum stands inside the walls, by the samurai quarter.
    It is drawn as a precinct with its own halls and gates, as a prestige monument near the seat of power. Its place is this project's own: Sendai's Zuihoden, the one attested instance, stands outside the castle, facing it across the river. Only a city draws one.
  • Where a walled compound's wall meets a neighborhood fence, the fence is drawn over it.
    A mausoleum, a manor or a merchant estate yields that side: the fence is that side of the precinct, with the compound's own wall beneath it. It is a map drawing convention that keeps two clashing parallel walls off the map.
