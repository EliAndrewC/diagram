How our maps draw a town's and a city's shrines

Our maps draw no district shrine of a town's own and no principal shrine for a city, though a Japanese county town kept a district shrine and every Chinese seat of government a City God temple. The game's setting gives a town its monasteries and a city its temples and wayside shrines, and names neither a town's shrine nor a city's principal shrine, and it has no City God (How our maps place shrines, temples and graves in each size of settlement). So none is drawn: a deviation from the history, the setting's own.

Were the setting to add one, the history would draw it as below.

  • A town's district shrine would stand on 500 tsubo (~17,800 sq ft) or more.
    That is the floor the rules of 1897 set for a district shrine. Its halls would be a village shrine's size, with a gate and lesser shrines, as at Sano.
  • A city's principal shrine would stand on raised ground near its seat of government.
    Edo's kept to slightly raised ground each time it was moved outward as the castle grew.

The small shrines drawn among a city's temples, or in a town's temple quarter, follow one rule.

  • How much ground does a small shrine in a town stand on?
    About 3,500 sq ft. The plot is a GUESS from one district's register, most of whose shrines are a hamlet's rather than a town's, and whose areas are today's.
  • A small shrine is a sanctuary some 6 ft square behind a worship hall some 18 by 12 ft.
    Those are the halls of the Kanzaki shrines.
  • At most a shrine office and a lesser shrine stand beside its hall.
    As at the Suwa shrine in Kanazawa's temple quarter.
  • Or a small shrine stands on a slice of a temple's precinct.
    As another shrine of that quarter did.
