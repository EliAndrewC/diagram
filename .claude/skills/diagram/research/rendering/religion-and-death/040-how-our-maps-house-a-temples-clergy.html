How our maps house a temple's clergy

On a city or capital map a temple complex is drawn as a walled compound with an empty court. Its celibate monks live inside, implied rather than drawn, and the families of its married clergy live in ordinary houses around it, as hereditary clergy historically lived round the temple or shrine they served. The setting's temples keep both kinds of clergy: most keep a few householders among many celibate monks, while the Fox clan's temples keep a small celibate council and hereditary families for everything else.

  • A city temple is drawn as a walled compound with an empty court.
    On a city or capital map, at 3 ft to the pixel, a major temple is its wall, its gate and a blank court, drawn like the governor's yamen: the inside of the compound is the subject of its own building plan, drawn when the players visit it. How big the compound and its halls are is at How our maps size and draw a city's temples (garan). The blank court is a map drawing convention.
  • Who lives inside a city temple's walls?
    Celibate monks, resident initiates and a few lay servants, some 25 to 45 of them in a provincial city's complex, in halls implied inside the wall, as the monks of a Japanese Zen temple lived in common in its monks' hall. The count is this project's own arithmetic, a GUESS. They are counted on top of the dwellings the map draws rather than housed in its streets, and they stand outside the lay caste bands, because the setting's clergy are not a lay caste.
  • A temple's married monks keep homes in its neighborhood.
    In the setting an adept may marry and raise children, and its campaign notes put married monks' families in or next to the temple complex. So a share of each complex's 15 to 30 monks are householders, whose families keep ordinary homes near the temple. Married clergy lived so historically, round the temple or shrine they served: Temple clergy, their families, and how a temple earned its keep.
  • How many clergy homes stand round a temple?
    Two or three round each major complex, a GUESS: no source we read counts the clergy homes round one temple.
  • Clergy homes are drawn exactly like a laborer's house, a deliberate deviation.
    They carry no label and no glyph of their own, so the eye cannot pick the temple families out of the town; only the map's manifest knows which houses are theirs. A real priest family's house could be picked out: at Kamigamo in Kyoto the shrine priest families' houses were one-story, gabled and tiled, where the town houses beside them were raised for an upper room, and each stood inside an earthen wall with a gate at its entrance.
  • How many initiates does a temple keep, and where do they live?
    About twice its monk count ("many are called, few are chosen", as the setting's scripture says of the monastic life), of whom only a few sleep inside: roughly 5 to 10 at a provincial city, 10 to 20 at a domain capital. The rest are laborers, carters, servants and shopkeepers who study while they work ordinary jobs, and on the map they are ordinary houses of their own caste, never buildings of their own.

The Fox clan's temples keep a different rule of ordination, and it changes both where their clergy live and where the temples stand.

  • In a Fox temple only its council of three is celibate, so its clergy are householders.
    The Three Bonds who run a Fox temple are its only monks bound to celibacy; every other position is hereditary, and "the temple's revenue sources are effectively family businesses passed down along bloodlines". That is close to the historical hereditary priesthoods, with a small celibate core that runs the house.
  • A Fox precinct is ringed by five to nine clergy homes.
    So a Fox precinct is a small walled workplace ringed by its temple families, and a Fox city's clergy housing grows with its number of precincts.
  • The Fox temples' trades are the trades of historical monasteries.
    In the setting's campaign notes the Seven Temples hold the use of the forest, and lend money with Fukurokujin and Ebisu, the setting's Fortunes of wisdom and mercy and of honest work, and make wedding loans with Benten, the Fortune of romantic love. Historical Buddhist monasteries were moneylenders and pawnbrokers, down to which institution lends: Temple clergy, their families, and how a temple earned its keep.
  • A Fox city's precincts stand where their trades are, not gathered into a temple quarter.
    Each precinct is an economic house, so the precincts spread across the city's quarters. The Japanese temple quarter along a town's rim was considered and not taken: it was a castle town's defensive arrangement - at Hirosaki the temples were gathered for the defense of the castle town and the control of its people - and the Fox temples are commercial houses first. How many precincts a Fox city draws is at How our maps place a city's temples and its temple quarter (teramachi).
  • A Fox precinct that stands alone needs no shrines beside it.
    The rule that a temple neighborhood carries shrines applies to each cluster of temples. A precinct sited by its trade is sometimes the only temple in its quarter, and it is exempt, as Tango's converted-estate Bishamon temple already is.

The rule the map follows: on a city or capital map (3 ft per pixel) a major temple is a walled compound of wall, gate and empty court. Two or three clergy homes stand within about 500 ft of each major complex, and none stands stranded away from every temple; a precinct whose clergy are hereditary householders carries five to nine instead, ringing its wall. Beyond those, at most two exception features stand apart - a retired abbot's hermitage, or a small daughter chapel with two to four monks of its own - drawn precisely because they are exceptions.
