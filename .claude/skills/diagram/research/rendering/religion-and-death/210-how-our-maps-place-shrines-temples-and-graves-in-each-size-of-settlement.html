How our maps place shrines, temples and graves in each size of settlement

Each size of settlement on our maps carries its own set of religious and funerary features, following the setting's ladder of clergy. A hamlet draws no shrine or temple, a village one shrine kept by its country monk, a town its monasteries and a city its temples, and the dead of each are buried and burned in grounds of its own or its village's.

  • The country monk lives in the main village and serves its whole district.
    A district is a village and its hamlets, usually about half a dozen. Within it the village alone keeps the shrine, the headman's house and the cremation ground.
  • Our maps give a hamlet no shrine or temple of its own.
    It shares its village district's, just as it has no headman. Modern counts give many small communities a shrine, but they reflect a wealthier, even post-industrial society, so this project draws a hamlet without one. It keeps only the odd household shrine (a hamlet's shrine).
  • A village's shrine is kept by its country monk.
    The monk is the one tax-free religious figure of the district, and their dwelling may double as the shrine itself.
  • A town's monasteries are each dedicated to a patron Fortune of the clan whose holdings include it.
    The setting puts a preceptor of each Order in the town monastery of its Fortune, so our maps draw one monastery for each patron Fortune, two by default.
  • Our maps give a city two major temple complexes, where a real walled city had many modest temples.
    The choice is the setting's own liberty (How our maps size and draw a city's temples (garan)); the historical count is at how many temples a city keeps.
  • Monks perform the funerary rites, so a village and a town each keep a cremation ground.
    Country monks perform them in every district, and many monks do in the cities.

Two rules set how a shrine, monastery or temple meets its streets, at every size of settlement that draws one.

  • A shrine, monastery or temple within reach of a road puts its approach between itself and the road.
    An approach runs from the busy road to the hall, so a hall within reach of a road or city street stands its avenue of gateway arches (torii) between itself and the road, judged by which side of the hall its arches lie on against the nearest major road. A hall with no road in reach faces where it will.
  • A street or path that runs straight up to a shrine, monastery or temple ends in an arch before it.
    The arch stands on that street just in front of the hall. How many arches the approach carries is set for each hall on its own, not by the length of the street, and the arches are drawn above the street so it passes beneath them. A hall set back from the grid, with no street running up to it, needs none.

Beyond the minimum by its temples, a city's small wayside shrines go where the history puts them.

  • Shrines to the roadside guardian deity (dosojin) stood at boundaries.
    They stood at a village's entrance and exit, a mountain pass, a crossroads, the foot of a bridge, so our maps put a city's wayside shrines at its gates, crossings and bridges.
  • Did small shrines stand by wells?
    At least one did: a dragon-god shrine stands near a named well at Mikurahana, so our maps put one at a city's public well too.
  • This project also places a wayside shrine at a city's landing, a threshold between water and land.
    It is dedicated, fittingly, to the Fortune of fishermen and honest commerce.
  • A city needs at least three wayside shrines by its temples, and more belong at thresholds elsewhere.
    Three by a cluster of temples is a floor, not a pattern to repeat: more placed at the gates, crossings, bridges, wells and landing read truer than one more shrine crowded by the halls.
  • Every small shrine is drawn as a vermilion-roofed shed with its own little gate, by convention.
    One glyph stands for the whole class of small shrines, so a reader can tell one from a temple at a glance. Vermilion is the mark of Inari's shrine buildings and arches, and a small shrine (hokora) is a simplified one; some of the Akiba cult's small shrines were of stone instead, because stone does not burn easily, and no page we read gives a small shrine a vermilion roof and a gate of its own. On the map nobody lives in one, and none has an approach of its own.

The rule the map follows: each size draws what its column of the table names, sized and sited as the section each cell names specifies.

  • A hamlet draws its wayside stones and, as its seed chooses, a burial ground of its own at its edge on its knob or none, and no shrine, arch or cremation ground.
  • A village draws one shrine with its arches, one burial ground, one cremation ground, its wayside stones and, on its knob, its wayside halls.
  • A town draws:
    • its monasteries - two by default, one for each patron Fortune of the clan that holds it;
    • one or two graveyards in their precincts and, if it is walled, a common graveyard outside the walls as well;
    • a cremation ground at its edge;
    • and a street shrine for each block.
  • A city draws:
    • its temples - two major complexes by default, more only under a declared exception;
    • two to four graveyards, one in every temple precinct that has not opted out;
    • at least three small wayside shrines by a cluster of two or more temples (a lone precinct among houses needs none);
    • the clan mausoleum inside the walls;
    • and the cremation ground with the pauper ossuary outside them, a funerary complex beyond a gate.
