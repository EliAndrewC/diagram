How our maps draw the swept ground around shrines and graves

Our maps claim no swept ground. Before 1868 the record shows the grave itself weeded, and no page we read attests a cleared band around a graveyard or a swept ground around a shrine's hall or its arches; what is attested of those is custom recorded in modern times, so the maps leave it out. The scrub of the commons and the reeds of a marsh are kept off only the ground a shrine or a grave plot itself occupies, and a shrine is marked by its grove, its approach and its arch.

  • A shrine's scrub stops at its precinct, not at a swept collar.
    The scrub keeps off the precinct's own ground, its buildings and its approach, and the shrine's grove, its buildings and every placement rule are unchanged. How far the precinct's open ground reaches past the hall is a GUESS.
  • An arch clears only its own footing.
    No swept ground is drawn under or around a shrine's arches.
  • A grave plot keeps the scrub off its own ground, and no wider.
    The graves themselves are clear and the scrub runs up to the plot's edge; no cleared band is drawn around it. The grove, the buildings and every placement rule ignore the plot's cleared ground.

Where the cleared ground runs past what a feature occupies - around a shrine's hall - its edge is drawn ragged.

  • Why is the cleared ground's edge ragged?
    Open ground around a shrine is made by use - feet, the traffic of the approach, whatever tending it got - spreading outward from the feature, and its edge falls wherever that use peters out into the scrub; nobody surveys it. That is this project's reasoning, since no page we read says how far round a shrine or a grave the clearing reached or where it stopped. Ruled edges belong to what is surveyed or built - a wall, a paddy bund, a house plot - which is the same reasoning that gives the perimeter dike and the common burial ground their shapes.
  • Tending came in bouts at set times of year, in the custom recorded in modern Japan.
    The graves, and in one modern popular account the paths to them, were cleaned on the day that opens Bon, and a village shrine was cleaned by representatives its own households chose, sometimes in rotation. In this setting families also clean their graves at the spring and autumn equinox festivals. How far out each bout reached is in no source read.
  • The edge is notched inward, never lobed outward.
    The irregular edge is cut into the rectangle the ground would otherwise have: shallow notches, deeper along the sides than at the corners. Outward lobes were tried and rejected, for two reasons. Cleared ground is a claim of upkeep, so drawing it irregular should mean the upkeep falls short of the ideal, never that it annexes ground it does not reach. And a lobe can newly cover scrub or marsh that is already there, as a graveyard's lobe reached a marsh at a slope's foot. Notches only make every patch a part of the rectangle, so nothing else on the map moves.
  • The same feature always draws the same patch.
    The ragged patch is a drawing decision only: the ground a feature reserves, which placement and clearance rules test against, stays the plain rectangle.
