How our maps draw a shrine's wood and what bounds its precinct

A village shrine on our maps stands in its own wood, with no fence, wall or hedge round its precinct and none round its sanctuary. Its first arch, where the approach enters, and its wood mark its ground. Which sides of the hall the wood takes is rolled for each settlement from the forms the ground allows.

  • No fence, wall or hedge surrounds a village shrine's precinct.
    No dated fence round a whole village precinct was found, and the fences found at ordinary shrines ring the sanctuary alone or line the approach (Shrine woods and fences), so the precinct is drawn open (accurate). The first arch stands where the approach enters it (How our maps draw shrine gateways and the approach to the hall (torii, sando)), and the wood stands on it; how large it is drawn is at How our maps site and size a village shrine.
  • No fence is drawn round the sanctuary, however rich the parish.
    Before 1868 such a fence is known only where the shogunate or a lord built the shrine, and at a village shrine only in modern times, so the maps do not draw one there (accurate). What else a richer parish gave its shrine is at How our maps draw a village shrine's precinct: halls, basin, sacred tree and offerings (keidai).
  • Which sides of the hall the wood takes is a knob of four forms.
    The forms are behind, behind and at the sides, at the sides, and all around. They are accurate as forms: a survey of shrine woods on slopes found each tied to where the hall sits, and behind and at the sides is also a naturalist's general description, while behind only and at the sides only rest on that one modern city survey alone.
  • The ground names the candidate forms.
    A hall at the foot of its slope takes behind, or behind and at the sides. A hall at the top of its slope, with the approach climbing to it, takes behind and at the sides, or at the sides. A hall on a rise, or on flat paddy ground, is wooded all around.
  • A hall midway on an even slope may take any of the slope's three forms.
    With no break of slope at it, it is in neither of the survey's classes, so it takes the forms of both - behind, behind and at the sides, or at the sides. This is a GUESS.
  • Between two or three candidates, a settlement rolls from its seed with equal weights.
    The only counts are the city survey's, and they are of no stated base, so the equal weights are a GUESS.
  • Where the wood meets scrub or slope, its edge is drawn ragged.
    It is the edge of its own crowns, never a ruled line, as a remnant of old forest's would be. Ruled edges belong to what is surveyed or built, as at Why does that swept ground have a ragged edge?. The ragged edge is a GUESS.
  • Where a wood in the paddy plain meets its fields, its foot is a second knob.
    The foot is either the fields' straight line, as a modern photograph shows, or its crowns' ragged edge, as a remnant stand's. Nothing shows either to be the rule, so a settlement rolls it with equal weights, a GUESS.
  • How far a flank reaches along the approach, and how many trees stand in it, are calibrated liberty.
    The wood stands on part of the precinct, and the rest is open ground. Single trees may stand along the approach, as the sacred tree does.
  • At Hoshigaoka the roll gave behind and at the sides.
    The hall there stands midway on the back-slope - the ground rises behind it toward the village and falls before it down the approach, with no break of slope at it - so its candidates are behind, behind and at the sides, or at the sides. The wood stands behind the hall, where the well is at its back edge, and down both its flanks to ragged tips beside the forecourt. The lower approach runs up through open ground, and the sacred tree stands alone beside it. The wood meets no paddy there: its ground is the scrub of the back-slope.
