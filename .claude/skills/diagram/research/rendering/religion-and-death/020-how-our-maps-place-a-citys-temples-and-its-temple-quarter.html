How our maps place a city's temples and its temple quarter

Our maps give a city two major temple complexes by default, though a real city kept many modest temples, and draw more only where a city is declared an exception. Where a map draws several temples together, they follow the forms the history shows: a temple quarter at the town's edge, its precincts along a street in one of two attested forms, with small shrines among them.

  • A city draws two major temple complexes by default.
    That is a choice for play, not a claim about history: a few named temples a player can visit read better than a dozen anonymous ones. How far their clergy outnumber a real temple's is at the setting's liberty.
  • A city draws more than two temples only under a declared exception.
    Minami, the Fox seat of 2,600 inhabitants, is one: it draws eight modest precincts. That moves it toward the history rather than away from it, though eight sits just below the smallest temple quarter found, Takayama's of over ten temples and shrines (How many temples a city keeps).
  • A temple quarter meets its street in one of two forms, a knob.
    Both are attested at Kanazawa, so the form is rolled for each quarter from the map's seed. In the walled row, the precincts stand side by side along a wide straight street, each with its wall and gate on the street. In the gate-land row, town houses on narrow deep lots line the street, and each temple stands behind them, reached by an approach from a gate set back between them.
  • How many precincts stand to a block?
    Two or three. An ordinary precinct is about 35,000 sq ft (What does a temple precinct hold?), and the Edo-period blocks surviving in Tokyo's Yanaka and Negishi-Shitaya average about 79,000 to 97,000 sq ft. The count is this project's arithmetic from those sizes.

The rule the map follows: the precincts of a temple quarter are ordinary precincts of about 35,000 sq ft, two or three to a block, sorted by sect, their main halls facing the street and their graveyards at the back; the quarter also carries town houses, on the street itself in the gate-land row.

A clan capital places its temples by its own rules, since it keeps both a pair of great temples and a larger number of modest ones.

  • A capital's two great temples stand on its government axis.
    The two sovereign temples, Benten's and Jurojin's, are the capital's great complexes, institutions of the domain flanking the government axis at equal offsets. Each grows its own gate-front neighborhood (monzen) with the capital's streets, so a capital has several temple neighborhoods, one to each great temple, and no single temple quarter.
  • A capital's modest temples stand at its edge, just inside the wall.
    The edge is where the castle towns set their temple quarters, and the reason the map gives for it, defense, is the usual reading rather than a settled fact (How many temples a city keeps). A capital has enough temples to spend them on its edge, where a provincial city gathers its few into a lane among its streets, as Nagahara's temple neighborhood does, because they serve the street's life; that contrast is this project's reading.
  • A capital's modest temples stand in one of three forms, a knob.
    One group against one stretch of wall, a line along one side, or several smaller groups along it, rolled from the map's seed: these are the forms the castle towns show, and none rings a whole town's edge, so a belt along the whole inner face of the wall would be more than the history shows.
  • Each modest temple of a capital reads as the patron temple of the house beside it.
    The Temple of Hotei beside the Tokiwa Estate is Tokiwa's temple, kept by that lineage out of a family tie to the Fortune or its monks, and the others pair likewise where the map already sets them side by side: Bishamon's temple near Hazama, Daikoku's near Anzu. That follows the Edo period, when a great house kept its own family temple (bodaiji), as the Tokugawa kept Kan'eiji and Zojoji. That the lineages keep theirs in the capital's rim is the setting's; that real retainers kept theirs in a castle town's temple quarter no page read confirms (How many temples a city keeps).

Two rules set how a city's temples meet its streets, at every size of settlement that draws them.

  • A temple within reach of a road puts its approach between itself and the road.
    An approach runs from the busy road to the hall, so a shrine, monastery or temple within reach of a road or city street stands its avenue of arches between itself and the way, judged by the arches' bearing against the nearest major way. A hall with no way in reach faces where it will.
  • A street that runs straight up to a temple ends in an arch before it.
    The arch stands on that street just in front of the temple. How many arches the approach carries is set for each temple on its own, not by the length of the street, and the arches are drawn above the street so it passes beneath them. A temple set back from the grid, with no street running up to it, needs none.

Among clustered temples, a city also draws small shrines.

  • Where two or more temples cluster within about 1,200 ft, at least three small wayside shrines stand among them.
    They stand clear of the temples, the wall and the streets, and are placed before the houses are packed, so the rows of houses flow around them. Small shrines several to a neighborhood are attested, but not in the ground between a quarter's temples: placing them there is a GUESS. More belong at a city's thresholds (How our maps place shrines, temples and graves in each size of settlement).
  • A lone temple among houses gathers no small shrines.
    A single temple is not a temple neighborhood: the city's warrior-fortune temple in the middle of the samurai quarter is drawn as a building, not a precinct.
  • Every small shrine is drawn as a vermilion-roofed shed with its own little gate, by convention.
    One glyph stands for the whole class of small shrines, so a reader can tell one from a temple at a glance. Vermilion is the mark of Inari's shrine buildings and arches, and a small shrine (hokora) is a simplified one; the Akiba cult's small shrines were often of stone instead, because stone does not burn, and no page we read gives a small shrine a vermilion roof and a gate of its own. On the map nobody lives in one, and none has an approach of its own.
