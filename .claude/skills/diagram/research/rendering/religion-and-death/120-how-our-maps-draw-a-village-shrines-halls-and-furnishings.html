How our maps draw a village shrine's halls and furnishings

At average wealth, a village shrine on our maps is its one-roof hall - the villagers' worship hall and the country monk's dwelling under a single roof - with the small sanctuary behind it, the arches over its approach, a sacred tree and a stone basin, all in a clearing inside the shrine's wood, and nothing else. What a richer parish gave its shrine, and a stage, a sumo ring or a bell, are knobs.

  • The sanctuary is about 6 ft square, one bay wide, at the back of the precinct.
    It is drawn at the size of the one village sanctuary we found measured, behind the hall on the approach's axis, where a sanctuary stood, at the rear of the shrine and on the worship hall's axis (accurate). That the approach runs on the same axis is a map drawing convention: neither source names the approach.
  • The villagers' hall is about 20 to 35 ft on a side.
    The band runs from the Saitama hall's 6.54 m (~21 ft) to the Ehime worship hall's 10.62 m (~35 ft) (accurate as a band). The subsidiary hall of 3.83 m (~13 ft) is not a hall where villagers gathered, so it does not set the small end.
  • Under one roof with the monk's dwelling, the hall makes a building of 2,100 to 3,600 sq ft.
    The band rests on one attested example, a city temple's, not a village's, and the only one found (accurate as a band, disclosed as such). The example, and the dwelling where it stands apart, are at the monk's dwelling; the size the maps draw is at How our maps site and size a village shrine.
  • A village shrine is bigger than a farmhouse, because it contains one.
    The hall alone is farmhouse-sized or smaller, and the monk's dwelling is a farmhouse in form; under one roof the two make a building of two to three farmhouses.
  • The first arch stands where the approach enters the shrine's ground.
    The others follow it along the approach, spaced as Torii spacing sets out.
  • No fence, wall or hedge surrounds the precinct.
    The reasons are at Was a village shrine walled or fenced?. The precinct itself is the shrine's wood, its area inside the registers' band (How our maps site and size a village shrine).
  • A sacred tree is drawn by default, beside the approach.
    The roped tree is accurate in kind; how common one was at a village shrine is a GUESS, since no source counts them.
  • A plain stone basin stands beside the approach at the innermost arch.
    That one stood beside the approach is accurate, and a parish gave its shrine one before modern times. It is drawn with no pavilion over it; neither the open basin nor the roofed one is attested at a village shrine before 1868, so its roof is a GUESS.
  • The shrine's dedication is named at the sanctuary.
    The kami the shrine serves is named beside its sanctuary; the hall itself carries no label.
  • A kitchen garden and a privy stand at the dwelling, and fire-water at the wooden buildings.
    This is a GUESS, from the farmhouse the dwelling is drawn as: no page we read names them at a village shrine.
  • A belfry is drawn only where the plan asks for one.
    At small temples a main hall and a belfry alone are the commonest arrangement, but no page says whether a village shrine kept by a monk had its bell, so the bell is a knob. No office building is drawn.

What a parish gave its shrine as it grew richer, our maps draw by the parish's wealth.

  • Guardian dogs, lanterns and strength stones are on a wealth knob, and none is drawn at average wealth.
    They are gifts that accrued with a parish's wealth (what a parish gave); the strength stones, set beside the approach, go with them.
  • A Bishamon shrine's guardian pair is drawn as tigers.
    That is accurate at named temples, and its reach to a village is a GUESS.
  • No fence is drawn round the sanctuary, however rich the parish.
    At a village shrine such a fence is attested only in modern times (a fence round the sanctuary).
  • A farmers' stage and a sumo ring are knobs, absent by default.
    Both were real, but not in every village.
  • Subsidiary shrines, an office, a votive-plaque hall and a portable-shrine store are not drawn.
    No page places them at a village shrine.
  • A sanctuary with no worship hall, under a covering house, is not drawn.
    The form is recorded, but the maps keep the one-roof hall (Does the country monk live at the shrine?).
  • China's opera stage facing the temple is not drawn.
    It is recorded, but it is not the setting's village default.

A magistrate's compound keeps a shrine of its own, drawn on the building plans (Every administrative compound keeps a shrine). Where it serves more than one kami, how they are housed follows the three forms the record found.

  • Which form a compound shrine takes is a knob, rolled from the map's seed.
    The pages we read attest three and rank none, so the maps choose among them by roll: altars side by side across the hall, one to a bay, the main kami's in the middle or on the right as you face it; a single altar for the main kami, the others kept behind it and not drawn apart; or a second small shrine of its own inside the compound.
  • Several altars side by side in one undivided hall are a GUESS.
    What the pages attest is the two-bay hall and small halls joined under one roof, not separate altars inside one undivided hall; a plan that draws them so is drawing the first form.
