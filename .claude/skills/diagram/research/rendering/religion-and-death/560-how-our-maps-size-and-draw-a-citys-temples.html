How our maps size and draw a city's temples

Our maps draw an ordinary temple at the size history gives it, and a city's great temple complexes with the plan every great temple read shares, but on a small fraction of a real great temple's ground. The setting also gives every city temple far more monks than history gave most temples, a deliberate deviation. How many temples a city draws, and where, is at How our maps place a city's temples and its temple quarter (teramachi).

  • An ordinary temple precinct is drawn at about 35,000 sq ft, a square some 190 ft on a side.
    This is accurate: an Edo temple of about 1,000 tsubo was called one of average size, and a Chinese county seat's temple stood on 3,300 m² (~35,500 sq ft). It is the size of a county town's temple and of a city's lesser temples.
  • An ordinary precinct holds a gate, a main hall, a kuri that is also the temple office, and a bell tower.
    At an ordinary temple the kuri often doubled as the office, so one building serves for both. The lecture hall, the sutra house, the monks' hall and the corridors belong only to a great temple.
  • How big is an ordinary temple's main hall drawn?
    About 60 by 40 ft. It is a GUESS from the one measured hall, Jorokuji's of 1629, 61 by 39 ft, which was built as its temple's abbot's quarters and later became its main hall.
  • A city's two great temple complexes are drawn at about 73,000 sq ft each.
    That is twice the ordinary precinct, and far less than history's great temples: Quanzhou's Kaiyuan temple stands on 78,000 m² (~19 acres), and Kan'ei-ji held over 305,000 tsubo (~250 acres). The two great temples whose monks are counted kept thousands, where a setting's city complex keeps 15 to 30. Keeping the great temple's plan at a provincial city's size is a deviation.
  • A great temple is drawn on one axis.
    This is accurate: every great temple read, Japanese or Chinese, of any sect, sets its gates and principal halls on one line and its other buildings to the sides (City temples: the precinct, its halls, bell tower and pagoda).
  • How big is a great temple's main hall drawn?
    About 90 by 70 ft, between an ordinary temple's 60 by 40 ft and the great halls' 150 by 115 ft - a GUESS, since no page we read gives a provincial great temple's main hall.

The rule the map follows: a great city temple is a walled precinct on one axis:

  • an outer gate, the main gate, then the main hall (and in a Zen house a dharma hall behind it) in a line, with a cloister or corridors round the central court;
  • the bell tower and the sutra house to the sides of that court, the kuri and the monks' quarters to one side, the abbot's quarters behind the main hall;
  • sub-temples as separate walled lots below or around the core, and the lords' tombs or the graveyard outside the cloister at the back;
  • where the ground slopes, the three parts climb it as terraces.

A temple's tall buildings are its bell tower and, at a great temple, its pagoda.

  • Every temple precinct carries its own bell tower, within its gate.
    A bell tower is part of the full plan in Japan, and a Chinese city temple kept its own apart from the city's, so this is accurate. It is drawn apart from the city's civic tower (The bell-and-drum tower - one per walled seat).
  • How big is a temple's bell tower drawn?
    About 20 ft square, a GUESS between a Japanese tower a single bay square and the Zhihua temple's 7.1 m (~23 ft) tower.
  • Which temples carry a pagoda?
    Only a great temple's precinct: the temples of the domain capital's grand abbots and of a provincial city's provincial abbot, the senior monks the Ministry of Rites posts to each. It is a GUESS: no page we read says how common pagodas were at ordinary temples, every measured pagoda read stands at a great or famous temple, and the pagodas ordered in the 740s stood at each province's own temples.
  • How big is a pagoda drawn?
    About 16 to 20 ft square at its base and 60 to 110 ft tall, for three stories. It is a GUESS from the three-story pagodas measured: Kohfukuji's 19.1 m (~63 ft) on a first story 4.8 m (~16 ft) across, Hokkiji's about 24 m (~79 ft) and Yakushiji's about 34 m (~112 ft).

The setting's city temples keep far more monks than history's ordinary temples did.

  • Every city temple in the setting is a major complex, a deliberate deviation.
    Historically most temples were small: a count late in the thirteenth century gives about five clergy to a Chinese temple on average. The setting instead makes every city temple a complex of many monks.
  • How many monks does a city temple complex keep?
    About 15 to 30 in a provincial city (~3,000 inhabitants) and 50 or more in a domain capital (~12,000). This is this project's own scale, a GUESS: no page we read gives a monk count for any Ming city monastery. The nearest Ming figure read, a frontier monastery founded by imperial order in 1442 and given fifty-five official monks, is the size of a domain capital's complex, as an establishment rather than a count.
  • A provincial city's clergy are two to four times history's share.
    Its two complexes, 30 to 60 monks between them, put clergy at about 1-2% of its population, against about 0.46% in Song China. In the setting the city's temples serve the whole province's worship, not just the walled city; across the population as a whole, the setting puts monks at under 1%.
