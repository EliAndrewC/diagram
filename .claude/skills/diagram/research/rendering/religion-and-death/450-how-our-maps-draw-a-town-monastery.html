How our maps draw a town monastery

Our maps give a county town two monasteries by default, one for each patron Fortune of the clan that holds it, as the setting has it. Each is drawn as a precinct of about 16,000 sq ft (over a third of an acre) round a main hall some 40 ft across the front, holding the hall, a kuri, a bell tower and a gate. Where the monasteries stand in the town is rolled per town between the two arrangements the history shows.

The sizes come from a register of the temples of one Saitama district, made after 1868, when Japan began to modernize. Our maps show the country before then, so each size is checked against the few temple figures from earlier times.

  • Two monasteries a town is a deliberate deviation from the history.
    A Japanese town of that size kept more temples: one Saitama town area's register lists nineteen temples across fourteen of its villages, and castle towns kept a dozen or twenty, Miyazu's twelve gathered into a planned temple quarter. Ming law allowed a whole county fewer, one monastery. The setting's two sit between.
  • A monastery's household is implied, not drawn.
    Each belongs to an Order, the network of one Fortune's temples, monasteries and shrines. Inside each live the preceptor, the monks under them and, where the Order's monks marry, a householder's family, as a temple's kuri and dormitory housed its head priest's family and its monks in training. That they lived there is accurate in kind; how many is a GUESS, since no page we read counts them.
  • How big is a monastery's precinct, and its main hall?
    The precinct is rolled between about 10,000 and 69,000 sq ft, and the main hall between 24 and 63 ft across the front: the ranges of the nineteen temples in one Saitama district's register. The ranges are accurate as measured after 1868; that they fit a county town is this project's inference.
  • No figure from before 1868 bears on the smallest and largest sizes.
    The ends of both ranges rest only on the register made after 1868, so they are a GUESS in degree.
  • Do the default sizes fit the few figures from before 1868?
    The precinct's default of about 16,400 sq ft, the register's median, is smaller than the land of about 700 tsubo, some 25,000 sq ft, that a Kannon hall and a shrine of the thirty guardian deities, in the care of two of the register's temples, held free of tax in 1843, as a precinct should be, since that land counted fields as well. The main hall's default of about 42 ft lies inside the range that holds a hall of 1697. Both are a GUESS in degree.
  • A monastery's precinct holds a main hall, a kuri, a bell tower and a gate.
    The kuri stands beside the main hall. The bell tower is about 6 ft square, as the one-ken tower in the Saitama register is. The gate stands at the end of the precinct's axis, as at a Sōtō temple in Aichi.
  • Was a monastery's precinct enclosed?
    The register's gates imply an enclosure it does not describe, and no page we read describes one, so the maps bound the precinct with a line broken only at its gates, a GUESS.
  • Where a town's monasteries stand is a knob, rolled per town between two attested forms.
    Either they stand in a quarter of their own at the rim of the town, apart from its street of shops, as a castle town's temple quarter did at Amagasaki, or inside the town, either side of its main street, as at the Chinese county seat of Pingyao. In the setting's own campaign, one town's monastery stands just outside the town, as the first form has it.
  • A monastery's graveyard is placed as the other graveyards are.
    Where it lies is decided at How our maps choose where a village's and a hamlet's dead lie

The rule the map follows: on a town sheet (1 ft per pixel):

  • each monastery's precinct is about 10,000 to 69,000 sq ft, about 16,400 sq ft by default;
  • its main hall is 24 to 63 ft across the front, about 42 ft by default;
  • the precinct holds the hall, a kuri beside it, a bell tower about 6 ft square and a gate at the end of its axis, and is bounded by a line broken only at its gates (a guess);
  • where the monasteries stand is rolled per town: in a quarter of their own at the rim of the town, near each other and apart from its street of shops, or inside the town, set either side of its main street.
