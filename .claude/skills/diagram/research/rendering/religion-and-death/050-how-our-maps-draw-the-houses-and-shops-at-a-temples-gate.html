How our maps draw the houses and shops at a temple's gate

On a town or city map, a temple's gate carries a shallow row of ordinary houses and shops facing its approach, and the forecourt inside the gate is left open. Nothing sacred is sold outside the temple's wall.

  • How many houses ring a provincial city's temples?
    About 40 to 80 around the two temple complexes of a provincial city, which hold 30 to 60 resident monks between them: roughly one or two lay households for each monk, for a city whose worshippers come from its own region. The twenty to forty houses each complex gets sits beside the twenty-two at one average Edo temple. The ratio to the monks is this project's own anchor, a GUESS calibrated on the drawn cities: no page read sets a gate town's households against its temple's monks.
  • The houses stand in a single shallow row about 18 ft (3 ken) deep, facing the temple's approach.
    That is the depth of the gate houses at the average Edo temple.
  • The row is ordinary zoning, not extra population.
    Its houses are drawn inside the caste bands the city already has, and its trades are a neighborhood's, as at that Edo temple's gate. Who owned the ground does not show on a map, so the row is drawn the same whether its houses stand on the temple's land or not.
  • How many gate households does a pilgrimage city draw?
    Ten lay households or more for each monk, the ratio turned over - the gate-town pattern, and what an especially pious city looks like on the ground. The figure is a GUESS.
  • No teahouse or eating house has a symbol of its own.
    At this scale a teahouse and an eatery are both the ordinary shop, so the map tells the story by where a shop stands - a shop beside the temple reads as a teahouse, as on Sensoji's approach - and never by its icon. This is a map drawing convention.
  • No shop selling amulets or incense is drawn outside a temple compound.
    A temple issued its own amulets, and what a visitor paid was taken as a donation to it, so there is no third-party shop of religious goods at the gate, and everything sacred on the map is inside the compound.
  • A temple's retained carpenters are drawn in workshops that are also dwellings, not storefronts.
    Temples kept carpenters living near their gate, and drawing them as workshop-houses is this project's GUESS.
  • The forecourt inside a temple's gate is drawn as open ground with no market stalls.
    On a fair day the temple's ground became the market, but a map shows one moment, so the fair is a use of ground the map deliberately leaves clear rather than a feature it draws.
