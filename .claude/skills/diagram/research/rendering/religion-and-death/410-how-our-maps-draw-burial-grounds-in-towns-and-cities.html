How our maps draw burial grounds in towns and cities

A town on our maps buries its dead in one ground of about 0.25 to 0.75 acre, and a city in 0.75 to 2 acres split across two or more yards beside different roads out. Beside them each seat keeps a ground for the unclaimed and the poor: by default a single mound, or, where a seat is drawn in the Chinese form, a fenced cemetery or several small charity grounds. The burial ground, the cremation ground and the pauper's ground are drawn close together outside the wall, their near edge a few hundred feet past it.

  • A town's burial ground is about 0.25 to 0.75 acre.
    The size is calibrated to the Qing charity graveyards, which ran from about 0.05 to 1.5 acres each, since no page we read gives the area of a Japanese ground before modern times. A village's ground stays below a town's, as How our maps draw village burial grounds (bochi) sets out.
  • A city's 0.75 to 2 acres is split across two or more yards, beside different roads out where the city's plan allows.
    Each yard is kept within the 0.05 to 1.5 acres of a Qing charity graveyard. A county town whose dead lie in several grounds beside different roads out is the pattern of Xuancheng's gazetteer.
  • No allowance for paths is added to a ground's graves.
    The factor of 1.5 to 2 for paths and buildings comes from a 1934 paper on the common cemeteries of Japan's cities, so the maps do not use it: a ground's size is its graves and the lanes the drawing gives them, not a multiple of its plots.
  • A city draws two to four burial grounds, however many temples it has.
    A city's count of grounds was a matter of land, not of foundations: Osaka's scattered graveyards were merged into a few on the city's rim. In this setting a graveyard is a parish ground inside a temple's or a shrine's precinct, as How our maps choose where a village's and a hamlet's dead lie sets out; what is shared is the ground's catchment, not a rule that each foundation has its own.
  • A Fox city does not draw more burial grounds for its eight precincts.
    The Fox precincts are economic institutions holding rights to forest land, not eight parishes, so they have no separate congregations of dead, and the maps already site burial grounds by the ground itself - off the fields, downstream, across the water where the bank allows - rather than by counting temples. Eight precincts sharing three burial grounds is this project's own arrangement, a GUESS: no page we read gives a ratio of temples to grounds. What it costs is that a reader who expects a graveyard behind each precinct will not find one.
  • A seat drawn in the Chinese form rolls between the two forms its pauper ground took.
    By its seed it draws either one pauper ground outside the walls - fenced, planted, laid in rows of graves about 8 ft apart, with a keeper's house at one side - or two to four small charity grounds, each about 80 to 140 ft on a side, beside different roads out. Both forms are attested, so the choice is a knob rolled per seat rather than a choice made once.
  • The single Song-form ground is drawn a few hundred feet on a side.
    The Song order was for three to five qing, some 42 to 70 acres, in the counties of the capital district, a ground far larger than the sheet. The size drawn is a GUESS, calibrated to the sheet.
  • A seat in the default Japanese form draws one pauper mound, 10 to 30 ft across.
    The size is a GUESS: no page we read gives the size of a Japanese mound for the unclaimed dead.

How far outside the wall the three grounds stand is set by how the record's cities placed theirs: the entrance at or just past the wall, the field running outward from it. A small compact feature drawn far out would put at the near end what history puts at the far end.

  • The near edge stands at least 150 ft past the moat's outer face.
    That clears the moat, the glacis and the verge, so the ground reads plainly as outside the city. It is a map drawing convention, not a fire setback: no premodern setback was found to calibrate to.
  • How far past the moat does the near edge usually stand?
    250 to 600 ft. The band brackets Rendaino's gate-temple, at the ground's mouth, about 0 to 300 ft, and sits well inside Toribeno's 1,334 ft from its entrance crossroads, at a city 25 times our size. Those distances are this project's measurements.
  • The near edge stands at most 900 ft out at a capital, and 750 ft at a provincial city.
    Every constraint on the record is met far inside that - the law asks only that the dead lie outside, and the pyre's fire is kept in its own yard - so 900 ft is generous. These constraints barely grow with a city's size, so a smaller city's edge reads tighter, not looser.
  • The three grounds stand within about 600 ft of each other.
    At Kozukappara the site of the execution ground lies within the temple Enmei-ji, and Eko-in, founded to bury its dead, stands on the land beside it; this project measured 258 to 287 ft between them on a modern map, from a point not recorded.
  • The pyre keeps no clearance ring; it stands in a walled yard.
    No premodern clearance was found; why is at How our maps draw cremation grounds and bone mounds (kasoba).
