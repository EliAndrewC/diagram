How our maps draw burial grounds of towns and cities, and paupers' grounds (yizhong)

A town on our maps buries its dead in one ground of about 0.25 to 0.75 acre, and a city in 0.75 to 2 acres split across two or more yards beside different roads out. Beside them each town and city keeps a ground for the unclaimed and the poor: by default a single mound, or, where the map follows Chinese practice rather than Japanese, a fenced cemetery or several small charity grounds. The burial ground, the cremation ground and the pauper's ground are drawn close together outside the wall, their near edge a few hundred feet past it.

  • A town's burial ground is about 0.25 to 0.75 acre.
    The size is calibrated to the Qing charity graveyards of Baoshan county, near Shanghai, which ran from about 0.05 to 1.5 acres each, since no page we read gives the area of a Japanese ground before modern times. A village's ground stays below a town's, as How our maps draw village burial grounds (bochi) sets out.
  • A city's 0.75 to 2 acres is split across two or more yards, beside different roads out where the city's plan allows.
    Each yard is kept within the 0.05 to 1.5 acres of Baoshan county's Qing charity graveyards. A county town whose dead lie in several grounds beside different roads out is the pattern of Xuancheng's gazetteer.
  • No allowance for paths is added to a ground's graves.
    The factor of 1.5 to 2 for paths and buildings comes from a 1934 paper on the modern public cemeteries of Japan's cities, laid out long after the period our maps show, so the maps do not use it: a ground's size is its graves and the lanes the drawing gives them, not a multiple of its plots.
  • A city draws two to four burial grounds, however many temples it has.
    A city's count of grounds was set by its plan, not by its foundations: Osaka's scattered graveyards were merged, for military reasons, into a few on the city's rim.
  • Each of a city's burial grounds serves the parishes of several temples.
    In this setting a graveyard is a parish ground inside a temple's or a shrine's precinct, as How our maps choose where a village's and a hamlet's dead lie sets out. A city's few grounds are each shared by the parishes around them, rather than each temple keeping its own.
  • A Fox city still draws two to four burial grounds, not one for each of its eight precincts.
    The Fox precincts are economic institutions holding rights to forest land, not eight parishes, so they have no separate congregations of dead, and the maps already site burial grounds by the ground itself - off the fields, downstream, across the water where the bank allows - rather than by counting temples. Eight precincts sharing three burial grounds is this project's own arrangement, a GUESS: no page we read gives a ratio of temples to grounds. What it costs is that a reader who expects a graveyard behind each precinct will not find one.

Each town and city also keeps a ground for the unclaimed and the poor.

  • A town or city drawn in the default Japanese manner has one pauper mound, 10 to 30 ft across.
    The size is a GUESS: no page we read gives the size of a Japanese mound for the unclaimed dead.
  • Where a map follows Chinese practice, each town or city takes one of the two forms China's pauper grounds took, chosen at random.
    It draws either one pauper ground outside the walls in the Song dynasty's form, the louzeyuan - fenced, planted, laid out in sections of graves with a Buddhist temple in one corner, each burial given eight chi of ground, about 8 ft by this project's conversion - or two to four small charity grounds, each about 80 to 140 ft on a side, beside different roads out. Both forms are attested, so each town or city gets its own draw from its seed rather than one choice for every map.
  • The single Song-dynasty pauper ground is drawn a few hundred feet on a side.
    The Song dynasty's order was for three to five qing, a Chinese land measure of a hundred mu - some 42 to 70 acres - in the counties of the capital district, a ground far larger than the whole map. The size drawn is a GUESS, fitted to the map.

How far outside the wall the three grounds stand is set by how the record's cities placed theirs: a temple marking the ground's mouth, and that mouth, by this project's measurement, at or just past the city's edge, the field running outward from it. Our grounds are small and compact, so drawing one far out would put its entrance where the record's burial fields ended, not where they began.

  • The near edge stands at least 150 ft past the moat's outer face.
    That clears the moat, the glacis and the verge, so the ground reads plainly as outside the city. It is a map drawing convention, not a fire setback: no premodern setback to calibrate to was found, as How our maps draw cremation grounds and bone mounds (kasoba) sets out.
  • How far past the moat does the near edge usually stand?
    250 to 600 ft. The band brackets the gate-temple of Rendaino, one of old Kyoto's three great graveyards, at the ground's mouth, about 0 to 300 ft, and sits well inside the 1,334 ft of Toribeno, another of the three, from its entrance crossroads, in a city 25 times the size of ours. Those distances are this project's measurements.
  • The near edge stands at most 900 ft out at a capital, and 750 ft at a provincial city.
    Every constraint on the record is met far inside that - Japan's ancient law code asks only that the dead lie away from the capital and the roads, and the pyre's fire is kept in its own yard - so 900 ft is generous. These constraints barely grow with a city's size, so a smaller city's edge reads tighter, not looser.
  • The three grounds stand within about 600 ft of each other.
    In Edo, the execution ground and the temple that buried its dead stood side by side. At Kozukappara the site of the execution ground lies within the temple Enmei-ji, and Eko-in, founded to bury its dead, stands on the land beside it; this project measured 258 to 287 ft between them on a modern map, from a point not recorded.
  • The pyre stands in a walled yard, with no cleared ring around it.
    No premodern record of a cleared distance around a pyre was found. How our maps draw cremation grounds and bone mounds (kasoba) explains the walled yard.
