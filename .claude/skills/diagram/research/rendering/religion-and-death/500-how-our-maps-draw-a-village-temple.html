How our maps draw a village temple (tera)

Our maps draw no village temple. A village draws its shrine and no separate temple, and a hamlet draws neither and is served by its village's shrine. This follows the setting our maps are drawn for, in which each village district, a village and the hamlets round it, has one country monk, who serves in the village's shrine and keeps the district's records of births and deaths.

  • Leaving the temple out is a deliberate deviation from the history.
    A real village might keep no temple of its own and use its neighbors', keep one, or keep several. The setting gives every village one hall, its shrine, and no temple.
  • The setting combines two historical arrangements: a village with its own temple, and one served by its neighbor's.
    A village has its own hall, as a village with a temple of its own did, and its hamlets are served from the village's hall, as a village without a temple was served by its neighbor's.
  • The village's shrine is its parish temple.
    In the setting the shrine does the work a village temple did. Whether a graveyard stands beside it is at How our maps choose where a village's and a hamlet's dead lie
  • A village temple's buildings are the measure for the village shrine's hall and the country monk's dwelling.
    The shrine's hall and the dwelling do the temple's work, so they are sized against it. A village temple's main hall was about 24 to 36 ft a side, and the one small temple whose kuri was measured, a shugen hall, had a kuri of about 12 by 15 ft; end to end, a hall and a kuri of those sizes run to about 36 to 51 ft. Our maps draw the shrine's hall and the country monk's dwelling under one roof, some 66 ft long, a little larger than that and of the same order.
