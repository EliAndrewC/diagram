How our maps choose where a village's and a hamlet's dead lie

Each village on our maps has one burial ground, in the Japanese form, and each village's seed chooses whether it lies in the yard of the village's shrine or on ground of its own apart from the shrine. A hamlet draws no burial ground: its dead lie in the village's. Some hamlets' farmsteads keep a few graves on their own land, and on some paddy maps a single grave stands in a field.

  • A village draws one burial ground of its own.
    A graveyard held by the settlement is one of the forms attested in Japan beside the temple's, the lineage's and the household's, and a single ground, not graves scattered through the fields, is the form the maps draw for a village. The setting puts China's geography underneath and Japanese customs on the surface, so a village's burial ground is never the Chinese scatter of family graves through its fields. How large the ground is drawn is at How our maps draw village burial grounds, and the Chinese measure it is checked against, the Qing charity graveyards, at Village burial grounds (bochi).
  • The lineage's and the household's graves are not drawn on a village map.
    They belong to a house or a kin group, and are too small to show at a village's scale.
  • In this setting, the hall that tends a village's dead is its shrine.
    This is a deliberate deviation. Real Shinto shuns death, but the state of this setting merged the Buddhist strand with Fortune worship, so any temple may host a graveyard, and the country shrines officially belong to the setting's Buddhist order even while they house Fortune deities. The country monk who keeps a village's shrine performs the funerary rites. So the shrine plays the part of the Edo parish temple, with which a household was registered and which buried its members; the setting reaches that outcome by another route than the Tokugawa campaign against Christianity. Its yard is therefore the setting's churchyard.
  • Each village's seed chooses where its ground lies, at even odds.
    Two Japanese forms are attested for a village's one ground: in the yard of the hall that tends its dead, and a ground of the village's own, apart from the hall. So the map rolls between them per village rather than choosing one. The even odds are a GUESS: no page we read counts the villages taking each form.
  • A ground in the shrine's yard lies within about 500 ft of the hall.
    The graves fill the yard around the hall and its torii arches, and never lie on them.
  • Our maps never put a village's graves at a shrine on a hill.
    We judge that graves would not climb a sacred hill, and a prominent hill shrine is not the humble village shrine where the country monk buries the dead, so a village whose shrine stands on a hill always takes a ground apart. A survey of two-grave villages points the same way, putting the shrine at the upper end of the village and the burial ground below the houses, never above them. That a hill shrine in particular kept no graves is a judgment about the setting, a GUESS: no source says it either way.
  • A ground apart lies downstream of the houses, beyond the last of them.
    Its side, and why no distance is drawn, are at How our maps draw village burial grounds.
  • A hamlet draws no cremation ground or shrine: both are the main village's.
    In this setting a village district is a main village and its hamlets, usually about half a dozen, and the main village alone keeps the shrine, the headman's house and the cremation ground. The country monk lives in the main village and performs the funerary rites for the whole district, so a hamlet's dead are burned at the village's cremation ground.
  • A hamlet draws no burial ground: its dead lie in the village's.
    The history puts a hamlet's dead by their own settlement in some places and at a parish temple in others, but that every hamlet kept a ground of its own rests only on records of the twentieth century. These maps draw no form attested only in modern times, so a hamlet's dead lie in the village's ground by the country monk's shrine, off the hamlet's map.
  • On about half of hamlet maps, some farmsteads draw a grave of their own.
    The household grave is attested beside the shared ground, so each hamlet's seed decides whether its farmsteads keep graves of their own - a knob. Where they do, about a third of the farmsteads draw a small grave of a few stones, at the scale of the plot, not as a burial ground. The shares are a GUESS.
  • A farmstead's grave stands at a corner of its plot, in its grove, or on its own land close by.
    The attested seats are the southeast and the northwest corners of the house plot, in or at the edge of the grove round the house, and the household's own field or wood close by.
  • About three paddy maps in ten draw a grave in one of their fields.
    The rate is the same on valley-floor, terraced and ribbon-valley paddy maps. How often is calibrated liberty: no source gives a rate, and the history argues such graves were common where the custom held.
  • Where a paddy map draws a field grave, it takes one of two forms, at even odds.
    An island inside a plot, which is the Chinese form, or a small mound in a plot's corner against its bunds, which is the Japanese one. Both are attested as placements of one thing, so the form is rolled per hamlet rather than fixed; the even odds are because no source weighs one against the other.
  • Divided remains are not drawn.
    One person's remains divided among several graves leave nothing visible at a map's scale.

Towns and cities keep their graveyards by their temples too; a walled one adds a common ground outside its wall. An unwalled town's grounds are all parish grounds.

  • A town's or a city's graveyard lies in its monastery's or temple's precinct.
    It lies within about 690 ft of the hall on a city map, and within about 230 ft on a town map. At a city's scale this binds only a ground inside the walls, since a common ground outside them belongs to the whole city.
  • A walled settlement keeps its parish grounds inside its walls and one common ground outside.
    The common ground outside is at least 1.3 times the largest ground inside, because there is room beyond the wall while the parish plots inside are hemmed in. The 1.3 is this project's choice, a GUESS.
  • A town draws its common ground larger, for its size, than a city does.
    A town has no pauper ossuary, as a city does, to take the poor, so its common ground carries proportionally more of its dead.
  • A farming quarter inside a city wall keeps a ground of its own.
    It takes the village's form.
