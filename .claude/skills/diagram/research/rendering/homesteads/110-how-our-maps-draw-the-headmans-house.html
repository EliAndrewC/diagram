How our maps draw the headman's house and the rich farmer's homestead (shoya, gono)

On our maps the headman's homestead is a farmstead like every other, and what marks it out is that its house is plainly the largest, about four times a plain farmhouse's area. The office adds a storehouse every headman keeps, and the village's tax rice waits either there or in a village store of its own. The rich farmer's homestead is drawn as the headman's at its grandest.

  • The headman's homestead gets the same work yard and garden as every other farm.
    The grandest house in the village gets its work yard and its dooryard garden like every other farm, and both are kept to an ordinary farm's sizes, because a big house does not mean a field-sized kitchen bed. On a map of dispersed farmsteads the headman's homestead brings its grove too, where there is room for one.
  • The headman's house is drawn as a plain farmhouse enlarged, marked out by its size rather than by a different plan.
    This is a map drawing convention. The maps' large-house glyph is not used, because it carries a storeroom wing that would stand outside the ground the homestead reserves and land on a neighbor's work yard.
  • How large should the headman's house be drawn?
    About 92 by 56 ft, some 480 square meters (~5,152 sq ft), against a plain farmhouse's 46 by 28 ft: four times its area, and larger than any house we read, so it is a GUESS on the large side. In one village's table of 1885, in the Meiji period, the leading houses were two to two and a half times the usual main house, one measured headman's house of 1805, in the Edo period, has 291 square meters (~3,132 sq ft) of building area over two stories, and the setting's notes give a village "a slightly larger headman's household".
  • Our maps always give the headman a storehouse (kura).
    An ordinary farm carries one only sometimes (Which farmhouses have a storehouse?). The office kept things: the village's records survive in the houses of the families that served as headmen, the tax rice came to the headman first, a headman's storehouse might hold it in place of a village store, and the one headman's plot a village record describes in full, the Ohara family's of 1849, has an earthen storehouse. Left to the ordinary farm's chance of a storehouse, a headman could come up without one, which would make an accident of the dice look like a fact of history.
  • One storehouse is drawn, though some headmen had two.
    One is the lower end of what the two headmen's farms we read carry: one at a headman-class farm rebuilt in a Chiba museum, two at the Yamada family's surviving homestead in Sennan.
  • Where a village's tax rice waits is a knob.
    The record attests two places: the headman's own storehouse, and a village store (gogura) standing apart. So each village picks one of them at random, fixed by its own seed, and the headman keeps their own storehouse either way.
  • On our maps a village store stands on its own among the houses, on dry ground or an empty house plot, never in the paddy.
    A handbook of 1794 puts it within the dwelling area, mostly on dry-field ground, or on a vacant house's plot. The handbook says "mostly"; "never" is the map's choice.
  • The headman's house is drawn with no gate for now - a deliberate deviation.
    The gate, a gatehouse (nagayamon) among them, was the headman's right to build, though the sources give the right and not how many used it. The Ohara family's plot has a gatehouse with a storeroom attached. So this project's choice is that a headman's house has its gate, and a village map generated by program, as the hamlet maps already are, will draw it. Our village maps are still drawn by hand and show none: a known gap in the drawing, not a claim that a headman went without one.
  • No wall is drawn round the headman's plot.
    On our maps the headman's house is marked out by its size alone. The record would allow either: the Yamada family's homestead has its earthen wall, and the headman-class farm rebuilt in a Chiba museum names none.
  • How large would a headman's plot be?
    About 2,500 to 2,800 square meters (~26,910-30,139 sq ft, about two-thirds of an acre), if drawn from the Yamada plot and the rebuilt Chiba farm. For an ordinary village's headman, whose plot no page we read measures, that is a GUESS.
  • Where on the map does the headman's house stand?
    In the district's main village, as the setting has it, and within it, where one can be told, in the settlement's oldest cluster - a GUESS. No page we read places the house, but the founding houses held the village's offices, and a founding house is likely among the first built. Nothing we read supports putting it on the road for the road's sake or beside the shrine.
  • A rich farmer's homestead is drawn as the headman's at its grandest.
    A rich farmer's family often held village office, and the rich farmers' homesteads we read are walled or gated compounds with two or more storehouses, so where a village has one it is best drawn as the headman's homestead at its grandest.
  • No page we read says how often a village had a second rich farm, and the setting leans against one.
    So how often a map draws one is a GUESS. In the setting most farmers are tenants and most landlords are merchants, so a map that draws a second rich farm beside the headman's goes beyond the setting's picture.
  • Where a village meets is a knob with three forms.
    The record attests the headman's house, the village's temple or shrine, and a meeting hall (kaisho) of its own, often standing inside the shrine precinct. The headman's house draws nothing new and the temple or shrine is already drawn, so only the meeting hall adds a building.
  • No page we read gives a meeting hall's size, so a drawn hall's size is a GUESS.
    The dictionary entries on the village meeting are silent on it.
  • Which villages build a meeting hall is a GUESS too.
    The halls attested from early times are of the Kinki region around Kyoto and Osaka, and in general meeting halls came into use only gradually.
