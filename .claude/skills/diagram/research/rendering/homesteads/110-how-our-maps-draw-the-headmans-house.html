How our maps draw the headman's house

On our maps the headman's homestead is a farmstead like every other, and what marks it out is that its house is plainly the largest. The office adds a storehouse every headman keeps, and the village's tax rice waits either there or in a village store of its own. The rich farmer's homestead is drawn as the headman's at its grandest.

  • The headman's homestead is a farmstead, not an exception.
    The grandest house in the village gets its work yard and its dooryard garden like every other farm, and both are kept to an ordinary farm's sizes, because a big house does not mean a field-sized kitchen bed. On a map of dispersed farmsteads the headman's homestead brings its grove too, where there is room for one.
  • The headman's house reads as the headman's by its size, not by a different plan or a compound wall.
    It is drawn about 92 by 56 ft, against a plain farmhouse's 46 by 28 ft.
  • How large is the drawn headman's house?
    About 92 by 56 ft, some 480 square meters (~5,152 sq ft): four times a plain farmhouse's area, and larger than any house we read, so it is a GUESS on the large side. In one village's table of 1885 the leading houses were two to two and a half times the usual main house, one measured headman's house of 1805 has 291 square meters (~3,132 sq ft) of building area over two stories, and the setting's notes give a village "a slightly larger headman's household".
  • The headman's house is drawn as a plain farmhouse enlarged.
    This is a map drawing convention: the large-house glyph carries a storeroom wing that stands outside the ground the homestead reserves and lands on a neighbor's work yard.
  • The headman always has a storehouse (kura).
    An ordinary farm carries one only sometimes (Which farmhouses have a storehouse?). The office kept things: the village's records survive in the houses of the families that served as headmen, the tax rice came to the headman first, a headman's storehouse might hold it in place of a village store, and the one headman's plot we read in full has an earthen storehouse. Left to the ordinary farm's chance of a storehouse, a headman could come up without one, which would be chance reading as doctrine.
  • Where a village's tax rice waits is a knob.
    The record attests two places: the headman's own storehouse, and a village store (gogura) standing apart. So a village rolls one of them from its own seed, and the headman keeps their own storehouse either way.
  • A village store stands on its own among the houses, on dry ground or an empty house plot, never in the paddy.
    A handbook of 1794 puts it within the dwelling area, mostly on dry-field ground, or on a vacant house's plot. The handbook says "mostly"; "never" is the map's choice.
  • The headman's house is drawn with no gate for now - a deliberate deviation.
    The record alone would make the gate a knob: a headman might build one, and the gatehouse (nagayamon) was theirs to build, but the sources give the right and not how many used it. This project gives the headman's house its gate, the gate being the headman's right; the one headman's plot we read in full has a gatehouse with a storeroom attached. The hand-drawn maps are not changed, and when village maps come to be generated by program, as the hamlet maps already are, a generated village gives its headman's house a gate. Until then the maps draw none - a known gap in the drawing, not a claim that a headman went without one.
  • No wall is drawn round the headman's plot.
    By the same reasoning a wall would be a knob: one surviving headman's plot has its earthen wall, and the other names none.
  • One storehouse is drawn; a second is attested.
    One is the lower end of what the surviving headmen's farms carry: one at the Chiba farm, two at the Yamada's.
  • How large would a headman's plot be?
    About 2,500 to 2,800 square meters (~26,910-30,139 sq ft), if drawn from the two survivors. For an ordinary village's headman, whose plot no page we read measures, that is a GUESS.
  • Where on the map does the headman's house stand?
    In the district's main village, as the setting has it, and within it, where one can be told, in the settlement's oldest cluster - a GUESS. No page we read places the house, but the founding houses held the village's offices, and a founding house is likely among the first built. Nothing we read supports putting it on the road for the road's sake or beside the shrine.
  • A rich farmer's homestead is drawn as the headman's at the top of its range.
    A rich farmer's family often held village office, and the rich farmers' homesteads we read are walled or gated compounds with two or more storehouses, so where a village has one it is best drawn as the headman's homestead at its grandest.
  • How often does a village carry a second rich farm?
    That is a GUESS: no page we read gives a village's count. The setting gives most land to merchant landlords, so a map that draws a second rich farm beside the headman's goes beyond the setting's picture.
  • Where a village meets is a knob with three forms.
    The record attests the headman's house, the village's temple or shrine, and a meeting hall (kaisho) of its own standing inside the shrine precinct. The headman's house draws nothing new and the temple or shrine is already drawn, so only the meeting hall adds a building.
  • How big is a meeting hall, and which villages build one?
    Both are a GUESS. No page we read gives a hall's size, and the attested halls are a regional case, of the Kinki region around Kyoto and Osaka.
