How our maps draw farmhouses - size, plan, roof, bearing and setback

Our maps draw each farmhouse as a thatched rectangle under one roof, longer than it is deep, its size and proportion varied a little from house to house. Each is turned from its village's common bearing, which is near south, by the curve of the lane it stands on, and its yard and garden beds turn with it. It stands 10 to 13 ft from the paddy, and never nearer than 6 ft.

  • The plain farmhouse is drawn 46 by 28 ft, the size of the farmhouses that survive.
    That is about 1,290 square feet, some 36 tsubo (this project's arithmetic, at 35.6 square feet to the tsubo): the size of a headman's or a well-off farmer's house among the survivors, and about twice the usual house of one village in 1885.
  • How big should an ordinary household's house be drawn?
    Near 35 by 20 ft: the 20 tsubo of the commonest house of the Utsunomiya villages at the survivors' proportions. A poorer house is smaller (a 10-tsubo house is about 25 by 14 ft), and 46 by 28 ft and upward is kept for the headman and the few better-off. Carrying the survivors' proportion of 1.6 to 2.0 over to houses no survivor measures is a GUESS.
  • The headman's house is the largest on the map.
    Why, and what else a headman's homestead holds, is at What makes the headman's house different?
  • On a scattered map, a homestead's wealth scales its house and its grove together.
    Each homestead takes a standing - poorer, ordinary or better-off - so a richer farm reads bigger across the board. The scaling reaches what is drawn and not the ground kept for it, so it never moves a neighbor and never changes how many households fit on the map.
  • On a clustered map, a house varies along its length more than across its depth.
    No two farmsteads were copies of one another, so the houses on a map vary. A house's length varies by roughly 0.85 to 1.35 of the base and its depth by only 0.90 to 1.10, so the proportions stay inside about 1.3 to 2.5 long to deep, as a house that grew along its long axis would. The surviving houses under one roof run 1.6 to 2.0.
  • No farmhouse is drawn squarer than about 1.3 to 1 or longer than about 2.7 to 1.
    The drawn band is wider than the survivors' on both sides, and its ends - and the 2.7 to 1 past which a house would be drawn wrong rather than drawn differently - are a calibration, not a finding: no page we read gives how proportions spread across a village's houses.

The plan and the roof are regional forms, so a settlement's farmhouses share them.

  • The plan is a knob, chosen per settlement.
    Each plan is attested and each regional: the straight house is the plain form everywhere; the L - magariya or chumon-zukuri - belongs in the horse-raising north, and there most of all to the headman and the better-off; the two-roofed bunto house belongs in the far south of Kyushu and on the Pacific side; and the three-sided court, or its single-range and L forms, in a Chinese-leaning village. How often each comes up is a GUESS: no page counts the share.
  • The roof shape is a knob, chosen per settlement and not per house.
    Hipped, gabled or irimoya, each a regional form; how often each comes up is a GUESS.
  • Each roof shape reads differently from above.
    Seen from overhead, a gabled roof is a rectangle split by a ridge running its whole length. A hipped roof has a ridge shorter than the house, with a slope running down from each end of it to the corners, so it reads as a long rectangle with a line down its middle and a diagonal into each corner. An irimoya reads like the hipped roof, with a small triangle standing at each end of the ridge.
  • Every farmhouse is thatched, the headman's as the tenant's.
    The headmen's surviving houses are thatched too. Tile belongs on the storehouse (see How big was a farm storehouse, and where on the plot did it stand?).
  • In a Chinese-leaning village, the farmhouses are tiled.
    So they are in the one region read, Huizhou. Whether the poorest there went thatched is unread, so a split between thatch and tile in such a village is a GUESS.

A row of houses all square to the map would read as drawn rather than built, so each farmhouse is turned a little.

  • Each settlement takes a common bearing within about 11 degrees of due south.
    That is half a compass point either side of south, so the bearing still reads as the south point of the sixteen. How near south is a GUESS; the survey's own example turned a whole village a quarter away from the fall of its ground, on a slope our maps do not draw.
  • Each farmhouse turns with the curve of the lane it stands on.
    Neighbors along a bending lane face a little apart, never more than about 30 degrees from the common bearing - the span of three points of the compass in the Okinawan survey, which puts the smaller turns down to curving roads. Within that, a house is turned from its lane's own line by up to about 12 degrees, most by much less, so a village reads as set out by eye rather than surveyed. How the turns spread inside the survey's range is a GUESS.
  • No farmhouse is turned a quarter away.
    The survey found about one house in nine turned to the right of its village's spread, but that count is modern and from one island region, and no count from before 1868 was found. The curving street that the survey gives as the cause of the smaller turns is seen in a village laid out in 1736, so our maps turn a house by its lane and leave the quarter-turned tenth undrawn.
  • A farmhouse's yard and garden beds turn with it.
    The yard is the ground in front of the house's south wall (see How our maps draw threshing and drying yards (niwa)), and a yard set square to the map beside a house turned four degrees would look like a mistake: a homestead was laid out as one piece. The homestead turns as one about the middle of the house, and the ground cleared for each part is the ground it is drawn on.
  • A yard's edge toward its house is straight and parallel to the wall.
    Its other edges wander a little, as a hand-laid plot's do.

No source states in feet how far a farmhouse stood from its paddy, so the least distance is built from three parts, two of them guesses.

  • The bund is taken as about 1.5 ft wide.
    That sits inside the one to two shaku the sources give (see Bunds between the paddies (aze)). Other bunds ran lower and narrower, or heavier, so 1.5 ft is one convention among several, and the floor leans on it lightly.
  • A path of about 1.5 ft runs along it.
    The bund was kept as a path for people and their tools; the path's width is a GUESS.
  • A thatched roof's eaves reach about 3 ft past the wall.
    No page we read gives the projection, so 3 ft is a GUESS: the gap our maps already keep between neighboring houses.
  • No farmhouse wall stands within 6 ft of a paddy.
    A wall nearer than bund, path and eave, 1.5 + 1.5 + 3 ft, would stand with its roof over the levee path and its drip line in the rice. The floor is tested at each of a house's four corners, never at its center. Two of its three parts are guesses, so it is a soft threshold rather than a measured minimum.
  • Our maps draw farmhouses 10 to 13 ft from the paddy.
    That is well clear of the floor, and close enough that a household works its own ground, with the house's back to the hill and its face to the water and the fields.
  • No farmhouse is held to a greatest distance from its fields.
    A cluster standing farther from its paddy than some figure is not thereby wrong; one that reads as standing beside its fields rather than on them is, and that is judged by reading the map, not measured.
