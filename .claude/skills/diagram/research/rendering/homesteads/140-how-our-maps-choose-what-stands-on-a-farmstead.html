How our maps choose what stands on a farmstead

Our maps draw on a farmstead what the records from before 1868 found there, in about the numbers they found it: a farmhouse, a privy, on average about one and a half outbuildings more, and the small fixtures of the yard. The fixtures are drawn at their real size; where no source measures one, its size is a GUESS.

  • The counts follow the villages from before 1868, not the 1972 survey.
    The 1972 survey found 4.4 outbuildings a household; Kakimochi's count of 1885 found about 1.4 besides the privy and the retirement house. This project draws only what is attested before modern times, so the maps take the premodern counts, and the 1972 figures show only the shape of a farmstead's outbuildings. Keeping the 1972 survey and cutting it down for a warmer lowland was not taken, because the survey is modern.

The rule the map follows:

  • Besides the privy, a farmstead carries on average about one and a half outbuildings. A few carry none, a headman's carries several, and the storehouse and the sheds go to the larger houses first.
  • A storehouse stands on about one farm in eight (see Which farmhouses have a storehouse?), a firewood shed on about four in ten (see Where was the firewood stacked?), and a storage shed on about one in three.
  • A shed is 12 feet deep and 24 feet long, the common size in the Kakimochi count.
  • These shares are a calibration against the two villages' counts, not a figure any one source gives.
  • The maps draw seven of the yard's small fixtures.
    The privy, the firewood stack, the manure heap, the bath shed and the persimmon, each large enough to see at the map's scale; the household shrine, rare but notable where it stands; and the chicken coop, drawn as it was kept in imperial China. Each has its own section on the research page, which says how many farms the map gives it and where on the plot.
  • How many fixtures does a map draw?
    Each kind's share is rolled per hamlet within its range, from the map's seed. On the sample hamlet the maps are tested on, of 15 houses, 14 have a privy, 14 a woodpile, 12 a coop, 9 a manure heap, 10 a persimmon, 1 a bath shed and 1 a household shrine.
  • The straw rick is not drawn.
    Like every seasonal thing, it waits for maps of a particular season; what it was, where it stood and for how long are at The paddy through the rice year.
  • The maps' wells are shared ones.
    Jointly owned wells were numerous, because digging one was costly, so a village's wells are drawn as the households' common wells, not one to a farm.
  • The stone at a village's entrance stands by the road, not on a farmstead.
    A boundary god (dosojin) stood chiefly at a village's entrances and exits, and in Matsumoto most stand at a settlement's entrance, so a map's entrance stone is drawn beside the road where it comes into the village.
  • A farm's byre is drawn standing apart, not as a wing of the house.
    The stable wing of the magariya belongs to the horse-breeding districts of the far north, and the maps' temperate farms take the separate byre.
  • What runs along the edge of a farm plot is a knob, rolled per settlement from the map's seed.
    It takes one of four attested forms:
    • a hedge, or a bamboo fence, along the plot line;
    • a grove, which is also the windbreak, on the sides its settlement rolls - two, three or all four (see How our maps draw groves of trees around farmhouses (yashikirin));
    • the plot closed in by its own house, barn and sheds set round the yard, the western form;
    • where a map stands for a flood plain or a storm coast, an earth bank under trees or a stone wall.
  • A hedge is a thin green line, and a wall a thin gray one.
    The hedge is broken for the way in. How thick the lines are is a map drawing convention, since no size was found.
  • An open plot, with nothing along its edge, is kept only as the rare case.
    It rests on no page we read, and is a GUESS.
