How our maps read a village's name before seating it

A place name in the empire our maps are set in usually says something about where the settlement sits relative to its fields, its water or its ground, so a map is drawn to be read back into its name: the name is read before the settlement is seated. Japanese names were often made the same way, from the lie of the land. No generator reads a name; this is a rule for whoever specifies the map.

  • A settlement whose name names its ground is seated to match the name.
    The worked example is 上田 (Ueda, "upper paddy"), whose paddies are the upslope ones, so its dwellings stand downslope of them, facing up-valley toward the fields. On that map's ground, high to the northeast, the cluster sits on the lower, southwest flank with most of the paddy rising above it.
  • A name that says nothing about the ground keeps the default seat.
    The default is a clustered village on the dry high flank, hugging its paddy - the reverse of Ueda's seat.
  • The maps read these parts of a name as its ground.
    Each cue reads its character's own meaning onto the map; the list is this project's choice of cues, not a historical one.
    • 下 (shimo, "lower") says the reverse of Ueda: the settlement stands above its paddies.
    • 川 (kawa, "river"), or -gawa at a name's end, puts the settlement hard by the watercourse.
    • 山 (yama, "mountain"), 坂 (saka, "slope"), 原 (hara, "plain or moor"), 浜 (hama, "shore") and 口 (guchi, "mouth, entrance or pass") each name the ground the place stands on.
    • 谷 (tani, "valley"), read dani or ya too, names a valley; a -ya name most often a shallow one cut into higher ground.
    • 沢 (sawa, "mountain valley or marsh") names one or the other, by the rule below.
  • A sawa name is read as a valley or as a marsh by the part of the setting the map stands in.
    The word means a mountain valley in eastern Japan and a marsh in western Japan, so a map reads it by its own region.
