How our maps give some farms a storehouse

About one farmhouse in eight on our maps carries a storehouse, and they are the largest farmhouses. It is drawn as a tiled annex against the house wall, 18 to 27 ft long. The share follows the one village count the record has from before 1868, and the storehouse marks a well-off farm at a glance because most farms lack one (Farm storehouses (kura)).

  • About one farm in eight carries a storehouse.
    The share is set from the Kakimochi count, 2 households in 16, and not from the Miyagi survey of 1972, because that survey is modern; the alternative, about 30%, just above the 1972 count, was not kept for that reason. It is one village, so the figure is a calibration rather than a finding; it keeps a storehouse uncommon enough to mean something at a glance.
  • On a generated hamlet the share is a count.
    One in eight of the hamlet's farmhouses, rounded to a whole house: one storehouse on a hamlet of ten or eleven farmhouses, two on twelve to nineteen, three on twenty. That comes to 11 of 82 farmhouses over the five hamlets drawn.
  • The hand-drawn maps keep the higher share they were drawn with.
    They carry 324 storehouses on 1,126 farmhouses.
  • The storehouses go to the largest farmhouses, measured by the main house's floor.
    No farmhouse without one is larger than a farmhouse with one. Making a strict rule of what one village's count shows as a strong tendency is a deliberate deviation: the map never leaves a great house without a storehouse while a smaller one has it, though Kakimochi's largest house, at 45 tsubo, had none. The alternative, a draw weighted toward the larger houses, would let a great house go without now and then; it was not taken, because the larger houses first is the tendency the record shows, and a draw would sometimes put a hamlet's storehouse on one of its middling houses.
  • Between two farmhouses of one size, a draw decides.
    Where two farmhouses are the same size the map has nothing else to tell their households apart by, so which of them has the storehouse is a random draw keyed to the map's seed, and the same map drawn twice gives the same answer. What decided it between two such households the records read do not say, so that is a GUESS.
  • A village headman's house always carries one.
    A hamlet has no headman, so on a generated hamlet the largest houses take them all.
  • The storehouse is drawn as an annex against the house wall, a drawing convention.
    It stands on the west wall of a scattered farm, and on the shaded north wall in a cluster, where the sunny walls belong to the garden and the north is the one side the garden never takes. The record contradicts this: the storehouses in the sources read stood free of the house, in front of it or behind, on no fixed side, and few were built against it.
  • Its size is a deliberate deviation.
    The annex is 18 to 27 ft long and 1.5 to 1.8 times as long as deep, the band of the two farm sheds measured from the end of the Edo period (Farm sheds and barns (naya)). The commonest storehouse in a new-field village west of Edo was about 15 by 18 ft, and Kakimochi's two were 2 by 3 ken, about 12 by 18 ft, so the annex is drawn as long as a storehouse was or up to half again as long, and at the short end of its band 10 to 12 ft deep, shallower than the commonest storehouse and no deeper than Kakimochi's.
  • The storehouse is drawn tiled even where the farmhouse is thatched.
    Both storehouses the record read are tiled; drawing every one so is this project's reading of two examples.
  • A storehouse's ground is reserved with the rest of the farm.
    So a neighbor never lands on it.
