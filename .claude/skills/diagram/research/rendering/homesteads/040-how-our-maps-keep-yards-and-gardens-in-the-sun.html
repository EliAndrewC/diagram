How our maps keep yards and gardens in the sun

Every threshing yard and kitchen bed on a map keeps clear ground to its south, where a neighbor's house would shade it, and to its west and southwest, where the village's windbreak throws its afternoon shadow. The distances are worked out from where the sun stands at 38 degrees north - the temperate middle of the empire our maps are set in - in the months either side of summer, when the sun is low and the crops and the drying still need it.

  • How much clear ground does a yard keep to its south?
    39 ft. Threshing and drying follow the autumn harvest, and at 38 degrees north - the temperate middle of the empire our maps are set in - a 20 ft ridge casts, late in autumn, the shadows below. The drying day that matters is taken, as a GUESS, to run from nine to three, so no neighbor's farmhouse may stand within 39 ft to the south of a yard.

  • How tall is a farmhouse, and so how far does its shadow fall across a neighbor's yard?
    A thatched roof has to be steep, and steeper the coarser its stems, and in the gassho farmhouses of the snow country the steep roof holds a large loft. A farmhouse on our maps is 46 by 28 ft, so a roof pitched at 45 degrees rises about 14 ft above a 7 ft eaves wall, to a ridge of about 20-22 ft. The pitch and the height are a reconstruction: no page we read gives either.
  • A neighbor's grove is kept out of the strip too.
    A grove there would shade the yard in the same way.
  • Rows of homesteads leave room for the sun.
    Rows are set 92 ft apart, and a house (28 ft deep), its yard (about 26 ft) and 39 ft of sun come to about 93 ft; the rule is what keeps a neighbor out of the gap.
  • The clear strip is a rectangle, not the wedge the moving sun sweeps.
    It is measured across the yard's own width - a simplification, taken knowingly.
  • Real homestead lots may also have avoided the shadow by staggering houses east to west.
    That is a GUESS - and the maps are free to do it, since the rule forbids the shadow, not the neighbor.
  • The older hand-drawn maps are exempt from the sun rule.
    Every hand-drawn clustered map breaks it - 45 of 85 yards shaded at noon on Ueda, 31 of 70 on Hoshigaoka, 27 of 55 on Kikuta, 21 of 36 on Ubame, 19 of 44 on Hoshizora, 3 to 10 on each hamlet, a neighbor's wall commonly 2-8 ft off a yard's edge - while the provincial cities, with their farms spread loosely around them, are clear. Re-packing a finished map to satisfy a rule written after it would move every feature on it, so the rule binds a map when a generator draws it, and on the hand-drawn maps a shaded yard is drawn anyway.
  • A kitchen bed keeps the same 39 ft.
    A bed's autumn daikon is in the ground in the same months as the yard's work, under the same low sun, so a farmhouse shades a bed 39 ft off just as it shades a yard; the summer's high noon sun throws only 7-12 ft, so autumn decides. The rule holds whichever is placed first, the bed or the house, and a bed may stand on any of the four sides of its house: one the southeast side would put in shadow moves to the other flank.
  • A bed shaded from the east by a neighbor's grove is moved a little south.
    A neighbor's windward grove stands against that neighbor's west wall - just east of our bed, in the way of the morning sun. The village's shared copse keeps a clear lane to its east as well as its south, and once every farm's grove is drawn, an east-shaded bed is nudged south into open ground, so the trees fall to its northeast. It is a best effort, not a rule every bed meets: a bed hemmed in to the south by paddy, lane or neighbor stays where it is.
  • How far does a windbreak's afternoon shadow reach?
    At 38 degrees north in the months either side of summer, the sun at three in the afternoon stands 28 degrees high in the southwest, so a belt throws a shadow 1.9 times its height to the northeast: a belt of 10 m (~33 ft) casts about 63 ft, of which about 50 ft reaches east, and one of 15 m (~49 ft) about 95 ft, 75 ft of it east. In midsummer the sun is higher and the shadow shorter, so the months either side of summer decide.
  • No windbreak tree stands within 50 ft west or southwest of a yard or bed.
    The strip runs from a plot's north edge to 50 ft below its south edge, since the afternoon shadow comes from the southwest. It is kept as the belt is planted: a clump that would shade a plot is refused and set deeper, and where the wind puts the belt to the west, its near face is moved out by the strip. Only the windbreak is held back; the farm's own persimmon stands at the sunny east front of the house.
  • The belt is drawn at its working height of 10 m (~33 ft).
    A belt of 15 m (~49 ft), an untended stand's height, would need 75 ft of clear ground - so much that the belt stands off past the edge of the drawn map, which is set by the houses, yards, beds and roads: tried on Inashiro, the belt fell from 131 clumps to 38 and broke apart. At 50 ft it stands whole, 81 clumps. So the belt's inner face sets the map's edge, with the same margin every other feature keeps, and its depth runs off the edge.
  • The 50 ft is wider than the old manual's reach.
    The Qimin Yaoshu's reach, one tree's height, is about 33 ft for a 10 m (~33 ft) belt; the maps keep the wider 50 ft that the afternoon sun's geometry gives.
  • The belt's strip is a square, and its face keeps a small stand-off.
    The strip is a square, not the wedge the afternoon sun sweeps, as the yard's is a rectangle; and the small gap the belt's face keeps from the plots is an average plot's overhang - what guarantees the plots their sun is the refusal of any clump that would shade one.
  • A hand-drawn plan's kitchen bed must get six hours of direct sun in autumn.
    A kitchen bed is a bed of sun crops such as daikon, which want about six hours or more of direct sun; counting them in the autumn months is this project's reading, a GUESS, and so is counting an hour when at least half the bed is lit. The scripted maps keep the placement rules above; this rule checks what a person draws by hand.
  • A bed of half-shade crops needs three hours - a knob.
    A plan may declare a bed of half-shade crops instead, which does with three hours of direct sun or with light dappled through leaves; it then needs three hours in which nothing but a tree's leaves stands between it and the sun - a GUESS at how to count dappled light.
  • The shade is cast at the least height the record gives each thing.
    A building at the farmhouse ridge's 20 ft, a compound wall at the 1.6 m (~5 ft) of the surviving earth wall, a tree at the 10 m (~33 ft) of a working belt - so a bed the check fails is surely shaded, an untended stand being taller still.
