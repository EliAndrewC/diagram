How our maps draw chicken coops

On our maps a chicken coop stands on most farmsteads, about four in five, as a small square enclosure beside the house. The coop is drawn as imperial China kept it rather than as Japan did: our maps take a farm's chickens from the Chinese record, so the coop is historically accurate to China. The farmstead's other small buildings, and how many of each our maps draw, are at The farmstead's fixtures.

  • How many farmsteads have a coop?
    Between about seven and nine in ten, rolled once for each settlement around the 82 percent of farms with chickens that a survey of the 1920s counted. Accounts from before 1912 agree that chickens were already that common, so the share holds for the empire. How wide the band is around 82 percent is calibrated liberty.
  • Is the share higher in rice country?
    Not on our maps. The survey found chickens more often in east-central China than in the north, but no page we read gives the cause, so reading it as wet rice against dry fields would be a GUESS, and every settlement rolls in the same band.
  • Every farm that keeps chickens has a coop.
    The sixth-century Qimin Yaoshu tells the farmer to roost chickens in a cage on the ground, not in the trees, and our maps take a farm with chickens to keep them in one. That every such farm did is this project's reading: the accounts from before 1912 do not say whether the yard's chickens slept in a coop.
  • How big is a coop?
    5 by 5 ft, square after the late-Ming coop excavated at Zhengzhou, whose page gives no size. The size is a GUESS.
  • Where does a coop stand?
    Beside the house, by the yard. The sources say only that chickens were kept "in their yard", so the seat is a GUESS.
