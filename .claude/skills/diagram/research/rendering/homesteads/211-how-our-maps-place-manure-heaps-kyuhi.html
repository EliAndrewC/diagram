How our maps place manure heaps (kyuhi)

On a map, a manure heap is a mound 8 by 6 ft drawn just beyond its farmstead's privy, on 40 to 70 farmsteads in every 100. Its place, its size and its share are all GUESSES: no page we read says where in the yard the heap stood, and no count of any date sets how many farms kept one.

  • A hamlet keeps its manure either in heaps or in pits.
    Each hamlet rolls one form; the roll is at How our maps furnish a dike-pond hamlet, and where a pit stands, beside the privy or out at a field, at How our maps place privies (benjo).
  • A manure heap is drawn beyond the privy.
    Under the Han dynasty a Chinese latrine was customarily built over the pigsty and piped to a cesspool, the privy and the muck one cluster. That a Japanese farmstead kept them together too is this project's reading, a GUESS. So a heap stands just past its privy, on the side away from the house, a few feet nearer or farther from one farm to the next; on a farmstead with no privy it stands behind the house or at its flank.
  • Each heap is 8 by 6 ft.
    The size is a GUESS: no page we read measures a heap.
  • How many farmsteads keep a heap?
    Between 40 and 70 in every 100, the share rolled for each hamlet from its seed. It is a GUESS set below the manuals' call for a manure house on every farm, and no count of any date sets it. The one count, of 1972, finds 0.24 compost sheds a household on the farms whose main house was built by 1944; that the open heap was commoner than its shed is a GUESS with no count behind it.

The privy stands on its farmstead's sunny side, as nearly three in four did in one study of farm privies, and the heap beyond it goes the same way - sometimes toward a neighbor.

  • A few heaps stand nearer a neighbor's house than their own.
    Measured over the 13 maps the generator is tested on, 6 of 66 privies and heaps stood nearer a neighbor's drawn house than their own, and 4 of 66 by the distance between centers. The two differ because a fixture can be far from a house's center and still stand against its wall. The worst was Kashikawa's heap, 32.0 ft from its own farmhouse's wall and 8.4 ft from a neighbor's. A reader takes such a heap to be the nearer house's; the interactive map says whose it is on a click.

  • Why the other ways were declined.
    The fan search, which worked for the privy itself, seats more heaps but none of them better placed. The sort over every seat gains by pulling the heap back toward the house, which breaks the rule that it lies beyond the privy; a slightly clearer map is not worth a rule taken from the record. The sort among the seats beyond the privy cannot break that rule, and it came out the same as the map on every count.
  • What is left is the lie of the land.
    Where a privy stands on the sunny side and the neighbor lies that way too, every seat beyond it lies toward that neighbor, as it would in a real close-set cluster of farms. So a seat clearly nearer the heap's own house is preferred wherever the ground offers one, and is never forced.
