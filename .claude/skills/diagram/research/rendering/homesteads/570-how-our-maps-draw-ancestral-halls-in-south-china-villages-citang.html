How our maps draw ancestral halls in south-China villages (citang)

On our maps a south-China village's ancestral hall stands where the record puts it, at the front of the village facing its pond, in the form the record finds commonest.

  • Whether a village has a hall is a knob with three settings.
    One hall at the village's center, a row of halls across its front, or none: the three layouts a historian of these villages finds.
  • A hall stands in the front row, facing the pond.
    Where the village has a hall and a pond, the hall stands in the front row with its door to the water, and the drying floor between them is left open, as the half-moon pond's own forecourt is left open (How our maps draw and label the half-moon pond (banyuechi)).
  • Every hall is drawn three bays wide and three halls deep.
    That is the commonest form of a hall of standing. The two-hall form of a small or less wealthy lineage's hall is not drawn: taking the one form for every hall is a simplification where the record has two.
  • How big is a hall drawn?
    Its footprint in feet is a GUESS, built from the one measured depth, the 8.5 to 9 m (~28-30 ft) of the rear halls at Langtou; no page we read gives a common hall's whole footprint.
