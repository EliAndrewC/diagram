How our maps draw and place byres (umaya)

Our maps draw a byre as a small shed of about 16 by 11 ft, in the homestead of the household that keeps the beast: against its farmhouse, or standing on its own near it. Somewhat under half a settlement's households keep one. Which beast it holds, an ox, a water buffalo or a horse, is chosen per settlement, and the byre is drawn the same for each.

  • Which beast a settlement's farms keep is a knob.
    Ox and horse are two recorded forms, so which a settlement keeps is rolled from the map's seed. A village modeled on Japan rolls toward horses where it stands for the east, toward cattle where it stands for the west, and between them for the south; a paddy hamlet modeled on south China keeps a water buffalo or an ox, the beasts of the rice country. A setting where a daimyo hands surplus horses to the farms may put a horse in cattle country. Which part of the setting stands for which region of Japan is this project's decision, not a finding, and how far each roll leans is calibrated liberty.
  • The byre is drawn the same for every beast.
    So the roll changes what the byre's description says, not what the map draws.
  • A byre is a shed of about 16 by 11 ft.
    It is drawn as a single-bay, open-fronted shed under a plank roof, with a dark stall mouth, distinct from the solid gray storehouse and from the dwellings. That is close to the frame of the Edo stable at Maizuru, about 15 by 12 ft, and the registered building areas, 30 and 34 sq m (~323 and ~366 sq ft), say a stable could be nearly twice that under its roof.
  • A byre is kept small on purpose.
    A larger one starts to read as a European barn of many stalls, and there are none on these maps, because the beast of an East Asian farm lived under the farmhouse's roof or beside it. A byre is a shelter for a beast or two, kept well under the farmhouse's own 120 sq m (~1,292 sq ft).

The rule the map follows:

  • A byre stands in a homestead and belongs to the household there, whether it owns the beast or has it on loan.
  • Where it stands is a knob, rolled per settlement:
    • the inner stable, drawn against the farmhouse as a part of it, since a stall under the house's own roof cannot be seen from above - a map drawing convention;
    • the outer stable, a small shed of its own in the homestead, about 6 to 12 ft off the farmhouse wall - the distance a GUESS, since no page gives it;
    • rarely, a shed on the common ground among the houses, about one to every four or five households, reached by several of them - a GUESS, found on no page we read, kept only until the record finds it or rules it out.
  • The inner stable is the commonest roll, the outer stable next, and the shared shed the rarest - about six, three and one settlement in ten. How much commoner each is, is calibrated liberty, since no page we read counts them.
  • On the two household forms, somewhat under half the households keep a beast: the share is rolled per settlement between 35 and 50 in every 100, the top the half one province's farm households kept from the early 1700s and the bottom the fewer that kept one later. How many fewer no page gives, so the bottom of the range is calibrated liberty.
  • No byre is drawn alone beyond the last house at the village edge.
  • A byre may stand near a well, and never on the wellhead itself.
  • Why may a byre stand near a well?
    This project takes the beasts to have been watered at the household's well, and no old rule we found kept the two apart, so a map that held its byres away from its wells would be drawing a modern idea of hygiene, not a village of the time. A byre near a well stands near the well its household draws from.
  • A byre 38 ft from a shared well is no closer than a farmhouse kept its beast to its own.
    On the Kashikawa map a shared byre stands 38 ft from the village's wellhead. In a farmhouse whose stable and well were both under its roof, the beast and the water were the width of the house apart, about 20 to 40 ft, a GUESS: no page we read places the well or gives the house's width.
