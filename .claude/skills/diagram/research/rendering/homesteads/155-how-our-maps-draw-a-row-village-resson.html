How our maps draw a row village (resson)

A row village on our maps strings its farms along one line, the street, on one side of it or both, with the holding of each farm on the far side drawn behind it. Each settlement rolls which line its row follows, which sides its farms take and how they draw their water, since the record shows each of these in more than one form; the spacing, the street and the holdings follow the planned rows of the Musashino plateau, the rows the record measures.

  • On flood-prone ground, the row runs along the dike.
    Where the map's ground is low and reclaimed behind dikes, or its houses stand on a dike, the row follows the dike. That is accurate for land reclaimed from the sea, whose farmhouses stood in a line on its dike; drawing it on all flood-prone ground is this project's reading.
  • Elsewhere, the map rolls between a street laid first and the dry edge.
    A street laid first is drawn straight. The dry edge is drawn curving with the field's margin, which stands on the map for a levee, a dike or a fan's foot - this project's reading, since the map knows a field's margin and not a levee's line. Even odds between the two are a GUESS.
  • One side of the street or both is a knob.
    Each settlement rolls whether its farms stand on one side of the street with the field across it, or on both sides. Both forms are accurate; even odds between them are a GUESS.

Along the line, the farms and their street are spaced to the planned rows.

  • The farms stand one farmstead frame apart, lot against lot.
    A frame is a farm's own ground and grove and the lane's room between two groves, and a grove farm cannot stand on a narrower lot. The frame's width falls at or just past the top of the record's frontages of 9 to 40 ken (about 54 to 240 ft), a GUESS; where a paddy row, or one that grew by itself, would fall in that range is not known.
  • The street runs about 24 ft off the dry edge.
    Near enough that the field reads as the row's own, and clear enough that the street is not on a bund (a convention).
  • The street is drawn one step wider than the lanes off it, with a 6 ft tread.
    Both are a convention; Santome's real roads were 6 ken wide, about 36 ft.
  • The road the map is entered by carries the street on off the sheet.
    It stands for the road the row stands on (a convention).
  • The street is laid where the dry edge holds the most farms.
    It runs along the stretch of the dry edge, within a row's length of the settlement's center, that holds the most farms - this project's method.
  • A row the line cannot hold grows another street, never a second rank.
    Further streets run parallel to the first, each with its own row, as Santome's colony was laid on roads running lengthwise and crosswise (accurate for Santome). How many farms a line holds before the next street is the ground's; at most six streets is a GUESS.

Where the farms stand on both sides of the street, the far row - the farms across the street from the paddy - has its holding drawn behind each farm, in the form of the row's line, and the near row, between the paddy and the street, has none.

  • On a street laid first, the holding is a strip one lot wide and three lots deep.
    It follows the planned row's order of house lot, then field, back from the road. Santome's strips ran 375 ken deep, about 2,240 ft, in a dry-field colony; a paddy row borrows the form, not that depth, so three lots is a GUESS, and so is a strip of dry field behind a paddy row's houses at all. A sheet shows only the near end of a strip as long as Santome's.
  • The strip is cut in plots about 150 ft deep.
    That is a convention; the real plots' size was not found.
  • On the dry edge, the holding is one lot deep, compact and near the house.
    That is accurate for a dike row, and drawn the same on a levee or a fan's foot, this project's decision; the depth of one lot is a GUESS.
  • The holding is drawn as dry field.
    Which crop it carried is a GUESS.
  • How much of a drawn row's land is dry field?
    About half. Three villages of the Kanto plain held from about a fifth of their land in dry field to about half and more than half; on Kashikawa the far row's strips come to 28.7 acres of dry field against 27.5 of paddy, inside that range. Giving the far farms all of it and the near farms none is a GUESS.

Each farm keeps its grove, and the street may run on its windward side.

  • A row farm's grove stands on its windward sides, whichever side its street runs.
    The record shows two forms: the grove kept to the windward north and west with the front open south where the road ran on the north, the bamboo set south where a row of grove farms across the road already broke the wind; and the house turned to face its road (both accurate, for the Musashino farms and the Asuka villages). The map draws only the first, a full grove on the windward sides.
  • A near-row farm with its street to the north reaches it by a path round its grove.
    Where the gate of such a farm stood was not found, so the path is a GUESS.
  • Which side a levee or dike row's grove took is a GUESS.
    The groves the record places stand on planned rows.

A row draws its water in one of two ways, rolled per settlement.

  • Each row has either a well at every farm or wells shared along its street.
    The two are rolled at even odds, a GUESS: no page we read says whether a row's farms dug their own wells or shared them.
  • A shared well stands at the middle of each stretch of the row no longer than 1.6 times the 760 ft a farm may lie from its water.
    So no farm is farther than that from one.
  • Santome's deep shared wells are not carried to a row on lower, wetter ground.
    They answered a water-poor upland, so a lowland row is not drawn with them on Santome's account - this project's decision.

A farm standing alone, outside any row, has water of its own.

  • A dispersed farm draws its own water.
    On the Tonami plain, where the water table lay deep and a well was hard to dig, in many areas a small channel was led into a farm's grounds (accurate); that the other areas dug a well of their own is this record's reading, a GUESS (Does a dispersed hamlet's outlying farm have its own well?).
