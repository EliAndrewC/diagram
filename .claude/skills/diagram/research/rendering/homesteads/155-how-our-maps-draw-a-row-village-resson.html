How our maps draw a row village (resson)

A row village on our maps strings its farms along a line, its street, on one side of it or both, with the holding of each farm on the far side drawn behind it. Each settlement rolls which line its row follows, which sides its farms take and how they draw their water, since the record shows each of these in more than one form; the spacing, the street and the holdings follow the planned rows of the Musashino plateau, the rows the record measures.

  • On flood-prone ground, the row runs along the dike.
    Where the map's ground is low and reclaimed behind dikes, or its houses stand on a dike, the row always takes the dry edge, which stands there for the dike. That is accurate for land reclaimed from the sea, whose farmhouses stood in a line on its dike; drawing it on all flood-prone ground is this project's reading.
  • Elsewhere, the row follows either a street laid out before the farms or the dry edge of the paddy.
    A street laid out first, as on the planned rows, is drawn straight. A row along the dry edge curves with the field's margin, which stands on the map for a levee or a fan's foot - this project's reading, since the map knows a field's margin and not a levee's line. Even odds between the two are a GUESS.
  • The farms stand on one side of the street or on both, rolled per settlement.
    Each settlement rolls whether its farms stand on one side of the street with the field across it, or on both sides. Both forms are accurate; even odds between them are a GUESS.

Along the line, the farms and their street are spaced to the planned rows.

  • The street is laid where the dry edge holds the most farms.
    It runs along the stretch of the dry edge, within a row's length of the settlement's center, that holds the most farms - this project's method.
  • The farms stand one farmstead frame apart, lot against lot.
    A frame is a farm's own ground and grove and the lane's room between two groves, and a grove farm cannot stand on a narrower lot. The frame's width, about 240 ft or a little more, falls at or just past the top of the record's frontages of 9 to 40 ken (about 54 to 240 ft), a GUESS; where a paddy row, or one that grew by itself, would fall in that range is not known.
  • The street runs about 24 ft off the dry edge.
    Near enough that the field reads as the row's own, and clear enough that the street is not on a bund (a convention).
  • Each street is drawn 6 ft wide, wider than the 3 and 5 ft lanes off it.
    Both are a convention; Santome's real roads were 6 ken wide, about 36 ft.
  • The street runs on off the map as the road into it.
    It stands for the road the row stands on (a convention).
  • When a street has no room for more farms, the map lays another street beside it, never a second line of farms behind the first.
    Further streets run parallel to the first, each with its own row, as Santome's colony was laid on roads running lengthwise and crosswise (accurate for Santome). How many farms a street holds before the next is laid depends on the length of ground it has. At most six streets is a GUESS.

Where the farms stand on both sides of the street, the far row - the farms across the street from the paddy - has its holding drawn behind each farm, in the form of the row's line, and the near row, between the paddy and the street, has none. On a one-sided row, whose farms face the field across the street, no farm has a holding drawn.

  • On a street laid first, the holding is a strip one lot wide and three lots deep.
    A lot here is one frame, so the strip runs back about three times the frame's width. It follows the planned row's order of house lot, then field, back from the road. Santome's strips ran 375 ken deep, about 2,240 ft, in a dry-field colony; a paddy row borrows the form, not that depth, so three lots is a GUESS.
  • The strip is cut in plots about 150 ft deep.
    Each lot of its depth holds a few of them. That is a convention; the real plots' size was not found.
  • On the dry edge, the holding is one lot deep, compact and near the house.
    That is accurate for a dike row, and drawn the same on a levee or a fan's foot, this project's decision; the depth of one lot is a GUESS.
  • The holding is drawn as dry field.
    That it was dry field rather than paddy is a GUESS.
  • How much of a drawn row's land is dry field?
    About half. Three villages of the Kanto plain held from about a fifth of their land in dry field to about half and more than half; on our map of Kashikawa the far row's strips come to 28.7 acres of dry field against 27.5 of paddy, inside that range. That a paddy row's far farms have a dry-field strip at all, and hold all of it while the near farms hold none, is a GUESS.

Each farm keeps its grove, and the street may run on its windward side.

  • A row farm's grove stands on its windward sides, whichever side its street runs.
    The record shows two forms: the grove kept north of the house even where the road ran on the north, the bamboo set south where a row of grove farms across the road already broke the wind; and the house turned to face its road (both accurate, for the Musashino farms and the villages of Asuka). The map draws only the first, a full grove on the windward sides.
  • A farm whose grove stands between it and the street reaches the street by a path round the grove.
    This happens on the near row, the farms between the paddy and the street, where the street runs north of the farm on its windward side. Where the gate of such a farm stood was not found, so the path is a GUESS.
  • A levee or dike row's groves are drawn on the windward sides too.
    The groves the record places stand on planned rows. Which side a levee or dike row's grove took was not found, so carrying the windward grove to them is a GUESS.

A row draws its water in one of two ways, rolled per settlement.

  • Each row has either a well at every farm or wells shared along its street.
    The two are rolled at even odds, a GUESS: no page we read says whether a row's farms dug their own wells or shared them.
  • Shared wells are spaced so that no farm is more than 760 ft from one.
    760 ft is the farthest our maps let any farm lie from its water. Each well stands at the middle of a stretch of the row no longer than about 1,220 ft, 1.6 times that distance, so along the row no farm is more than about 610 ft from one.
  • A lowland row's shared wells are not modeled on Santome's deep ones.
    Santome's answered a water-poor upland, so a lowland row that is rolled shared wells has them on the even-odds GUESS above, not on Santome's account - this project's decision.

A farm standing alone, outside any row, has water of its own.

  • A dispersed farm draws its own water.
    On the Tonami plain, where the water table lay deep and a well was hard to dig, in many areas a small channel was led into a farm's grounds (accurate); that the other areas dug a well of their own is this record's reading, a GUESS (Does a dispersed hamlet's outlying farm have its own well?).
