How our maps draw threshing and drying yards (niwa)

Every farm's threshing and drying yard is drawn beside its house, about 900 sq ft as a rule, larger or smaller with the household. It is drawn part-covered in straw mats, as the harvest left it, and no neighbor's house or grove stands within about 40 ft to its south, because the yard's work was autumn work that needed the sun. A south-China hamlet dries its grain on one of two kinds of floor.

  • How big are a map's yards?
    Centered at 25 tsubo (~890 sq ft): the Okayama count of about fifty mats a farm, the middle of the forty to sixty mats remembered at Kitamoto. Two undated counts of remembered practice are a thin base, so 25 tsubo is this project's choice, fitted to those two counts.
  • Rice yards and dry-field yards are drawn the same size.
    A household growing rice and one growing unflooded crops such as barley get yards centered alike. The maps do not size a rice yard from how thick the grain was spread, since the only depth found is modern.
  • How do a map's yards vary?
    Lopsidedly: most yards near the center and a few much larger, drawn at random from a spread that matches both the house sizes a village register of 1771 gives at Kamikanai, where most houses sit near the middle and a few are much larger, and the forty to sixty mats remembered at Kitamoto, with a few yards past a hundred.
  • No yard is smaller than 8 tsubo (~280 sq ft).
    So a landless household's yard is small, but never missing.
  • A larger household has a larger yard.
    Almost always; a mismatch is possible, but rare.
  • Some settlements give every farm the same yard.
    Some planned colonies issued equal homesteads, as Santome did by 1696, so a settlement may be set to draw every yard alike - a knob.
  • Our maps draw fewer mats than a real yard held.
    A real yard held forty to sixty on an ordinary farm; the maps draw between a third and two thirds of those, because dozens of mats edge to edge would read as a textured floor, not as mats - a map drawing convention.
  • The mats are drawn in rows, a little askew.
    Each at the 3 by 6 ft of a mat woven at Nishidani, with a little bare ground around each, most laid a little askew as if by hand. The rows are a GUESS: no page we read says how the mats were laid.
  • How much clear ground does a yard keep to its south?
    39 ft. Threshing and drying follow the autumn harvest, and at 38 degrees north - the temperate middle of the empire our maps are set in - a 20 ft ridge casts, late in autumn, the shadows below. The drying day that matters is taken, as a GUESS, to run from nine to three, so no neighbor's farmhouse may stand within 39 ft to the south of a yard.

  • How tall is a farmhouse, and so how far does its shadow fall across a neighbor's yard?
    A thatched roof has to be steep, and steeper the coarser its stems, and in the gassho farmhouses of the snow country the steep roof holds a large loft. A farmhouse on our maps is 46 by 28 ft, so a roof pitched at 45 degrees rises about 14 ft above a 7 ft eaves wall, to a ridge of about 20-22 ft. The pitch and the height are a reconstruction: no page we read gives either.
  • A neighbor's grove is kept out of the strip too.
    A grove there would shade the yard in the same way.
  • Rows of homesteads leave room for the sun.
    Rows are set 92 ft apart, and a house (28 ft deep), its yard (about 26 ft) and 39 ft of sun come to about 93 ft; the rule is what keeps a neighbor out of the gap.
  • The clear strip is a rectangle, not the wedge the moving sun sweeps.
    It is measured across the yard's own width - a simplification, taken knowingly.
  • Real homestead lots may also have avoided the shadow by staggering houses east to west.
    That is a GUESS - and the maps are free to do it, since the rule forbids the shadow, not the neighbor.
  • The older hand-drawn maps are exempt from the sun rule.
    Every hand-drawn clustered map breaks it - 45 of 85 yards shaded at noon on Ueda, 31 of 70 on Hoshigaoka, 27 of 55 on Kikuta, 21 of 36 on Ubame, 19 of 44 on Hoshizora, 3 to 10 on each hamlet, a neighbor's wall commonly 2-8 ft off a yard's edge - while the provincial cities, with their farms spread loosely around them, are clear. Re-packing a finished map to satisfy a rule written after it would move every feature on it, so the rule binds a map when a generator draws it, and on the hand-drawn maps a shaded yard is drawn anyway.
  • South-China hamlets dried their grain on one of two kinds of floor, chosen per settlement.
    Each settlement takes one of the two forms recorded: each household's own floor before its house, drawn as a Japanese farm's yard is; or one shared floor at the front of the village, beside its pond or before its great house, with little or no yard at each house - a knob.
  • A shared floor is drawn as a plain rectangle.
    Most floors were tamped earth washed with cow dung, so its surface is not marked, and its size is a GUESS scaled to the households it serves.
