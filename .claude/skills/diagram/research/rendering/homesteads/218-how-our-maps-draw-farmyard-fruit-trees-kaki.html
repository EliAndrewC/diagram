How our maps draw farmyard fruit trees (kaki)

Our maps draw a persimmon beside most farmhouses, in front of the house or behind it, with a crown about 23 ft across. A farmstead may also have one to three other fruit trees near the house - persimmon, chestnut or plum, chosen per village - and some villages plant tea along their field banks. What the record says of all of these is at Fruit trees in the farmyard: persimmon, chestnut and plum (kaki); the other small fixtures of the yard are at How our maps draw the farmstead and what stands on it (yashiki).

  • How many homesteads have a persimmon?
    80 to 95 in every 100, the share chosen at random per hamlet within that range. The accounts put one in every dooryard; drawing a few homesteads without one is a GUESS.
  • The persimmon stands in front of the house, most often, or behind it.
    Both places are attested, and the front is the commoner, so how often each is drawn is chosen at random per hamlet, with the front the likelier; how much likelier is a GUESS. In front, it stands at the edge of the work yard where the farm has one, also a GUESS. No persimmon is set on the house's flank, which is attested nowhere.
  • How wide is the persimmon's crown?
    About 23 ft, a GUESS: it is a full-grown tree's spread, about 7 m (~23 ft), from a modern horticultural reference. No record from before 1868 gives an ordinary dooryard persimmon's crown, and the one Edo-period tree measured, the largest in Japan, spreads about 20 m (~66 ft) today, so 23 ft sits well inside the one measured extreme, with no premodern figure to set it by instead.
  • The persimmon is drawn a yellower green than the groves, with four fruit dots.
    The dots are a drawing convention that names the tree; they do not mark a season.

The other fruit trees and the bund tea follow the same record.

  • A village's fruit trees are persimmon, chestnut or plum, chosen per village.
    All three are attested at farmsteads, so each village's kind is chosen at random from the three, weighted toward chestnut in a cold northern district, where chestnut was among the commonest trees, and toward persimmon elsewhere. The weights are a GUESS.
  • How many fruit trees stand at a farmstead, and where?
    One to three, near the house: no source counts them, and the sources speak of them as part of the yard, not an orchard. Their count, their distance from the house and their side are a GUESS.
  • Some villages plant tea along their dry-field banks.
    Bund tea is attested, but not how many villages kept it, so whether a village has it along some of its dry-field banks is chosen at random per village.
  • No loquat is drawn at a farmstead, and no mulberry outside the cash-crop planting.
    A folk belief kept the loquat out of the homestead, and mulberry was a crop of the field. A village's mulberry is one of its cash crops on rice land, not a dooryard tree.

The rule the map follows:

  • The persimmon stands in the dooryard in front of the house, at the edge of the work yard where there is one, or behind the house, the front the likelier, with its crown about 23 ft across.
  • A farmstead given fruit trees stands one to three single crowns in its yard within about 30 ft of the house, most often on the side with the storehouse and shed rather than the grove's windward side.
  • A village given bund tea draws a single row of low tea bushes along some of its dry-field banks.
