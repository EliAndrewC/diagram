How our maps choose and draw a village's form

Each settlement our maps draw is one of three forms, rolled from the map's own seed: clustered, scattered, or a row. The clustered village was the general form in Japan and the scattered village a regional one, and no page we read gives a share for either, so the maps draw all three and let the map's seed choose.

  • How often is each form drawn?
    Half our settlements are clustered, three in ten scattered and two in ten rows. The clustered village was the general form, so these shares are deliberately flatter than the real ones, a calibrated liberty: each form is one the record shows existed, and drawn at their true rate the rarer two would all but vanish from the maps. The row is drawn least, because the Japanese rows whose layout is measured were planned colonies. The exact shares are arbitrary.
  • The place card says whether a map's form is an ordinary one.
    A cluster of houses with its fields around it is the general form; a scattered settlement is a regional one. The card is at How our maps draw and state each size of settlement.

The form decides which other rules apply, because a rule that is true of one form and false of another is two rules, not one rule with an exception.

  • Only a clustered village holds every farmhouse to a lane.
    A clustered village reaches each house by a lane (Is every farmhouse reached by a lane, and in what form?). A scattered hamlet has no lane network inside it at all: each farm stands in the middle of its own fields, the road out joins it to the world, and the field paths join the farms to one another. Drawing a web of lanes there would erase the one thing that makes the form legible at a glance.
  • In a row, the road comes before the houses.
    The row is the one form whose houses rightly front a way that was there before them: on planned new fields the road was laid first, and on a levee, a dike or a spring line the ground set the line (Along what a row ran, and what came first). So on the map the road in from outside and the spur to the field are laid before the settlement, and the lanes inside it are drawn from the houses once they are placed.
  • How long is a row?
    A row's farms front a street laid along the road, and the row runs as long as its households need at the frontage a planned row gave each farm (How wide was a farm in a row?). How long a row that grew by itself ran is a GUESS.
  • Where farms carry their own groves, no village shelter belt is drawn.
    Each Tonami farm stands in its own grove, so a scattered or a row settlement draws a grove at each farm, while a clustered village shelters behind one belt of trees at its edge (How our maps draw the shelter belt: its sides, its trees, and why it runs off the edge of the map). The scattered form's most visible mark on the map is the village belt that is not there.

A scattered farm fronts its own fields, so each house has to stand within reach of one.

  • A scattered farm is measured against the rice that is drawn, not the field the map claims.
    The irrigation network claims a smoothed outline drawn round its plots, but the planted plots stop where the drainage stops them. The gap between the two is a tail of empty ground, invisible on the map but real to any distance measured from the outline, so a farm hugging that tail would count as beside its field while standing well past the last rice. The claim is therefore sized to what is planted, and the farms are not nudged.
  • Two hand-drawn villages keep their tails of empty ground.
    Their tails run about 420 ft, but their houses are seated as one cluster and never ride the outline, so a tail there changes nothing, and reshaping two finished fields to satisfy a rule that protects nothing on them would be change for its own sake.

The rule the map follows: a scattered settlement needs roughly twice the room per farm, in the ground beside the field where a settlement's houses are seated, that a clustered one does - each homestead carries its own grove and keeps its own spacing, and the whole line is hugged to the field edge in single file - so a settlement declared at a clustered village's household count will not fit that many scattered farms in the same ground. Draw a scattered settlement on a roomier field, or declare fewer households; do not squeeze the homesteads to make them fit. This is a rule about fitting the drawing, not a finding about how far apart scattered farms stood.

A clustered village shares its wells among houses gathered round a center; a scattered farm has no center to share with, so it carries its own water.

  • Does a scattered farm draw a channel or a well?
    Either, rolled for each scattered settlement at even odds, a GUESS - no page we read says how many areas used each. The channel is historically accurate: on the Tonami plain many areas led a small channel into the house's grounds. That the other areas dug a well of their own is this project's reading, a GUESS. A shared well with a reach of 500 ft, the rule of towns and cities, is the wrong rule for this form.
  • The channel is led off the nearest irrigation ditch the map draws.
    That is a main or a branch ditch, never the drainage ditch, or the brook where the brook is nearer. Taking the nearest is a map drawing convention; the brook standing for the irrigation water the ditches are fed from is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • Can a later farm's channel be led off one already drawn?
    Yes, on our maps, so that a farm whose way to the ditch is blocked by a channel already drawn does not go without. Whether neighbors shared a channel no page we read says, so this is a GUESS.
  • Where does the channel end?
    In the dooryard, a step off the threshing yard, after running round the buildings, yards, gardens and the other farms' groves and through the farm's own grove into its grounds. Where in the dooryard it ended no page we read says, so that is a GUESS.
  • The channel's way back to the field is not drawn.
    This is a deliberate deviation: no page we read says where it left the lot.
  • The channel is drawn at the field channel's 2.5 ft bed.
    That is a map drawing convention, the narrowest water the map draws legibly; no page we read gives the real width of a channel into a farm's grounds.
  • Where does a scattered farm's well stand?
    In the dooryard, inside the farm's own lot, nearest its work yard and off its way in. Where on the lot the well stood no page we read says, so its place is a GUESS. Where the roll is a channel, a farm no channel can reach has its own well instead.

A village, larger than a hamlet, varies more. Two villages fed by water running the same way once came out nearly identical, down to where the headman lived, because one value was held fixed on every part of the layout. So a village rolls each part of its layout from the map's seed, each value with a rule about when it is possible, so that the result comes out coherent rather than merely various. The parts are rolled one by one rather than drawn as whole village "types": that gives the widest variety and lets a map fix one by hand and roll the rest.

  • Where does a village's cluster sit?
    On the higher, drier ground beside its paddy, as old settlements stood on the natural levee. Which corner of a valley that is depends on the valley, so the high margin on the windward side is one case among several; how common it was is a GUESS.
  • What shape does a village's cluster take?
    The shape of its dry ground: a lump at a hill's foot or on a terrace, a string along a levee, a crescent at a field's edge, or a split village on two flanking patches. The lump and the line along a levee or a road are the attested forms; the crescent and the split village are this project's extension of them, a GUESS.
  • What shape do a village's lanes take?
    A spine along a ridge, a T where a spur meets the field track, a Y where two approaches merge, a cross at a small market center, or a lane along the water in a village by a stream. These lane forms are a GUESS: no page we read describes the shapes a village's lanes took.
  • Where does a village's water come in?
    From wherever its catchment can deliver it above the fields: where a stream crosses the valley's edge, or from a pond in a corner, midway along a margin, or a chain of ponds. The stacked pond is a named form; that the inlet is not always the same corner, and that a small village might keep a chain of ponds rather than one reservoir, are a GUESS.
  • How are a village's plots divided?
    Three ways. Small irregular plots, laid out by hand, where the fields grew up by themselves - their shape a GUESS. Rectilinear blocks where a survey laid a grid over the land, as the jori grid did. Long strips where a planned new-field colony gave each farm its field and woodlot behind its house. A Chinese counterpart to the surveyed grid, the remains of an allotment system, is a GUESS.
  • The grain of a village's paddy may drift off the compass.
    Real valleys let it: even the surveyed grid, usually laid north-south and east-west, was not seldom tilted with the region or the ground.
  • The settlement's form makes the biggest difference between two villages.
    Two villages that share a water direction and differ only in their inner details still look like one place twice, because a cross of lanes against a spine of lanes is a detail, while a ribbon of houses against a round cluster is not. So the form - clustered, strung along a line, scattered, or built on the water - is weighed on its own and counts as the largest difference there is.

The rule the map follows (village): a village rolls its cluster's position and shape, its lane pattern, where its water comes in, its plot size and regularity, and the drift of its field grain, each from its own seed, and a map may fix any one of them by hand. A hamlet rolls none of these. A rolled value its own rule forbids is never drawn:

  • a lane along the water needs water running along the cluster;
  • a split village needs room for two hamlets to read apart, so a hamlet never splits;
  • a gridded plot pattern needs a surveyed field;
  • water entering where a stream crosses the valley's edge needs a stream, and water drawn from a pond - in a corner, midway along a margin, or a chain of ponds - needs a pond;
  • a water source below the fields it feeds is refused outright, because water does not run uphill.

How tightly a clustered village packs is a calibration against the drawn villages, not a historical figure: no page we read gives a clustered village's built share of its ground, its houses to the hectare or the size of its house plots. The 200 m (~656 ft) gap a 1956 survey used to tell one settlement from the next is a modern counting rule, so the maps do not hold their villages to it.

  • A clustered village is drawn as a compact fabric, not a wide hollow arc.
    Which of the two the map draws depends not on the shape the houses are seeded in but on its width. The program that seats them pulls every homestead in to hug the paddy edge and then packs it along that edge, so a wide scatter of starting points is strung out by that pull while a narrow, deep one packs into a round cluster. On one drawn village, built coverage was 0.20 from a wide seed and 0.31 from a narrow one, and the cluster's span about 1,900 ft against about 1,070 ft.
  • How much of a village's ground does the map build on?
    Coverage here means how much of the ground inside the houses' own outline is built - houses, gardens, work yards and homestead groves. The hollow arc measures 0.20; the rolled villages that look right measure 0.28 to 0.31; the tightly hand-placed villages measure about 0.40. A floor of 0.25 sits clear below the healthy range and above the hollow arc: it catches the arc without demanding hand-placed density.
  • A rolled village tops out near 0.31.
    Hugging and packing along an edge is looser than a person seating houses one at a time, and that is accepted: a few back-row houses still stand about 700 ft from the crops, which reads as the back of a compact village rather than as a stranded arc.

The rule the map follows (village): a clustered village of a dozen houses or more keeps at least a quarter of the ground inside its own outline built. A hamlet is rightly loose and is not held to it, and neither is a cluster too small for the measure to mean anything.

  • The measure cannot see a village thin when its grove spreads.
    It counts homestead groves as built ground, and the grove fills the smallest rectangle that encloses the houses, so the built ground and the outline move together, and a clump of trees that would fall where a house stands is moved to the next gap instead of being left out. A cluster that spreads therefore grows its grove into the gaps it opened, and its coverage can rise while its houses thin. On the hamlets measured on 2026-09-13, of two clusters of the same household count, the one whose houses had spread carries 246 clumps of grove against the other's 22, its coverage is 0.339 against 0.294, and its houses, gardens and yards alone cover 0.130 against 0.279, while its outline is more than twice as large.
  • The 0.25 floor was set on villages whose groves had not spread.
    So on a village whose grove has spread it can pass a fabric that has thinned. No village is drawn today, and hamlets are exempt, so nothing now drawn turns on it.
