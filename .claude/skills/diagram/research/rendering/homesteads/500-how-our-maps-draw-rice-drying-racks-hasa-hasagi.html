How our maps draw rice-drying racks (hasa, hasagi)

On our maps a rice-drying rack stands by every farmhouse where the settlement's harvest weather is changeable, along one side of its yard, and by none where the weather is settled. Racks out on the paddies stood only for the month or so after the harvest, and are drawn only on a map of that season.

  • A settlement's harvest weather decides whether its houses have racks, never a roll.
    The drying method has four attested forms, and which one a country used followed its weather and ground over whole regions, not a village's choice, so the method is not rolled village by village. It follows the harvest weather the settlement's own description states, and neighboring settlements in one climate draw the same.
  • Where the harvest weather is changeable, every farmstead gathers its rack by the house.
    Racks were built together near the house on the changeable San'in coast, so that the threshing could be done at home.
  • Where it is settled, no rack is drawn at the house.
    That settled weather kept the racks on the fields is this project's reading: the survey names the gathered racks only for the changeable coast, and racks were mostly built on the fields.
  • A settlement that states nothing takes settled weather.
    The racks gathered by the house are one region's form, so a settlement whose weather is not stated takes the other - this project's decision, as the regional wind is.
  • Which side of the yard does a rack take?
    Along one side, in the half nearest the house where that leaves it room, and never in the yard's southern half, so that it shades the ground north of it and never the drying floor, which needs the sun from the south (How our maps keep yards and gardens in the sun). No page we read says which side of the house a rack stood on, so this is a GUESS.
  • How long is a rack by the house?
    From the yard's edge by the house to its middle, or along the whole side where the near half has no room - a GUESS.
  • A rack is drawn thicker than its poles, so that it reads.
    Its poles at their real width would be too thin to see at the map's scale - a map drawing convention.
  • A map of the month after the harvest would draw racks on the reaped paddies.
    The sheaves hung usually twenty to thirty days, so a map of any other season draws no rack on the fields. One of that month would draw them as thin lines on the reaped paddies and along their bunds, up to about 330 ft long, the longest single-tier rack recorded.
  • How much rack did a household put up?
    A GUESS until the record finds a figure: no page we read gives one.
  • A row of drying trees along a lane is not drawn.
    Such a row can be a modern arrangement: one was planted in 1943-1945, when a district's paddies were consolidated.
