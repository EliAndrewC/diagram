How our maps draw firewood sheds (kigoya)

Every farm's firewood on a map is kept in a wood shed of its own, a building 24 by 12 ft standing a short step off its house, on about four farmsteads in ten. The share and the size are a calibration against one village's count from the end of the Edo period.

  • A wood shed stands on 35 to 45 farmsteads in every 100.
    The share is rolled for each hamlet from its seed, around Kakimochi's 6 households of 16.
  • The larger houses get theirs first.
    In the same town history the houses with the most outbuildings had the largest main houses; giving the wood shed to the larger houses first is this project's reading of that.
  • Every shed is drawn 24 by 12 ft.
    That is 4 by 2 ken, the commonest size in the Kakimochi count, three of its seven sheds; the smaller sheds of the count, down to about 9 by 9 ft, are not drawn.
  • Where does the shed stand?
    Behind the house or beside it, about 6 ft (a ken) off a wall, and never out across the yard in front. This is a GUESS: no page we read says where on the plot a wood shed stood.
  • The maps draw no open stack and no stack along the windbreak.
    The open stack under the eaves is found only on a present-day page, and the stack along a farm's windbreak grove only in records of the twentieth century and today, with no date for the custom. The maps draw what is recorded before modern times, so the firewood is drawn in its shed.
