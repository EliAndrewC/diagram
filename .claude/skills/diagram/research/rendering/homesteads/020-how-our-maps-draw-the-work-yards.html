How our maps draw the work yards

Each farm's yard is drawn beside its house at a size rolled for that household, covered in straw mats as the harvest leaves it, and kept clear of a neighbor's shadow.

  • How big is a map's yard?
    Centered at 25 tsubo (~890 sq ft): the Okayama count of about fifty mats a farm, the middle of the Kitamoto band. Rice yards and dry-field yards are centered the same. The maps do not size a rice yard from how thick the grain was spread, since the only depth found is modern. Two undated counts of remembered practice are a thin base, so the center is a calibration and this project's choice.
  • How do a map's yards vary?
    Lopsidedly, as the old registers do: most yards near the center and a few much larger, rolled from a spread that matches both the Kamikanai houses and Kitamoto's band of mats. No yard is smaller than 8 tsubo (~280 sq ft), so a landless household's yard is small but never missing. A yard follows its household - a large household almost always has a large yard, and a mismatch is possible but rare.
  • Some settlements give every farm the same yard.
    Planned colonies issued equal homesteads, as at Santome in 1696, so a settlement may be set to draw every yard alike - a knob.
  • Our maps draw fewer mats than a real yard held.
    Between a third and two thirds of the mats that covered the yard, each at its real 3 by 6 ft, in rows with a little bare ground around each, most laid a little askew as if by hand. Dozens of mats edge to edge would read as a textured floor, not as mats - a map drawing convention. The real count was forty to sixty on an ordinary farm. The rows are a GUESS: no page we read says how the mats were laid.
  • How tall is a farmhouse, for its shadow?
    A thatched roof has to be steep, and steeper the coarser its stems, which is why a farmhouse carries a large loft. Our farmhouse is 46 x 28 ft, so a roof pitched at 45 degrees rises about 14 ft above a 7 ft eaves wall, to a ridge of about 20-22 ft. The pitch and the height are a reconstruction: no page we read gives either.
  • How much clear ground does a yard keep to its south?
    39 ft. Threshing and drying are autumn work, and at 38 degrees north in the tenth month a 20 ft ridge casts the shadows below; the drying day that matters runs from nine to three, so no neighbor's farmhouse may stand within 39 ft to the south of a yard, and inside about 21 ft the yard would be shaded even at noon. A neighbor's grove is kept out of the same strip, for the same reason.

  • Rows of homesteads leave room for the sun.
    Rows are pitched 92 ft apart, and a house (28 ft deep), its yard (about 26 ft) and 39 ft of sun come to about 93 ft; the rule is what keeps a neighbor out of the gap.
  • Two simplifications, taken knowingly.
    The clear strip is a rectangle across the yard's own width, not the wedge a moving sun sweeps through the day. And real homestead lots also avoided the shadow by setting houses off from one another east to west, rather than by spacing rows - which the maps are free to do, since the rule forbids the shadow, not the neighbor.
  • The older hand-drawn maps are exempt.
    Every hand-drawn clustered map breaks the rule - 45 of 85 yards shaded at noon on Ueda, 31 of 70 on Hoshigaoka, 27 of 55 on Kikuta, 21 of 36 on Ubame, 19 of 44 on Hoshizora, 3 to 10 on each hamlet - while the provincial cities, with their loose farm belts, are clear. Re-packing a finished map to satisfy a rule written after it would move every feature on it, so the rule binds a map when a generator draws it, and on the hand-drawn maps a shaded yard is drawn anyway.
  • A south-China hamlet's drying floor is a knob.
    Each settlement takes one of the two forms recorded: each household's own floor before its house, drawn as a Japanese farm's yard is; or one shared floor at the front of the village, beside its pond or before its great house, with little or no yard at each house. A shared floor is drawn as a plain rectangle, since most floors were tamped earth washed with cow dung, and its size is a GUESS scaled to the households it serves.
