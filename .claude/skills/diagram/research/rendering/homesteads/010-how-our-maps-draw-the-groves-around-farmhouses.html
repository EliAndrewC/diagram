How our maps draw the groves around farmhouses

Each farm's grove is drawn tree by tree at its real size; which way the groves face, how deep they run and how many sides of the house they take are chosen for each map from what the record shows.

  • Our maps draw every canopy tree.
    Each crown is drawn at its real size on real spacing - a mean crown radius of 8.5 ft, about 13 ft from one trunk to the next, no crown seated under another (Forest density and crown size) - so each grove appears as the dense stand of dozens of trees the survey counted. The Edo record attests a dense stand. Its tree count on our maps is a GUESS set from the 1987 survey.
  • Bamboo is drawn by a convention.
    Its stems are too small to see at this scale (How our maps draw bamboo, when one culm is too small to see).
  • Which way do a map's groves face?
    Since the windward side changes with the region, each map chooses it as a knob. The maps put the deep stands on the northwest by default, as on the Sendai plain; a map with a Tonami-like wind puts it on the south and west faces. That the Tonami plain's damaging wind comes from the south and west is a GUESS read off its planting, since no page read names the wind's quarter.
  • How deep are the groves on their windward sides?
    Knowing how many trees stood in a grove still leaves how they were arranged - a single line along the house, or a deep block. No page read gives a grove's depth, so the maps draw each windward stand 1.57 house depths deep - about 44 ft at a farmhouse 28 ft deep, some two and a half crowns - sized so each whole grove covers about six times the ground its house stands on. That depth and that six-to-one ratio are a GUESS, calibrated on the tree counts and crown sizes above.
  • How many sides do a map's groves take?
    Two, three or four. We know of farms whose grove took each of those shapes: two sides on the Sendai plain, three on the Tonami plain, all four on the Izumo plain before the Meiji era. How often each occurred is on no page read. The maps make two-sided groves the commonest, then three-sided, then four-sided - this project's choice: two sides are what the Sendai domain's first lord urged, and the Izumo ring itself gave way to a two-sided hook after the Meiji era. The shares are in the rule below.

The rule the map follows:

  • One choice per settlement. A settlement's farmstead groves take their sides from one random choice, the same at every farm, because the pages read report the shape region by region.
  • The shares.
    • Two sides half the time, three sides three times in ten, all four sides twice in ten.
    • On flood-prone ground - fields of reclaimed low ground behind dikes, or houses on a dike - four sides rise to four times in ten and the other two keep their 5 : 3 ratio (two sides 37.5%, three sides 22.5%), because the Izumo ring's own stated cause was flood. A map may declare its ground either way.
    • Every one of these shares is a GUESS: no page read counts how many farmsteads took each shape, on any ground or in any period.
  • Which sides.
    • Two sides are the windward pair, as the Sendai domain's first lord urged.
    • Three leave open only the front, as on the Tonami plain. The maps put that front on the side away from the wind, where the yard and the way in are, as the Tonami plan does - this project's decision.
    • Four close the ring, as on the Izumo plain.
  • Deep sides and thin sides.
    • The windward sides are the deep stand; the other planted sides are a thinner band of lesser trees, as on the Tonami plain.
    • That band is one tree deep: 17 ft, two mean crown radii (Forest density and crown size). Carrying the Tonami pattern round a full ring is a GUESS, and so is the band's depth, since no page read gives it.
  • The way in through a ring. A grove on all four sides is broken once, at the middle of its front, for a way in about 36 ft wide. The break is a physical necessity, since the farm must be reached; its place at the middle of the front and its width are a GUESS, since no old page read gives where in a ring its opening stood or how wide it was. The width is set by the lane that must pass it: the maps lay a lane with a footpath's clearance and some working room kept off the trees on each side, about 22 ft of the opening, and at 12 ft, two treads, no lane can be laid through.
  • A service strip behind the house. The windward stand stands about 24 ft off the house, off its back wall and off its windward end: a strip where the wood shed stands a step off the wall and the bath room is joined at the stable end. Hard against the walls, the stand would take those places and put the bath room inside the grove. The strip is sized to the shed, 24 by 12 ft, with a step and a wall gap to spare - a GUESS, since no page read says where on the plot a wood shed stood.
  • Room for a lane between two farms. Two neighbors' groves stand at least 32 ft apart: packed tighter, the groves wall off the fronts and no lane can reach a door. The gap is a physical necessity, since a farm between two others must still be reached, and its 32 ft is a GUESS, since no page read gives the least gap between two farms' groves.
  • Not the village's shelter belt. The shelter belt is a different planting, and it is not chosen this way: it stays on one or two windward sides (How our maps draw the shelter belt: its sides, its trees, and why it runs off the edge of the map).
