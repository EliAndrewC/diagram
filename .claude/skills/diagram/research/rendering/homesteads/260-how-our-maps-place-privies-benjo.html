How our maps place privies (benjo)

Our maps draw a privy on most farmsteads, a small building of its own near the house, at one of the sizes one village's count of 1885 gives. About three in four are sought first on the house's sunny side, south to southeast, where a survey of farm privies found most of them stood, though the work yard leaves fewer than half there; the rest, and those that find no room there, stand under the eaves by the stable, in the yard, in the front yard or inside the barn, the four places the record names. Where a farmstead keeps a night-soil pit, it stands beside the privy or out at the edge of a field or a road.

  • A privy stands on 85 to 95 farmsteads in 100, the share rolled per hamlet.
    A 1959 survey of privies in farm villages found an outdoor privy at more than nine households in ten, a register of 1824 from Hasuda, in the Edo period, at most houses, and the 1885 count at the village of Kakimochi at all 16, and the range sits among those counts. Our maps draw what is attested before modern times, and the outdoor privy is.
  • Each privy takes one of the sixteen sizes of the Kakimochi count, each as likely as the next.
    They run from 5 by 5 feet to 27 by 15 feet, most of them 18 by 12 feet or smaller. The count is one village's, read back by its historian to the last years of the shogunate, so the sizes are a calibration against one table, not a figure any source gives as usual for a region.
  • A privy not seated on the sunny side stands in one of the four places the record names, rolled per house.
    They are under the eaves by the stable, a separate outhouse in the yard, the front yard, and inside the barn. No source says how often each was used, so the four take base weights of 35, 30, 20 and 15 in 100, which each hamlet scales up or down by as much as half from its seed. The weights are a GUESS.
  • An outhouse in the yard stands a ken behind the house.
    No page says where in the yard it stood, so the spot behind the house, a ken (about 6 ft) off the back wall, is a GUESS.
  • A privy in the front yard stands 8 ft out from the front wall.
    No page says how far out from the house it stood, so the 8 ft is a GUESS.
  • A privy inside the barn is drawn against the barn's outer wall.
    A tub under the barn floor cannot be seen from above, so the maps draw the privy against the barn's wall instead - a map drawing convention.
  • About three privies in four are sought first on the house's sunny side.
    Each house rolls against 72.7%, the share of farm privies Wang and Ochiai found south or southeast of the house, taken as given rather than rounded. A privy that finds no clear ground there falls back to the four places the record names (under the stable's eaves, the yard, the front yard, the barn), weighted as its hamlet weights them.
  • The privy is not set downwind of the house.
    No source we read gives a wind rule for a privy: the one survey that measures where privies stood places them by the sun.
  • The sunny side is searched from the house outward, nearest first.
    The search walks the bearings from southeast round to south, at distances stepping out from 18 ft, and takes the first spot clear of the yard, the beds and the other buildings.
  • The work yard takes a slice of the sunny side, not all of it.
    The farmsteads Wang and Ochiai surveyed, 72.7% of whose privies stood south or southeast of the house, had yards too: open ground in front of the entrance, where rice was threshed and vegetables dried. Measured on the Sawada map, one of the five sample maps this project tests its rules on, every one of its 19 houses had free ground on the sunny side, 49 to 151 clear spots each, the nearest 24 to 32 ft out.
  • A privy on the sunny side stands no more than 48 ft from its house.
    Wang and Ochiai's survey gives a direction and no distance, and none of the pages that name the four attested places gives a distance either, so the reach is this project's choice. It was measured at three reaches over five sample maps:

  • Beyond 48 ft, privies stray out of their own farmsteads toward a neighbor's house.
    Past that reach a privy walks beyond its own work yard and, in a cluster where the next farmhouse is 50 ft away, out of its own farmstead: at 72 ft, 15 of 86 privies and manure pits stood nearer another house than the one they serve, against none of 52 when every privy kept to the four attested places. A reader takes a privy or a pit to belong to the nearest house, so the reach is set by what the map lets a reader see, not by a figure from the record.
  • How often does a privy end up on the sunny side?
    About 46 in 100, against the 72.7% the houses roll. The gap is the work yard: the near sunny side is the threshing floor, and a privy may not stand on it. The reach is not widened until the number comes out right, which would fit the map to the figure rather than to the place.
  • A night-soil pit stands beside its privy, or out at a field or a road.
    The 1959 survey found both, the field pit at 2 households of 83 in one village and 15 of 18 in another, so each hamlet rolls its own share at the fields between 2 and 85 in 100, and the rest stand beside their privies. A field pit is sought at the edge of the household's nearest paddy or road within 160 ft, a reach that is a GUESS.
  • A manure heap is drawn beyond the privy.
    Under the Han dynasty a Chinese latrine was customarily built over the pigsty and piped to a cesspool, the privy and the muck one cluster.
