How our maps draw pigs and ducks in south-China rice villages

A plain paddy hamlet on our maps keeps pigs, as the seventeenth-century farm manual has a rice farm do, on most of its farms, but draws no ducks at its houses: the ducks of the rice country fed out on the reaped fields after the harvest, which a summer map does not show.

The rule the map follows: on a plain paddy hamlet, about two households in three, the share varying from hamlet to hamlet, have a small pig sty against the farmhouse or one of its outbuildings, on the household's own plot; no duck pen is drawn.

  • How many households keep a sty?
    About two in three, the share rolled for each hamlet around that figure. It is the 1920s survey's share for the rice-growing east-central provinces, nearly two farms in three, read back into the late imperial village as a calibration against the only count there is, not as a premodern figure: no count from before 1912 was found.
  • The sty stands against the farmhouse or an outbuilding - a GUESS.
    No page we read says where an ordinary paddy farm kept its sty; the one house type whose sties are placed, the Hakka enclosed house, kept them inside its walls, in its rear ring of rooms. The placement holds until the record finds how an ordinary farm placed it.
  • No duck pen is drawn in the yard.
    The ducks of the rice country are recorded out on the reaped fields, gleaning the fallen grain, and no page we read says whether a rice farm also penned ducks at the house.
