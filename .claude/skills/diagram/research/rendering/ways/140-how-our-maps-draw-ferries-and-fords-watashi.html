How our maps draw ferries and fords (watashi)

Where a road meets a river too wide to bridge, our maps end it at a ferry landing on each bank, or, on any road but the Imperial road, run it down into a ford. A stream, and any river narrow enough to bridge, is bridged instead, by the rules of How our maps place and draw road bridges (hashi).

  • A ferry belongs only where a road meets a river too wide to bridge.
    On the Tokaido only the great rivers went unbridged, and the lesser ones were bridged.
  • Ferry or ford is a knob, rolled per map.
    Where a village's, a town's or a city's road meets such a river, the crossing is a ferry, with a landing on each bank, or a ford, the road running down into the shallows on both sides. Both were how the Tokaido crossed its great rivers.
  • The Imperial road never fords.
    The setting's canon names bridges over major rivers and ferry stations among the Imperial road's engineered works, and this project reads that as the whole of its crossings.
  • A landing is a cleared stretch of bank where the road reaches the water, with boats drawn up at it.
    On one bank stand a river office, a notice board and a boatmen's hut, as at the Rokugo ferry's landing on the Kawasaki bank.
  • A highway ferry keeps up to about 14 boats.
    Its foot boats are about 30 ft long and its cargo boats about 43 by 9 ft, the Rokugo ferry's count and the Banyu ferry's boats.
  • How many boats does a village's ferry keep? Two or three, a GUESS.
    No page we read says how many boats a ferry kept by one village held.
