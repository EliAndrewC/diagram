How our maps draw town streets and alleys

Our maps draw a town's own streets as earth, its main street 24 ft wide and its back alleys 10 ft, and keep the Imperial road through it at the 30 ft it has on every map. A town's plan, the drain down its main street and the Imperial road's surface are each one of several forms the record shows, rolled per settlement from the map's seed, and the road is to bend twice at right angles at each end of the built street, as it did at a post town.

  • A town's main street is drawn 24 ft wide.
    That is the top of the 12 to 24 ft a post town's street ran, and inside the 13 to 26 ft one popular account gives an ordinary Chinese city's main street. Both ends of that range are as well attested, and where a town's street fell in it is a matter of degree, not a choice between forms, so the maps take one figure in it.
  • A side lane may be drawn narrower than the main street, down to about 12 ft.
    Its width is a GUESS: no page we read gives a side lane a width, only that the back alley was narrower still.
  • A back alley is drawn 10 ft wide, a map drawing convention.
    The real alley between the house rows ran about 3 to 6 ft; at 10 ft it reads as a way between the rows at a town's scale.
  • The Imperial road through a town stays 30 ft wide.
    It is drawn at that width on every map, as How our maps draw and label the highway and How our maps lay out a city's streets and blocks set out.

Underfoot, the streets follow what the record found.

  • A town's own streets are drawn as earth.
    Even Edo's streets were never fully surfaced, and were deep in mud after a little rain. A village lane is drawn the same way (How our maps draw village lanes).
  • The Imperial road's stretch through a town is earth or stone slab - a knob.
    Both are attested for an official road: earth in Edo, bluestone slabs where a Chinese official road ran through a post station. In the setting the Imperial roads' upkeep pays for stone-cutting, so stone is one of the two forms rolled per settlement.
  • A town's main street takes one of three drain forms - a knob.
    Each town rolls one:
    • an open channel down the middle of the street, as in a post town;
    • an open ditch about 9 in wide along each edge at the house fronts, as in Edo;
    • no visible channel at all, where the drain runs covered under stone lids, as in the Chinese city of Yichang.
  • A middle channel is drawn at a width of our own choosing.
    That width is a GUESS: no page we read gives it.
  • A back alley's ditch is not drawn.
    It ran under boards down the alley's middle, 7 to 8 in wide, and is too small to show at a town's scale.

A town's plan and the way its road comes in are drawn from the post town and the Chinese market town.

  • A town's plan is rolled among the forms set out in How our maps lay out a town's streets.
    A post town's single main street, built up on both sides of the road, is one of them. Two main streets meeting in a T, as at the Chinese market town of Zhouzhuang, is another.
  • The road through a town is to bend twice at right angles at each end of its built street.
    A post town's highway was bent into a crank at both its ends, twice at right angles, so that an enemy would find it hard to get in. That a town other than a post town bends its road too is a GUESS. No generator bends a town's road yet - the one dogleg our maps draw is the walled box outside a castle's main gate (masugata) - so the bend is the rule for when the town tier is generated. A city is drawn the other way: there the Imperial road runs straight from gate to gate as a deliberate deviation.
  • An earth bank or stone wall may mark the end of the built street.
    The ends of a post town on the Five Highways were marked by a mitsuke of stone walls or earth banks.
  • Side lanes are laid only where houses need them.
    Their number is a GUESS: no page we read counts the side lanes off a town's street, so each is set by the houses that need it, as How our maps lay out a town's streets requires of every street.

A packed commoner quarter, in a town or a city, is threaded with back alleys rather than streets.

  • A packed commoner quarter of any size carries narrow back alleys between its house rows.
    One runs every few rows of the quarter's depth. How many rows apart is a map drawing convention, chosen so the quarter reads as fabric at map scale, not a figure from a record.
  • A back alley is drawn as a path, and captioned as a path if at all - never as a street.
    It served only the houses on it: almost no one used it but those connected with the buildings along it, and a gate at its mouth was shut at dusk.
  • Every town's warren has at least one lane, alley or street into it, walled or not.
    Without one it would hang straight off the Imperial road with no way into it, and read as a scatter of identical boxes rather than as fabric.
