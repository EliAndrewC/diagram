How our maps draw moving goods: cart lanes and boat landings (kashi)

Freight on our maps goes by water where there is water to carry it, as Edo Japan's tax rice went by river, and by cart where there is not, since this setting puts carts on its roads. A settlement on a navigable river or the coast has a boat landing; a compound that ships or receives bulk goods has a cart gate, a cart yard and a cart lane; and no transport canal is drawn unless the map is set where the setting has one.

  • A settlement on navigable water has a boat landing or jetty as its main freight link.
    The river or the coast is its main way to the world, as the river was for the tax rice of Edo Japan. A settlement with no navigable water beside it moves its freight by its land road, and that road is its main link.
  • A transport canal is never drawn because a settlement needs to move goods.
    In the setting a canal is a domain's work, dug only where an inland river is worth canalizing, as the Lion's canal from Toshi Ranbo to the Drowned Merchant River is. So a map draws a canal only in Lion lands, or where it is asked to carry one.
  • A compound that ships or receives bulk goods has a cart gate, a cart yard and a cart lane.
    Rice bales and charcoal come and go by cart, and the road outside the gate carries carts. This is a deviation: Edo Japan kept its carts to the towns, and the setting puts wagons and carts on its roads, a charcoal merchant's cartload among them.
  • How wide is a cart lane?
    About 6 ft, a GUESS. The Edo hand cart's bed was 8 shaku long and 2 shaku 5 sun wide, about 8 by 2.5 ft, but how much wider its wheels ran is on no page we read. How wide the road is at a compound's gate is at How wide is the road at a compound's gate?
  • An Imperial road is drawn as a kept road, wider than a village track.
    The setting's Imperial roads run some 50,000 miles, with about 5,000 staffed waystations and no tolls, tolls having been outlawed by an Emperor, and they are repaired by corvee labor from the counties along them. So where a map shows one it may read as a maintained road of earth or gravel, wider than the village track; its width and surface are a map drawing convention.
  • A settlement's own lanes, and the road joining it to the network, stay humble tracks.
    The setting's Imperial network is sparse, within a domain a through-route and the odd spur, so most settlements stand off it, and this project draws their own ways as the village lanes of the history (Village lanes).
