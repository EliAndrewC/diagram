How our maps mark where a village begins

Every lane or road that leaves a village for the fields or the next settlement has a boundary god's stone beside it where it passes the last house, with a small trodden patch of open ground beside the stone. Some villages add a rope across the road, a straw figure or serpent, or a great enoki tree, each picked at random for each village. No line between villages is drawn. A town on the Imperial road marks both ends of its street with a pair of earth banks, a crank in the road and a tall post.

  • A village has one entrance for every road leading out.
    Its people felt the village's edge to be where each road from outside reached the houses, so there are as many entrances as roads out, and each takes a boundary god's stone at the roadside, where the dosojin usually stood (Village boundaries and their markers).
  • How big is the open patch beside the stone?
    Small, and its size is a GUESS. The entrance was a gathering place that served the village as a plaza, so a patch of trodden ground is drawn beside the stone, but no page we read gives its size.
  • What stands beside the boundary god is left to chance.
    A village might cut the road with a rope or a straw serpent, or set straw figures at its boundary, so the form is a knob rolled per village among the forms on record. About half the villages have nothing more, about a quarter a rope stretched across the road there, and about a quarter a straw figure or serpent beside the stone. The shares are a GUESS: the record says which regions favored which form, not how many villages kept each.
  • About one village in two has a great enoki beside its boundary god.
    A village kept an enoki by the boundary god or a small shrine at its entrance, but no page we read says how many did, so the tree is a knob rolled for about one village in two, a GUESS. It is drawn as one broad crown, larger than a farmstead's trees, beside the stone or small shrine where the lane leaves the settlement.
  • No great tree is drawn at a crossroads or a well.
    No page we read puts one there, so a tree there would be a GUESS, and none is drawn.
  • No line between villages is drawn.
    The line was fixed on the survey but seldom marked on the ground or felt as the village's edge, and these maps draw no administrative lines, a map drawing convention.
  • Where an Imperial road crosses a village's map, it may carry one boundary post.
    A highway's crossing of the line between villages was posted, so one tall wooden post may stand beside the road where it leaves the village's own fields for the next village's. Where that falls, and whether it falls inside the map, is a GUESS rolled per map. No post stands beside a lane.
  • A town on the Imperial road marks each end of its street with a pair of earth banks.
    The banks face each other across the road, each about 14 ft thick at the base and 8 ft high and fenced on top, the size of Kanagawa's. The road takes a crank just inside them, and one tall boundary post stands beside the road.
  • An Imperial road may carry a milestone pair, but on most maps it does not.
    A milestone pair is two mounds about 30 ft square, one each side of the road, each carrying one tree's crown, the tree rolled among enoki, pine and cedar in the recorded shares. A mound stood only at every ri, about 2.4 miles, so a map carries a pair only when a ri mark falls on its stretch of road.
