How our maps draw plank bridges

Wherever a farm ditch is too wide to step across, our maps lay a small crossing over it, square across the water, with a short abutment at each end. All the crossings on one map take the same form - a single log or board, logs under trodden earth, or a planked deck - rolled for each settlement. A crossing is about 4 ft wide and spans about 8 ft, and it is laid over water 2 ft wide or more.

  • Why does each settlement roll the form of its crossings?
    All three forms are attested over small water, and no page we read says which crossed a paddy ditch, so the form is a knob rather than a choice. A settlement's ditch crossings all take one of the three, each with an even chance. The even shares are a GUESS; the one proportion we read, for river bridges, would make the planked deck far rarer than logs under earth. Where a crossing is laid, and at what width, does not change with the form.
  • The planked form is drawn with its seams running across the deck.
    So a reader sees boards laid from bank to bank.
  • Why is a crossing laid only over water 2 ft wide or more?
    A crossing is taken to be laid only over water a person cannot step across, a GUESS, and no page we read says at what width a farm ditch was bridged, so the line is this project's choice. It sits at about 2 shaku, the width the old farm water channel may have had - itself a GUESS - and at about the width at the head of our delivery ditches, which run 2.5 ft at the head and taper toward 1.2 ft, drawn no finer than 1.5 ft. So a standard farm ditch earns a crossing and the thin tails do not.
  • A ditch is judged by its widest point.
    A ditch wide at its head and a thread at its tail is worth a crossing somewhere, and the crossing is seated where the water is wide enough for one. Where houses, crops, other decks or the joins of ditches rule out every wide seat, the crossing takes the widest seat left rather than none.
  • On the Inashiro map the line lays ten crossings.
    One stands on each delivery branch wide enough for one, each over 2.0 ft of water or a little more, and two more down the main ditch, over 2.8 and 2.4 ft; the two branches of 1.8 ft have none.
  • A branch's crossing may stand well down the branch.
    On Inashiro the three branch crossings stand 160-250 ft below the heads of their branches, each where a bund crosses mid-field, because the head itself is a join with the main ditch. This is accepted: a crossing at the head would stand about 120 ft from the main ditch's crossing at the junction.
  • Branches with and without a crossing look alike.
    The unplanked 1.8 ft branches are drawn 2.7 ft wide, against 3.0-3.7 ft for the planked ones, too close to tell apart on the page, so the map shows some branches with a crossing and some without. Where two branches run 5-25 ft apart for 300 ft, one may have a crossing and its twin 12 ft away none.
  • A crossing stands where a path along a bund meets a ditch.
    That is what it is taken to be for, a GUESS, so no path leads to one - the way to it is the bund - and it is drawn square across its ditch. It stands at about the middle of a long stretch, and a very long stretch takes further crossings at intervals along it.
  • Both ends of a crossing land on ground worth crossing to.
    That is cultivated ground, wet or dry, the settlement itself, or a walked dike, and never reed marsh, scrub commons, or the edge of the map. A ditch along a field's outer margin with nothing beyond it carries no crossing, and a crossing that can find no such spot is not drawn.
  • The rule on where a crossing's ends may land rests on a census of every crossing on every map.
    The drainage ditches at a field's foot and the diagonal drains along its outer boundary were where a crossing had nothing on its far side, so the rule takes the crossing off them, while every field-to-field, dry-to-wet and settlement-to-field crossing stands.
  • A crossing never sits on a farmhouse.
    A bridge may lie on the water it spans - that is what it is for - but its deck is tested against every house on the map, and a crossing near the settlement's edge slides along its own ditch, staying over the water, until it clears them.
  • A crossing is about 4 ft wide and spans about 8 ft, a map drawing convention.
    It is a single-file crossing with a short abutment at each end; a board any wider or longer would read as a jetty. It is kept narrower and shorter than a cart lane's deck on purpose: the deck that carries a way is as wide as the way, and a crossing that wide would read as a road bridge.
