How our maps place and draw road bridges (hashi)

Wherever a road, a ring road, a street or a lane crosses a river, a canal or a ditch, our maps carry it over on a railed deck, drawn above both the water and the road and laid along the way. Each town uses one form for all its road bridges, chosen at random when its map is made. Each deck is as wide as the way it carries, lies exactly on its crossing, and runs about 10 ft past the water onto dry ground at each bank.

  • A town's road bridges take one of two forms, a knob chosen at random for each settlement.
    The form is either an earth-decked timber bridge, the commonest river bridge in Japan, drawn as a flat deck on posts, or a stone arch, the bridge of the Chinese towns built on canals.
  • Every crossing of a way and a watercourse is bridged, one bridge to each.
    A way does not simply run through open water. So the count of a town's bridges follows from the streets that meet its river and canals, a GUESS: no page we read counts a town's bridges.
  • What is not a crossing needs no bridge.
    A way that merely runs alongside water is not bridged, and a buried conduit recorded only to show which waters connect is not a crossing at all, since there is no seam on the ground.
  • How wide and how long are a town's bridges?
    Its deck is as wide as the way it carries, and runs from bank to bank. No page we read gives a town bridge's size, so the one figure on record bounds it: no town bridge is drawn wider than about 26 ft or longer than about 225 ft, the deck of Nihonbashi, the shogun's capital's chief bridge. That bounds the width better than the length, which follows the river a bridge crosses rather than the rank of the place.

Then each deck has to be fitted to its crossing.

  • A deck lies on its crossing and runs along its way.
    One near its crossing but at the wrong angle reads as a road running through the water past a crooked plank.
  • A crossing is solved from the water and the way, never placed by hand.
    A deck typed at fixed coordinates drifts off its crossing as soon as the way it belongs to is redrawn - by tens of feet and tens of degrees, about 50 ft and 39 degrees on the worst deck measured.
  • A deck must lie within about 24 ft and 8 degrees of its crossing.
    Every deck the maps solve lands within about a yard and a degree of its crossing - rounding, and nothing else - and a hand-placed one drifted more than ten times as far, so the line separates the two with no honest deck anywhere near it.

Where the deck meets the bank, it runs on past the water.

  • Every corner of a road bridge's deck stands on dry ground.
    Where a way crosses water at an angle, the water under the deck is wider than the channel - the channel's width divided by the sine of that angle - and the deck's own width pushes its outer corners further out again before they clear the bank. So the crossing is solved for that angle exactly, and the test is made on the deck's four real corners rather than on the ends of its centerline, because a corner is what runs out of bank first.
  • How far past the water does a deck run?
    About 10 ft on each bank, a GUESS: no page we read, premodern or modern, gives a distance from the water's edge to a small bridge's abutment. What the record offers is the reason a deck lands past the water: an abutment's footing has to be protected against the current's scour, and the abutment holds back the road's embankment behind it. The girder's seat on the abutment does not account for it: a timber beam bears on 10 to 24 inches, a foot or two of timber, not feet of deck. The Edo-period cantilever bridges (hanebashi) at Road bridges over rivers and canals (hashi), carried out from their banks, show that a deck reaching past the water onto its banks is an old practice, but give no length to calibrate the 10 ft against.
  • A footplank over a ditch keeps a short landing.
    Its ends simply rest on the bank, and giving it a bridge's landing makes it read as a jetty. No way carries it, so it is not a carried deck and is outside the crossing rule, and it crosses its ditch square by construction: How our maps draw plank bridges over farm ditches (itabashi).

A walled city's canals come in through water gates, and its ring road runs just inside the wall.

  • A deck may stand hard by a city's water gate, and that is deliberate.
    The ring road runs at a fixed distance inside the rampart, so it crosses a canal entering through a water gate a few paces inside the arch, and the deck and the water gate share ground. Shortening the deck does not separate the two.
  • Is a bridge just inside a water gate historical?
    Partly. A bridge hard by a city's canal gate is attested: Suzhou's Pan Gate pairs a road gate with a water gate, and the Wumen Bridge carries the main way in and out of that gate, near the gate tower. But that bridge stands outside the wall, over the Grand Canal the water gate leads out to, and no page we read puts a road along the inside of the wall bridging each canal there. The inside crossing is this project's geometry, a GUESS as history.

A road meeting a river too wide to bridge is the one exception to the bridge rule: it ends at a ferry landing or runs into a ford, at How our maps draw ferries and fords (watashi).
