How our maps draw and label the highway

On our maps the highway is the Imperial road, drawn 30 ft wide on every sheet, as How our maps lay out a city's streets and blocks records - about the 9 m (~30 ft) that Japan's highways were set to in 1605. Approaching a town it runs between two rows of trees; through a town it is a street built up on both sides; through a village it is lined by the village's houses, and sometimes by a rest stop's teahouses.

  • The Imperial road approaching a town is lined on both sides by a row of trees of one kind.
    Japan's shogunate kept avenues along its main highways, and China's Yuan dynasty ordered trees along its roads.
  • Which trees line the road is a knob, chosen at random per settlement.
    Pine or cedar, as on the Japanese highways, or elm, willow or pagoda tree, as on the Chinese.
  • The avenue runs from the edge of the sheet to where the gate market or the town's first houses begin.
    That stopping point is a GUESS: no page we read says where a highway's avenue ended at a town.
  • A distance mound is drawn only where the road's measured interval falls on the sheet.
    A low earth mound planted with a tree stands beside the road there, and nowhere else; how it is drawn is at How our maps mark where a village begins.
  • Where the Imperial road runs through a town, it is built up on both sides with no gaps.
    On the busy highways each lot's main house filled its whole frontage on the road, and the setting's Imperial road is the through-route that wires the domains into one trade web.
  • The lodgings for officials and the larger inns stand near the middle of the built stretch.
    The smaller inns, teahouses and shops stand around them, and the trades toward the ends, in the order a post town kept from its middle out.
  • The label "Imperial Road" is placed on the road beyond the town's built edge, never on its street.
    In the setting a county's minor road and bridge repairs are the county's own business, and this project reads the stretch of the road inside a town as the town's street, as it reads the stretch inside a city's walls. A city's label is placed by the same reasoning, at How our maps draw the Imperial road through a city, and the shops that line it.

Through a village the road is lined more loosely.

  • A village on the Imperial road fronts it on both sides.
    A village strung along a road lines both sides of it with its houses.
  • Whether the village is a road village or a street village is a knob, chosen at random per village.
    A road village has its houses spaced along the road with yards between them; a street village has its houses closer and a few of them shops facing the road, as a street village lived from the road.
  • About three villages in ten also keep a rest stop.
    The share is calibrated on the Tokaido through Kanagawa, where 16 of 56 villages kept one.
  • A rest stop is two to four teahouses at one or both ends of the village's row of houses.
    That is where a rest stop's teahouses gathered. They are open-fronted buildings facing the road, as a teahouse was built open. The count of two to four is a GUESS.
  • A town on the road carries the same cluster of teahouses at one or both of its ends.
    The teahouses gathered at the ends of a street's row of houses.
  • No village draws an inn.
    Even an in-between station, a larger place than a rest stop, was in principle forbidden to lodge travelers, and the maps follow that ban, though one account says inns were set up in some.

A compound that stands on the road, such as a magistrate's, opens its gate onto it.

  • The road at a compound's front gate is the road the compound stands on, at that road's width.
    That is 15 to 24 ft where it is a highway, the widths read for the Five Highways and for one highway through a castle town.
  • Any wider ground before a compound's gate is a GUESS.
    No page we read gives the width of the road before an official's gate.
  • A lane to a side gate or cart gate is about 6 ft where carts use it, a GUESS.
    Its reasoning is at How our maps draw moving goods: cart lanes and boat landings (kashi), and a lesser road into a village is drawn as at How our maps draw village lanes.
