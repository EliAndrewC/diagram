How our maps draw the open hills past the grove

Past a village's fields, and behind its grove, our maps draw open hill ground: coarse grass, low brush and a few scraggly pines, running off the edge of the map. A village's grass ground was more than ten times the area of its fields, too large for one map, so what the map shows is its near edge, where the grass ground begins as the fields end.

  • A village's meadow is not a plot among its fields.
    At more than ten times the fields it cannot fit on a hamlet or village map, so the map draws only its nearest part: open grass, or grass with thin low pine, on the hill ground past the fields, meeting the edge of the map. The ratio is a matter of degree, not a form to draw: the map need not show all of it, only that the grass ground begins where the fields end and does not stop at the map's edge.
  • Which form a village's grass ground takes is rolled per settlement.
    Three forms are attested, so the form is a knob: the grass hill where the map has hills, the floodplain where it has a river flat, and now and then a small grass plot beside one paddy. How far past the edge of the map the ground runs is a GUESS.
  • Why is the hill past the grove drawn as open scrub, not more forest?
    The hill past a village's protected wood ran from grass and brush through red pine to bare ground, and the map draws the middle of that range - open scrub with a few scraggly pines, neither forest nor bare earth. Where on that range a given map sits is calibrated liberty, not a finding. More forest out there would undo the point of the protected grove, which was green because it alone was spared.
  • The commons lies beyond the grove, never between the grove and the houses.
    The order away from the houses is village, then back grove, then fuel-and-fodder commons, then whatever lies past the map, so the commons stands on the high, windward side, further out than the grove. That order is this project's reading of a fengshui grove standing immediately behind and upslope from its village.
  • Rough grazing, pasture and woodland patches are not held to that order.
    The hill country around a village was a mosaic of woods, fields and grassland, so these stand wherever the ground is marginal. That the order binds the fuel commons alone is a GUESS.
  • Why does the scrub have no outline?
    A filled four-sided patch reads as a literal rhombus lying on the hillside. So the commons carries no solid fill and no hard outline, and its scatter of grass tufts, brush and pines feathers out to nothing at its margin. The feathered edge and the absent outline are map drawing conventions.
  • The scrub is drawn open and sparse, so it reads apart from the grove.
    Against the dense, dark, closed canopy of the grove, the open cover reads at a glance as different ground. The open cover is the finding above, drawn at the middle of its range.
  • The scrub band runs under the grove's edge.
    It is a ragged strip along the grove's back, of a width that varies at random, drawn under the grove so the canopy laps its inner edge and wood and scrub meet with no bare seam between them - a map drawing convention.
  • The scrub runs as one band around the settlement.
    No commons of any kind is drawn on the flooded rice fields, and the dry margins are filled as one continuous band around the settlement rather than as separate corner patches. That in a valley every edge of the map is marginal land of the same sequence is a GUESS.

The rule the map follows (the grass ground): on a map with hill ground beyond its fields, the first ground past the fields on the hill side is drawn as open grass or grass with scattered low pine, and it meets the edge of the map; on a map with a river flat and no hills, the grass ground is the floodplain beside the river. A small grass plot tied to one paddy, when rolled, is one grass patch of about the paddy's own size on the slope above it. The plot's size and how often it is rolled are a GUESS.

The rule the map follows (the commons): the fuel-and-fodder commons is drawn beyond the windbreak on the high, windward side - village, grove, commons, off-map - and never between the grove and the houses. Rough grazing, pasture and woodland patches are not held to that order and clothe any dry marginal ground. No commons of any kind is drawn on the flooded rice fields, and the dry margins are filled as one continuous band around the settlement.
