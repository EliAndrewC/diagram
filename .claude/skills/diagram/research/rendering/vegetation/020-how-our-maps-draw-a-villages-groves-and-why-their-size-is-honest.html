How our maps draw a village's groves, and why their size is honest

A clustered village on our maps draws three groves and no more, at the places the record gives them: a back grove, the village's windbreak, on the windward and high side of the houses; a smaller water-mouth grove at the low entrance, where the stream or the track leaves; and a loose copse of bamboo and fruit trees in the gaps between the houses. Two groves, the back and the water mouth, is the number villages are recorded as keeping (A village's groves).

The back grove is drawn at about 1 to 2 ha (~2.5-4.9 acres) and the water-mouth grove at about 0.1 to 0.5 ha (~0.25-1.2 acres). The field side, the drying yards and the main road stay open, and no clump of trees stands on a house, a yard, a garden or a crop.

  • A clustered village draws one shared windbreak, not a grove for each house.
    A cluster packed close cannot fit a house-sized grove onto every farm, and the one village-scale wood is taken to be the southern Chinese pattern, a GUESS at the research section. A farmstead's own grove is drawn only where a farmhouse stands apart (Groves of trees around farmhouses (yashikirin)).
  • The back grove is drawn as a crescent on the windward, high back of the houses, wrapping round their flanks.
    Hong Kong's village woods stand behind their villages, typically in a crescent shape; that ours wraps the cluster's flanks as well is this project's choice, not a shape a page gives. Which sides it takes, and on how many, is at Does a shelter belt wrap the settlement?
  • The back grove's outline is ragged, never a ruled wall - a map drawing convention.
    A real grove stands where the terrain puts it, so ours is an irregular footprint filled with clumps of trees, deepest in the middle and tapering into horns round the corners; a rectangle or a square reads as machine-planted. The raggedness is drawn so the grove does not read as a planted line.
  • The back grove is drawn 80 to 120 ft deep.
    That depth holds three or four rows of overlapping crowns; a band of about 30 ft holds one row and reads as a row of blobs, not a wall, so the grove is never drawn thinner than 80 ft. A convention: no source gives a grove's depth.
  • The back grove's outline stands back from the front row of houses.
    So the clumps inside it are not thrown away for landing on roofs; the copse, not the back grove, fills the gaps between the houses.
  • Why is the back grove drawn at 1 to 2 hectares (~2.5-4.9 acres)?
    It sits in the upper half of the measured range, where Hong Kong's median wood is about 1 ha (~2.5 acres) - a deliberate choice, so that the grove reads as a wood at two feet to the pixel. Beside a built cluster of about 11 ha (~27 acres), a grove of 1 to 2 ha (~2.5-4.9 acres) is about a tenth to a fifth of it. The Hoshigaoka map draws a back grove of about 1 to 2 ha (~2.5-4.9 acres), a water-mouth grove of about 0.3 ha (~0.7 acre) and the copse.
  • Why is the water-mouth grove drawn at 0.1 to 0.5 hectares (~0.25-1.2 acres)?
    No Chinese water-mouth grove of ordinary size has been measured, so the size is a GUESS set inside the spread of the Korean village groves that stand at the same place, and smaller than the back grove. It stands where the stream leaves the village.
  • How many trees does each grove hold?
    The back grove draws some 100 to 300 mature canopy trees over a dense understory of bamboo and shrubs, and the water-mouth grove a few dozen big old trees. Both are a GUESS, bracketed by the Korean village groves' counts and by the Pearl River Delta's density, which would put some 3,400 to 6,800 stems of every size in a grove of 1 to 2 ha (~2.5-4.9 acres). Huangling's water-mouth grove, with over a hundred old trees, holds more than ours draws.
  • The groves are drawn at the sizes measured today.
    Both groves are old forms, and the maps draw them as accurate. Their sizes are modern measurements of old groves, and there is no earlier figure to draw instead; that the surviving groves keep the extent they had before 1912 is a GUESS.
  • The copse is spread through the gaps between the houses.
    So the houses stand among green rather than beside a few trees. Fruit trees and bamboo were the useful trees planted in a fengshui wood; spreading them through the whole cluster is this project's choice, on no page read. How large each farmstead's share is drawn is at How big was the village's dooryard copse?
  • The paddies, the drying yards and the main road stay open.
    The rice lay open to the sun in front of the village, and the maps keep the copse and the groves off the drying yards and the main road as well - the same logic of crops and drying that a farmstead's grove follows.
  • A clump of trees stands against a house, never on it.
    A homestead's grove stood round the dwelling, so trees right up against a wall, with a few inches of canopy over the eaves, are drawn so; a clump drawn on a roof, a threshing yard or a garden hides the building the map exists to show. Every clump's center keeps clear of each house, yard, garden, byre and shed by that building's own circumscribing radius plus the clump's full drawn radius.
  • The windbreak keeps out of the gardens' afternoon sun.
    Drawn at a working height of about 10 m (~33 ft), its clumps keep 50 ft from the west and southwest of every yard and kitchen bed, and its near face moves out wherever the wind puts it west of the houses. The geometry, and the wider lane priced and declined with it, are at How our maps keep yards and gardens in the sun, and the old rule of keeping trees off the crops' sun at Sunlight and shade on the farm. The copse is not held back: its fruit trees and bamboo are the farmsteads' own.
