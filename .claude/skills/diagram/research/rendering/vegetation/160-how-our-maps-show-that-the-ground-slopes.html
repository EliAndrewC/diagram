How our maps show that the ground slopes

Our maps are deliberately not topographic: they draw no contours, no spot heights and no hillshade. They show that the ground slopes by what the slope does instead - a hill drawn as a picture of a hill, terraces on steep ground, and water that runs from the high side of a village to the low.

  • High ground is drawn as a picture of a hill.
    A hill is a shaded mound of tiers, hachures and a wooded cap. A hill drawn in profile is the oldest way maps showed relief, a picture of the hill rather than lines of survey, and hachures came later, standardized in 1799. The pictorial mound is a map drawing convention.
  • Surveyed estate maps seldom showed relief at all.
    Land surveyors drew estates to record their boundaries and areas, and few tried to show the rise of the ground.
  • Terraced paddies are drawn only where the slope is steep.
    There they are long, narrow, curved strips along the contour with a riser between them. A gently tilted village is drawn without terraces, since a real valley floor shows no visible steps, and terraces there would make it read as mountainous. That steep ground alone shows terraces rests on general reading; no source is cited. How the terraces themselves are drawn is at How our maps draw terraced and valley paddies (tanada, yachida).
  • On gentle ground, the water shows which way the land falls.
    The source and the pond sit on the high side, the ditches that share out the water fan in from there, and a drainage ditch leaves the low corner and runs off the map. Wet rice needs water out as well as in, so a drain is a feature the reader expects anyway, and it doubles as the downhill cue. Both rest on general reading; no source is cited. How the drains are drawn is at How our maps draw field drains (akusuiro).
  • The maps draw no shading or cast shadows to show a slope.
    On a plan map, shaded relief reads as a light source - a sun in the northwest - rather than as rising ground. It fights the flat plan the rest of the sheet is drawn in, and it misleads. Leaving out hillshade is this project's own drawing decision, a map drawing convention.

The rule the map follows: no contour lines, no spot heights and no shaded relief are drawn on any map. High ground is a pictorial mound; the fall of the land is read from the water - source and pond high, ditches fanning downhill, a drainage ditch leaving the low corner off the map - and terraced paddies are drawn only where the slope is steep enough for a reader to expect risers.
