How our maps draw bamboo, when one culm is too small to see

A culm of bamboo is at most about 10 cm (~4 in) across, a fraction of a pixel at our maps' scale of one foot to a pixel, so a stand drawn stem by stem would show nothing. Our maps draw each bamboo stand as an area whose place and size are to scale, marked inside with paired culm strokes and a leafy fork in pale yellow-green. The marks are a map drawing convention: they stand for the bamboo, they do not count it.

  • Why is bamboo not drawn as trees?
    A mark one culm across reads as nothing at fit zoom, pale dots in a dark wood: a map drawn that way has bamboo that no reader can see. Nor does bamboo grow one stem at a time through a wood; a stand is a thicket of its own, in most cases of nearly one species.
  • Japan's modern topographic maps give bamboo a symbol of its own.
    The legend of the national mapping agency gives bamboo groves an entry and a symbol of their own, beside broadleaf and conifer forest, and separate from, though like, the symbol for low bamboo grass. So the three can be told apart at map scale.
  • A separate map symbol for bamboo is found only on modern maps.
    It is on the national survey's maps of about 1910, and no page we read dates its introduction or traces it to a map from before 1868, in the Edo period.
  • Before modern times, a map or a picture drew the growth itself.
    A village map of 1801 draws a local lord's house surrounded by a thicket, as a feature of its own, though the page does not say what the thicket was. An early-Edo screen paints settlements ringed by bamboo groves, and the pictorial maps of the period more often than not carry no legend for what their colors mean.
  • Our maps keep the modern mark, as a convention.
    A stand's place and size are historical; its mark is taken from a modern map legend, knowing where it comes from. What our maps show is kept to what existed before 1868, and a bamboo stand did; the mark only stands for it, and the old maps offer no symbol to put in its place, only a picture of the growth.
  • The marks sit on a 7 ft grid.
    Paired culm strokes with a leafy fork, jittered on a 7 ft grid across the stand, in pale yellow-green with no fill beneath them.

Where a settlement's bamboo stands follows the two places the record finds it: on the farmsteads, and in a thicket of the village's own.

  • Whether a settlement has bamboo, and where, is a knob.
    It is rolled for each map: no bamboo, bamboo on the farmsteads, a thicket of the village's own, or both. The record shows both places, and bamboo stopping where the winters are hard, so two maps may honestly differ.
  • How many farmsteads keep their own bamboo?
    About three in five, on a map that rolled bamboo on its farmsteads. This is a GUESS: no page we read gives a share, and bamboo named as one of several lesser plants of the grove reads as common but not universal.
  • A farm's bamboo is a strip of 22 by 16 ft.
    Its size is a GUESS, no page giving one. It is seated with the sheds and gardens, so the lanes and wells keep off it.
  • Which side of the house does the strip take?
    It is rolled for each farmstead: behind the house most often (35 in 100), then on the windward side (30), beside the shed (25), and least often on the other flank (10). The sources give bamboo with the storehouses, at a wet edge, and on the wind side in the grove; the seat behind the house is one no page we read names, and every weight is a GUESS.
  • On the Inashiro map, 8 of 15 farmsteads keep a strip.
    Six stand at the windward northwest corner and two on the far flank. Every shed on that map stands on the north side of its house, so behind the house and beside the shed are one place, and no strip found room there; a strip rolled for either takes the windward corner.
  • A farm drawn with its own grove keeps its bamboo inside that grove.
    There it is a patch of culms the strip's size, 22 by 16 ft, on the house side of each windward band of the grove, as bamboo grew among the Tonami grove's lesser plants. The patch's size, its seat on the house side and a patch in every windward band are each a GUESS. The Tonami grove held its bamboo from the west round to the north of the house, while its tall trees stood on the south and west, so the bamboo side and the windbreak side share only the west.
  • A grove's bamboo shows only between its crowns and along its edge.
    Bamboo filled the low part of a grove, under the trees, and a crown is drawn from above, so bamboo under one is hidden. Where a bamboo plant stands in the open it is drawn as the culm mark, beneath the crowns. About one plant in twelve of a windbreak clump is bamboo, in the farm's grove and in the village's shelter belt alike; that share is a GUESS, no page giving one, and the dooryard and alder plantings carry none.
  • A village's thicket stands on dry ground just beyond the back row of houses.
    The record finds settlements ringed by bamboo before 1868, and bamboo wanting high, dry ground; so the one thicket stands on dry ground at the settlement's edge. Its side of the settlement, its being held in common and its size, 84 by 58 ft, a working stand that is cut and regrows, are each a GUESS.
  • A stand narrower than 14 ft is not drawn, and no stand overlaps a paddy.
    Below 14 ft on its short side a stand does not read at fit zoom; a farm's strip, 16 ft deep, does. The floor is a convention.
