How our maps stock woods and size crowns

Our maps draw every canopy tree of a wood at its real size, on real spacing, so a wood is a dense mass of crowns. A hill wood is stocked at about 600 trees a hectare, one to every 13 ft, its crowns about 17 ft across; a village's coppice more thickly, about 1,700 a hectare on 8 ft centers, its crowns 8-9 ft across. No page we read gives a crown's width, so every crown size is a GUESS.

  • A hill wood is drawn at about 600 trees a hectare, one to every 13 ft.
    That is the middle of the 500-800 canopy trees a hectare (~200-320 an acre) guessed for a mixed hill wood, a figure on no page read. A hectare is 107,639 sq ft, so 600 a hectare is one tree to about 180 sq ft, a mean spacing near 13 ft.
  • Each crown in a hill wood is drawn about 17 ft across, some smaller and some larger.
    The mean crown radius is 8.5 ft, at the low end of the 5-8 m (~16-26 ft) guessed for a canopy crown, and each crown is drawn between 0.75 and 1.4 times that.
  • The crowns overlap, so the wood reads as closed.
    Crowns about 17 ft across on 13 ft centers overlap. Some classifications divide woods into open and closed by their crown cover, and this project reads a closed wood as one whose crowns meet, so the packed look is meant.
  • No crown is enlarged so that it can be seen.
    At one foot to a pixel a crown is drawn 12-24 pixels across, and it shrinks with the map at coarser scales, as the buildings do.
  • A windbreak's crowns are drawn the same real size as a hill wood's.
    One crown size serves the hill woods and the windbreak's cedars alike: on the Inashiro map the windbreak's crowns have a median radius of 7.6 ft, about 15 ft across, 325 of the map's 505 crowns. No page we read gives a crown width for a cedar windbreak. An Edo rule planted coastal sugi thicker where the wind was fierce (How thickly trees stood in a wood, and how wide their crowns) - a denser planting, which says nothing of a wider crown.
  • The windbreak and the other woods keep the hill wood's figures.
    Only the village coppice has been stocked from figures of its own; the windbreak and every other wood keep the hill wood's spacing and crowns until they are researched in their own right.

Two rules keep the crowns of a wood from piling on one another.

  • No canopy tree is drawn under another's crown.
    A crown is drawn only if its center lies outside every crown already on the map, and no drawn crown's center lies inside it. A crown wholly inside another's is not part of the canopy layer, and the canopy layer is what the map draws: a drawing convention.
  • Two crowns may still overlap at their edges.
    Crowns closer than the sum of their radii may overlap at the edge, a drawing allowance, since in some forests the crowns of fully stocked trees hold a gap rather than interlacing. On the Inashiro map, of 787 crowns none stands under another and 737 (94%) still touch a neighbor, so the wood still reads as closed, at about one crown to each 13 ft square.

A village's coppice stood more thickly than a hill wood, and the maps stock it from modern counts of konara woods.

  • A village's coppice is drawn at about 1,700 crowns a hectare, on centers near 8 ft.
    The figure is that of a planted konara stand of 29 years at Tsukuba, inside the 1,460-2,063 a hectare of woods left to grow to 26-31 years, one crown to about 63 sq ft. Those stands had grown past the age a worked wood was cut, so the figure is probably thinner than a worked wood at its cut, and the coppice may read a little more open than it stood.
  • Why not the 2,560 a hectare of a younger stand?
    The 23-year Kiso stand's 2,560, on centers near 6.5 ft, is closer in age to a cut, but it is one stand, counted unthinned as found. This project keeps the 1,700 until a worked wood at its cut is counted. A wood cut more recently stood thicker still, to about 5,000 stems a hectare at 15 years; the maps do not draw that younger stage.
  • Each crown in a coppice is drawn about 8-9 ft across, so that neighbors just meet.
    That is a GUESS, sized from the spacing, because no page we read gives a crown width for a konara, a kunugi or any tree of a worked fuel wood. A coppice is drawn as a thicket, denser than a hill wood, with smaller crowns.
  • No wood is stocked from the old Edo planting figures.
    The Edo record counts timber on the day it was planted, in plantations of sugi, hinoki and pine, not a grown wood, a village's fuel wood or its windbreak (How thickly trees stood in a wood, and how wide their crowns). So every density the maps draw rests on modern measurements or on none, and is labeled so.
