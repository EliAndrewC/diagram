How our maps draw the marsh's edge

On our maps a marsh's edge is the cut, open margin a village would have kept: grass thins into the reeds with no line between them, and brush and pines stop at the marsh's edge. Where a village's shelter belt runs down into the marsh, its trees there are drawn as alder. The margin follows the record: a marsh grades from reed through sedge and wet meadow to dry ground, the trees that stand at a reed edge in Japan are alder, and the cutting is what kept the reed bed open.

  • No pine or dry brush is drawn inside the reeds.
    No source we read stands a pine on wet ground: the red pine of our scrub grows on dry, poor ridges, rocky slopes, sandy soils and seashores. That it is absent from a marsh is this record's reading of those habitats. With this rule, one hamlet has only four scrub marks inside its marshes, all of them on a marsh's own edge.
  • Grass grades into the reeds across the marsh's margin.
    It stands for the sedge and wet-meadow band between the reed and dry ground, and is drawn with the commons' grass mark, a map drawing convention.
  • The grading is drawn as a mixed band, along a rounded, waving edge.
    The scrub's grass and the marsh's reeds are each one small block repeated across their ground, and where the two meet a narrow band either side of the boundary is drawn with a third block that mixes them - grass tufts and reed tufts at about half their own densities, with a little of the marsh's wet haze - so the grass thins out into the reeds and the reeds into the grass, as the margin itself grades. The band follows the marsh's edge, which is drawn rounded at its corners and gently waving along its length rather than as the straight strips it is laid out in. Both are map drawing conventions.
  • Where a stream or a ditch crosses the marsh, or a drain runs along a paddy's foot, the reeds are drawn right to the water's edge.
    That reeds stand at the water is the record's own finding for a reservoir's shore.
  • A narrow band of denser, slightly darker reeds lines each bank, so the water stays easy to follow.
    The band is a map drawing convention.
  • A shelter belt's trees in the marsh are drawn as alder.
    In Japan alder grows on excessively wet ground, and it takes a mire's ground as the mire dries. That a village's shelter belt runs on into the marsh as alder is this project's reading.
  • The alder crowns are drawn blue-gray green, though real alder is a plain dark green.
    The color sets them apart from the shelter belt's other trees, a map drawing convention.
  • Marshes need no rule of their own against the scrub.
    A marsh is drawn after the scrub, which already keeps out of it by its own rule. The marsh's reeds already keep off crops and buildings, and out of the water itself.
  • Our maps draw the marsh's edge cut and open, and not yet the alder and willow wood an uncut edge grows.
    The record supports two forms of a marsh's edge: the managed one, its reeds cut each winter and the margin kept open, and a wet wood of alder and willow along an edge nobody cuts. Each settlement should take one or the other; so far our maps draw only the first, a shortfall. That a village cut its own marsh as thatch fields and Lake Biwa's reed beds were cut is this record's interpolation.

Scrub could meet a marsh in other ways, and our maps draw none of them.

  • Scrub left to scatter fills the reeds.
    With no rule, the brush, grass and pines of dry ground scatter through the reeds just as they do outside them.
  • Stopping all the scrub, grass included, at the marsh's boundary leaves a ruled line.
    The reeds are drawn thinning out toward the marsh's boundary rather than ending on it, so stopping every kind of scrub dead there leaves a ruled line and a bare strip along a straight edge of the marsh. Our maps do not draw that form.
  • Letting every kind of scrub thin into the marsh leaves pines standing in the bog.
    It closes the line, but stands a row of pines along some 1,600 ft of one marsh's northern edge, and pines do not belong there. Our maps do not draw that form either.

The rule the map follows: no dry-ground scrub is drawn inside a marsh. Grass thins into the reed edge and is gone beyond it; brush and pines stop at the marsh's edge, and every patch of rough ground a map lays down after the marsh keeps out of it the same way. Where a stream or ditch crosses a marsh, or a drain runs along a paddy beside it, the reeds run to the water and a narrow band of denser reeds lines each bank. A shelter belt's trees standing in the marsh are drawn as alder.
