  • no publicly readable source The Japanese firebreak ground, the hiyokechi, is well attested - open ground left vacant to stop a fire spreading from one building to the next, and a place to take refuge during one - but that is deliberately cleared ground in the shogunal capital. Nothing readable treats the incidental gaps between the holdings of an unwalled country town as doing the same work, so this is an attested mechanism applied to a settlement class the record does not discuss.
  • hinomi-yagura-kotobank - 「In the Kyoho era (1716-36) one fire tower was provided to every ten machi, and in a machi without a tower a fire ladder was provided on top of the jishinban hut.」 (translated; ) and, from the second published encyclopedia reprinted on the same page, 「In Edo one was built for about every ten machi, and in the other machi there were places where only a ladder and a bell were fitted on the roof of the jishinban hut.」 (translated; ) and, for the official stations and for what WAS per-machi, 「After the Meireki fire (1657), besides the daimyo fire brigades, which lacked quickness, the shogunate's own standing fire watch of Edo - the jobikeshi - was organized, and fire-brigade mansions were established at several places in the castle town. Fire towers were set up there, with fire watchmen standing lookout day and night, who signaled with a drum when they spotted an outbreak. The townsmen's side also formed a fire organization for its own protection, and for security a jishinban was established in every machi and a kidoban at the machi boundaries.」 (translated; ) (two published encyclopedias on the same page agree on the ratio independently.)
