How our maps draw dyers, weavers and papermakers (kon'ya)

Our maps give a county town a dyer, whose vats stand inside the house because dyers worked at home, with a drying ground beside the house and water within reach to rinse in. Weaving and papermaking have no works of their own on any map, because both were done at home too: the looms stay inside ordinary houses, and paper shows only as drying boards beside a village's farmhouses, since it was a farm's side job. How big a dyer's drying ground is drawn, and how a city's dye works is sited, are at How our maps draw shops and trades in towns and villages.

  • A county town keeps a dyer.
    The setting's county has its dye-house owner among its wealthy tradesmen, and historically dyers settled in the towns and in the villages alike.
  • A dyer's vats are inside the house.
    Dyers worked at home, so the house holds the vats.
  • A dyer's drying ground lies behind or beside the house.
    This is this project's GUESS: no page we read gives the plan of a dye house's plot.
  • A dyer keeps rinsing water within reach: a stream, a channel or a moat.
    The dyers of Yamatokoriyama rinsed their cloth in a channel fed from the castle moat, and the maps ask the same of a city's dye works. They extend it to every dyer, with a stream or a moat standing in where a town has no channel; that extension is this project's GUESS.
  • A county town has one or two dyers, a number of this project's choosing.
    It is this project's GUESS: no page we read counts the dyers of a town of a given size.
  • Why may a city's dyers stand together in a street, but not a town's?
    Historically dyers in the towns lived together in a craftsmen's ward, and the castle town of Yamatokoriyama keeps its dyers' street, Konya-machi, where the dye shops gathered, with a waterway down its middle. A county town of our maps has one or two dyers, too few to make a street of, so this project keeps the dyers' ward for a city.
  • A village dyer, where a map keeps one, is a house like the rest.
    Dyers settled in villages too, a few to a village, and worked at home.
  • No map draws a weaving works.
    A town's weavers and its clothier live in ordinary houses and shops, and the looms are inside them, since cloth was woven at home or put out to farm households.
  • A city may keep a weavers' quarter, drawn as houses.
    Kyoto's Nishijin is the model: weaving had gone on in Kyoto since ancient times, and after the Onin war it revived in Nishijin, where its weavers formed a guild. Drawing the quarter as houses, not as works, is this project's GUESS from weaving done at home; no page we read gives its plan.
  • Papermaking belongs to a village, and draws as drying boards, never as a mill.
    Where a village map declares it, drying boards stand propped in the sun beside the farmhouses in the off-season, since paper was a farm's side job and its sheets were dried on boards in the sun.

The rule the map follows: a county town keeps one or two dyers, each a house with its vats inside, a drying ground behind or beside it, and a stream, a channel or a moat within reach; a city's dyers may stand together in a street on a channel, and a village dyer is an ordinary house. No map draws a weaving works or a paper mill; a city may keep a weavers' quarter of houses, and a village that declares papermaking draws drying boards beside its farmhouses in the off-season.
