How our maps draw dyers, weavers and papermakers (kon'ya)

Our maps give a county town a dyer, with a drying ground beside its house and water within reach to rinse in. Weaving and papermaking have no works of their own on any map: the looms stay inside ordinary houses, and paper shows only as drying boards beside a village's farmhouses. How big a dyer's drying ground is drawn, and how a city's dye works is sited, are at How our maps draw shops and trades in towns and villages.

  • A county town keeps a dyer.
    The setting's county has its dye-house owner among its wealthy tradesmen, and historically dyers settled in the towns and in the villages alike.
  • A dyer's vats are inside the house, and its drying ground behind or beside it.
    Dyers worked at home, so the house holds the vats, and the drying ground is the ground the house needs besides.
  • A dyer keeps rinsing water within reach: a stream, a channel or a moat.
    The dyers of Yamatokoriyama rinsed their cloth in a channel fed from the castle moat, and the maps ask the same of a city's dye works.
  • How many dyers does a county town keep, and where does the drying ground lie?
    One or two houses, the drying ground behind or beside each. Both are this project's GUESS: no page we read counts the dyers of a town of a given size or gives the plan of a dye house's plot.
  • A town is too small for a dyers' ward, but a city's dyers may stand together in a street on a channel.
    The dyers' wards recorded are those of castle towns, like the dyers' street at Yamatokoriyama.
  • A village dyer, where a map keeps one, is a house like the rest.
    Dyers settled in villages too, a few to a village, and worked at home.
  • A town draws no weaving works.
    Its weavers and its clothier live in ordinary houses and shops, and the looms are inside them, since a clothier wove in their own house or put the work out to farm households.
  • A city may keep a weavers' quarter, drawn as houses.
    Kyoto's Nishijin is the model.
  • Papermaking belongs to a village, and draws as drying boards in the sun.
    Where a village map declares it, drying boards stand propped in the sun beside the farmhouses in the off-season, since paper was a farm's side job and its sheets were dried on boards in the sun. No map draws a paper mill.

The rule the map follows: a county town keeps one or two dyers, each a house with its vats inside, a drying ground behind or beside it, and a stream, a channel or a moat within reach; a city's dyers may stand together in a street on a channel, and a village dyer is an ordinary house. No map draws a weaving works or a paper mill; a city may keep a weavers' quarter of houses, and a village that declares papermaking draws drying boards beside its farmhouses in the off-season.
