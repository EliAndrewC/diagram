How our maps draw iron refining forges (chao)

A refining forge on our maps is a town works where pig iron, smelted out in the hills where the charcoal is made, is refined into wrought-iron bar. It is drawn as an open-sided shed over two hearths, one for each stage of the refining, with its own charcoal store, a quench trough, stacked bar iron and a slag heap - the waste and the product together are what tell a reader it is a refinery and not a smithy. The furnaces that smelted the iron are never drawn.

  • Smelting and refining stand on two sites: the furnace at the fuel, the forge in the town.
    That was the arrangement of Guangdong's large ironworks, smelting in the forested mountains and refining in a town downriver, and of Japan's, where the tatara made pig iron and the okaji refined it. The reason is the fuel. A kiln burns about four and a half parts wood to one of charcoal, and two charcoal furnaces were not put close together, because that would double the load on the forest for little or no gain; so a trade strung along scattered stands of oak cannot bring its fuel to one place, and in this project's reading its furnaces go out to the fuel, miles from the town, and the cold pig iron comes down to be melted again at the town's forge.
  • The single site, a fining basin beside the furnace, is not drawn.
    A seventeenth-century Chinese treatise runs the iron from the furnace straight into a basin a few chi away, but one site needs ore, fuel and labor gathered in one place, which dispersed fuel does not allow.
  • A town map draws no smelting furnace and no charcoal kiln.
    The furnace belongs out at the fuel, as Guangdong's stood in the mountains rather than in the town that finished and sold the iron.
  • The forge has two hearths, one lit and one banked.
    The okaji refined in two stages, and the two hearths of a two-stage process stand at different heats at any moment.
  • A refining forge keeps 60 ft from the houses round it.
    That is the distance our maps give a very large fire that is stoked and watched, the same as a kiln's; the ladder of distances is at How our maps draw shops and trades in towns and villages.
  • The forge is drawn at its true size.
    A glyph that reads badly is not enlarged to fix it; what makes it read is its grammar, not its size.
