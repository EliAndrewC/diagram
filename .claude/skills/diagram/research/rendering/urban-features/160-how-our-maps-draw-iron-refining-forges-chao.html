How our maps draw iron refining forges (chao)

A refining forge on our maps is a town works where pig iron, smelted out in the hills where the charcoal is made, is refined into wrought-iron bar. It is drawn as an open-sided shed over two hearths, one for each stage of the refining, with its own charcoal store, a quench trough, stacked bar iron and a slag heap - the waste and the product together are what tell a reader it is a refinery and not a smithy. The furnaces that smelted the iron are never drawn.

  • Our maps put the smelting furnace out at the fuel and the refining forge in the town, so a town map draws no furnace and no charcoal kiln.
    That was the arrangement of the large ironworks of Guangdong, in south China, smelting in the forested mountains and sending their iron down to a town that refined or cast it.
  • Why does the furnace go out to the fuel?
    Charcoal takes a great deal of wood: a modern industrial kiln burns about four and a half parts wood to one of charcoal, and two charcoal furnaces were not put close together, because that would double the load on the forest for little or no gain. So, in this project's reading, ironworks fed from scattered stands of oak cannot bring their fuel to one place: the kilns and furnaces go out to the fuel, miles from the town, and the cold pig iron comes down to be melted again at the town's forge.
  • Our maps do not draw the other arrangement, a single site where the iron runs from the furnace into a fining basin beside it.
    A seventeenth-century Chinese treatise describes it, the iron running from the furnace straight into a basin a few chi away, but one site needs ore, fuel and labor gathered in one place, which, in this project's reading, dispersed fuel does not allow.
  • Our maps draw the forge with two hearths, one burning hot and one banked (covered over to burn low).
    The okaji refined in two stages, and the two hearths of a two-stage process stand at different heats at any moment.
  • On our maps, a refining forge keeps 60 ft from the houses round it.
    That is the distance our maps give a very large fire that is stoked and watched, the same as a kiln's; every fire's distance is listed at How our maps draw shops and trades in towns and villages.
  • The forge is drawn at its true size.
    What makes so small a drawing read as a works yard rather than a machine is how it is drawn, not how big it is:
    • The charcoal store is one bay under one roof with a single division, as a fuel store is one heap. Three evenly spaced dark bins would look like the switches on a control panel.
    • The quench trough is a muted slate green, not bright blue. Bright blue would be the only strong blue on the map and would catch the eye like a warning lamp. The trough's shape and its place by the anvil already say it holds water.
    • The shed roof has a ridge line, the one mark that says a building is seen from above. Without it the roof is a plain box, and what is inside looks like parts mounted on a panel.
    • There is open floor between the hearths and the iron stacked beside them.
