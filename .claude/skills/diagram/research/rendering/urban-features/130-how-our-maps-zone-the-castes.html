How our maps zone the castes

Every town and city on our maps keeps its castes apart. A town's burakumin quarter stands at the settlement's edge in ground of its own, held off its neighbors by a band of open ground; a walled city keeps at least one of its burakumin neighborhoods inside the wall; the poor lodge in a quarter of their own; and the commoners' blocks are closed by gates at night. The quarter holds the setting's own count of households, since no source counts a provincial town's.

The rule the map follows: at town scale a burakumin quarter stands off every other dwelling by 60 ft of open ground, measured wall to wall, and that collar is held open before anything else is allowed to grow into it. A walled provincial city draws at least one burakumin neighborhood inside its walls.

  • A quarter holds the setting's count: 12 households in a county seat, 30 in a provincial city, 120 in a capital.
    The counts are the setting's own, and the dwellings are packed close, as the 232 houses of the Edo enclosure at Asakusa were crowded together. The history counts one great quarter and whole regions but no small town's, so the setting's 12 stands on the setting alone.
  • A city's quarter draws one religious building of its own, a shrine or a temple hall, rolled per settlement.
    Both are attested, so which one is a knob: a small shrine to the quarter's tutelary god, as many quarters kept the Hakusan god, or a small Jodo Shinshu temple hall, the commonest sect among them.
  • A county seat's quarter draws the smaller of the two, a shrine.
    That so small a quarter kept even that is a GUESS: the sources speak of the quarters in general, never of one of twelve households.
  • The quarter's dwellings are drawn as small houses, and it draws its water where its neighbors do.
    Both are GUESSES, for want of any page that describes a quarter's houses or its well.

Where a town's quarter stands follows the sites the record gives.

  • A town's quarter stands at the settlement's edge, on a road where it leaves the town or on the riverbank below it, rolled per settlement.
    Both forms are attested, so the choice is a knob. The lords housed their leather workers at the edge of the castle town, at a road chokepoint or on a riverbank; Matsumoto gathered its kawata at the castle town's entrances and Takada set its quarter along the Kaga road at the town's edge, while Kyoto's oldest quarter was a village of riverbank outcastes.
  • The quarter shares its side of the town with the tanning yard and the execution ground.
    Their bearings are set at How our maps site tanning yards.
  • Where the edge offers better and worse ground, the quarter takes the worse.
    Low, wet or cut-off ground, by a degree rolled per settlement rather than a fixed rule. It is this project's reading of the poor sites many quarters sit on, not anything a source states of one town.

The collar of open ground is what makes a quarter read as segregated rather than merely crowded.

  • Why 60 ft?
    Dwellings inside a quarter stand 10 to 30 ft apart on these maps, so the gap must be several times that to read as a gap rather than a wide lane. The figure is a calibration against the drawn maps, not a finding: the historical outcaste hamlet stood apart from the peasants' plots within the village's own territory, at no fixed distance out.
  • The collar is well below the 120 ft a tanning yard keeps from dwellings.
    It says who lives beside whom, not how far a pollution keeps off: it is not a buffer against kegare.
  • The gap is measured wall to wall, and held open before the farmland around the quarter is laid.
    Both are needed. A gap measured between house centers counts a house clear while its wall stands inside the collar, and farmland laid around a finished quarter closes in on ground nothing held open. Miss either and farmhouses stand within feet of the huts, and a quarter that runs on into the farms around it is not a segregated quarter at all.
  • Cities keep no collar.
    A city zones its quarters by ward, wall to wall, so its segregation is the ward rather than the gap, and the seams between neighboring quarters on the drawn cities run about 30 ft.

A walled city's quarter departs from the history on purpose.

  • A walled provincial city keeps at least one burakumin neighborhood inside its walls, a deliberate deviation.
    The burakumin handle what a city cannot do without and will not touch - the dead, the hides, the punishments - and a city shut up under siege needs those hands as much as its granary. The history puts the quarters at the edge, and nothing found ties a quarter's place to a siege: the neighborhood inside the wall, and its reason, are this setting's own.
  • The neighborhood inside is the floor, not the whole.
    Not all of a city's burakumin need be inside; rows outside the wall are usual.

The other castes are zoned by the same maps.

  • A city's flophouse stands in its poor quarter, on the road in.
    So it was in Osaka's Nagamachi, where the poor quarter and the road out to Sakai were one place. A town's stands on the road at its edge (The market-day flophouse - who actually stays over).
  • A small provincial city closes its commoner blocks with kido gates, not walled wards.
    Each block's own gates, with a watchman and a wicket, shut at night, and the samurai quarter lies beyond them. The continuous walled wards of a Tang-style capital did not outlast the Song and Yuan dynasties. The gates are drawn as at How our maps draw the night watch and ward gates (kido).
