How our maps draw the night watch and ward gates (kidoban)

Our maps bar a county seat's main street at night with a ward gate at each end of the built core, a keeper's hut beside each gate and a watch house at one end. A city draws a watch house for its commoner wards and guard boxes at the crossings of its samurai quarter, and a village may keep a single watchman's hut where its lane comes in. The maps draw the huts, not their keepers: the watchmen were hired from the lowest status, as many outside Edo were, and in the setting that is the hinin.

  • A county seat draws a ward gate at each end of its main street, where the road leaves the built core.
    That is the post town's form, a gate at each end, and the castle town's at a small town's grain of one gate to several blocks.
  • How many more gates does a county seat draw between the two?
    At most one more, across the street between them. How many is a degree chosen for each settlement from the map's seed, within the small town's grain of one gate to several blocks.
  • Beside each gate stands the keeper's hut, about 6 by 9 ft.
    That is the fixed size of an Edo gate keeper's hut.
  • How big is the watch house our maps draw?
    From about 9 by 12 ft up to 12 by 18 ft, a degree chosen for each settlement from the map's seed, standing at one end of the street against the gate. The source's "some 2 to 3 ken" does not say which side it measures, so reading the larger house as 2 by 3 ken is a GUESS.
  • A county seat draws no mitsuke banks.
    The earthen banks at the entrance belong to a post town, not to a market seat.

A city draws its watch at a larger count, on the same sizes.

  • A city draws one watch house to each commoner ward, or one to two or three small wards.
    That is Edo's rule, which a provincial city takes over at its own number of wards. Each is sized and set as a county seat's is: nominally about 9 by 12 ft, often larger, against the gate at the end of the ward's main street.
  • A city's samurai quarter draws guard boxes, about 12 by 9 ft, set into a compound's wall.
    That is the Edo guard box's usual size and seat.
  • How many guard boxes does a samurai quarter draw?
    One to each crossing of the quarter's streets. This is a GUESS: no page we read gives how many stood to a ward.

A village's watch is a single hut, where the village keeps one at all.

  • Whether a village keeps a watchman is a knob.
    Each village rolls it from the map's seed: one form keeps a hired watchman and draws his hut, the other keeps none. How often a village keeps one is a GUESS, since no page we read says.
  • Where does a village's watch hut stand?
    Where the lane enters the village. The one placement found is a watch hut at a village's entrance, and a watchman who guards the approaches and moves vagrants on is best placed there, in this project's reading.

The rule the map follows: each village rolls from its seed whether it keeps a watchman (about one village in two, a GUESS); one that does draws a single small hut, about 9 by 6 ft (a GUESS), beside the lane where it enters the village. A hamlet keeps none (a GUESS).
