How our maps draw shops and trades in towns and villages

Our maps draw a shop as the street front of its keeper's house: one building that is both shop and home. A few trades whose premises outgrow a house - the brewery, the dye works, the oil press, the pawnshop, the bathhouse, the kiln, the farrier and the lumber yard - are drawn as works of their own. Everything else a place trades in stays in the rows of ordinary shops.

  • The shop glyph is drawn larger than most real shops, by convention.
    It is about 48 by 32 ft. Its 32 ft matches the depth of the Edo front shop, but its 48 ft (8 ken) frontage falls between Kyoto's ordinary and large houses, about 1.6 to 2.7 times the 18 to 30 ft of an ordinary shop, and its area is roughly twice one.
  • How big would a shop be if drawn at its real size?
    One building about 18 to 30 ft along the street and about 30 ft deep, its width rolled along that band for each building, a calibrated liberty. This is the rule for a map drawn at real size; our maps still draw the 48 by 32 ft glyph. A small shop-house about 9 to 12 ft wide and 12 ft deep belongs in the same roll, but how many of a town's shops were small no page we read says, so its share is a GUESS.
  • How many shops does a county seat draw?
    On the order of 50 to 90 households with a shop or a trade, for a county seat of about 1,200: one for every 13 inhabitants at Hachijo, one of the nineteen Yashio villages near Edo whose trades were recorded, and one for every 23 across all nineteen. Where a place falls in that range is a degree along a continuum, rolled for each settlement from the map's seed, a calibrated liberty.
  • A county seat's shops follow the mix recorded at one village near Edo.
    As at Hachijo, among the Yashio villages, most are small general stores for liquor, soy sauce and sundries, with a few taverns and hairdressers; then one bathhouse, two or three pawnshops, one brewer, one oil presser and a dyer or two.
  • A shop count is a count of houses with a shop front.
    Each ordinary shop is the front room of its keeper's house, so the count adds no buildings.
  • How many storefronts does a city draw?
    About one to every 25 to 30 inhabitants, this project's own estimate: no trade census of a city was found, and the Yashio village of Hachijo, at one trading household to every 13 inhabitants, suggests the estimate is sparse rather than generous.

A village's trades are drawn differently from a town's.

  • A village's trade marks its house and adds no building.
    The trades were kept by households that also farmed, so a trade is a farmhouse that also sells or makes something. How the trade shows on the house is a drawing convention.
  • What share of a village's houses keep a trade?
    Each village rolls it between about 1 in 25 and about 4 in 10, most often about 1 in 4 - the range of the Yashio villages near Edo, used for a village anywhere as a calibrated liberty, rolled from the map's seed. For a village of about 350 inhabitants the center of the range is a dozen or more households; a remote village with none at all is the far end of the same range.
  • Which trades does a village draw first?
    A sake shop that is also the village store, a tavern, a carpenter, a cooper and a smith; a pawnshop or a barber only in some villages - the Yashio survey found pawnshops in 11 of its villages and hairdressers in 10 - and our maps give them to the villages whose share is high, a calibrated liberty.
  • A sake brewery larger than a household's own is drawn in the county seat, not in a village.
    In this setting every brewery above the household level holds an annual license, and a county counts its sake brewer among the notables of its seat; placing the licensed brewery there is a drawing convention.

In towns and cities, most trades stay in the rows of ordinary shops, and the larger places set some of them together by street or ward.

  • The small trades are drawn as ordinary shops, never as works of their own.
    The cooper, the tatami maker, the tofu maker, the noodle shop, the teahouse, the apothecary, the carpenter and the rice dealer work and sell in the keeper's house, or work away from it, as the research section's entries on them show (Shops and trades in towns and villages); none of the pages we read gives one a yard, a kiln or a water supply of its own. The ordinary smith is drawn the same way: his work on hooves changes what he makes, not the size of his shop.
  • A castle town or a city may set one trade's shops together in a street.
    As the castle towns did with their coopers and Edo with its drug sellers.
  • A provincial city and a capital lay out part of their commoners' ground as wards of one trade.
    The smiths', carpenters', dyers', drapers' and oil sellers' wards among them, each a block of the trade's shop-houses, while the main street, by our maps' convention, stays a street of mixed shops. The grouping and the names are accurate; how many trade wards a city holds, and of which trades beyond these five, is a GUESS.
  • A town below a city draws no trade ward.
    No page we read speaks of settlement by trade below the castle town.

Every provincial city keeps a set of these works.

The rule the map follows: every provincial city keeps

  • a brewery, drawn as the largest commercial building in the place, a convention: a vat hall about 50 to 62 ft on its long side, the span of the Edo-period halls (How big was a brewery's vat hall before modern times?), a shopfront, a rice storehouse and its own private well, sited on good water and left out of every count of public wells;
  • a dye works whose rinsing and drying yard stands on water, within about 120 ft of a stream, channel, canal, pond or moat, and rinses to the drainage side, never into a channel that supplies the fields;
  • an oil press toward the edge of town;
  • a pawnshop, told by its walled court of two storehouses for pledges;
  • a bathhouse with a fenced fuel yard behind it;
  • a kiln works, strictly outside the walls;
  • a farrier on a caravan yard at a gate, within 250 ft of a stables and at least 6 ft clear of every other building (How our maps draw smithies and farriers).

Three works are drawn only where the place calls for them:

  • a river-port city keeps a riverside lumber yard within about 180 ft of the bank, touching the water but never over it, since stacked timber stands on dry ground; a landlocked city has none;
  • a town keeps a farrier only if it stands on an Imperial road, a relay town working courier and caravan animals hard enough to keep a forge where an ordinary market town does not;
  • a charcoal district or an iron district is drawn only where the map declares one.

The tanning yard is the one work of this kind that a place smaller than a city draws too (Tanning yards - hides come from fallen draft stock, and water is the gate).

  • A landlocked city keeps its dyers, but only a river port keeps a lumber yard.
    Dyeing needs water to rinse in, not water to carry its goods, so both provincial city maps, Tango and Nagahara, keep a dye works. Timber is the one trade that needs water transport at scale, so only the river port, Nagahara, keeps a lumber yard.
  • How big is a dyer's drying yard?
    About 2,000 to 5,000 sq ft, with its bolts of cloth hung 35 to 40 ft long. The vats and the boiling hearth fit inside a deep shop-house; the drying does not. Both figures, and that fit, are a GUESS: the one page we read on dyers describes the vats but gives no yard's area and no bolt's length.
  • How big is a lumber yard, and where does it stand?
    About 3,000 to 8,000 sq ft, at the edge of town near the downstream gate or the wharf. Both are a GUESS: the page on Edo's timber quarter gives the whole district's 90,000 tsubo, not one yard's area or where it stood.
  • How big is an oil-press barn?
    About 40 to 60 by 25 to 30 ft, toward the edge of town, sized to hold a press log, a roasting pan and a mill that may want an ox or a stream (Does a town keep an oil press, and what does the press need?). Its size and its siting are a GUESS.
  • Our maps draw a pawnshop as an ordinary shop with more storehouses.
    A Buddhist monastery that lent money often kept the pledges in a storehouse, and our maps give the lay pawnshop the same, a GUESS, so the pawnshop multiplies the ordinary storehouse glyph rather than drawing a new kind of building. In the Yashio villages a pawnshop was often combined with another trade.
  • How many bathhouses does a place draw?
    One for each full 2,000 inhabitants, plus a chance of one more equal to the remainder, and never fewer than one: a seat of 2,500 keeps one and rolls a quarter chance of a second, and a seat of 4,000 keeps exactly two. The 2,000 is the sparse end of Edo's own ratio, more than 600 bathhouses for 1.0 to 1.2 million inhabitants (How many bathhouses does a place keep, and does a county town have one?).
  • Our maps draw a bathhouse as an ordinary street-front building with a furnace, a chimney and a woodpile behind it.
    No page we read gives a plan of the building or its woodpile, so the fuel yard's form is a GUESS.

Works with a fire or a nuisance keep their distance from what they endanger, at one of four distances. Each distance answers a different hazard rather than a different degree of the same one, and the four are reasoned against one another rather than measured from a record: only the 30 ft carries a derivation of its own (Is a fire gap kept round a charcoal yard?).

The rule the map follows: nuisance separations come in four distances:

  • about 6 ft between an attended open forge and anything that burns - the farrier's shed against hay and timber;
  • 30 ft around an unattended charcoal stack, about one flame-height clear of a stack fully alight;
  • 60 ft around a kiln body and around a refining forge, the distance for a very large attended fire, one being stoked and watched;
  • 120 ft between a nuisance carried on the air - the crematory, the tanning yard - and any dwelling not of the caste that works it.

Using the 120 ft for charcoal as well was considered and declined: it defends against smell over distance, and would push a charcoal yard off the cart route it exists to serve.
