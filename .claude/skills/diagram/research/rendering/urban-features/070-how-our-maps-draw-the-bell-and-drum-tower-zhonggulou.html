How our maps draw the bell-and-drum tower (zhonggulou)

Every walled seat on our maps - a provincial city or a walled town - draws exactly one combined bell-and-drum tower at a corner of its main street crossing: about 36 ft square in a city and about 30 ft in a town, a heavy masonry platform carrying a timber pavilion with the drum and the bell, built visibly heavier than the braced-frame fire towers. The tower follows the wall, not the population: a town without a wall draws none.

  • How big is the tower our maps draw?
    Between 30 and 45 ft on a side, about one rowhouse's width: about 36 ft in a city and about 30 ft in a town, a GUESS within the band. The band is set by Pingyao's county-seat Market Tower, about 38 ft square. The towers stood out by their height, not their plan.
  • The tower stands at the main street crossing, on a corner rather than astride the street.
    Xingcheng's and Dai county's towers stand at the exact center of the town, at the crossing of its main streets. Setting ours on a corner of the crossing is a GUESS. It stands near the magistrate's compound, not inside it.
  • The official compound's own drum tower, the qiaolou, is not drawn.
    It is a recognized variant, a building of the official compound itself.
  • A garrison city may draw the tower on a platform astride the street, 50-66 ft square.
    That is the form of Xingcheng, a Ming garrison city, whose platform is 21 m (~69 ft) square with its passages running through it. The drawn size is a GUESS, and the form is garrison-town fabric, not a seat of about 3,000 inhabitants, so it is kept as a variant for a garrison city.
  • A capital may take the full pair, a drum tower and a bell tower.
    The paired towers facing each other are the fuller form, as at Beijing. A capital's time bells are at How many time bells does a capital keep, and where do they hang?
  • A town without a wall draws no tower.
    It has no gates to close, and its time signal is its monastery's bell, as most of Edo's time bells hung at temples. That most provincial towns likewise relied on a temple bell is this project's extension of Edo's case, a GUESS. The bell is implied within the monastery's precinct and has no glyph of its own, as on the Hoshizora map.
  • The fire towers do not stand in for the time tower.
    Fire watch and timekeeping were separate offices (see Fire watch towers and firefighting gear (hinomi yagura)), so our fire towers do not cover the timekeeping and the curfew.
  • The tower's caption reads "drum/bell" over "tower".
    The drawn building is the combined tower, both instruments in one pavilion.

One tower is loud enough for any seat our maps draw.

  • How far does the tower's sound have to carry on our maps?
    On the Tango map the farthest resident inside the wall stands about 500 m (~1,640 ft) from the tower, and the farthest drawn building in the gate suburbs about 790 m (~2,592 ft). On the Nagahara map the two are about 435 m (~1,427 ft) and 920 m (~3,018 ft).
  • The towers of history carried much farther than that.
    Edo's Kokucho time bell was paid for by some four hundred blocks within earshot, a radius of about 1.2-1.4 km (~3,937-4,593 ft) on this project's arithmetic, a GUESS, in a far denser and noisier city. Beijing's drum and bell towers struck the evening for an inner city about 6.5 km (~21,325 ft) by 5.3 km (~17,388 ft).
  • How loud would the tower be at the edge of a seat?
    A large bell or great drum runs about 110-130 dB near the tower. At a city edge 500-730 m (~1,640-2,395 ft) off, it arrives about 40 dB above a night-time noise floor of about 25-35 dBA, before modern times, when the watches matter most - loud, not merely audible, everywhere inside the walls and the suburbs, and its low notes bend over one-story roofs. These figures are a GUESS: no page we read gives them.
  • When would a seat need a second tower?
    Only past about 2-3 km (~6,562-9,843 ft) of daytime town noise, this project's estimate, a GUESS. So even a capital of some 12,000 inhabitants, about a mile across, needs no second tower to be heard. Where a city outgrew one bell, Edo multiplied its fee-district bells, while China kept one great bell-and-drum set and sent night watchmen with clappers and gongs through the streets to announce the watches. A capital on our maps follows China's pattern and still draws one set.
