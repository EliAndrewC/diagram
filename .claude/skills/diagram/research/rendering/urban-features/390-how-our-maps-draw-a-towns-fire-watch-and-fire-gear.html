How our maps draw a town's fire watch and fire gear

Our maps draw a walled town's fire watch as one tower or one watch house with a ladder on its roof, and a city's as a few towers spread among its commoner quarters. An open town, an unwalled seat and a village draw none. Nothing else of a town's defense against fire is drawn: the rain tubs are too small to show at a town's scale, no page we read puts a fire pond in a street, and the firebreak is left off (How our maps leave out firebreaks (hiyokechi)).

  • Only a walled town or a city draws a fire watch.
    This follows this project's reading that a packed core behind a wall is where a fire grows great and is kept in, and that the open ground of an unwalled town breaks a fire - both GUESSES, since no page we read says either.
  • A walled town draws one fire watch, as a tower or as a watch house with a ladder and bell - a knob.
    The form is rolled per settlement between the two the history shows: a free-standing fire-watch tower, or a watch house carrying a ladder and bell on its roof. By Edo's ratio of one tower to about ten wards, a town of a few wards falls below one tower. A free-standing tower stands clear of the rampart, apart from the guard towers on the rampart itself.
  • A provincial city draws at least two fire towers.
    A real city kept many, so each tower on the map stands for a quarter's whole fire watch - a map drawing convention. In practice that is one tower amid each major commoner quarter: three for a city with laborer, servant and merchant quarters to cover.
  • A fire watch stands in a commoner quarter, never in the samurai ward.
    It stands among the laborer and merchant blocks, on ground with sightlines: a cleared block or an open seam between clusters of buildings.
  • The fire watch is placed last, into a gap the finished map already has.
    So adding it moves nothing else. The townsmen's tower stood beside a ward gate at the end of a street; siting it on an open gap instead is a map drawing convention.
  • No two fire towers stand within about 690 ft of each other.
    That is one watch radius. Taking a tower's useful watch to be its own neighborhood of rooftops is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • Each fire tower stands amid the district it watches.
    A floor on the distance between towers is not enough by itself: two towers a generous gap apart can both stand on one side of the center and leave the city's largest mass of roofs with no tower amid it. So every commoner dwelling inside the walls belongs to its nearest tower, and a tower stands within the larger of 0.9 of that district's radius and one watch radius of the district's center of mass.
  • A fire tower keeps at least 15 ft of open ground all round, and the same from every wellhead.
    An open braced tower's footing and ladder stand off its neighbors' eaves, and it must never block the court a quarter draws its water from.
  • A fire tower is drawn as an open braced frame with a red bell.
    The open legs are history: a town's tower was not allowed to have its legs boarded in, and was kept lower than the brigade's. The bracing and the bell's red are a map drawing convention, so that the tower reads apart from the solid guard towers on the rampart: a fire tower stands among the roofs it watches, not on the wall.
  • A fire tower is drawn about 26 ft square, a map drawing convention.
    No page we read gives a tower's footprint.
  • A town's rain tubs are not drawn.
    A corner tub at most some 3 1/2 ft across is about one pixel at a town map's 3 ft to the pixel, so it is left off - a map drawing convention of omission. The ward's fire gear was kept inside its watch house, which is at How our maps draw the night watch and ward gates (kido); a building plan's own tubs are at How our maps draw fire water and the fireproof storehouses (dozō).
  • No fire pond is drawn.
    No page we read describes one in a town street.
  • A village or hamlet draws no fire-watch tower, fire ladder or fire pond.
    The bell on a ladder came with the towns' fire brigades, the towers standing in the countryside now are modern, and a village fought its fires with its young men's association and five-household groups, which leave nothing to draw. For the village this is a GUESS, since no page we read shows a fixed fire apparatus there either way.
  • An unwalled seat draws no fire tower.
    Its defense against fire is carried by the fireproof storehouses of the intendant's compound, the headman's house and the merchants' houses. A small bell frame at the intendant's compound would be the one defensible addition, and drawing nothing is as accurate; the maps draw nothing.
