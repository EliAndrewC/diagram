How our maps draw smithies and farriers

A village either keeps one smithy or none. A county town draws one, for its master smith, on its shop rows, and a provincial city draws its smiths together on a street of their own. A farrier's shed and apron, where a horse is shod, stand only where horses gather: the caravan yard at a city gate and the relay town on an Imperial road. The farrier is one of the provincial city's trade works listed at How our maps draw shops and trades in towns and villages.

  • Whether a village keeps a smith is a knob, rolled per village from the map's seed.
    About one village in two keeps one smithy; the rest keep none. The share is a GUESS: no page we read counts the villages with a smith.
  • A hamlet keeps no smith.
    A GUESS: the trade needs more customers than a handful of households.
  • A village smithy is drawn as one of the village's ordinary houses.
    It stands among them, fronting a lane, clear of its neighbors by the ordinary gap between houses; its size and its gap are GUESSES, since no page we read gives a gap round a village forge. The one plan we have, the museum's 21 by 18 ft, was built from a workshop surveyed in modern times and undated, and this project draws nothing attested only in modern times.
  • A village smith who shoes builds no shoeing shed.
    He trims hooves and ties on straw from his own small workshop.
  • A county town draws one smithy, for its master smith, on its shop rows.
    It takes a shop-house's plot, and stands about 21 ft wide on the street, the frontage of the early-Edo core block of the Sakai gunsmith's house; its undated side blocks are not counted. It runs as deep as the shop-houses beside it, a GUESS, since no page from before 1868 we read gives a town smithy's depth; the 18 ft of the modern village model is not used.
  • A provincial city draws its smiths together, along one street of its commoners' quarter.
    That is what the castle towns did, setting their craftsmen in streets by trade.
  • How many smiths line a city's smiths' street?
    Between about 8 and about 28, rising with the city's size: 8 for a small castle town like Gujo Hachiman, and 26 to 28 for one the size of Kofu. The number is a degree between the two towns counted; how a given city's size maps onto that range is a GUESS.
  • Swordsmiths are not drawn apart from other smiths.
    No page we read separates them on the ground.

The farrier is drawn for this setting's shod horses, a deliberate deviation from Japan (Smiths and farriers (kajiya)).

  • A town smith who also shoes is drawn as an ordinary shop.
    Shoeing changes his work, not his premises, so no new establishment joins the shop rows. In the West a farrier worked from a forge with a yard and a blacksmith from a small shop; a forge with a stable yard does not earn its ground at a city's scale, in this project's drafting judgment.
  • A farrier's shed and apron are drawn only where horses gather.
    At a city gate's caravan yard and in an Imperial-road relay town. Qing Beijing's shoeing shops stood the same way, mostly near the horse inns and the horse doctors, most with a frame before the door to tie the animals to.
  • The farrier's shed is not built against the stables.
    An open forge against a range of hay and timber stalls is a fire the yard would not survive, so the shed keeps the fire gap between wooden service buildings that the buildings research gives, about 6-8 ft, measured from its whole footprint, shed and apron - deliberately cautious, since the shed stands at the apron's far end. No page we read speaks of setting a forge apart from a stable range.
  • The apron carries an ox-shoeing frame, drawn in its Western form.
    This setting's draft animal is mostly the ox, and oxen were shod in north China. The frame is the Western one that slung an ox to shoe it, about 7 by 4 ft, a size of our own, drawn at its real size. The Western form is drawn because no page we read shows an East Asian frame that slung an ox before modern times. A cloven hoof taking two plates a foot is a GUESS from the hoof's form.
  • How big are the farrier's shed and apron?
    The shed is 18-20 ft, the size of a village smithy, and the apron a horse's length (~8 ft) with room to lead one in and turn it - both GUESSES, drawn at that size and not enlarged to read better, since no page we read gives a smithy plan or an apron's depth.
  • A provincial city draws exactly one farrier, at a gate's caravan yard.
    Not one per gate. This setting keeps few horses - peasants work oxen, and the horse is a samurai's and the Imperial service's animal - so farriery is not a mass trade, and the count does not rise with the population as the bathhouses do.
  • A town draws a farrier only if it stands on an Imperial road.
    With the waystations about 10 miles apart, plenty of towns are not on the network, and that unevenness is the point: the rule keys on the road, not on the town's size.
  • A village or a hamlet never draws a farrier.
    Too few horses pass through to keep one.
  • A county magistracy keeps no forge of its own.
    It keeps only 2-4 horses, per the buildings research, so the smith walks over from town.
