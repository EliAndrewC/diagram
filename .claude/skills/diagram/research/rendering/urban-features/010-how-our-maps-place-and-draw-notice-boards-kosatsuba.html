How our maps place and draw notice boards (kosatsuba)

Every settlement on our maps, down to a hamlet, carries a notice board. It stands beside the busiest main way its map has, its face turned broadside to the traffic, as the boards stood where people passed in greatest numbers. It is drawn as a roofed frame 16 ft long and 6 ft deep, the size of the boards measured at two post towns, on a stone footing inside a fence. A city draws several boards and labels one.

  • Even a hamlet carries a board.
    The record reads the sources as putting a board in every town and village, and a board needs only one reader in a place: the headman copied out the circulars and had to read and write. So the maps give a board even to a hamlet of 50 inhabitants.
  • At a hamlet or a village the board stands 6 ft from the edge of the way.
    Every site the record names is on a way - a verge, the front of a gate, the foot of a bridge - and never on open ground set back from it. Where no roadside seat fits, and in towns and cities, the board may stand anywhere within 60 ft of the way. The 60 ft is not a finding: it was the first placing search's radius.
  • The board is squared to the way it faces.
    Its 7 by 3 ft face meets the traffic broadside rather than edge-on, within 30 degrees of some stretch of that way inside the siting distance. That still shows about seven eighths of the face, and it clears the worst case on the drawn maps, a board taking a road bend's bearing about 18 degrees off its nearest stretch. The face's size and the 30 degrees are this project's own; the 30 degrees is a calibration against the drawn maps.
  • A city draws a set of boards and labels one.
    The principal board stands at the central market, and there is another on the approach to every main gate, each within about 800 ft of its gate. The label goes to whichever board has room for it, since an unlabeled board fits a tight gate verge that a labeled one cannot. The 800 ft is a calibration against the drawn maps.

The map chooses the board's seat as the people who set up a board did: by the passers-by it would meet. Nakatsugawa's board was set at a crossroads some 20 m (~66 ft) up the slope from where its replica stands, so that more people would see it.

  • Every board is sited by the map's own search, never picked by hand.
    A hand-picked seat loses the traffic the board exists for: on the two town maps, the seats the search finds stand among 19 and 29 buildings within 250 ft, against 3 and 10 for the seats a hand would choose.
  • The caption counts as part of the seat.
    The drawn board is small and fits almost anywhere, but its caption does not, and the busiest frontage is exactly where there is least room for one. A search for ground big enough for both walks away from the traffic: one such seat stood across the bridge from the town, 120 ft past the last shop, among 8 buildings within 250 ft against 23 on the high street. So the search scores the caption instead: a seat whose caption fits outranks any whose caption does not, and among those the busiest wins. A board with nowhere to put a caption is still placed.
  • Whether a board stands on the traffic is judged by a person.
    There is no mathematical rule for "lots of foot traffic", so each finished map's siting is reviewed by eye.
  • The traffic is the one reason the map follows.
    A village divided between two lords once set its board at about the same distance from each half's headman's house; our maps draw no such divided village, so they site every board for the traffic.
  • The board is the last thing placed, after the map is cropped to its frame.
    The people who decided where a board went looked at what already stood in their settlement, and might even move a board already set up, so the board is placed once everything else on the map is drawn.
  • The board clears no ground.
    No one needed to clear ground to put up a board at the side of a path. The one ground a real board claimed was its own site, closed in with a palisade, a stone wall or a bank of turf, and no page we read says a board's site was cleared of trees.
  • A board may stand under a tree's canopy, because its label is drawn above every crown.
    The label says where the board is even where a crown hides it, on the picture of a map as much as the interactive one. On all five hamlet maps checked, the caption is drawn above every tree crown.
  • The board stands on a main way.
    Every road is a main way, and so is a street the map declares as its avenue from the gate to the magistrate's office; where a map declares either, the search looks only along those, so a board on a side lane cannot arise.
  • A map with no main way is exempt.
    A web of lanes, or a town none of whose streets is marked as main, has nothing "main" to stand on, so the search's count of buildings near each seat stands in for the judgment.
  • The punishment ground is not held to the main way.
    The board must catch the eye of everyone passing through; the punishment ground need not. That is this project's choice for the map, not a finding about where punishment grounds stood.

Once seated, the board is drawn at the size the record measured.

  • A board is drawn as a roofed frame 16 ft long and 6 ft deep, on a stone footing inside a fence.
    Its long side runs parallel to the way it faces, and the footing is about 2 ft wider all round, with a fence line at its edge. The size is that of the frames measured at Kanagawa-juku and Totsuka-juku, about 16 to 18 ft along the road and 5 to 6 ft deep.
  • A village's or a hamlet's board is drawn at the same size - a GUESS.
    No village board was measured on any page we read.
  • The siting still measures the board by its 7 by 3 ft face.
    That face, this project's own figure, is shorter than every frame measured; the drawn frame is the larger.
  • At city scale the board is drawn as an oversized marker - a drawing convention.
    At town scale the true size can be drawn; at city scale a frame about 16 to 18 ft along the road and 5 to 6 ft deep is too small to see, so a larger marker stands for it and the label carries the read.

A magistrate's compound draws a board of its own.

  • A magistrate's compound stands a freestanding board at its own gate - a GUESS.
    A Chinese county office posted edicts, the magistrate's own notices and bans - wanted notices among them - and verdicts on the walls flanking its gate, so a "bounty board" at the gate has a counterpart before modern times. In Japan the boards hung before the gates of village officials' houses, but no page we read puts one at an intendant's office or a magistracy. A freestanding board rather than the gate's walls is joined from the two.
