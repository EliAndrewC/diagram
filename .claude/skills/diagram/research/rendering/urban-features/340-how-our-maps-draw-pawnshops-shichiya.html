How our maps draw pawnshops (shichiya)

A county seat on our maps keeps three or four pawnbrokers, and a city more, in proportion. Most are an ordinary shop-house that also takes pledges, and what shows on the map is the store where the pledges are kept. Every provincial city keeps at least one by the rule at How our maps draw shops and trades in towns and villages.

  • Why does a county seat draw three or four?
    Hachijo, a crossroads village of 856 near Edo, had three, and at that rate a county seat of about 1,200 has three or four.
  • Most pawnbrokers are drawn as an ordinary shop-house.
    Pawnbroking was often a sideline to another trade, so the shop itself is a shop like any other; only the pledge store sets it apart.

The pledge store's form is a knob, rolled for each settlement between the two forms the record knows.

  • The Japanese form is one to three earthen storehouses in a walled court behind the shop.
    Keeping pledges called for large, solid storehouses. The count of one to three and the walled court are a GUESS: no page we read gives either.
  • The Chinese form is a larger, storied, high-walled building on the street, with a smaller court.
    So the Qing pawnshops of Qinxiang and Yaowan stood, up to about 59 by 66 ft for a large one.
  • A storehouse is drawn with the ordinary storehouse glyph.
    A drawing convention: the pawnshop multiplies the storehouse rather than drawing a new kind of building.
  • How big is either court?
    The size is a GUESS: no page we read gives the size of a pledge store or its court.
