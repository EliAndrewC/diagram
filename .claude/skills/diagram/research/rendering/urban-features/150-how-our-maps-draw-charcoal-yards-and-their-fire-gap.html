How our maps draw charcoal yards and their fire gap

A charcoal yard on our maps is a tamped cart yard 88 by 58 ft, with roofed stacking sheds on its far side and a weighing floor at its road edge, where every bale is weighed before the tally is written. It draws no cooling ground, because before modern times charcoal was cooled at the kiln, and it keeps no measured fire gap: a 30 ft gap can be reasoned from modern wildfire guidance, but no premodern figure for one was found.

  • A charcoal store is drawn as a supervised, tallied depot, not a shop's back room.
    Charcoal was an industrial fuel the Song state supplied to its iron-smelting households, and in Japan the stock of a wholesaler who was also a warehouse-keeper, so this project draws a store of it as a depot the county office supervises and tallies. It is the relationship the county magistracy's own plan draws: "charcoal and bar iron, sealed here, never owned".
  • How big is a charcoal yard?
    88 by 58 ft, a GUESS pitched against the other bulk-goods yards our maps draw, the lumber yard's 90 by 60 ft and the dye yard's 80 by 52 ft. A yard has one or more stacking sheds, each 34 by 18 ft, and a weighing floor of 16 by 14 ft.
  • The yard draws no cooling ground.
    Standing fresh charcoal in the open for a day is today's handling guidance, and the older record cools it in or beside the kiln, so the charcoal reaches a town cooled and the yard is covered sheds and a weighing floor.
  • Every bale is weighed on the weighing floor before it is tallied.
    A bale of no standard size cannot be traded by count, in this project's reading, though no page we read says a dealer weighed the bales at sale. That is also why the office's hold on the trade is a written record rather than ownership: a seal on the tally is worth something because the quantity is not plain from the load.
  • The weighing floor weighs on a steelyard, not a two-pan balance.
    It is a wooden beam with a large hook at one end for the bale and a weight hung on the other, slid along the beam until it balances. That is the historically accurate form.
  • What does the steelyard hang from?
    A frame or post on the weighing floor, a GUESS: no page we read describes how a heavy steelyard was hung at a depot.
  • How far does a charcoal yard stand from the houses?
    No measured distance is kept; where a map sets a yard is a GUESS. No premodern figure for a gap round a charcoal store was found, and what Edo did with a stock of fuel it feared was to move the trade: after the fire of 1641 it gathered its timber dealers onto Eitaijima.
  • Where does a 30 ft fire gap come from?
    It is reasoned, not found - a modern figure. It fits among this project's other separations - about 6 ft between an attended forge and hay, 120 ft for a nuisance carried on the air - and today's wildfire guidance, whose safety-zone rule, for people rather than timber, is four flame heights. 30 ft is about one flame height clear of a fully-involved stack of perhaps 10-12 ft, the stack's height a GUESS, and a rule of thumb for the piloted ignition of timber that no page we read gives.
  • Our maps keep no fire gap of a set width round a charcoal yard.
    The 30 ft rests on modern guidance, with no premodern figure beside it, so it is not drawn.
  • A charcoal store in a plastered storehouse stands at the ordinary spacing of the buildings round it.
    A storehouse was built to protect what it held against fire, and kept gunpowder behind its wall, so its own wall does for its charcoal what a gap would do for an open stack. On the Ubame map the county office keeps its charcoal in one. That charcoal was kept in a storehouse at all, and at no more than the ordinary spacing, is a GUESS: no page we read says so or gives the spacing.
