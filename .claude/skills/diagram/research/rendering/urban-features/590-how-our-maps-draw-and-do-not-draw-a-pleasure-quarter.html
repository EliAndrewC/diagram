How our maps draw (and do not draw) a pleasure quarter

No map draws a walled pleasure quarter, and no map draws a house of their own for an inn town's serving women. In the setting, prostitution is illegal by Imperial decree, a law honored more in breach than observance, and the trade goes on in a city's geisha houses, outside the law. That is also the unwalled form most Japanese pleasure districts took.

  • A capital's entertainment district is drawn as teahouses, geisha houses and playhouses among the townspeople's streets.
    Nothing in the setting's notes sets out a licensed quarter, so none is drawn. For a capital this is a deliberate deviation from the history: Edo, Kyoto and Osaka each had a licensed quarter.
  • Which form would a licensed quarter take, if a capital had one?
    None is drawn. Were a capital to license one, the history offers three forms, which would be rolled per capital as a knob: inside the town with a fence on one side, at the town's edge with an earthen wall, or cut off beyond the town by a wall and a ditch with a single gate. Each would stand on new ground, and its size would fall between the Yoshiwara's two sites, 720 to 840 ft square, taking the smaller of the two figures the record gives for the second site.
  • An inn town draws nothing more for its serving women.
    They lived and worked in the inns, which kept them as employees, so the inns already on the map are the feature; there is no separate house, quarter or gate to draw.
