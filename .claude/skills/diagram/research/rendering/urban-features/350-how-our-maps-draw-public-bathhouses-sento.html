How our maps draw public bathhouses (sento)

Our maps draw public bathhouses only where townspeople lived: a county seat keeps exactly one, a city one for every 1,300 to 2,000 inhabitants, and the farmhouses around them none, because country households each had a bath of their own. Each bathhouse is a street-front building with its furnace at the back and a fenced yard of firewood behind, because a bathhouse had to secure its firewood and water.

  • How many bathhouses does a county seat draw?
    One. Hachijo, a village of 856 in what is now Yashio city, kept the only bathhouse of nineteen villages, and a county seat of about 1,200 is larger and, like it, the chief place and traffic hub of its district.
  • The farmhouses around a county seat draw no bathhouse.
    Country households each had a bath of their own.
  • How many bathhouses does a city draw?
    One for every 1,300 to 2,000 inhabitants, the figure chosen at random for each city within that band, and never fewer than one. The band runs from the provincial castle town of Takada, about one per 1,300, to Edo, one per 1,700 to 2,000.
  • Why is a bathhouse drawn with a fuel yard behind it?
    A bathhouse had to secure firewood and water, and of the two only the firewood takes room on the ground: a stock of it stacked behind the furnace, fenced from the street. The layout is our GUESS.
  • A bathhouse is drawn about 40 by 30 ft, its fuel yard 1,000 to 2,000 sq ft.
    The building is sized to hold a single bath shared by men and women, with its changing floor, and the yard a season's stacked firewood. Both sizes are a GUESS: no page we read gives either.
