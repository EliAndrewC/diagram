How our maps draw roofed playhouses (shibai goya)

Our maps give roofed playhouses only to a capital, three to five of them, drawn either on a Japanese theater street or inside the entertainment precincts of a city of Song-dynasty China. Below a capital, towns and provincial cities stage their plays in a temporary hut or on a shrine's stage (how our maps site a town's theater stage). That limit is our own GUESS, explained below.

  • Our maps give a capital three to five roofed playhouses.
    The count is a knob, chosen at random for each capital between Edo's three and Osaka's five.
  • Our maps draw a capital's playhouses in one of two forms, chosen at random for each capital.
    In the Japanese form they stand side by side on one theater street. In the form of Song-dynasty China, the performance halls (goulan) stand inside the city's entertainment precincts (washe).
  • Each playhouse on the theater street is drawn about 80 by 145 ft.
    That is the size of the Kanamaruza, the oldest surviving playhouse.
  • Why do our maps give no town or provincial city a roofed playhouse?
    It is a GUESS. Roofed playhouses stood mostly in the great cities, though the Kanamaruza, the oldest surviving playhouse, shows that a shrine's temple-gate town could keep one. Our maps draw none below a capital, so that a playhouse stays a mark of the great city.
