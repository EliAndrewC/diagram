How our maps draw timber yards and log booms

A town or city on our maps keeps its timber in one of two forms, a knob rolled from the map's seed: a timber yard on the water, or a retail timber dealer's shop. On the water, the stock is drawn only in the forms recorded before modern times - rafts moored along a slack bank, or a pond dug off the river - and never as a floating log boom.

  • A town or city with a river or a channel may keep its timber on the water.
    It is drawn as a yard on the bank with the logs held in the water beside it, as on a river port's waterfront.
  • Any town, river or not, may instead keep a retail timber dealer.
    Retail dealers were found in every region, so a town without a river is not without timber; it is without the log pond.
  • What does a dealer's yard look like?
    A shop with a small yard of stacked sawn timber behind it. The yard and its size are a GUESS: the dealer is recorded as keeping sawyers, but no page we read says where they sawed or how the timber was stored.
  • A timber city's stock is drawn as raft-mats moored along a bank, or as a pond off the river.
    Those are the forms on record before modern times: rafts moored at a stretch of bank, and ponds joined to the waterways by which the rafts came in and went out. The moored stock lies close along a slack bank, its bank edge on the shore line, off the lumber yard it feeds; a pond is dug off the river and joined to it by a canal.
  • No floating fence and no pile clusters are drawn round the stock.
    A fence of floats strung on a rope is on record in East Asia only from modern times, and our maps draw nothing known only from modern times.
  • How much of a navigated river may the moored stock take?
    About a third of its width, and never more than two fifths, so the boats keep their channel - as the St. Croix's boom works had to leave a navigation channel beside their log channels. The shares are this project's choice, a GUESS: no source gives one.
  • The catch rope (tsunaba) is never drawn at a port.
    The rope across the water belongs upstream, where the loose logs came out of the gorge and were made up into rafts, and a rope across a river would close it to boats.
  • On the Minami map, rafts moor along the Hayakawa's bank.
    The Hayakawa is 120 ft wide and navigated, with a wharf, a dock basin and a cargo canal, so the city's timber lies moored along its bank. The catch rope is off the map, at the Fox catch-works at the gorge mouth upstream, where the loose drive becomes the rafts that reach the city.
