How our maps draw the offices of the six ministries (liubu)

Our maps draw each of the six ministries as a walled compound of its own, in a provincial city as in a domain capital, all six alike in size. Below the capital, history gives the six offices no compounds of their own - they were rooms in a yamen - so a provincial city's ministry compounds, and their size, are a GUESS at the setting's own arrangement. A domain capital sets its six in a government ward outside the castle, along the approach avenue to the castle's main gate.

  • How big is a provincial city's ministry office?
    About 110 by 80 ft to 140 by 95 ft, the range the drawn provincial cities give: a GUESS, since history puts these offices inside a yamen rather than in compounds of their own. Minami draws each ministry at 114 by 78 ft, Nagahara and Tango at 130 by 90 ft, and Tango its Ministry of Rites at 140 by 95 ft.
  • A ministry office holds a hall of business, its bureaus' and clerks' rooms and a document storehouse, and no treasury or granary.
    The Ministry of Revenue worked through its bureaus, and the things it managed - a mint, treasuries, granaries - were offices of their own under it. Our maps take the other five ministries to have been organized the same way.
  • A provincial city's ministries stand around the governor's compound.
    Where they stand, and how they front the street, is set with the compound (How our maps place and draw the governor's compound (yamen)).

A domain capital places its ministries differently, by the lord's castle.

  • A domain capital's six ministries stand in a government ward outside the castle's outermost gate, flanking the approach avenue (ote-suji): a deliberate deviation.
    A Japanese domain's offices began in the lord's palace, as Matsumoto's did; our maps take the Chinese capital's arrangement instead, for three reasons that reinforce one another:
    • It is where both traditions go as a government grows: Beijing's ministries flanked the approach to the palace, outside its walls, and Matsumoto moved its county and town offices out of the castle into the town when the palace proved too small. Since our capital maps must reconstruct so much, they take the arrangement both traditions point to.
    • It makes the castle's front the map's axis, as the castle town's main roads ran to the castle's front (Domain capitals: the daimyo's castle town (jokamachi)). A ceremonial avenue lined with paired ministry compounds is the plainest way for a map to say the lord's seat rather than merely a large city.
    • It keeps the ministries out of the castle, whose inside the capital maps leave undrawn, and lets a ministry compound be drawn just as it is in a provincial city.
  • In a domain capital, the domain school stands on the approach avenue beside the ministries.
    A domain school was founded in the domain's own territory to educate its retainers' children, so our maps set it with the government it serves.
  • A domain capital's ministry office is at most about half again as large as a provincial city's: this project's judgment between the two traditions.
    The Chinese evidence is that a higher seat's six offices grew a little, three bays to each at a governor-general's yamen against generally two at a county's, in a plan broadly the same as a county's. The Japanese evidence is that they did not grow: the shogunate's offices at Edo, Nagasaki and Takayama were of about one size (Offices of the six ministries (liubu)). Neither gives a higher seat's office more than half again a lower one's, so our maps take half again as the ceiling, and a capital's ministry may be drawn at any size from a provincial city's up to it.
  • A larger city's government grows mainly by the institutions it adds.
    The castle, the chancellery, the Imperial Magistrate's compound, the domain school and the granaries come with a capital, while its ministries grow little, since this project reads a ministry at any level as a bureau of clerks and papers: even the Ministry of Revenue worked through bureaus, its mint, treasuries and granaries offices of their own under it.
  • The drawn sizes do not yet follow this rule.
    A domain capital's ministry is drawn at 224 by 148 ft, about three times the area of the provincial cities' 114 by 78 to 140 by 95 ft and about twice what the rule allows. At half again Nagahara's and Tango's 130 by 90 ft it would be about 160 by 110 ft.
  • All six ministries of a city are the same size.
    This project reads the six ministers as peers of one rank, so their offices are drawn alike, as a yamen's six rooms were alike, generally two bays each. A city where one ministry plainly outbuilds the others - a Ministry of War swollen by a border command, a Ministry of Revenue fat on a boom - would be saying something about that city, so it is drawn only as a declared exception for that city, with its reason written down, never as the default. Tango is the one exception as drawn: its Ministry of Rites is 140 by 95 ft against its other five's 130 by 90 ft, with no reason yet recorded.
  • A domain capital's ministry captions sit on the compound; a provincial city's sit beside it: a map drawing convention.
    On a domain capital's map each caption sits on the compound in near-black, in two stacked lines, "Ministry of" over the ministry's name - as an estate's caption sits on its own compound - which a 224 by 148 ft compound drawn at 3 ft to the pixel has room for. A provincial city's compound stands in tighter streets, so its caption stays beside it.
