How our maps draw servants' quarters in the samurai ward

On our maps a samurai household's servants never get a house of their own. A large household's servants are drawn in a long, thin range on its street edge, as servants historically lived in the ranges along a residence's street front. A small household's are not drawn at all: a smaller house had a wall in place of a range, and its servants sleep in the house the map already draws.

  • A servant dwelling in a samurai ward is never a freestanding house.
    It is lodging bound to the samurai household it serves, drawn on that household's street edge as a long, thin range in the proportion of a long-house gate, which was a range with a gate cut through it.
  • How deep is the drawn range?
    About 15 ft, the depth this project takes for an ordinary retainer's range, a GUESS: no page we read measures one.
  • The smallest households draw no servant building at all.
    Their few servants are taken to sleep under the main roof, a GUESS, so they are inside the house the map draws.
  • A provincial city has about one servant household for each junior samurai household, and two for each senior.
    By the setting's budget for a provincial city, 72 of its 120 servant families are attached to samurai households - 30 to wealthy samurai at the setting's property rating 2, and 42 indentured to samurai at property rating 1 - against 60 samurai families. They are property-holding families in the setting's economy, so each is housed, counted and taxed.
  • What matters is where the servants are drawn, not how many.
    A rank of detached servant houses unattached to any samurai household reads as more commoners' houses in the samurai neighborhood - exactly what the ward's fence exists to keep out. So a servants' range is drawn only against the household it serves.
  • The range stands in for a compound wall our city maps do not draw - a deliberate deviation.
    Both Japan's evidence and China's put a household's servants inside a compound wall, but our samurai houses inside a city's wall are drawn unwalled (How our maps draw the samurai quarter and count its households (bukechi)). Historically the range was the wall: the street-facing walls of a large house were the ranges its servants lived in. So the range alone, a long, thin building hard on the street line, gives the same read at the city map's scale without walling the ward's houses.
