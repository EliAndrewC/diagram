How our maps draw a samurai's lot and house (bukeyashiki)

A samurai's lot on our maps is drawn at the size the domain of Fukui granted its holder's rank, the only ladder of lot sizes by rank we found, from about 2,000 sq ft for a junior samurai to about 35,000 for a minister or the governor. It is scaled up by how generous the town is, and the house on it covers a small part of the ground. Every lot is enclosed, by an earthen wall, a board fence or a hedge as its rank calls for, and has its own well inside the gate, except in the foot soldiers' rows at the town's edge, which share one.

  • The setting's ranks are set against Fukui's ladder by their place in the hierarchy, not by stipend.
    The setting's stipends are far smaller than the historical ones: a stipend is the rank squared, so a rank-10 minister draws 100 koku. A lot therefore cannot be read off Fukui's table by koku, and placing the ranks by their place in the hierarchy is this project's decision. A city's senior samurai take the middle of the ladder, 225 to 400 tsubo (about 8,000 to 14,200 sq ft), and its junior samurai the lower end, 55 to 96 tsubo (about 2,000 to 3,400 sq ft). A domain minister or the governor takes the lot of about 1,000 tsubo (about 35,000 sq ft) that a 1,000-koku retainer held.
  • How large is a county seat's senior staff officer's lot?
    The domain's 96 tsubo (about 3,400 sq ft), not Edo's 300. Fukui's yoriki held 96 tsubo and the Edo town magistrates' about 300 (about 10,700 sq ft). This project reads the two as one rank at two scales, a domain's and the shogun's capital's, a GUESS, and puts a county seat's officer at the domain's end.
  • How much of its lot does the house cover?
    A small part, so a lot is mostly open ground. On a senior lot the house covers about 1,200 to 2,400 sq ft: the top is the size of a senior retainer's house at Matsue, and the bottom is a GUESS. On a junior lot it covers about 700 to 1,000 sq ft, the range of the Kaga foot soldiers' houses and a low-ranking Kakunodate family's.

Fukui was not the most generous domain, so the ladder is scaled per town.

  • How much roomier can a town's lots be?
    Up to about twice the ladder's sizes. Yonezawa gave its lower samurai lots of 150 to 200 tsubo (about 5,300 to 7,100 sq ft), against Fukui's 55 to 96, and the factor of about two is a little under the gap between the middles of the two ranges. It is a calibration against the one figure from the period we found.
  • Each settlement's generosity is set for it, not rolled.
    How generous a town's lots are is a matter of more or less, not a choice between distinct forms, so each settlement is given its own factor rather than a random one.
  • Today's Aoyagi samurai estate at Kakunodate is not used to size lots.
    The Aoyagi estate is just under a hectare (~2.5 acres) today, but whether that is one lot of the Edo period or several joined is not on record, so the maps do not scale their lots to it.

How a lot is closed off follows its rank.

  • A senior house is walled with earth and entered by a long-house gate on its street side.
    The senior retainers' houses had the earthen wall and the long-house gate, so the gate is drawn as a building about 15 ft deep, as a large one in Edo is, that doubles as the gatekeeper's and the servants' quarters.
  • Whether a settlement's junior houses have board fences or hedges is a knob.
    Both are attested, so which a settlement's junior houses carry is rolled per settlement, each with a plain gate.
  • A stone wall topped by a clipped hedge, as at Chiran, is a regional third form.
    Chiran's lanes run between stone walls with clipped hedges behind them, but the maps do not draw it yet: the roll above is between the board fence and the hedge only.
  • Below the junior samurai, the foot soldiers' and the lowest-ranked houses are hedged or fenced with brushwood, with no formal gate.
    A low-ranking Kakunodate family had two posts and a brushwood fence, and the Kaga foot soldiers' plots were hedged.

Inside the gate, the maps follow the houses that survive.

  • The house stands back from the gate behind a front garden, with a storehouse near the gate.
    Kakunodate's samurai houses each have a front garden, and through the Aoyagi house's gate are a roofed well, a rice storehouse and the main entrance.
  • The kitchen wing stands to the side and rear of the house, away from the gate.
    That siting is a GUESS: no page we read names a kitchen yard or which side of the lot the service ground took.
  • Every lot in the samurai quarter has its own well, inside the fence near the gate, and the quarter has no public well.
    Kakunodate's surviving samurai houses have their own wells, while the townsmen's quarters share theirs (Communal wells and the samurai exception).
  • Do the foot soldiers' rows at the town's edge have wells of their own?
    No: the maps give them one well to a row of plots, a GUESS. Most wells were shared, and no page we read gives the foot soldiers' plots one each.
