How our maps draw a samurai's lot and house (bukeyashiki)

A samurai's lot on our maps is drawn at the size the domain of Fukui granted its holder's rank, scaled up by how generous the town is, and the house on it covers a small part of the ground. Every lot is enclosed, by an earthen wall, a board fence or a hedge as its rank calls for, and has its own well inside the gate.

  • The setting's ranks are set against Fukui's ladder by their place in the hierarchy, not by stipend.
    The setting's stipends are far smaller than the historical ones: a stipend is the rank squared, so a rank-10 minister draws 100 koku. A lot therefore cannot be read off Fukui's table by koku, and placing the ranks by their place in the hierarchy is this project's decision. A city's senior samurai take the middle of the ladder, 225 to 400 tsubo; its junior samurai the lower, 55 to 96; and a domain minister or the governor the lot of about 1,000 tsubo that a 1,000-koku retainer held.
  • A county seat's senior staff officer takes the domain's 96 tsubo, not Edo's 300.
    Fukui's yoriki held 96 tsubo and the Edo town magistrates' about 300. This project reads the two as one rank at two scales, a domain's and the shogun's capital's, a GUESS, and puts a county seat's officer at the domain's end.
  • A senior samurai's lot is about 8,000 to 14,200 sq ft, and a junior samurai's about 2,000 to 3,400.
    A senior staff officer's is about 3,400 sq ft, and the governor's manor or a minister's compound about 35,000 sq ft.
  • The house covers a small part of its lot, so a lot is mostly open ground.
    On a senior lot the house covers about 1,200 to 2,400 sq ft: the top is the size of a senior retainer's house at Matsue, and the bottom is a GUESS. On a junior lot it covers about 700 to 1,000 sq ft, the range of the Kaga foot soldiers' houses and a low-ranking Kakunodate family's.

Other domains gave their samurai more ground than Fukui did, so the ladder is scaled per town.

  • How much roomier can a town's lots be?
    Up to about twice the ladder's sizes. Yonezawa gave its lower samurai lots of 150 to 200 tsubo (about 5,300 to 7,100 sq ft), against Fukui's 55 to 96, and the factor of about two is a little under the gap between the middles of the two ranges. It is a calibration against the one figure from the period we found.
  • A town's generosity is a degree set per settlement, not a knob between forms.
    How generous a town's lots are is a place along one scale, so it is calibrated for each settlement rather than rolled.
  • The present-day Kakunodate estate is not used.
    The Aoyagi estate is just under a hectare (~2.5 acres) today, but whether that is one lot of the Edo period or several joined is not on record, so the maps do not scale their lots to it.

How a lot is closed off follows its rank.

  • A senior house is walled with earth and entered by a long-house gate on its street side.
    The senior retainers' houses had the earthen wall and the long-house gate, so the gate is drawn as a building about 15 ft deep, as a large one in Edo is, that doubles as the gatekeeper's and the servants' quarters.
  • Whether a settlement's junior houses have board fences or hedges is a knob.
    Both are attested, so which a settlement's junior houses carry is rolled per settlement, each with a plain gate. A stone wall backed by a clipped hedge, as at Chiran, is a regional third form.
  • The lowest houses are hedged or fenced with brushwood, and have no formal gate.
    A low-ranking Kakunodate family had two posts and a brushwood fence, and the Kaga foot soldiers' plots were hedged.

Inside the gate, the maps follow the houses that survive.

  • The house stands back from the gate behind a front garden, with the well and a storehouse near the gate.
    Kakunodate's samurai houses each have a front garden, and a roofed well and a rice storehouse stand just inside the Aoyagi house's gate.
  • The kitchen wing stands to the side and rear of the house, away from the gate.
    That siting is a GUESS: no page we read names a kitchen yard or which side of the lot the service ground took.
  • Every samurai lot in the ward has its own well, inside the fence near the gate, and the ward has no public well.
    The surviving provincial samurai houses have their own wells, while the townsmen's quarters share theirs (Communal wells and the samurai exception).
  • The foot soldiers' blocks at the town's edge share one well among a row of plots.
    Most wells were shared, and no page we read gives the foot soldiers' plots one each; the shared well is a GUESS.
