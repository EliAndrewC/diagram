How our maps size and place a city's drill ground and riding ground

A provincial city or county seat on our maps draws one drill ground, and a castle town or provincial city one riding ground in its samurai's ground; an archery range is drawn only where it stands outside a compound.

The rule the map follows:

  • A drill ground is about 5 to 6 acres, closed by an earth wall.
    It has a gate on one side and a small review hall with its commander's platform on the opposite side, facing across the ground, as at Chongming.
  • Why 5 to 6 acres?
    It is a calibration from the two cases the record holds: Chongming's 37.5 mu (~6 acres) and Fuzhou's more than thirty mu (~5 acres).
  • Whether the drill ground lies outside a gate or in a corner inside the wall is chosen at random for each settlement.
    Both are attested, and a city that places its great ground outside may still keep a small practice ground inside a corner, as Chongming did. Inside the wall it counts against the cap on a city's reserve ground (How densely is a quarter built, and what counts as empty ground?).
  • A riding ground is a banked, fenced strip about 600 by 60 ft.
    That is the common shape of a castle town's riding ground. Where it runs along a moat or a river bank it may be up to about 900 by 30 ft, as Ako's was. A small viewing stand stands at the middle of one long side.
  • The riding ground lies in the samurai's ground, near the castle or the governor's compound.
    That is where a castle town's samurai rode. A capital may draw more than one.
  • An archery range is about 250 by 8 ft, with a butt at one end.
    That is the fixed size of a samurai house's range.
  • Whether an archery range is drawn on the city map is chosen at random for each settlement.
    A range stood inside the castle or a residence, or in the thinly built outskirts. Inside a compound it is part of that compound's own building plan; otherwise the city map draws it in the thinly built edge of the town.
