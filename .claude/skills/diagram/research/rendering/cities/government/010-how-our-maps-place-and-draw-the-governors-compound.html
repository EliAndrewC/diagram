How our maps place and draw the governor's compound

Our provincial city maps set the governor's compound in the city's interior, with the six ministry offices around it, and draw it as walls and a gate with its interior implied. A seat of the size the surviving ones give is a whole city block or more, so the compound is drawn at its full size rather than shrunk to fit. Its gate opens to the south, as a yamen's did, and how a street reaches that gate is rolled per city between the two forms the record shows.

  • The governor's compound, the six ministry offices and a samurai neighborhood stand in the city's interior.
    They stand inside the rampart and clear of the drawn wall and its moat, as a yamen usually stood at the center of its city.
  • The compound is the grandest thing in the city.
    It is at least three times the size of any ministry office, and at least as large as any country estate.
  • Five ministries cluster around the compound, within about 1,440 ft of it.
    They thread into the samurai quarter. The distance is the maps' own rule, not a measured figure.
  • The Ministry of Rites stands apart, in the temple neighborhood.
    It oversees the setting's shrines and temples, so this project seats it among them, and there is exactly one of it. Ritual buildings did take fixed places in a Chinese city - two Confucian temples in Shanxi were both built in their city's southeast corner - but no page puts an office of rites among them; the arrangement is the setting's own.
  • Every ministry fronts a city street, within about 255 ft of one.
    In practice that means laying a short government avenue - an L wrapping the compound serves well - and seating the offices along it. A street fronted by ministries counts as a built-up street, as much as one lined with houses and shops.
  • The offices front an official avenue and never float in the middle of a block: a map drawing convention.
    It is carried over from the main street of the tatemachi castle town, which the front entrances of townhouses and samurai residences lined from the castle's main gate; no page we read places the government offices themselves along it.
  • A government office stands in its own ground.
    Ordinary city houses may touch one another, but a ministry office or the governor's compound keeps about 42 ft clear of every other building and compound.

The compound's size is rolled within the span the surviving seats give.

  • How large is the governor's compound on our maps?
    Between about 1 and 3.6 hectares (~2.5-9 acres), on the order of 330 by 330 ft to 650 by 600 ft, rolled per city from its seed within that span and never below the largest county magistrate's compound. The lower bound is Japan's one surviving provincial office, at Takayama, and the upper the smaller of the two figures for the Chinese prefectural yamen at Nanyang.
  • Why are the largest Chinese figures left out?
    A compound of 7 hectares (~17 acres) would crowd a walled provincial city of a few thousand inhabitants. That is a GUESS, a judgment of this project's, not a measured limit.
  • Japan's ancient provincial offices are left out of the span too.
    They were an older form, a laid-out district of halls rather than one household's compound, and their sizes range too widely to narrow anything.
  • The drawn provincial cities sit near the bottom of the span.
    Minami's compound is 525 by 300 ft, and Nagahara's and Tango's 436 by 366 ft, each about 1.5 hectares (~3.6 acres) - half again Takayama's, a fifth to a half of a Chinese prefecture's. That leans to the Japanese tradition, which the record takes first for the interiors of administrative compounds (administrative culture is Japan-first), and keeps the governor's compound well above a county magistrate's, which the record brackets between about 1,900 and 9,800 square meters (~20,000-105,000 sq ft) (the Mode A scale).

What stands inside is drawn only when the compound has a sheet of its own.

  • On a city map the compound is walls and a gate, its interior implied.
    The city map shows the compound's extent, not its courts.
  • Drawn as its own sheet, it takes a county magistracy's plan, enlarged by adding courts rather than by swelling the halls.
    The plan is the magistracy's (office in front, residence behind), as the Qing rule gave a senior official the full set of a yamen's parts and a junior one a reduced set. In order:
    • a front court with the gatehouse and the guards;
    • the office hall, with the courtroom as its room and the clerks' rooms flanking the court before it;
    • a second office hall behind it;
    • then the residence;
    • the archive, the storehouses and the granary on a side line of courts;
    • the shrine;
    • and the jail (where a city keeps its jail).
  • The number of courts is a degree, from a county's two to a governor-general's five.
    The Zhili governor-general's yamen ran in five courts from its gate to its rear storehouse.

The compound faces south; the street that reaches its gate takes one of two forms.

  • The governor's compound opens its gate to the south.
    A yamen's gate opened to the south.
  • Its approach is a knob, rolled per city from the seed.
    It is either an axial avenue about 30 ft wide running south from the gate with the ministries along it, as at Japan's ancient provincial office of Shimotsuke, or a cross street running east and west past the gate, with a screen wall on its far side facing the gate, as at a Ming or Qing yamen.
  • The three drawn provincial cities keep their north- and west-facing gates.
    Minami's and Nagahara's compounds open north and Tango's west. They are frozen exhibits; a generated city does not copy them.

The city's gates carry the setting's tariff.

  • A walled provincial city collects its tariff at its gates: a deviation, the setting's own.
    The setting's Ministry of Revenue inspects and tolls the caravans entering a walled city at its gates. Japan's historical barrier stations stood on the roads instead, most often at mountain passes and river banks.
  • The streets just outside a city's gates hold markets.
    The streets themselves are attested; the markets in them are this record's reading, set out at the gate market exists for traffic, not taxes and what stands outside a city gate.
