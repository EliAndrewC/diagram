How our maps house a city's garrison

Our maps give a provincial city's garrison barracks, as the setting's own city has, and draw no quarter of foot soldiers' row-houses like a Japanese castle town's. Their placing borrows from both the walled cities of Qing China and the castle towns of Edo-period Japan, so the rules below draw on each.

The rule the map follows:

  • Where a city's barracks stand is chosen at random for each city.
    Our maps draw two ways a Chinese city housed its standing troops. One is a garrison quarter in one corner of the city, laid out in rows of barracks with its own drill ground, stables and armory, as the Qing's banner garrisons lived. The other is barracks flanking the drill ground at a gate, as at Fuzhou. Both are attested and neither was the rule, so neither is our maps' only form.
  • A city large enough to have districts may spread its barracks one to a district.
    That is how the setting's own city houses its garrison.
  • Each gate carries its guard post, with the guard commander's house beside it.
    At Fukui, a castle town of Edo-period Japan, the head of the company that held a gate had their official residence beside it.
  • A city's armory and powder store stand inside its castle or governor's compound, so the city map does not draw them.
    The city map leaves that compound's interior to its own plan (How our maps draw the castle in a capital).
  • A capital may also have a powder store outside its wall, on a main road.
    The one such store found in the sources was the shogun's, on a military highway out of Edo.

What our maps leave out, and why:

  • Our maps draw no quarter of foot soldiers' row-houses (ashigaru nagaya).
    A castle town's foot soldiers lived in blocks of their own at its edge, often at its entrances, but they counted among the lower samurai. The setting's foot soldiers are peasants, levies who live as the peasants they are, so they have no claim on a samurai foot soldiers' quarter, and the city's garrison lives in its barracks.
  • If a map ever draws rows of small, identical houses, they stand at the town's edge, outside the samurai ward.
    The edge of the town is where uniform small dwellings in ranks belonged, not inside the fence of the samurai ward, where a samurai household's servants are drawn only against the household they serve (How our maps draw servants' quarters in the samurai ward).
