How our maps place and draw the governor's compound (yamen)

Our provincial city maps set the governor's compound in the city's interior, with the offices of five of the six ministries around it, and draw it as walls and a gate with its interior implied. The surviving seats of provincial government each cover a whole city block or more, and on our maps the compound is the largest enclosure in its city. Its gate opens to the south, as a yamen's did, and how a street reaches that gate is rolled per city between the two forms the record shows.

  • The governor's compound, the six ministry offices and a samurai neighborhood stand in the city's interior.
    They stand inside the rampart and clear of the drawn wall and its moat, as a yamen usually stood at the center of its city.
  • Five ministries cluster around the compound, within about 1,440 ft of it.
    They thread into the samurai quarter. The distance is the maps' own rule, not a measured figure.
  • The Ministry of Rites stands apart, in the temple neighborhood.
    It oversees the setting's shrines and temples, so this project seats it among them, and there is exactly one of it. Ritual buildings did take fixed places in a Chinese city - two Confucian temples in Shanxi were both built in their city's southeast corner - but no page puts an office of rites among them; the arrangement is the setting's own.
  • Every ministry office fronts a street, within about 255 ft of one, never the middle of a block: a map drawing convention.
    The maps lay a short government avenue for them, often an L wrapping the compound, and seat the offices along it. The convention is carried over from the main street of the tatemachi castle town, which the front entrances of townhouses and samurai residences lined from the castle's main gate; no page we read places the government offices themselves along it.
  • A government office stands in its own ground.
    Ordinary city houses may touch one another, but a ministry office or the governor's compound keeps about 42 ft clear of every other building and compound.

The compound's size is rolled within the span the surviving seats give.

  • How large is the governor's compound on our maps?
    Between about 1 and 3.6 hectares (~2.5-9 acres), on the order of 330 by 330 ft to 650 by 600 ft, rolled per city from its seed within that span and never below the largest county magistrate's compound, which the record brackets between about 1,900 and 9,800 square meters (~20,000-105,000 sq ft) (the record's figures for a county magistrate's compound). The lower bound is the Takayama jin'ya, the shogunate's intendant's office for Hida and the only one of its kind surviving in Japan, and the upper the smaller of the two figures for the Chinese prefectural yamen at Nanyang.
  • No compound in or around the city is larger than the governor's.
    It is at least three times the size of any ministry office, and at least as large as any country estate.
  • Chinese compounds of about 7 hectares (~17 acres) are left out of our maps' size range.
    A compound that size would crowd a walled provincial city of a few thousand inhabitants. That is a GUESS, a judgment of this project's, not a measured limit.
  • Japan's ancient provincial offices (kokuga) are left out of the size range as well.
    They were an older form, a laid-out district of halls rather than one household's compound, and their sizes range too widely to narrow anything.
  • The drawn provincial cities sit near the bottom of the span.
    Minami's compound is 525 by 300 ft, and Nagahara's and Tango's 436 by 366 ft, each about 1.5 hectares (~3.6 acres) - half again Takayama's, a fifth to a half of a Chinese prefecture's. That leans to the Japanese tradition, which the record takes first for the interiors of administrative compounds (administrative culture is Japan-first), and keeps the governor's compound well above a county magistrate's.

On a city map the compound is its walls and gate, showing its extent but not its courts; what stands inside is drawn only on a map of the compound alone.

  • On a map of its own, the governor's compound follows a county magistracy's plan, enlarged by adding courts rather than by making the halls larger.
    The plan is the magistracy's (office in front, residence behind), as the Qing rule gave a senior official the full set of a yamen's parts and a junior one a reduced set. In order:
    • a front court with the gatehouse and the guards;
    • the office hall, with the courtroom as its room and the clerks' rooms flanking the court before it;
    • a second office hall behind it;
    • then the residence;
    • the archive, the storehouses and the granary on a side line of courts;
    • the shrine;
    • and the jail (How our maps place and draw the city jail (roya)).
  • The higher the official, the more courts the compound has: two for a county magistrate, five for a governor-general.
    The Zhili governor-general's yamen ran in five courts from its gate to its rear storehouse.

Which way the gate faces is fixed; how a street reaches it is chosen per city.

  • The governor's compound opens its gate to the south.
    A yamen's gate opened to the south.
  • The street to the gate takes one of two forms, a knob chosen per city from its seed.
    It is either an axial avenue about 30 ft wide running south from the gate, as at Japan's ancient provincial office of Shimotsuke, with the ministries along it, or a cross street running east and west past the gate, with a screen wall on its far side facing the gate, as at a Ming or Qing yamen.
  • The three drawn provincial cities keep their north- and west-facing gates.
    Minami's and Nagahara's compounds open north and Tango's west. They are kept as they were drawn; a generated city does not copy them.

The city's gates carry the setting's tariff.

  • A walled provincial city collects its tariff at its gates: a deviation, the setting's own.
    The setting's Ministry of Revenue inspects and tolls the caravans entering a walled city at its gates. Japan's historical barrier stations stood on the roads instead, often at mountain passes and river banks.
  • Our maps set markets in the streets just outside a city's gates.
    The streets themselves are attested; the markets in them are this record's reading, set out at the gate market exists for traffic, not taxes and what stands outside a city gate.
