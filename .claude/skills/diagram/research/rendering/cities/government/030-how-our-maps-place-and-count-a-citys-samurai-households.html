How our maps place and count a city's samurai households

A city's samurai quarter is sized to its samurai cohort, about a tenth of its population, and holds only samurai households, their servants and government ground. About two thirds of the city's samurai households live inside its wall, most of them as single houses of every rank in the samurai quarter; the governor's compound and the walled country estates outside the wall hold the rest.

  • The samurai quarter is one district of a mostly commoner city - a deliberate deviation.
    A historical castle town's samurai ground was often half of it or more, but this setting's provincial city is only about a tenth samurai.
  • At least about two thirds of the samurai households a city's declared population implies are drawn as samurai houses.
    A token cluster of a couple of dozen houses is not this cohort. The two-thirds floor is calibrated against the maps already drawn, not a finding.
  • The houses vary by rank: at least three large senior houses among a clear majority of small junior ones.
    A castle town's plots were granted by status and stipend, so a quarter of houses all one size is not a castle town's. The large houses are interleaved through the rows so they front the streets prominently, rather than grouped by the castle as the upper retainers' were, and both counts are calibrated against the maps already drawn. How big each rank's lot is drawn is at How big is a samurai's house lot and house, by rank?
  • Samurai houses line their streets: at least a third of them stand within about 270 ft of a lane.
    The deeper lots sit behind them. The third and the 270 ft are calibrated against the maps already drawn.
  • No samurai house inside the wall is walled - a deliberate deviation.
    A provincial castle town's samurai plots were enclosed by earthen walls, board fences or hedges, but here the only walled samurai compound inside the city is the governor's, and a walled samurai estate stands outside the wall. How the quarter as a whole is fenced and gated is at How our maps draw city wards and their gates (machi, kido).
  • No free-standing samurai house stands outside the wall.
    The only samurai homes beyond it are the walled country estates; an unwalled house beyond the wall would be defenseless and anchored to nothing.

How many of the households live inside the wall is a guess, but the history points one way.

  • How many of a city's samurai households live inside its wall?
    About two thirds, with the rest on country estates outside it. The two thirds is a GUESS: no page we read gives a share. On the evidence the true figure leans higher, since the history puts a castle town's samurai inside its enclosure where it had one, as at Funai and Hagi, and its country samurai out in the villages. The space budget built on it is at How big is a city's wall for the population inside it?
  • The low-ranking samurai at the town's edge are counted inside the wall.
    A castle town's lower ranks, such as foot soldiers, lived at its edge, and in this setting ashigaru are peasants, but its other low-ranking samurai are samurai still. Whether their quarters stood inside an enclosure or beyond it the sources do not say, so counting them inside is a GUESS.
  • A samurai household drawn outside the wall is a country estate among the fields, not a quarter against the wall.
    In this setting a samurai family keeps at most a country estate near some farmland, as the country samurai of history had residences scattered through the farming villages.

Inside its fence, the samurai ward keeps commoners out, as a castle town kept samurai and townsmen apart.

  • The samurai ward holds only its own residents.
    Samurai houses large and small, their live-in servants and government ground stand inside the fence; laborers, merchants, shops, inns and burakumin do not. A housing pass that reaches across the fence seats those households elsewhere in the city instead.
  • A monks' house may stand inside the ward, with its temple.
    A temple may stand inside the ward - the warrior fortune's temple beside the garrison quarter is the case - and its clergy's row belongs with it.
  • The ward is laid out before the commoner quarters.
    So no barred household is ever placed inside it.
  • A ward that cannot take commoner rows fills its share of the city's population with more samurai households.
    Each comes with its own servants' range. That suits the city's emptiest quarter and keeps the samurai within their share of the population.
