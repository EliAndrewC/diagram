How our maps draw a merchant's walled compound

A provincial city on our maps draws one to three walled merchant compounds, each a walled court in the core of a block with the merchant's large house inside it; the rest of its very rich merchants take large unwalled houses. A compound's wall stands on dry ground of the merchant's own, never in water, across a street or round the town's fire watch.

  • Only a family granted the right has a walled compound.
    A city of about 3,000 in this setting holds a dozen or so very rich merchant families, and most of them could pay for a wall and a gate. A gated compound is a right the daimyo grants a particular family, of a piece with the other privileges a merchant may be granted: an audience with the daimyo at the New Year's celebration, a surname carried down the generations without being samurai. So the wall marks legal standing rather than money, and a very rich merchant without the grant keeps a large unwalled house like anyone else's, only bigger.
  • The grant follows the real gatehouse, which went with rank and office.
    The gatehouse with rooms either side was laid down by a warrior house's rank, and allowed to commoners only where they held office, as village officials or town elders. Its commoner form had no guard post, and its side rooms held men, attendants, sheds and stables, as a merchant compound's gate range would. The surname and the sword, too, were a lord's grant.
  • That wealth cannot buy the grant is a deviation.
    From the middle of the Edo period the domains granted the surname for money contributions. It stayed the lord's favor, which the setting keeps; that no amount of money buys it is this setting's own departure from the later record. No page we read attaches the money to a wall or a gate.
  • How many compounds does a city draw?
    One, two or three, a knob rolled from the map's seed: one 30% of the time, two 40%, three 30%. A rolled count keeps two cities honestly different from each other. A town places its compounds by hand and rolls nothing.
  • A compound stands in the core of a block - a deviation.
    It is set well inside the rampart, clear of the moat and off the temple neighborhood, rather than jammed against the wall. The surviving great merchant houses are shop-houses at the street edge with their storehouses behind and a wall round the rest of the lot, so the court set back in a block is the map's placement, not the record's.
  • A compound's court keeps clear of everything else.
    The whole court, not just the house inside it, is kept clear for placement, so it overlaps no temple, ministry, house or other court.
  • How large is a compound's court?
    About 230 by 170 ft, with a light wall about 2 ft thick and a cart gate about 10 ft wide. The court's size is a GUESS; the attested sites run from about 98 by 171 ft to a frontage of about 200 ft.

Where the wall may stand follows from what the ground around a compound is for.

  • A compound's wall never stands in water.
    The whole perimeter, the gate gap included, stands clear of canal, dock basin, moat, river and pond, though it may come up to the quay. The reasoning is this project's own: a wall footed in water is undermined, and a canal or dock edge is working waterfront - the boats, the porters and the towpath that make the merchant rich.
  • A compound's wall never encloses a fire tower.
    The fire watch was the town's: every town kept a watch post manned around the clock. That its tower therefore stands on public ground, reached by a public way, never built into a private wall or shut inside a private court, is this project's reasoning, not the source's.
  • A compound's wall may line a street but never stand in it.
    A street, an alley, a road or the ring road is the public way, so the wall keeps out of its cleared band and may run alongside it.
  • A compound's gate opens onto open ground.
    It never opens into the face of the building next door: a wall that abuts a temple turns its gate to the free side. Water and fire towers are drawn before the compounds, in towns and cities both.
  • The wall stands inside the lot's back edge.
    A great merchant's house in Kyoto has a high wall round its whole estate, and a castle-town merchant's lot runs back to the block's shared drain, which is also the ward's boundary; that the wall stands on the merchant's own lot, inside that line, is this project's reading.

The record's great merchant houses stand on the street, and the rule written from them, which no map yet draws, follows the record rather than the court.

The rule the map follows for a rich merchant's house and its storehouses:

  • The house fronts a commercial street.
    Its main house, about 100 ft along the street and 50 ft deep , stands at the street edge with the shop at the front, and the lot runs back about 170 ft to the back of the block. There is no gatehouse and no court set back from the street.
  • Its frontage runs from about 70 to 200 ft.
    It is rolled per estate , a degree along a continuum, accurate at both ends.
  • A wall runs round the sides and back of the lot.
    As the Kyoto house's does.
  • Three to five storehouses stand at the back, in a row or a hook, and in some estates one more at the front.
    The one at the street front, beside the entrance, is a knob rolled per estate between the Kyoto form and the castle-town form.
  • Each storehouse is 14 to 20 ft square.
    That is accurate, the Kyoto house's three.
  • The storehouses stand close together.
    The spacing is a GUESS, since no source gives one.
  • Who on a street has storehouses?
    A walled merchant estate carries three to five, a large unwalled merchant house one or two, and an ordinary shop-house none. This is a GUESS: an ordinary provincial street most likely held fewer than Osaka, and no page we read says how storehouses were shared along one.
  • How many storehouses does a city hold?
    Roughly four for each walled estate plus one or two for each large merchant house, and never more than about one to every ten houses of the city, or every 35 to 55 households. That cap is a calibration to Osaka's counts of 1724, the one premodern town figure, on which the two accounts agree for houses and differ for households. Kitakata's one to every four households is not used, being a ratio of after 1880.
