How our maps zone a city's lots

Our maps zone a city's blocks as the history did: the shops and merchant houses stand on the street, and the laborer and burakumin homes pack the block cores behind them, reached by alleys. Behind the street the homes stand in back-to-back pairs of rows, never more than two deep, each door opening onto a lane.

  • Our maps put the shops on the street and the laborers' homes in the block cores.
    The better merchants kept shops on the main street and almost all the rest rented in the back lanes, so a street grid cuts a quarter into blocks, the businesses line the streets, and most of the homes fill the deep interior, reached by alleys rather than streets.
  • Our maps draw the merchant and laborer quarters as opposites.
    Merchant housing is varied and roomy: the very rich behind compound walls, the rich in large houses, the rest in small ones. The laborers' housing is uniform, small and packed close, with a clear minority of wealthier master laborers taking the best back-street lots.
  • The wealth bands are the setting's own, not a historical finding.
    Of a provincial city's merchant households about 8% are very rich, 12% rich, and the remaining 80% poor or middling, and about one laborer household in eight is a master laborer.
  • Our maps give a more mercantile quarter more streets.
    More shops want more street front. This is a calibration against our drawn cities, not a finding.

The rule the map follows - frontage:

  • almost every shop and merchant building stands within about 255 ft of a street, and most laborer and burakumin homes do not: they pack the block cores, off the street, where the alleys reach them (a calibration against our drawn provincial cities);
  • the exception is the larger master-laborer home, drawn at 6 to 20% of the laborer homes, a generous band around the setting's one in eight because the big homes are limited to the frontage there is room for, and placed on back-street frontage with room around it (a calibration);
  • samurai may front their streets, and a merchant building counts as a business and as a home at once;
  • merchant homes are spread out: their median gap to the nearest neighbor is at least 1.3 times the laborers', so a merchant quarter is not cut into shallow strips by too many streets, since the walled compounds need deep block cores (a calibration).

Which way a house faces, and how deep its rows stack, follow from where its door can open.

  • A farmhouse faces south; a city house faces whatever open ground it has.
    A farmhouse faces south wherever it stands, for the sun on its dooryard garden and its threshing ground, and that is the setting's own, so it is exempt from the city's rule. No page we read gives a city house any rule of facing the sun.
  • Our maps open every city door onto ground a person can walk on.
    A door opens onto a street, an alley or a court, and never into the back wall of another house an eave-gap away. The pages we read give every home an entrance of its own and put the alley's mouth between the street-front shops, but none states the rule against a door into a back wall; it is this project's choice.
  • How much clear ground do our maps leave in front of a door?
    At least 7 ft, a GUESS: the line is set between an eave band of about 6 ft or less, taken as for drainage rather than for entering, and a walkable band of about 10 ft, and no page we read gives either width.
  • Our maps stack rows at most two deep, in back-to-back pairs.
    The back tenement was a ridge-split row, two ranks under one roof sharing a rear wall and opening to opposite sides, and a shop's frontage carried one row along its alley or a row on each side, with no third rank described. A third row cannot occur on our maps, because its middle rank would be walled on both faces with no way out to a lane.

The rule the map follows - doors and rows:

  • every city home's door opens onto at least 7 ft of clear ground, at every map scale (a guess);
  • rows stack at most two deep: in a street-fronting pair the front row faces the street and the row behind it faces the back lane, and the back tenements are entered from the lane, through a gate between two front shops (accurate);
  • a house standing on its own takes whichever side is open;
  • the farmhouse, facing south, is exempt;
  • town and city maps both.

The alleys themselves are drawn wider and simpler than the real ones, so that they read at a map's scale.

  • Our maps draw a back alley 12 ft wide, where the real one was 3 to 6 ft.
    The real alley between one tenement building and the next was about 3 to 6 ft wide. At three feet to the pixel a true alley is one or two pixels wide, too thin to read as a way or carry a caption, so the maps widen it two to four times, a map drawing convention.
  • Our maps run one alley down a block's middle, where a real alley ran in from the street between two shops.
    The real alley's mouth lay between two merchant houses on the main street, rather than threading the block as one spine; a spine is what a map at this scale can draw legibly, a map drawing convention.
  • The boards down an alley's middle are accurate; the earth either side is a guess.
    Our maps draw an alley as a line of boards down its middle, over a small ditch, which is accurate. The ground either side of the boards is drawn as plain beaten earth, a GUESS: no page we read says what it was.

The rule the map follows - alleys:

  • an alley keeps a street's rules: it never runs over a building and never crosses the wall or the moat;
  • it earns its length by serving homes no other lane serves: each home is credited only to the lane nearest it, at about one such home per 90 ft of the alley's own length, which fails both a lane running off into a half-empty corner and a lane laid beside one that already serves the block (a calibration);
  • the rule of thumb is one lane per block, a single spine, never a cross-arm the spine already reaches and never an alley shadowing a street a few tens of feet away (a convention);
  • an alley running on past its last building to a ward gate serves the gate, not the block, so that last stretch is not counted against it;
  • no large cluster of homes, more than about thirty, sits with no street or alley within about 285 ft: every deep block, including any band between two quarters, is laced with alleys so that each home can be reached (a calibration).
