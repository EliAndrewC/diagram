How our maps draw ward walls and ward gates

Our maps draw no wall round any townsmen's ward. A city's commoner ground is divided into wards, each the houses along one block-length of street, with a ward gate across the street at each end. What a provincial city does enclose is its samurai and government quarter, which a fence with gates seals off from the commoner city; a capital draws instead the mesh of night gates the history shows, and lets each samurai estate's own wall do the sealing.

  • A ward is about 400 ft of street, with a gate at each end and none at its back.
    An Edo ward's gates stood at the two ends of its main street, the keepers' hut and the watch house built against them, and a large town set a gate to every block. A small town's gate might serve several blocks; our cities give every ward its own.
  • One headman's house stands for every few wards.
    It stands inside one of them, a larger house among the others and not a separate office, as an Edo headman governed several wards from his own house, living in one of them (City wards and the gates that shut them at night).
  • How many wards does a city draw?
    As many as its streets give: the number follows from the city's street length, since no page we read counts a provincial city's wards. For a city of about 600 households the drawn blocks give a dozen or two, a GUESS.

A provincial city fences its samurai and government quarter, and the fence is drawn so that its gates cannot be walked around.

  • A provincial city draws no walled wards.
    The largest of Tang Chang'an's walled wards covered 233 acres, more than a whole provincial city at the scale our maps draw, and the walled ward belonged to the great early capitals.
  • The samurai and government quarter is fenced, not walled.
    A castle town set fences and gates for each town division, shut at night and kept by gatekeepers. That our maps draw one continuous light earthwork and palisade round the samurai and government quarter alone, its ends on the rampart, is this project's reading of that passage, a GUESS. The research on castle towns found no fence round a whole samurai district, so the drawn fence is likely more than the history supports.
  • The fence is continuous and its ends reach the rampart.
    The point of it is that a gate cannot be walked around: a gap between gates, or a fence end that stops short of solid wall, makes the seal decorative.
  • A gate stands at every crossing of the fence.
    At least two ward gates stand near the governor's compound, and a gate stands at every street, road and alley that crosses the fence, and on the wall's own patrol road at both points where it meets the fence, within about 96 ft of where the way meets it. The quarter's access streets are run through the fence line so that each crossing is a gate rather than a gap, and commoner housing and alleys stay outside.
  • Both fence ends land on solid rampart.
    Each comes within about 135 ft of the city wall and lands on solid rampart, never in a gate's opening, where the wall is cut and the fence would meet nothing.
  • The rampart is drawn over the fence's ends.
    So the fence reads as passing under the wall rather than painted across it. The short cap that does this follows the rampart through any corner in its span, within about 12 ft of the wall line at every point, because a straight cap where a fence meets a corner juts past it and reads as two wall sections overlapping instead of one wall bending.
  • The quarter is the smaller piece the fence cuts off.
    Of the two regions the fence and the wall cut between them, the enclosed quarter is the smaller: a ward is carved off the city, never the larger half. On the drawn cities it runs a fifth to a quarter of the walled area.
  • A commoner lane that approaches the fence ends at a gate.
    Laborers came in to work in the samurai quarter, so the lanes they used run right up to a gate rather than stopping a sliver short. A lane end within about 180 ft of the fence, heading at it within about 37 degrees, with nothing built between, sits on the fence with a gate within about 102 ft of it. A lane may stop short of the city wall, the city's own edge, and a lane inside the quarter is no entry at all. The run past an alley's last building to its gate is a gate spur, and does not count against the rule that an alley serves the buildings along it.

A capital is drawn the way the history shows the great cities ran.

  • A capital draws a mesh of ward gates and no ward fence.
    Gates stand at the mouths of its blocks and lanes, far more of them than a provincial city has and placed by their own rule; its samurai live in several districts with townsmen's strips on the thoroughfares between; and each estate's own wall does the sealing, as at How our maps draw a capital differently from a provincial city. The capital of Shiro Daika is drawn this way.
  • The bell-and-drum tower sounds the hours the gates shut and open.
    Beijing's city gates shut and opened on the towers' bell and drum (City wards and the gates that shut them at night); that the ward gates kept the same hours is this project's reading.
  • How a clan walls its wards is a knob.
    The mesh is the default for every clan. A clan of deliberately archaic or martial administration, the Lion above all, may draw the fang ward instead: walls round each district with gated openings and a curfew drum, the Tang walled ward kept alive, closing off samurai districts and temple-gate towns alike and trading commerce for control, visibly.
  • Why offer the walled ward at all?
    The two forms are two periods of history, not history against invention: the Tang walled its wards and the Song opened them. A clan that keeps its cities compartmented runs the older imperial system on purpose, and offering it extends to the city the project's practice of varying its settlements within what the history shows.

Two rules fix how a gate and its guard box sit on the ground.

  • A ward gate stands square across the way it bars.
    A gate exists to shut a way, so its bar stands square to the roadbed through it, within about 7 degrees, and the fence meets it at whatever angle the fence runs. No page we read gives a gate's angle to its street, so this is a convention drawn from what the gate was for.
  • Why not square it to its fence?
    The two agree wherever a lane crosses its fence squarely, which is most crossings, and part on an oblique one. On a 44 degree jog of fence barring a patrol road at 172 degrees, a gate squared to the fence stands 38 degrees off its own roadbed and reads as a glyph dropped on the road.
  • A gate with no way through it squares to its fence.
    A fence running at 30 degrees gets a gate at 30 degrees, never a box squared to the page, as a rampart tower is turned to its wall. A lane running alongside the fence, within 25 degrees of it, is not a way the gate bars, and a gate more than about 48 ft from every fence is left alone.
  • The guard box stands on the ward's own side of its gate.
    An Edo ward's keepers' hut was built against its gate and run at the ward's cost, so the box hangs on the ward-interior flank of the gate and turns with it. Which flank is this project's placement.
  • The guard box stands on the verge, never in a roadbed.
    The box is the one solid building at the gate, and a road with a shack in its bed is no road. So the box walks outward from the end of the gate, trying the near side of the opening and then the far side at the same distance, until it clears every lane by about 12 ft and stands off the rampart, the compound walls and any wall tower.
  • Where its own flank cannot hold it, the box takes the other.
    Where the gate cannot move and a thinly covered stretch of the city wall needs its tower, the box yields to the other flank of its own gateway, an equally plausible spot for a watch hut.
