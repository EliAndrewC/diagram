How our maps route and label the Imperial road through a city

Where a city stands on the Imperial road, our maps run the road straight north to south through it, gate to gate and off both edges of the sheet, and line both its sides with shops, merchant houses and inns. The words "Imperial Road" are written beyond a gate, outside the walls, never on the stretch between them.

  • In the setting, the Empire pays for the Imperial road's upkeep, and the domains keep their own roads.
    The setting's notes make the upkeep of an Imperial road a permanent Imperial commitment funded from the central treasury, while a domain's everyday roads are built and kept up by its own daimyo, provincial governors and county magistrates.
  • This project reads the road inside a city's walls as a city street, so the label sits outside the gate.
    The setting's notes say nothing of the stretch of an Imperial road inside a city's walls; this project reads it as one of the local works, kept by the city's own government rather than by the Empire. The label names a jurisdiction, and at the gate the jurisdiction changes.
  • No other road on a settlement map is labeled.
    Only the Imperial road carries its name.
  • The road runs straight from gate to gate, a deliberate deviation.
    Most Chinese city plans deflect the main street so that none runs uninterrupted from one gate to the opposite one. A road the Empire drove through the country, with the city grown against it, runs straight through on our maps, and with the east-west axis it implies, it quarters the city. The road brings the caravans and the travelers, so this project treats it as a city's best frontage.

  • Both sides of the road through a city carry shops and lodging for travelers.
    A castle town built up both sides of its trunk road without gaps, and a post station lined its road with inns, cheap lodgings, teahouses and shops, so a city on the Imperial road, walled or not, lays it as a true two-sided market street rather than a single row, with the backs of houses or bare ground on neither side.
  • Where along the road do its eating houses gather?
    Where it enters the city, as well as along it. The travelers' teahouses moved in from the hills to the edges of the post towns; that they gather where the road enters a city is this project's reading of it.
  • The stretch that counts is the one through the built city.
    It is bounded by the wall where there is one, and by the edge of the buildings where there is not. The fault to watch for is a dead stretch between two quarters that both turn their backs on the road.
  • How densely is the road lined?
    At least one commercial building - a shop, a merchant house or an inn - per 390 ft of the stretch through the city. The figure is a floor that catches a bare road, calibrated against the three provincial cities drawn so far, not taken from a source.
  • How many storefronts does a whole city keep?
    About one per 25 to 30 inhabitants - about 105 shop, merchant and inn frontages for a domain capital of 3,000. This is this project's own estimate, a GUESS, not a figure from a source. A city on the road sits at the denser end of that band, the difference concentrated along the road.
  • A provincial city's merchants are about a quarter of its inhabitants.
    In the setting's caste tables a provincial city's merchants are a quarter of its inhabitants, the same share as a domain capital's and two and a half times a town's, which is what lets a city fill a road-long market street with households as well as with ground.
  • A domain capital always carries a market street along the Imperial road.
    In the setting the Imperial road touches every domain's capital, so every capital has one.
  • A city with no Imperial road through it keeps its commerce in the market district.
    Without the road, there is no through-traffic for its shops to line.
