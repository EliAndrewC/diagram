How our maps lay out a city's streets and blocks

Our maps lay a city's commoner quarters out as a grid of blocks about 400 ft on a side, the size of the blocks of Heian-kyo and of Edo's commoner quarter, and fill them densely enough that buildings line every larger street. The Imperial road is drawn 30 ft wide on every settlement map, and the avenue that approaches a domain capital's castle 45 ft.

  • Our maps make a block either a square with an open middle or a rectangle, most often the rectangle.
    Both forms are on record: the square of about 400 ft with open ground at its middle, which outside Edo only a few towns used, and the rectangle with no open core, which the great majority of castle towns laid out. So a block's shape is a knob, chosen for each settlement and weighted toward the rectangle, the usual form; the weight itself is arbitrary.
  • A square block's open middle is drawn as open ground or crossed by an alley.
    Both were done: Kyoto's blocks were threaded by a lane in 1591, and Ginza's after 1657.
  • How many house lots did a block hold along its streets?
    About fifty in an Edo block, worked out here from its 60 ken side, about 120 m (~394 ft) in the Kyoto measure, and the frontages a lot came in, not a count any source gives; the maps aim at that order as a calibration.

The rule the map follows - the block:

  • a city's commoner block is about 400 ft on a side, the size both Japanese plans give (accurate);
  • its street frontages are cut into lots about 130 ft deep, most of them 25 to 30 ft wide, with a few large houses taking 60 ft or more (accurate);
  • tenement rows stand behind the street-front lots, and the block's middle may be left open;
  • about fifty street-front lots to a block is the order to aim at (a calibration).

Within a quarter, the streets are laid out so that each one reads as a street.

  • Our maps build a quarter from two streets each way, making nine blocks.
    Two streets running north-south against two running east-west is a calibration of ours, not a sourced figure.
  • A quarter is packed to its real counts of households, not to a comfortable spacing.
    Each quarter holds its real counts of households by caste. Density is what makes a grid read: a sparse fill leaves a stretch of street with nothing along it.
  • Were a planned town's larger streets lined with buildings?
    A castle town's trunk road was lined with houses on both sides with no gaps, and in Edo's commoner quarter the bustle gathered on the main streets, not the alleys, so a wide bare margin along a street reads as an oversight. Carrying Edo's pattern to every planned town is a GUESS, and the maps line every larger street.
  • The distances are calibrations, not historical findings.
    Each tolerance below is the threshold at which a drawn street stops reading as a street, calibrated against the three provincial city maps this project has drawn. The one with a historical yardstick, how far a building may stand off a street, is looser than the Edo lot of about 130 ft deep that it would otherwise follow.

The rule the map follows - lining:

  • no larger street runs a stretch longer than about 420 ft with its nearest building more than about 175 ft off it;
  • narrow alleys threading a block's interior are exempt, and so is a domain capital's approach avenue (ote-suji), whose frontage is the compounds of the domain government's ministries, spaced along it, rather than shops and houses;
  • two streets are never drawn on the same line - a doubled corridor pushes the buildings out of fronting range.

The rule the map follows - joining:

  • the streets are one connected network joined to the Imperial road, and two roads count as joined only where their paved beds actually touch: a road that stops even a roadbed short of the next one is a separate network, so each quarter's road-facing street is run out until it meets the Imperial road;
  • a ward gate does not break a run - the centerline goes on under it;
  • roads never come almost together without meeting: a pair closing to within about 6 to 90 ft without crossing, and a road overshooting a crossing by a sliver of about 9 to 150 ft, both read as mistakes, so roads either cross and run on or end cleanly at the junction, and the two outermost streets of a grid end at their shared corner rather than each running past the other;
  • two roads of any kind heading straight at each other - within about 37 degrees of aligned, less than about 240 ft apart, with clear ground between - simply connect; a gap that a building, a ward fence or a wall genuinely blocks is no fault, because there the road is correctly routing around something.

The rule the map follows - drawing (map drawing conventions - how the sheet reads, not how the ground was built):

  • where two roads cross, the wider is drawn over the narrower - the Imperial road over the city streets it crosses, a street over an alley;
  • a junction is drawn as a crossroads: every road's dark curb goes down before any road's paved bed, so the beds merge into one continuous junction instead of one road's curb ruling a line across the other's mouth;
  • walls are drawn over the roads: a road, street or alley that meets the city rampart or an internal ward fence passes under it, and breaks it only at a gate, where the wall itself carries a real gap so the roadway shows through the opening.

The widths of the two widest roads on a city map, the Imperial road and the approach avenue, are set from the widths of Edo-period highways.

  • The Imperial road is drawn 30 ft wide on every settlement map.
    In 1604 the Tokaido's width was set at 5 ken, 29.8 ft at the 1.818 m (~6 ft) ken, and the maps round it to 30 ft: the width is accurate, the quarter-foot rounding a deviation.
  • How wide is the avenue that approaches a domain capital's castle (ote-suji)?
    45 ft, half again as wide as the 30 ft Imperial road it meets. A page on Ginza's streets puts an Edo-period highway wider than the Tokaido's 5 ken of 1604, at 7 to 8 ken, 13 to 15 m (~43-49 ft), and the Tokaido where it ran through Edo as Ginza-dori at about that; the approach is drawn inside that band. That a castle town's approach was as wide as a highway is a GUESS: no page we read gives its width.
  • The maps draw no avenue on the scale of the ancient capitals'.
    The central avenues of Heian-kyo, 82 to 84 m (~269-276 ft), and of Tang Chang'an, 147 m (~482 ft), belonged to the planned imperial capitals, and the Edo-period widths this record has read are the highways', so the maps keep the approach within the highway's band.
  • The domain government's ministry compounds front the approach avenue closely.
    They are set back only about 21 ft from it, so they line the avenue like a corridor instead of standing apart from it.
