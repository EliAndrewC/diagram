How our maps draw a city's street front

Our maps draw a city's commoner housing as rows of houses touching their neighbors, as the townsmen's houses stood with no gaps between them, and its market streets as shops standing shoulder to shoulder along the street they front. Only the samurai quarter keeps detached compounds in yards of their own.

  • Commoner housing goes down in rows, not as separate buildings in their own clearings.
    The laborer, servant and burakumin homes and the small merchant houses are laid as touching rows. Obstacles break a row naturally and the occasional gap is honest, but a gap is not the usual texture.
  • Touching homes keep a hairline seam between their outlines.
    They were separate houses built against each other, and the seam, a map drawing convention, lets each one read as its own.
  • Rows back to back keep the real lane between them.
    The lane between one back tenement building and the next was about 3 to 6 ft wide, with a drain down its middle that took the roofs' rain, so the maps keep a gap of about 3 to 6 ft for the eaves and the drain.
  • The samurai quarter keeps detached compounds with yards.
    A samurai residence was enclosed by its own hedge, wall and gate, and the contrast with the commoners' rows is what makes the rows read.

The rule the map follows - commoner rows:

  • at least 55% of a city's commoner homes touch a neighbor, and the median gap from one to its nearest neighbor is no more than 6 ft (a calibration);
  • back-to-back rows keep a gap of about 3 to 6 ft (accurate);
  • an idobata court opens every second row, and the block's wells are drawn before the rows, so the rows break into courts around them (a calibration);
  • a quarter's alleys go down first and are fronted tightly, while a real street keeps a band clear for the shop frontage.

Along a market street the rows are shops, and the maps draw them closer than a residential row and not quite touching.

  • Shops in a market row stand shoulder to shoulder along the street they front, and a row may front a street that bends.
    The shophouses of a busy street stood side by side with no gaps between them, so a market row is drawn as a line of shops, each facing the street.
  • Why do our maps leave a gap of about 18 ft between shops that really touched?
    So a reader can count the shops and see which way each one faces. Real shophouses adjoined, the gable wall of one meeting the next, and the homes of a residential row shared their walls, a gap of zero. Inside a residential row our maps keep the homes touching; it is the market rows that carry the wider joint, a deliberate deviation. The drawn gaps have a median of 18 ft on the north and south gate markets of the capital, Shiro Daika, 9 ft on its crowded southwest strip, and 48 ft on the loose roadside market at its east gate, which is a different thing and stays loose. The relative sizes stay true; only the joint is drawn wider than life.

A town's street front follows its own rule, which turns on its traffic: Does a town's street front stand wall to wall, or house by house?
