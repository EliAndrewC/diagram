How our maps draw a city's wall (chengqiang)

A city's wall enclosed it all the way round, and the walls of important cities took in far more ground than was built on, so our maps give a provincial city a closed ring of wall with open ground left inside it.

  • The wall runs a regular circuit, not a skirt round the houses.
    A provincial seat's rampart is drawn as a defensive loop of its own, not the shortest line a town might throw round whatever it had built. That is this project's reading, a GUESS: no source we read states it.
  • Our maps give a city a few gates, never fewer than two.
    A county seat ordinarily opened four gates and a prefectural city eight, so our maps gather a city's traffic through a few grand gates rather than many small openings.
  • The ring is drawn as a rounded polygon, roughly an ellipse - a house style, not a researched form.
    The four provincial cities drawn so far carry it, and keep it for now: reshaping them would regenerate every city made from them, a change to make deliberately if at all. Of the shapes our research found, it sits closest to the long loop a Japanese castle town's outer enclosure (sogamae) ran over the ground, and furthest from the Chinese rectangle.
  • A wall truer to history would be a rectangle, or a loop that follows the ground.
    A rectangle on flat open ground, a loop following the ground on river or hill ground: those are the forms our research found.
  • A castle's outer wall is drawn rectangular.
    The plains castles our capitals resemble have rectangular, straight-lined enclosures, so the outer wall is drawn with straight sides. How the castle itself is drawn is at How our maps draw the castle in a capital.
  • Every government compound is drawn rectangular.
    The government offices (yamen), the magistrates' manors, the governors' mansions, the samurai lineage compounds and the country estates are all rectangles, as the yamen of Song-dynasty and Qing-dynasty China were laid out on an axis in courtyards closed on four sides.
  • No city wall is drawn round.
    The two round forms our research found are Shanghai's county wall, raised against pirates coming upriver, and the tulou, the clan houses of the Hakka in the mountains, dwelling and defense in one. Neither is how a daimyo's seat or a governor's city announces itself: that judgment is this project's.
  • No map draws a postern or a sally gate.
    A defended city of Ming China sent its troops out through the enclosed court (wengcheng) before a main gate, a protected staging ground larger than a European barbican, so our maps draw only the main gates.

The rule the map follows: a provincial city's wall is a closed ring with at least two gates, each a genuine gap in the rampart with a road running through it. The ring is regular, drawn as a defensive circuit rather than hugging the built-up ground, and the open ground inside it is left open on purpose. No map draws a postern or a sally gate.
