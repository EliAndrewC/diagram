How our maps draw a city's wall (chengqiang)

Our maps draw a provincial city's wall as a closed ring with at least two gates, each a real gap in the rampart with a road running through it. The ring runs its own regular circuit rather than hugging the built-up ground, and the open ground inside it is left open on purpose, as the walls of important cities took in far more ground than was built on.

  • The wall runs a regular circuit, not a skirt round the houses.
    A provincial seat's rampart is drawn as a defensive loop of its own, not the shortest line a town might throw round whatever it had built. That is this project's reading, a GUESS: no source we read states it.
  • A city has few gates, and at least two.
    A county city ordinarily opened four gates and a prefectural city eight, so our maps gather a city's traffic through a few grand gates rather than many small openings.
  • The ring is drawn as a rounded polygon, roughly an ellipse - a house style, not a researched form.
    The four provincial cities drawn so far carry it. Of the shapes the record found, it sits closest to the long loop a Japanese castle town's outer enclosure (sogamae) ran over the ground, and furthest from the Chinese rectangle.
  • A map that wants the Chinese form would draw a rectangle or a loop that follows the ground, not a truer circle.
    A rectangle on flat open ground, a loop following the ground on river or hill ground - those are the forms the record found. The four cities already drawn keep their rings.
  • A castle's outer wall is drawn rectangular.
    The plains castles our capitals resemble have rectangular, straight-lined enclosures, and a round keep would read European castle building into an East Asian map. How the castle itself is drawn is at How our maps draw the castle in a capital.
  • Every government compound is drawn rectangular.
    The government offices (yamen), the magistrates' manors, the governors' mansions, the samurai lineage compounds and the country estates are all rectangles, as the yamen of Song and Qing China were laid out on an axis in courtyards closed on four sides.
  • No city wall is drawn round.
    The two round forms the record found are Shanghai's county wall, raised against pirates coming upriver, and the tulou, the clan houses of the Hakka in the mountains, dwelling and defense in one. Neither is how a daimyo's seat or a governor's city announces itself: that judgment is this project's.
  • No map draws a postern or a sally gate.
    A defended city in this tradition went out through the barbican court before a main gate, not through small doors in the wall, so our maps draw only the gates.

The rule the map follows: a provincial city's wall is a closed ring with at least two gates, each a genuine gap in the rampart with a road running through it. The ring is regular, drawn as a defensive circuit rather than hugging the built-up ground, and the open ground inside it is left open on purpose. No map draws a postern or a sally gate.
