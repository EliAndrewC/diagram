How our maps draw a city's moat (hori, chenghe)

Our maps draw a moat outside every city wall and every walled county town built on the Chinese model, and outside a Japanese town only where the town encloses itself. The moat widens with the settlement's standing, from a narrow ditch at a county town to a broad one at a provincial seat, and whether it rings the whole wall or leaves one side to a river depends on how close the wall stands to the bank.

  • A provincial seat's moat is drawn 66 ft across, wider than any period figure supports.
    The period figures put a provincial seat's moat nearer the Ming treatise's floor of about 35 ft, and 35 ft is the width the maps should draw there. The 55 to 100 ft of the great cities is measured on moats that survive today, and the only older figures near it are Zhengding's undated ten zhang and a Han capital that two encyclopedias measure differently, so the 66 ft rests on no period figure.
  • How wide should a county seat's moat be?
    About 10 ft, as Pingyao's was when the town was rebuilt in 1370. That a county seat takes the lesser end and a provincial seat the greater is this project's calibration: the treatise sets one floor for every city.
  • A walled county town's moat is rolled between about 13 and 63 ft.
    The width is a knob along a continuum from Pingyao's moat as it stands today, 4 m (~13 ft) wide, to Shanghai's 6 zhang, about 63 ft. The roll's floor sits above the 10 ft of Pingyao's moat in 1370.
  • A county town's moat may change width from side to side.
    Zhenhai's did, from about 47 ft on the south to about 136 ft on the west.
  • A side that a river, a lake or the sea already guards may go without a moat.
    Zhenhai dug none on its north, where the wall faced the sea.
  • The moat's depth is not drawn.
    The treatise asks 15 to 21 ft, but a map seen from above shows only the width of the water.
  • The broad lake moat is not drawn.
    Shallow and hundreds of feet across, as at Xiangyang and Guide, it is attested only in present-day measurement (at City moats: how wide and deep, and whether they ring the wall (hori, chenghe)).
  • A Japanese town that encloses itself draws a moat about 33 ft wide; one that stands open draws none.
    Medieval Sakai's moat was about 10 m (~33 ft) wide.

The capital and the provincial river cities stand on rivers, and there the moat takes one of two shapes.

  • A wall on its river's bank takes an arc of moat; a wall standing back takes a full ring.
    A river already flowing past could serve as a natural moat, so a wall on the bank takes the river as one side and digs only the landward arc, closing onto the river at both ends. A wall standing back from the bank closes its own ring, and connects to the river, if at all, by cut channels. That the wall's distance to the bank decides, and the arc's shape, are this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • The capital's moat rings its wall.
    Shiro Daika's rampart stands about 600 ft off the bank, because the wharf suburb, the two granary complexes and the towpath hold the waterfront, and a rampart on the bank would have taken the working shore the capital exists to use. So its moat is a full ring, fed and drained through channels with sluices.
  • The provincial river cities keep the arc.
    Minami's and Nagahara's walls reach the bank, so their moats are open arcs whose ends meet the river, the river completing the circuit. A capital drawn on its bank, as the edge-castle knob already allows, would take the arc too.

The rule the map follows: a moat stands outside every Chinese-model city wall and walled county town, and outside a Japanese-model town only where it encloses itself (about 33 ft). A provincial seat's moat is drawn 66 ft across today, and 35 ft is the width the period figures support. A walled county town's is rolled between about 13 and 63 ft, may vary by side, and may be left off a side water already guards. Neither the depth nor the broad lake form is drawn. A wall on its river's bank takes an arc closed by the river; a wall standing back takes a full ring.
