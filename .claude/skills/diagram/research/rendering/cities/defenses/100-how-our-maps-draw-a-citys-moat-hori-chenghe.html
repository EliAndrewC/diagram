How our maps draw a city's moat (hori, chenghe)

Our maps draw a moat outside every city wall and every walled county seat built on the Chinese model, and outside a Japanese town only where the town encloses itself (sogamae). The moat widens with the settlement's standing, from a narrow ditch at a county seat to 66 ft across at a provincial seat, and whether it rings the whole wall or leaves one side to a river depends on how close the wall stands to the bank.

  • A provincial seat's moat is drawn 66 ft across, wider than any dated period figure we found.
    A Ming treatise sets every city moat a smallest width of about 35 ft, and 35 ft is the width the maps should draw there. The 55 to 100 ft of the great cities is measured on moats that survive today, and the only older figures near it are Zhengding's undated ten zhang and a Han-dynasty capital that two encyclopedias measure differently, so the 66 ft rests on no period figure.
  • How wide is a county seat's moat?
    Pingyao's was about 10 ft when the town was rebuilt in 1370, and is 4 m (~13 ft) wide as it stands today. Shanghai's was 6 zhang, about 63 ft. A Ming treatise sets one smallest width for every city, so giving a county seat the narrower end and a provincial seat the wider is this project's choice. Whether the maps draw a county seat's moat at about 10 ft, or at a width chosen at random between about 13 and 63 ft, is not yet settled.
  • A county seat's moat may change width from side to side.
    Zhenhai's did, from about 47 ft on the south to about 136 ft on the west.
  • A side that the sea or a river already guards may go without a moat.
    Zhenhai dug none on its north, where the wall faced the sea.
  • The moat's depth is not drawn.
    A Ming treatise asks 15 to 21 ft, but a map seen from above shows only the width of the water.
  • Broad, shallow lake moats, hundreds of feet across, are not drawn.
    As at Xiangyang and Guide, they are attested only in present-day measurement.
  • A Japanese town that encloses itself draws a moat about 33 ft wide.
    Medieval Sakai's moat was about 10 m (~33 ft) wide.

The capital and the provincial river cities stand on rivers, and there the moat takes one of two shapes.

  • Which walls take an arc of moat, and which a full ring?
    A river already flowing past could serve as a natural moat, so a wall on the bank takes the river as one side and digs only the landward arc, closing onto the river at both ends. A wall standing back from the bank closes its own ring, and connects to the river, if at all, by cut channels. That the wall's distance to the bank decides, and the arc's shape, are this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • The capital's moat rings its wall.
    Shiro Daika's rampart stands about 600 ft off the bank, because the wharf suburb, the two granary complexes and the towpath hold the waterfront, and a rampart on the bank would have taken the working shore the capital exists to use. So its moat is a full ring, fed and drained through channels with sluices. A capital drawn on its bank, as the edge castle knob already allows, would take the arc too.
  • The provincial river cities, Minami and Nagahara, take an open arc of moat.
    Minami's and Nagahara's walls reach the bank, so their moats are open arcs whose ends meet the river, the river completing the circuit.

The rule the map follows: a moat stands outside every Chinese-model city wall and walled county seat, and outside a Japanese-model town only where it encloses itself (about 33 ft). A provincial seat's moat is drawn 66 ft across today, and 35 ft, a Ming treatise's smallest width, is the width it should be. A walled county seat's is between about 10 and 63 ft (the exact rule is not yet settled), may vary by side, and may be left off a side water already guards. Neither the depth nor the broad lake form is drawn. A wall on its river's bank takes an arc closed by the river; a wall standing back takes a full ring.
