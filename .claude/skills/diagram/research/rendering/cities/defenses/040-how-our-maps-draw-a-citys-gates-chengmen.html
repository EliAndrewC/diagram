How our maps draw a city's gates (chengmen)

Every building at a gate is drawn at its real size, not as a fixed marker. A city's road gate is a 30 ft opening between masonry piers, with a gate tower of about 52 by 30 ft, a guard house and an inspection hall beside the road, and, outside it, a crossing of the moat as long as the moat is wide.

  • A city's road gate is drawn 30 ft clear, between masonry piers 15 ft across.
    So the gap in the rampart is 60 ft overall. The 30 ft is the width of the trunk road the gate passes, the opening sized to pass the road and no more. It is wider than the passages measured at Nanjing and Xi'an, about 16 to 20 ft.
  • Was a gate ever wider than the road through it?
    The maps take a gate to narrow the road that passes through it and never to widen for it. No page we read sets a gate's width beside its road's, so the comparison is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • The masonry gatepost is drawn 15 ft across by 24 ft along the opening, a GUESS.
    No page we read gives a gatepost's size.
  • A water gate passes a 36 ft cargo canal.
    It is drawn 60 ft clear, with about 12 ft of pier on each side.
  • Every figure is a length in real feet.
    So a town map drawn at one foot to the pixel is not changed by a city map's three. Narrowing the opening frees no ground for housing - it sits inside the gap in the wall and the patrol road beside it - so no quarter has to be re-packed when it changes.

The buildings at and beside the gate are each drawn at the upper end of the range the record gives for them, where that end is still legible, and no further: a real size, never one inflated to read better. Strokes keep their own minimum width so they can be seen, and the footprints take no license.

  • A gate tower is drawn about 52 by 30 ft.
    That is the measured three-bay tower of Zuo'an Gate, a side gate of the capital's outer city. It lies inside the range from a county seat's main gate tower, about 46 by 33 ft, to Chaozhou's, about 85 by 43 ft.
  • How large is a gate's guard house, or its inspection hall?
    A guard house is drawn about 34 by 20 ft and an inspection hall about 44 by 22 ft, at the upper end of bands of about 15 to 35 ft and about 25 to 45 ft. Both are estimates: no page we read gives either one's bay count or size.
  • The wall's own tower is drawn as a mamian, about 65 by 40 ft.
    A mamian is a tower-like structure projecting from the wall, and Xi'an's project 12 m (~39 ft) from the wall and are 20 m (~66 ft) wide. So it is drawn as a rectangle projecting from the wall, longer along the wall than deep.
  • How large is the tower building on a mamian, and a corner tower?
    The building set back on a mamian is drawn about 30 to 40 ft, and a corner tower would be about 50 to 66 ft; both are estimates, since no page we read measures either.

A walled county town draws its gate on one of two models, following the setting's choice of whether a county seat is walled (Was a county seat walled?).

  • A town on the Chinese model draws each gate as a passage with a gate tower over it.
    The passage is drawn 13 ft clear, the lower end of the city's range, and the tower about 40 by 24 ft. Both are GUESSES scaled down from the city's figures, on the principle that a gate tower grows with its wall; no page we read gives either figure for a county gate.
  • A town on the Japanese model draws no gate, but a mitsuke at each end of its main street.
    Two banks about 14 ft deep and 8 ft high stand square to the road on either side of it, each with a fence along its crest, and the road passes open between them, as at Kanagawa.

Every road gate crosses its moat, and the crossing is as long as the moat is wide (How our maps draw a city's moat (hori, chenghe)). Which model a city follows is the setting's.

  • On the Chinese model, a drawbridge, shown lowered.
    It stands in line with the outermost gate - before the barbican's own gate where there is one - and is drawn as a plank deck as wide as the gate passage, about 16 to 20 ft. The width is a GUESS from the passage it serves: no page we read gives a crossing's width.
  • On the Japanese model, an earthen causeway to the main gate.
    It is drawn as a strip of ground with stone faces, about 30 to 40 ft wide. The width is a GUESS; what the page says is that such causeways were very broad.
  • A castle's rear gate is reached by a timber bridge.
    It is drawn about as wide as the drawbridge, a GUESS as well.
  • Where a causeway crosses a wet moat, the map may shade the water on its two sides differently.
    A causeway could hold the water higher on one side than the other.

The rule the map follows:

  • A city's road gate: 30 ft clear between masonry piers 15 ft across, 60 ft overall. A water gate: a 36 ft canal, 60 ft clear, about 12 ft of pier each side.
  • At the gate: a gate tower about 52 by 30 ft, a guard house about 34 by 20 ft, an inspection hall about 44 by 22 ft; on the wall, mamian towers about 65 by 40 ft, projecting, with the tower building set back on them.
  • A walled county town on the Chinese model: a 13 ft passage under a tower about 40 by 24 ft. On the Japanese model: a mitsuke at each end of the main street, two fenced banks about 14 ft deep and 8 ft high, the road open between them.
  • The crossing of the moat, as long as the moat is wide: a lowered drawbridge about 16 to 20 ft wide on the Chinese model; on the Japanese model a stone-faced causeway about 30 to 40 ft wide to the main gate and a timber bridge about as wide as the drawbridge to the rear gate.
