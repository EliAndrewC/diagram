How our maps draw a city's gates (chengmen)

Every building at a gate is drawn at its real size, not as a fixed marker. On our maps a city's road gate is a 30 ft opening between masonry piers, with a gate tower of about 52 by 30 ft, a guard house and an inspection hall beside the road, and, outside it, a crossing of the moat as long as the moat is wide. A walled county town gets a smaller gate under a tower, or on the Japanese model a mitsuke at each end of its main street.

  • A city's road gate is drawn 30 ft clear.
    The 30 ft is the width of the trunk road the gate passes, the opening sized to pass the road and no more. It is wider than the passages measured at Nanjing and Xi'an, about 16 to 20 ft.
  • The masonry piers on either side of the opening are drawn 15 ft across and 24 ft deep, a GUESS.
    With them, the gap in the rampart is 60 ft overall. No page we read gives a pier's size.
  • Was a gate ever wider than the road through it?
    The maps never draw a gate wider than the road through it, and the city's gate is drawn exactly as wide. No page we read sets a gate's width beside its road's, so the comparison is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • A water gate is drawn 60 ft clear, with about 12 ft of pier on each side.
    It passes a 36 ft cargo canal. Both figures are this project's, a GUESS: no measured width of a water gate was read (Where a canal passes the city wall, what is the water gate?).

Where the record gives a building's size only as a range, the building is drawn at the upper end of that range, where it is easiest to see, and no further: a real size, never one enlarged to read better. Lines keep a minimum width so they can be seen, but the buildings themselves are never enlarged.

  • A gate tower is drawn about 52 by 30 ft.
    That is the measured three-bay tower of Zuo'an Gate, a side gate of Beijing's outer city. It lies inside the range from a county seat's main gate tower, about 46 by 33 ft, to Chaozhou's, about 85 by 43 ft.
  • A gate's guard house is drawn about 34 by 20 ft and its inspection hall about 44 by 22 ft, both estimates.
    Each is at the upper end of a band, about 15 to 35 ft and about 25 to 45 ft. No page we read gives either one's bay count or size.
  • The wall's own towers are drawn as mamian, each about 65 by 40 ft.
    A mamian is a tower-like structure projecting from the wall, and Xi'an's project 12 m (~39 ft) from the wall and are 20 m (~66 ft) wide. So each is drawn as a rectangle projecting from the wall, longer along the wall than deep.
  • The building on each mamian is drawn about 30 to 40 ft, set back from the tower's face, an estimate.
    No page we read measures one.
  • A corner tower is drawn about 50 to 66 ft, an estimate.
    No page we read measures one.

Every city gate carries a guard house, an inspection hall and its own gate tower, and they stand at the gate itself. A gate is where a walled city controlled who came in and collected its tolls, so the maps never set the posts back a few hundred feet along the wall, where travelers would meet them only after scattering into the streets. The arrangement follows the barrier at Hakone and that reasoning, not the Chinese customs stations, which were a handful on the trade routes (Barriers and inspection posts at a town's entrance (bansho)).

  • The guard house and the inspection hall face each other across the road.
    They take opposite sides of the opening, so that arrivals pass between them, as the two guard houses face each other at the rebuilt Hakone barrier.
  • They are drawn about 105 to 135 ft inside the opening, a GUESS.
    They stand at the gate's inner mouth. At Hakone, as this project reads the rebuilt plan, both stood within the 59 ft between its two gates, so the drawn distance is about twice that of the one barrier read, and rests on no source.
  • The guard house, the inspection hall and the gate tower stand square to the wall.
    Each is turned to the line of the wall where it actually sits, not to the gate's own line, which on a curving wall has already turned away, to within about 6 degrees, and is set on the patrol road's center so that the road runs lengthwise through it.
  • A gate's tower stands at its gate, beside the opening.
    It is never drawn out among the wall's own towers: where one side of the opening is blocked, it takes the other side.
  • The buildings at a gate never overlap.
    On a small, tightly curved wall the guard house and the inspection hall close in on each other as they move out from the opening, so the inspection hall steps further out, about 66 ft at a time, until the two clear each other.
  • Each keeps about 36 ft of clear ground around it.
    That is enough because the road's own strip already keeps the houses off one side.
  • A fire-watch tower keeps 15 ft of open ground from a graveyard.
    That is the same clearance its braced footing keeps from wells and buildings.

A walled county town is drawn on one of two models, following the setting's choice of whether a county seat is walled (Was a county seat walled?).

  • A town on the Chinese model draws each gate as a passage with a gate tower over it.
    The passage is drawn 13 ft clear, narrower than the city's 30 ft opening and the 16 to 20 ft passages measured at Nanjing and Xi'an, and the tower about 40 by 24 ft. Both are GUESSES scaled down from the city's figures, on the principle that a gate tower grows with its wall; no page we read gives either figure for a county gate.
  • A town on the Japanese model draws no gate, but a mitsuke at each end of its main street.
    Two banks about 14 ft deep and 8 ft high stand on either side of the road, each with a fence along its crest, and the road passes open between them, as at Kanagawa.

A town keeps its watch at its own entrances, and which kind follows the town's model (Barriers and inspection posts at a town's entrance (bansho)).

  • A walled town on the Chinese model draws no inspection building apart from its gate.
    The gate itself is the post, shut and locked at night. A small guard room is drawn just inside it, a GUESS: no page we read shows one.
  • An open town on the Japanese model draws a kido, with a watch house beside it on the town side, where each road enters.
    A kido was a gate across the street with a watch house attached, and beyond the main post of the castle town of Kitsuki there were no houses, only the road, so no houses are drawn beyond it.
  • The guard room and the watch house are drawn about 12 by 18 ft, a GUESS.
    No page we read gives a town's watch house's size.
  • A domain's border post is not drawn on a town map.
    No page we read places one at a town's own entrance.

Every road gate's road crosses the moat, and the crossing is as long as the moat is wide (How our maps draw a city's moat (hori, chenghe)). On the Chinese model the crossings are at the city's gates; on the Japanese model they are at a castle's, its main gate and its rear gate. Which model a city follows is the setting's.

  • On the Chinese model, the moat is crossed by a drawbridge, drawn lowered.
    It stands in line with the outermost gate - before the barbican's own gate where there is one - and is drawn as a plank deck about 16 to 20 ft wide, the width of the gate passages measured at Nanjing and Xi'an. The width is a GUESS from those passages: no page we read gives a crossing's width.
  • On the Japanese model, an earthen causeway leads to the castle's main gate.
    It is drawn as a strip of ground with stone faces, about 30 to 40 ft wide. The width is a GUESS; what the page says is that such causeways were very broad.
  • A castle's rear gate is reached by a timber bridge.
    It is drawn about as wide as the drawbridge, a GUESS as well.
  • Where a causeway crosses a wet moat, the map may shade the water on its two sides differently.
    A causeway could hold the water higher on one side than the other.

The rule the map follows:

  • A city's road gate: 30 ft clear between masonry piers 15 ft across and 24 ft deep, 60 ft overall. A water gate: a 36 ft canal, 60 ft clear, about 12 ft of pier each side.
  • At the gate: a gate tower about 52 by 30 ft, a guard house about 34 by 20 ft, an inspection hall about 44 by 22 ft; on the wall, mamian towers about 65 by 40 ft, projecting, with the tower building set back on them.
  • The guard house and the inspection hall on opposite sides of the road, facing each other, about 105 to 135 ft inside the opening; all three buildings square to the wall within about 6 degrees and centered on the patrol road; the gate tower beside its own opening; the inspection hall stepped out about 66 ft at a time until nothing overlaps; about 36 ft clear around each; a fire-watch tower 15 ft from a graveyard.
  • A walled county town on the Chinese model: a 13 ft passage under a tower about 40 by 24 ft. On the Japanese model: a mitsuke at each end of the main street, two fenced banks about 14 ft deep and 8 ft high, the road open between them.
  • A town's watch: on the Chinese model the gate, with a guard room about 12 by 18 ft just inside it; on the Japanese model an open town's kido across each entering road, with a watch house about 12 by 18 ft on the town side and no houses beyond. No border post on a town map.
  • The crossing of the moat, as long as the moat is wide: a lowered drawbridge about 16 to 20 ft wide on the Chinese model; on the Japanese model a stone-faced causeway about 30 to 40 ft wide to the main gate and a timber bridge about as wide as the drawbridge to the rear gate.
