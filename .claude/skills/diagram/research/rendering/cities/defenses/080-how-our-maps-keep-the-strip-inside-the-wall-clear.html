How our maps keep the strip inside the wall clear

Every walled city on our maps runs a patrol road as a closed loop just inside its rampart, as Chinese cities ran a street along the inside of their walls that began as a road ridden to patrol them. The city's quarters are packed inside the road rather than up to the wall, and nothing but the wall's own works stands on it.

  • The patrol road runs about 102 ft inside the rampart and is about 20 ft wide.
    Both figures are GUESSES, since the one wall-side street measured is a capital's, 66 to 82 ft wide.
  • Nothing may stand on the road's bed.
    No house, no ministry, no governor's compound, no temple, no field. The road is kept clear so that troops could be moved along the wall wherever they were wanted, which is this project's reading of what the street was for, a GUESS.
  • The wall's own works may line it.
    The gate guard houses, the inspection stations and the mural towers stand beside it, and a ward fence may cross it, gated.
  • Houses are held off by a clear corridor along the road.
    The corridor reaches half the road's width plus 63 ft from its center, so that even a large or turned house's nearest corner stays well clear of the bed.
  • The patrol road need not be built up.
    Unlike an ordinary city street, it is exempt from the rule that a street must be lined with buildings, its wall side being bare by design.
  • A street or alley running at the patrol road ends on it.
    It reaches the road's bed and ends there, neither stopping a sliver short in open ground nor poking a stub out the far side. A genuine crossroads that carries well past is a different thing, and is fine.
  • Keeping the strip clear costs a city about eight percent of its building ground.
    Pulling the built ground in off the wall costs about one perimeter row of buildings, by this project's own measurement. A map that adds the patrol road makes that back somewhere, usually by a small rise in density in one or two quarters, rather than quietly housing fewer inhabitants than the city is declared to hold.

Where a ward's fence reaches the city wall, our maps end it on the rampart.

  • A ward fence ends on the rampart; it does not cross it.
    On our maps the rampart is what seals the ward: the fence's ends abut solid wall so that the ward gate cannot simply be walked around. A fence continuing out through the rampart would enclose nothing on the far side, and would draw as two walls crossing rather than one wall closing against another.
  • Ending a ward fence on the rampart is a GUESS that runs against the one city read.
    At Tang Chang'an every ward was walled on all four sides and a wall-side street ran along all four sides inside the outer wall, so no ward wall met the city wall at all. The Tang form would close the fence along the inner edge of that street instead of borrowing the rampart as the ward's fourth side.
  • Where two lines meet on our maps, one of them ends at the junction.
    A lane stops at the through-lane it reaches instead of poking a stub out the far side, and a channel joins another at a T or a Y instead of running across it. A ward fence meeting the rampart is the same junction, a convention of these maps.
  • A fence may stop up to about 30 ft short of the wall or past it.
    A fence stopping short of the wall leaves a gap the commoners could walk around, and the rule that catches that allows about 30 ft of slop in either direction. So a fence end standing 13 or 15 ft outside the ring sits comfortably inside the tolerance.
  • An overshoot shows only in the drawn ink.
    The rampart's stroke covers only its own half width, about 16 ft, while the fence line is drawn with a rounded end that inks about 7 ft past its last recorded corner. Fifteen plus seven against sixteen leaves about 6 ft of palisade standing on the moat's bank outside the rampart, at three feet to the pixel.
  • The check measures the drawn tip, not the recorded corner.
    Both stroke widths are read from the drawing's own records, so that what is drawn and what is judged cannot drift apart. A fence's interior corners are measured too: a fence that dives out through the wall and back mid-run is the same crossing, further from its end.
  • Each fence end is snapped onto the wall before it is drawn.
    Placing the ends by hand did not work: on all three drawn cities they were 3 to 15 ft out, invisible while authoring. Each end is snapped onto the wall's centerline, trimmed or extended along its own last segment to where that line crosses the ring. That is the rule a lane follows when it is extended to the through-lane it should meet, because a snap straight to the nearest point kinks the fence's last stretch off its line. A last segment running parallel to the wall never meets it, and falls back to the nearest point on the ring.
  • An end more than 72 ft from the wall is left where it is, and reported.
    That is not a junction but a fence failing to reach the rampart, a real hole in the ward, and dragging it onto the wall would hide it.
