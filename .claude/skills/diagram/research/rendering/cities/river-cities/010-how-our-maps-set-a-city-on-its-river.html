How our maps set a city on its river

Most of the provincial cities our maps draw sit on a river; Tango, whose stream runs off the map, is the exception. A river city's map shows its trunk river running off the sheet at both ends, drawn heavier than anything else on it, the dug moat included, because on that side the river is the city's moat. The moat covers the landward faces, and a canal brings boats in through a water gate.

  • The moat is an open arc, not a closed ring.
    It covers the three landward faces, and both of its ends run onto the river, which closes the ring of water itself, so every point of the wall stands behind either the moat or the river.
  • A navigable canal leads from the water through a grated water gate to a dock basin inside the wall.
    It is drawn lighter than the moat and heavier than a field ditch. Its water gate is grated as the Pan Gate's at Suzhou was; the dock basin inside is a GUESS, since no page we read describes one.
  • The wall has a matching gap at the water gate.
    No tower stands on that corner of the wall.
  • The canal reaches the dock, and buildings keep out of its water.
    It joins the dock as a street joins a road, with no visible gap.
  • Jetties are fingers from the near bank, never bars across the river.
    Each planked jetty is rooted at the bank, with its tip in the near half of the water.
  • The wharf stands outside the gate that faces the river.
    Its jetties, stores and market stand there, as Shanghai's wharf district lay between a gate of the county city and the river; it is the river city's form of the market outside a city gate.
  • A river city has no Imperial road through it.
    Its highway passes off the sheet, so what the map draws instead is a road net that leaves the view in at least two directions.
  • The dead cross the river.
    The moat's set-back from water (how far a burial ground keeps from open water) leaves no dry landward edge to put them on, so the funerary ground sits on the far bank, downstream of the city and clear of the bridge road.

A river city also keeps the rules our maps give every city.

  • A gate's two posts stand square to the wall where it is.
    They stand to either side of the opening, square to the wall's local line, so the posts of a gate in an east or west wall stand north and south of it, not upright on the page.
  • A samurai country estate faces the capital, and stands clear of the highway out of the gate.
    The rule is at how our maps place country estates around a city.
  • A burial or cremation ground sits beside the farmland, never on a paddy or its ditches.
    In southern China graves were kept far from water and off good farmland; the set-back from a flooded field is part of how far from water a burial ground lies.
  • A city holds a temple to each of its clan's two patron Fortunes, and those are the temples labeled.
    That is the setting's own rule; the warrior Fortune's may be added where there is a samurai quarter, and everything else is a small wayside shrine, however fitting a larger dedication would be.
