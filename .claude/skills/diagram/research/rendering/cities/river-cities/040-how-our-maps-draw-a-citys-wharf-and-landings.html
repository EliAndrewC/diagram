How our maps draw a city's wharf and landings

Our maps draw a river city's working face as its bank: a band of stone along the water with a coursing line, two or three flights of steps notched into it, each of four treads going down toward the water, and mooring posts along its top. They draw it so because a flight of steps in a faced bank meets a moored boat at whatever height the river stands. The faced edge follows the river's own line, so if a river is redrawn its wharf moves with it.

  • The quay is drawn as a line along the bank, not as a building.
    It is drawn like the towpath, a map drawing convention.
  • A pier springs from the faced bank.
    A pier is there for reach, out to deeper water, so on our maps it starts at the revetment, and the two meet by design.
  • How many piers does a city's wharf need?
    Fewer than its stores, because the bank, continuous along the frontage, is the unloading face, and its capacity is measured in feet of mooring rather than in piers. A river boat ran to 73 ft in an Edo-period record and to some 89 ft on the Tone River, so a few hundred feet of faced bank moors four or five of the middling boats abreast of the stores they fill. That is ample, a GUESS, for the six granaries and three warehouses of Shiro Daika, a domain capital our maps draw, whose traffic includes the tax rice.
  • Shiro Daika keeps its three piers.
    For a faced bank, which needs few piers or none, three may be slightly many, but a port that also lands stone wants at least one, a GUESS, and timber wants none, since it came downriver afloat and was kept in the water. The faced edge runs along Shiro Daika's granary frontage, the part of the bank that does most of the work.
  • Our maps draw no boardwalk and no floating stage.
    A floating dock on pontoons, as marinas use, is today's answer to water that rises and falls; the steps were the answer before modern times.

Two of our cities let their boats inside the wall, and one does not.

  • Shiro Daika unloads at a bank quay, not at a dock inside the city.
    Its river runs past its wall with a live current, so it takes the river landing and the towpath. Nagahara and Minami, which stand on still water at grade, draw a water gate and a dock basin instead, their canals joining the moat and the moat's river junction the one way in for boats. Which form a city gets follows its water, not its size, as the history behind it sets out.
  • Shiro Daika's moat is not opened to boats.
    Nagahara's and Minami's moats meet their rivers at grade, so a barge passes from river to moat to canal through the water gate. Shiro Daika's moat stands off the bank behind two sluiced feeder channels whose whole job is to hold its level, and opening them to boats would give up the control that keeps the moat wet. Its sluices are at How our maps draw water gates and sluices (shuimen).
  • A wharf is never only grain.
    A landing was its town's commercial hub, and many landings were markets, so behind the wharf our maps plan a chain: the wharf, the domain granary, the brokers' row, then the entertainment district. Its granary and brokers are at How our maps draw a capital's rice storehouses and brokers' row (kura, fudasashi).

An official's compound on a river, as at Hayakawa, keeps its landing in another way.

  • The compound's wall stands back from the bank, with the bank street between.
    Samurai residences on Hiroshima's rivers were built back from the revetment and walled.
  • Its landing is a flight of steps across that street, reached from a gate in the wall.
    The steps are cut into the revetment, and no back gate opens onto them, since samurai residences are thought to have had no gated landing. That the landing is the compound's own rather than a public one is a GUESS: no page we read describes an official's compound with a landing of its own.
  • A pier springs from the revetment only where the bank shelves.
    Elsewhere the steps serve.
  • Our maps draw no merchant's landing behind a back gate.
    Our maps draw nothing attested only in modern practice, and the private landings, the back-gate kind among them, are dated to the modern period. A landing in the townsmen's quarters is drawn as the early-modern one: a flight of steps on the bank where boats berth and goods come ashore, not one reached through a house's own gate.

The rule the map follows for a town on navigable water, which gets a landing as its freight link:

  • A town's landing is a stretch of faced bank with one or two flights of steps and mooring posts.
    An open unloading ground lies behind it, as a landing had its moorings and its unloading ground.
  • The landing wholesaler's house and a few storehouses stand at its head, with a tea house or an inn near it.
    A landing was run by its wholesaler, and kept tea houses and inns for shippers and travelers.
  • The landing stands where the highway or the town's main street meets the water.
    Landings flourished at crossings with the main highways; placing a town's landing there is this record's reading of that.
  • How long a town's faced bank runs is a GUESS, scaled down from a city's wharf.
    No page we read gives a town landing's length or the number of its flights of steps.
