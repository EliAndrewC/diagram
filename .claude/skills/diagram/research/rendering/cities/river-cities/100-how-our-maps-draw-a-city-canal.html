How our maps draw a city canal

A city's map carries a canal only where the setting has one. In the setting canals are a domain's and a clan's work, dug only where a sizable inland river is worth canalizing, as the Lion Clan's canal from Toshi Ranbo to the Drowned Merchant River is; so the maps draw none by default (where a village's freight goes). Where a map does carry one, its width, its banks, what lines it and its mouth on the river decide how it looks.

  • How wide is a city canal on our maps?
    Between about 25 and 200 ft: toward the narrow end for a barge cut feeding the town, toward the wide end for a channel carrying the city's through freight. The two ends are Kyoto's Takase canal, about 8 m (~26 ft) wide, and Osaka's Dotonbori, about 36 to 62 m (~119-203 ft) in 1787. Matching the width to the canal's job is a GUESS from those two figures. The width is a degree along the range, chosen for the canal's job, not rolled.
  • Both banks are drawn faced in stone.
    Suzhou's canals were faced with stone revetments on both banks.
  • Where a bank is the castle's, it is the castle's wall.
    Edo's outer moat was walled in stone on the castle side, with a goods landing on the other bank.
  • What lines a reach of canal is a knob, rolled per settlement.
    Either house backs with small stepped water landings at intervals, as at Suzhou, or an open landing with a row of storehouses behind it, as at a Japanese landing. Both are attested, so neither is the one right answer.

Where the canal meets the river, a river city's map has one mouth for boats, not two.

  • A canal never taps the river beside the moat's own mouth.
    Two mouths that close together spoil each other's reading, drawn as a doubled channel with a sliver of bank forking between them. What the map must not draw is a canal tapping the river inside the corridor where the moat joins it.
  • Which of two forms a city's mouth takes is a knob, rolled per city.
    Both forms are attested, so neither is the one right answer.
  • In the first form, the canal ends on the corner of the moat's downstream arm.
    Boats run from the river into the moat's arm, through the water gate and into the dock basin, so the moat's downstream junction with the river is the one entrance for boats. It is the Suzhou form, where the water gate joins the city's canals to its moat. A separate mouth further downstream would be the worse drawing as well, since it would have to cross the moat's southern arc at grade.
  • In the second form, the canal opens its own mouth on open bank, well away from the moat's junction.
    It is the Osaka form, where canals were cut straight off a river. That the mouth stands well away from the moat's junction is this project's drawing rule, from the reading problem above, not a finding.
