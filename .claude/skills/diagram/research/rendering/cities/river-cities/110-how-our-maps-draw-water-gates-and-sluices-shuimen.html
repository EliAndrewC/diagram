How our maps draw water gates and sluices (shuimen)

Wherever a canal passes through a city wall our maps draw a water gate: an arch on two piers over the canal, with the bars of its grille across the water, and the canal running visibly beneath it. A sluice is drawn as a timber board across its channel between two posts, with the crossbeam and windlass that lifted it, and its name beside it.

  • A water gate is drawn as an arch on two piers, barred across the water.
    The bars stand for the gear that closed the passage: Pan Gate's sluice gate and palisade, and the lattice gates Kaifeng let down to the water at night. How far the wall is opened for it is at How our maps draw a city's gates (chengmen).

The rule the map follows for a water gate, where a generator does not yet draw it so:

  • The passage through the wall is as wide as its canal.
    That is a GUESS: no page we read gives a water gate's width.
  • A grille crosses the water at the passage's outer and inner faces, and the channel between them is faced on both sides.
    So Pan Gate was built, a sluice gate and a palisade front and back, with revetments along the channel between its two gates.
  • A canal passes through one opening.
    That is a GUESS: no page we read counts the openings of Pan Gate's water passage.
  • A river crossing the wall is drawn as a row of arched openings.
    So Nanjing's East Water Gate carried the Qinhuai, through eleven vaulted openings.
  • How many water gates a city has is a knob, rolled from each map's seed.
    Either every land gate has a water gate beside it and the canals are laid to reach them, as at Suzhou, or a water gate stands only where a watercourse crosses the wall, beside a land gate when one is near and alone when not, as at Kaifeng.

A sluice is drawn wherever a channel changes water: where a tap on a moat or river hands off to a field's own canal, or a field drain hands off to its outfall culvert.

  • A sluice is drawn as its frame above the water.
    The board between its two posts, the crossbeam over them and the windlass drum at the beam's center are the part of a sluice that shows from above. The frame is this record's reasoning from the gate's form, a GUESS.
  • The frame and its drum are drawn larger than life.
    They are drawn at the smallest size the map draws anything, a map drawing convention, as the wells are: a windlass drum about 2 ft across would be under a pixel.
  • A field channel's sluice spans about 16 to 24 ft, and a wider channel's spans bank to bank.
    On the capital's 66 ft feeder channels the frame is stretched until its posts stand on the banks, as a real frame spans from abutment to abutment, its keeper walking the crossbeam.
  • The board lies across the channel it blocks, a few steps down the channel from the junction.
    Across the channel is how a board blocks it, and set off the junction, "across" is plain to the eye.
  • Every sluice is one glyph, with no open or shut form.
    In this record's reading, a GUESS, a moat's gates rest partly raised, trimmed by a few inches, which at 3 ft to the pixel is too small to see. Separate open and shut glyphs would show a state the gates did not have. The state worth showing, a drawdown or a siege, is an event, for a caption on the day it matters.
  • A sluice carries its name.
    Most of a real gate is under the water, so at the whole map's zoom a sluice reads as a bare dark bar; the word does the explaining.
  • The capital's moat is fed at its east and drains at its southwest.
    Its water enters by the feeder channel on the east, runs round the ring and leaves at the southwest, so it has a gate at each end, its water running through rather than standing.
  • A field sluice has a season, but the maps name no month for it.
    No page from before 1868 that we read gives the months a rice field's intake opened and shut.
