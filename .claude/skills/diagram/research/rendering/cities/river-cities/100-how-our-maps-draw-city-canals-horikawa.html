How our maps draw city canals (horikawa)

A city's map carries a canal only where the setting has one. In the setting canals are a domain's and a clan's work, dug only where a sizable inland river is worth canalizing, as the Lion Clan's canal from Toshi Ranbo to the Drowned Merchant River is; so the maps draw none by default (where a village's freight goes). Where a map does carry one, it is drawn between about 25 and 200 ft wide by its job, its banks faced in stone, and it meets the river at a single mouth for boats. The maps draw it from the city canals of Edo-period Japan and of Suzhou in China.

  • How wide is a city canal on our maps?
    Between about 25 and 200 ft: toward the narrow end for a barge cut feeding the town, toward the wide end for a channel carrying the city's through freight. The two ends are Kyoto's Takase canal, about 8 m (~26 ft) wide, and Osaka's Dotonbori, about 36 to 62 m (~119-203 ft) in 1787. Matching the width to the canal's job is a GUESS from those two figures. It is chosen, not rolled.
  • Both banks are drawn faced in stone.
    Suzhou's canals were faced with stone revetments on both banks.
  • Where a canal runs beside the castle, the castle's wall is its bank.
    Edo's outer moat was walled in stone on the castle side, with a goods landing on the other bank.
  • A reach of canal is lined either by house backs or by an open landing and storehouses, rolled per settlement.
    Either house backs with small stepped water landings at intervals, as at Suzhou, or an open landing with a row of storehouses behind it, drawn from the Japanese landing, where merchants' wholesale houses and storehouses gathered. Both are attested, so neither is the one right answer.

Where the canal meets the river, a river city's map has one mouth for boats, not two. That mouth takes one of two forms, both attested, so neither is the one right answer: the form is a knob, rolled per city.

  • A canal never taps the river beside the moat's own mouth.
    Two mouths that close together are hard to tell apart: they draw as a doubled channel with a sliver of bank forking between them.
  • In one form of mouth, the canal ends on the moat's downstream corner, so boats enter by the moat.
    Boats run from the river into the moat, through the water gate and into the dock basin, so the moat's downstream junction with the river is the one entrance for boats. It is the Suzhou form, where the water gate joins the city's canals to its moat. A separate mouth further downstream would be the worse drawing as well, since it would have to cross the moat's downstream arc at grade.
  • In the other form of mouth, the canal opens onto the river by itself, on open bank well away from the moat.
    It is the Osaka form, where canals were cut straight off a river. That the mouth stands well away from where the moat joins the river is this project's drawing rule, not a finding: two mouths close together would draw as one doubled channel.
