How our maps draw a capital's aqueduct (josui)

A capital map draws its aqueduct above ground wherever the history put it above ground: the intake works on the river, the open channel running to the city, a flume where the channel must cross a watercourse, and its end at a gate of the city. Inside the wall the history buried the water in pipes, so the map draws what a resident saw of it there, its wells. No arch is drawn anywhere: an arched aqueduct is the one form the research found no trace of in East Asia.

  • The open channel is drawn as blue water between earthen banks.
    The Tamagawa ran open to the sky all the way to the city's gate, so its water shows. The map draws the channel about 10 ft wide, far narrower than the 36 ft cargo canal; no page we read gives a josui's width, so the width is a GUESS. Its banks are drawn darker and wider than life, a map drawing convention: the true banks of so narrow a channel would vanish on the page.
  • The intake is drawn as a sluice on the river.
    Two head-posts, each about 3 ft square, and a lifted board across the channel's mouth make the intake read as built water works rather than a stray stream - a map drawing convention. The historical intake was a weir, as the research tells.
  • At what angle does the intake leave the river?
    About 35 degrees, pointing downstream with the current, and downstream of the point where the moat's feeder leaves the river. No page we read gives the angle at which a josui's intake left its river, so the angle is a GUESS; which way a channel leaves a river, and why, is at Which way does an offtake leave a river.
  • The channel runs short and direct to the city.
    Edo's long josui wound along a ridge to hold their gentle fall across many miles of country; where a capital's river is near, the map runs the channel straight to its gate, every stretch of it running downhill along the map's declared fall of the land, from northeast to southwest. That a near source runs direct is this project's reading.
  • A bridge is drawn only where the channel crosses other water.
    Edo carried the Kanda Josui over the Kanda River in a flume, and a route that crosses no water carries no bridge, so a map whose channel crosses nothing shows none. A road that crosses the open channel is given a bridge like a road crossing any other watercourse.
  • The channel ends in a basin at the east gate, clear of the moat.
    The Tamagawa ended at the Yotsuya Okido, where a water-watch station stood and the water went into buried pipes. The map ends its channel in a square basin, about 16 ft across, on the moat's outer bank at the approach to the east gate. That the gate held a basin is a GUESS: the pages we read describe a station that regulated the flow, not a tank. The basin stands clear of the moat so that the drawing says that the supply enters the city here, not that a stream runs into the moat.
  • Where does the channel's surplus water go?
    On our maps, into the moat. Edo's surplus was let out through a spillway into a valley and the Shibuya River; that on a walled capital's map the moat is where it would go is this project's reading.
  • Inside the wall, the pipes are not drawn, and their wells are.
    Edo's buried pipes ran to the residences and row houses, where wells were installed for drawing water. The map's draw-points inside the wall are these aqueduct wells, the josui-ido; in use a resident drew from one just as from a well sunk to ground water, so they are drawn as wells, among the ordinary wells the map already has. The ground the capital sets aside for the aqueduct inside its wall pays for these works, not for a channel, since the channel is buried.
