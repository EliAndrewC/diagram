How our maps draw a capital's aqueduct (josui)

A capital map draws its aqueduct above ground wherever the history put it above ground: the intake works on the river, the open channel running to the city, a flume where the channel must cross a watercourse, and its end at a gate of the city. Inside the wall the history buried the water in pipes, so the map draws what a resident saw of it there, its wells. No arch is drawn anywhere: we found no East Asian aqueduct that stood on arches.

  • The open channel is drawn as blue water between earthen banks.
    The Tamagawa Josui, Edo's main aqueduct, ran open to the sky all the way to the city's gate, so its water shows. Its banks are drawn darker and wider than life, a map drawing convention: the true banks of so narrow a channel would vanish on the page.
  • The channel is drawn about 10 ft wide.
    No page we read gives a josui's width, so the width is a GUESS. It keeps the aqueduct far narrower than the capital's 36 ft cargo canal.
  • The intake is drawn as a sluice on the river.
    Two head-posts, each about 3 ft square, and a lifted board across the channel's mouth make the intake read as built water works rather than a stray stream - a map drawing convention. The historical intake was a weir.
  • At what angle does the intake leave the river?
    About 35 degrees, pointing downstream with the current, and downstream of the point where the moat's feeder leaves the river. No page we read gives the angle at which a josui's intake left its river, so the angle is a GUESS; which way a channel leaves a river, and why, is at Which way does an offtake leave a river.
  • The channel runs short and direct to the city.
    Edo's Tamagawa Josui followed a ridge across many miles of country; where a capital's river is near, the map runs the channel straight to its gate, every stretch of it running downhill with the slope of the map's land, which falls from northeast to southwest. That a near source runs direct is this project's reading.
  • A flume is drawn only where the channel crosses other water.
    Edo carried the Kanda Josui over the Kanda River in a flume; a channel that crosses no water needs none, so none is drawn.
  • A road that crosses the channel is given a bridge.
    The open channel is a watercourse like any other, so a road crosses it the way it would cross a stream.
  • The channel ends at the capital's east gate.
    The Tamagawa Josui ended at the Yotsuya Okido, a gate where a highway entered Edo. A water-watch station stood there, and the water went into buried pipes. On our maps the capital's wall and its east gate stand in for the Okido, a map drawing convention.
  • The channel ends in a square basin, clear of the moat.
    The basin, about 16 ft across, stands on the moat's outer bank at the approach to the east gate. That the gate held a basin is a GUESS: the pages we read describe a station that regulated the flow, not a tank. The basin stands clear of the moat so that the drawing says that the supply enters the city here, not that a stream runs into the moat.
  • Where does the channel's surplus water go?
    On our maps, into the moat. Edo's surplus was let out through a spillway into a valley and the Shibuya River; that on a walled capital's map the moat is where it would go is this project's reading.
  • Inside the wall, the pipes are not drawn, and their wells are.
    Edo's buried pipes ran to the residences and row houses, where wells were installed for drawing water. The map's draw-points inside the wall are these aqueduct wells, the josui-ido. The ordinary wells stay, and the aqueduct's wells are drawn among them as wells, since on this project's reading a resident drew from one as from any other well.
  • The aqueduct's ground inside the wall holds its wells, not a channel.
    A capital sets aside ground inside its wall for its aqueduct. Because the pipes there are buried, that ground goes to the wells and their works rather than to an open channel.
