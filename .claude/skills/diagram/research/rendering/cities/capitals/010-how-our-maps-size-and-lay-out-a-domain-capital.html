How our maps size and lay out a domain capital, the daimyo's castle town (jokamachi)

Our maps draw a domain capital as a castle town: a castle, rings of samurai houses graded by rank, a rim of temples and the government's approach avenue, with the townspeople's wards making up most of the city. The walled city is drawn about 1.2 by 1.1 miles, its castle about 2,550 by 2,100 ft, and only about one inhabitant in eight is a samurai. The form is the Japanese castle town's, because a domain capital is a daimyo's seat, a Japanese institution; Chinese practice is still used where it speaks, and settles what the Japanese record leaves open. Every size the maps draw for a capital was measured in feet and checked against a size recorded for a real place.

  • At the capital, the Japanese castle town leads and Chinese practice breaks ties.
    The rest of our maps take Chinese practice first, with Japan to settle ties. A Chinese prefectural seat has no castle keep (tenshu), no rank-graded rings of samurai houses and no rim of temples (teramachi), so for the capital alone the order is reversed - a deliberate choice of this project.
  • Villages, county towns and provincial cities keep Chinese practice first.
    They are administrative and agrarian settlements, where Chinese practice is the better guide, so the reversal changes nothing below the capital.
  • The setting's capital is a merchant city with a castle in it, not a garrison with a market attached.
    The setting's capital is about 13% samurai, where in Hikone, a real Japanese castle town, warriors were, by one estimate, more than half the town, so the maps do not copy a Japanese castle town's proportions. The samurai rings are drawn, but thin, and the townspeople's wards (machi) are the bulk of the city.
  • The 53 wards of Hikone, a Japanese castle town, set how many named wards a capital carries.
    Hikone's townspeople lived in 53 separate wards in 1695, in the Edo period, and our maps take that count as the guide to how many named wards a capital has.
  • A capital's clan changes its names, not its layout.
    Every clan has the same needs and the same material world, so a capital's layout, its buildings and its proportions are the same whichever clan rules it. The castle towns of Japan's many domains were zoned by one rule, rank nearer the castle, and the setting gives no clan a capital laid out its own way; rules of layout by clan would be an invention with nothing behind them.

The rule the map follows: a capital's clan changes only names, dedications and labels:

  • the patron fortunes to which its two sovereign temples are dedicated;
  • the lineage names on its compounds;
  • the names of its trade wards, taken from their trades.

No layout rule, no count and no proportion varies by clan.
