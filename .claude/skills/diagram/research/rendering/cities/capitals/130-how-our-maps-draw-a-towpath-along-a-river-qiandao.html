How our maps draw a towpath along a river (qiandao)

A river capital's map draws a towpath on the wharf's own bank of the river, and no road beside the river. The towpath is a narrow hauling path running along the bank to the wharf and no further, and it is drawn unlike any road, so that a reader does not take it for one. Inside the wall the river's edge is the quay of the landing, which belongs to the wharf.

  • No trunk road runs beside the river.
    Water carried bulk goods far more cheaply than land, so on this project's reading a road shadowing the river would be redundant, as the research sets out. A capital's overland roads leave it in the directions its river does not serve.
  • The towpath is drawn as one thin line on the bank.
    It has no roadbed and no dashed center line, only a single fine line, a map drawing convention for a path beaten by the haulers' feet. The map keeps a narrow strip beside it clear, so buildings stand off the bank.
  • The towpath runs to the wharf and stops there.
    A towpath served the boats, and on this project's reading it went only as far as they did, so the map draws it along the bank up to the wharf it serves and no further.
  • The quay inside the wall is the wharf's frontage, not a road.
    A river landing was a place where cargo was loaded and unloaded, and a town in its own right, not a route, so the map draws the quay with the wharf district. How the map draws the wharf is at The wharf's working face.
  • A towpath out in the water is not drawn yet.
    At Shaoxing part of the towpath runs in the watercourse itself, parallel to the bank, on stone slabs laid across stone piers. The bank path is the common form and the map's default; the causeway in the water is held for a capital whose bank is too steep or too built up to carry a path.
