How our maps house a capital's samurai

A capital map houses its samurai in three kinds of dwelling: a labeled walled compound for each lineage of the ruling house, walled estates and detached houses for the senior samurai, and retainer terraces - row houses - for the junior. Because the setting's capital is staffed with the domain's senior samurai, the large houses are most of its samurai ground and the terraces the least of it: of about 312 samurai households, about 218 senior and 94 junior.

  • A capital's senior houses outnumber its junior ones, the reverse of a provincial city.
    The provincial housing rule wants a minority of large senior houses among many small junior ones, which is right for a provincial city at 27% senior. At the capital's 70% the mix flips, and the large houses and walled estates are the majority of the samurai fabric (the rank shares).
  • A capital map draws no foot soldiers' quarter; its junior samurai live in retainer terraces.
    The setting's ashigaru are peasants of the countryside, not the lowest samurai, so the dense rows a castle town kept for its foot soldiers have no one to house here, and the rank the rows would have held is the smallest in a capital, not the largest. What stands in their place is a retainer terrace: modest row housing for the capital's roughly 94 junior (Rank 1-4) households, among them castle guards, household retainers of the daimyo's retinue and junior officials in training.

The budget model sizes a city's wall from the ground its households take, so a capital needs a figure for each of its two new kinds of dwelling.

  • How much ground does a walled samurai estate take?
    About 0.86 acre, some 37,000 square feet. The anchor is the 1,000-koku Suganuma plot at Fukui, 28 by 32.5 ken, about 167 by 194 ft or 32,400 square feet. A plot already holds its own yard, and its wall is its boundary, so it carries far less shared margin than a detached house; a modest 1.15 times for street margin gives the estate's gross ground.
  • The estate's size is checked against what the maps already draw.
    It is 1.7 times an ordinary in-wall samurai house's gross ground, 1.3 times the drawn merchant-estate court of 0.59 acre, and comfortably under the extramural country manor of about an acre, which has land a city plot does not. That acre is at the small end of the history, whose moated country residences run from about 1.2 acres to about 3 (How big was a samurai's country manor, and what did it hold?).
  • How much ground does a retainer terrace take per household?
    About 5,900 square feet gross (0.14 acre), for a drawn unit of about 990 square feet at a row-housing gross-up, just above the laborer's row house at 891. The terrace houses Rank 1-4 samurai, poor but reaching 16 koku at the top, so its unit sits above the historical foot soldiers' unit - about 430 square feet a household at Shibata, 18 ft of frontage each - and below the detached samurai house we draw at 2,322 square feet. That drawn house matches the Matsue main house of about 67 tsubo almost exactly, though Matsue's households were retainers of 500 to 1,000 koku, well above the terrace's band. Where the terrace's unit falls between the two is a GUESS.
  • How firm are the two figures?
    Both are in use as the capital's ground costs, and both rest on measured anchors. Both are interpolations, and the terrace's is the softer: it is bracketed by real numbers at both ends, but its place between them is a judgment. The estate's rests directly on a measured plan.
  • How big a wall do these figures make?
    Priced in the budget model - 2,160 packed households, a samurai cohort split by rank into about 60 walled estates, 160 detached houses and 94 terrace units, a castle of about 50 ha (~124 acres), about 15 ha (~37 acres) of the capital's larger civic program and 7% of the ground for streets - the capital needs an interior of about 270 ha (~667 acres), a ring about 1.2 by 1.1 miles across with a circuit of about 3.6 miles.
  • A capital's packed household is priced at about 0.20 acre.
    A capital's drawn fabric comes out at about 0.28 acre of gross ground per packed family - estates, works and training halls set in the quarters, generous courts - so pricing its wall from the provincial 0.14 acre would under-build it by some 40% and spill more than half the cohort into suburbs a capital does not allow. The capital's packed household is priced at a packed-tight 0.20 acre, between the provincial figure and that loose fabric, and the drawn fabric has to meet it rather than merely be measured against it.
  • Streets take 15% of a capital's interior, against 7% in a provincial city.
    A capital carries a street mesh, lanes, block alleys, its ceremonial and rear approaches and the clear ground kept round forty-odd walled compounds - about a fifth of the interior in the loose fabric - so 15% is declared for the packed-tight one. This correction and the packed household's nearly cancel, so the wall as drawn lands close to the corrected minimum.
  • How many of a capital's samurai live inside its wall?
    85%, against two thirds in a provincial city, whose other samurai keep estates outside and come in. Nearness to the daimyo's court is the point of a capital posting, and the walled estate is what makes staying inside attractive to the senior samurai who would otherwise build outside. The figure is an estimate - a GUESS the budget spends - and it moves about 40 households of the costliest kind of housing.

The lineages of the ruling house - its great families, each holding many of the domain's samurai - keep compounds in the capital that no provincial city has.

  • A lineage's compound is sized by how many of its households live in the capital, not by its head's rank.
    The history graded a great house's estate by its holding, and a lord's estate housed the lord's people, so holding and household came to the same thing there (the Edo scale and the row houses inside). Where the two part is a provincial chancellor, whose rank alone would buy a large estate but most of whose lineage lives out in its province. This project lets households decide, so that chancellor's compound is the smaller one.
  • How big is a lineage's compound?
    From about 36,000 square feet (~0.8 acre), near the 1,000-koku plot at Fukui, for the smallest house below the House Chancellery's line, to about 90,000 square feet (~2 acres), near the smallest lords' estates in Edo, or somewhat more, for the largest seated chancellor's. None approaches the Honda estate's 356,000 square feet: that was the greatest of the Kaga domain's eight elder houses, and our capital is a Hikone-scale town. Where the Chancellery's line falls against those anchors is a GUESS, since no page we read gives the estates of a mid-sized domain's elder houses.
  • Each lineage compound is a walled estate with a row-house gate.
    It takes the form a senior retainer's estate had, with earthen walls and a gate built into a row house.
  • The compounds stand in the town, nearest the castle, the chancellors' nearest of all.
    Both sitings the history records are real forms - nearest the castle by standing, or inside the castle's third bailey - and two attested forms make a knob. But the castle is drawn blank, and a labeled lineage compound inside it would vanish from the map, so for now the maps draw only the first form.

The rule the map follows: a capital's junior samurai live in retainer terraces of about 5,900 square feet of gross ground per household, and its senior samurai in detached houses and walled estates of about 37,000 square feet each; 85% of them live inside the wall. Each lineage compound is a walled estate with a row-house gate, sized by its households resident in the capital, from about 36,000 square feet for the smallest house below the Chancellery's line to about 90,000 square feet, or somewhat more, for the largest seated chancellor's (a GUESS at where the line falls), never approaching 356,000; the compounds take the samurai ground nearest the castle, the chancellors' nearest of all.
