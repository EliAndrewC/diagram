How our maps scale a capital's program up from a provincial city's

Our maps draw a capital as a provincial city's program made larger. Most features are the provincial city's, multiplied - gates, fire towers, wells, bathhouses, storehouses - and every multiplied count is a GUESS, scaled from the provincial figure by the capital's larger area and population rather than researched one by one. A few works are gathered into one place instead, a few change their form, four provincial rules are reversed, and a set of features no provincial city has is added.

The provincial rules a capital reverses

  • Walled samurai compounds are ordinary inside a capital's wall.
    In a provincial city the governor's is the only walled samurai compound inside the wall. A capital's councilors, domain ministers and their deputies live inside the ring in walled houses, as a castle town's upper samurai did near the castle (the history), and those walls are what the lineage compounds need. Senior housing is the larger share of a capital's samurai houses and the retainers' row houses the smaller, the reverse of a provincial city's (The capital's samurai are SENIOR-heavy).
  • A capital draws no governor's mansion.
    The ruler is the daimyo, in the castle, and the governor belongs to the provincial city, so the provincial rules that want a governor's mansion, and want it large, have nothing to apply to.
  • A capital draws no farmland inside its wall.
    The setting puts both kinds of city's farmers in the villages around them. A provincial city may still declare a farmed district inside its wall, and a capital may not - this project's own rule. A city's farming is at Does a city farm inside its walls?
  • The Imperial road always runs through a capital.
    So the commercial ribbon that follows an Imperial road is never optional here.

What multiplies

No page we read counts a castle town's gates, fire towers, wells, stages, outcaste quarters, storehouses or merchant estates against a smaller town's, so each capital count below is a GUESS scaled from the provincial figure; only the named wards follow the castle towns' trade wards.

  • Everything that hangs on a gate multiplies with the gates.
    The gate market, the flophouse outside it, the caravan ground, the notice board and the inspection station come with each gate, so a capital repeats them at every one of its gates.
  • A capital of about 12,400 draws six bathhouses, all one size.
    Edo kept more than 600 bathhouses for a million or more inhabitants, about one to every 2,000, and our maps roll a city's count from its population on that ratio. That the capital's baths were no grander than a provincial city's, only more numerous, is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • A capital draws two or three pawnshops and implies the rest.
    Edo's ratio would give a capital some thirty. The map draws two or three, each with its storehouse court for pledges, as a representative sample, and the rest are taken to stand in the shop rows unmarked - a drawing convention.
  • Fire towers go by area.
    The rule the sources give is a spacing, not a head count, so a capital's towers are counted from its built area, some five times a provincial city's.

The rule the map follows: inside a capital's wall walled samurai compounds are ordinary, senior housing is the larger share of the samurai houses, and there is no governor's mansion and no farmed district. The Imperial road always runs through. A capital draws, at the counts in the table:

  • four gates or more, with the full gate program at every one;
  • about 10-15 fire towers and 160-240 public wells;
  • several theater stages with an entertainment district;
  • two burakumin quarters with two tanning yards;
  • about 20 merchant storehouses and 4-8 walled merchant estates;
  • dozens of named wards.

What is gathered into one place

  • A capital's second kiln works stands beside the first.
    Kilns gathered into quarters, so a capital's second works shares the first one's clay pit rather than standing apart. That a capital has exactly two is a GUESS.
  • A capital draws one cremation ground, made larger, not several.
    Southern Song Lin'an kept dozens of crematoria, but Edo gathered its temple crematoria to one site in 1667, and our capitals follow the Japanese form. How the ground is drawn is at How our maps draw cremation grounds and bone mounds (kasoba).
  • A capital shows more burial grounds than the graveyard rule's two to four.
    No page we read gives how many burial grounds a city of this size kept. Our maps' graveyard rule wants two to four community grounds, but every temple also keeps its own parish ground, and a capital has seven or eight temples, so it shows more cemeteries than the rule's band - the two rules count different things.

What gathers in the capital, and what stays one to a seat

  • A capital draws several roofed theaters and an entertainment district.
    The roofed theater's footprint is a GUESS; its size is at How big is a roofed playhouse?
  • Every seat draws one pauper ground.
    One to a seat is the Song order, not the only form the grounds took; the mound our maps draw, and its size, are at How our maps draw burial grounds in towns and cities.

What changes its form

  • A capital's dyers are a row of three to five lots on one bank of its water.
    Cloth use grows with the population, so the number of vats grows too, but a capital draws three to five dyers' lots side by side on one bank rather than one larger yard. The lot count is a GUESS, and so is setting the row downstream of the town.
  • The castle's own dyer is at most a purveyor within the row.
    Court cloth-making on the Qing model stands above a domain capital, and no page we read says a domain's castle was served by one dyer of its own, so at most one of the row's lots is a purveyor to the castle - a GUESS.
  • A capital draws the ruling family's tomb precinct and two to four crypts of senior lineages.
    The family's precinct takes one of two forms: a family temple in the town, or a mausoleum on a wooded rise, which may stand outside the walls. The two to four crypts of the senior retainers' (karo) lineages stand in the temples of the temple quarter; they and their count are a GUESS.
  • A provincial city's single mausoleum does not multiply.
    In the setting, the governor of a stewardship province is appointed by the daimyo, and their city's one mausoleum belongs to the local great lineage. A dynasty province's governorship is hereditary in practice, so whether its city grows a tier of lineage tombs too is a GUESS.
  • A capital's official kiln on the map is a tile works at its edge.
    Both forms of official kiln are attested - the building-tile works at a capital's edge and the fine-ware kiln sent to a guarded site - so our maps draw the tile works, and a fine-ware domain kiln is taken to stand off the map.

What only a capital draws

  • The Imperial Magistrate's compound is drawn in an ink of its own.
    It is foreign sovereign ground and must not read as another domain office, so it takes the walled manor's form in an ink no other building on the map uses. Its size is at How big is the Imperial Magistrate's compound?
  • Each lineage of the ruling house but one has a named, walled compound.
    The ruling lineage's seat is the castle and needs no compound of its own. The others' compounds are labeled with their names, and eight named compounds are what make a map read as one particular domain's seat rather than a generic large city.
  • A lineage's compound is sized by how many of its households live in the capital, not by its head's rank.
    The house our capital was first worked out for carries nine lineages, and a lineage holding a tenth or more of the domain's samurai usually takes a seat in the House Chancellery: six chancellors, three houses below the line. One of the six is a provincial lineage, seated out in its province, and its chancellor still lives in the capital and holds a compound there, because a Chancellery seat is held in person; the compound is smaller only because most of the lineage lives in that province. How the history graded a great house's estate is at How big is a lineage's compound in the capital?
  • A capital's lineage compounds come in four sizes.
    The ruling lineage has the castle; the cosmopolitan chancellors take the largest walled compounds after it; the provincial chancellor a markedly smaller one; the houses below the Chancellery line modest ones.
  • The sovereign temples are head houses, not larger precincts.
    Each of the two holds its order's abbot's residence, its administration and its library.
  • A witch hunter's office is rolled, not given.
    The office was ordered for every capital and some of those orders were later rescinded.
  • A capital draws no chancellery compound.
    Its council meets in the castle's palace: The chancellery meets in the castle.

The rule the map follows: a capital draws, and a provincial city never does:

  • the Imperial Magistrate's compound, in its own ink, as foreign sovereign ground;
  • one labeled walled compound for each lineage of its ruling house but the ruling lineage, sized by that lineage's households resident in the capital, not by its head's rank;
  • the Emperor's granaries outside the castle, beside the Magistrate's compound or at the wharf;
  • the domain school on the government avenue;
  • retirement housing for the elders who move to the capital, beside a temple;
  • the two sovereign temples as head houses of the domain's orders, each with an abbot's residence, administration and library;
  • a rolled witch hunter's office;
  • trade-named commoner wards, each named for the trade that fills it.

It draws no chancellery compound.
