How our maps fill a city's quarters and leave its empty ground

A walled city on our maps keeps plenty of open ground, as the walled cities of history did under crops and water, but every open space is claimed by something the map draws - a right of way, a field, a working yard. The map divides its interior into declared quarters, judges how densely each is built one quarter at a time, and treats ground that nothing claims as a fault it has to be able to find.

  • A city-wide average cannot tell a good city from a broken one.
    Two cities on our maps, Tango and an early draft of Nagahara that had to be redrawn, had the same number of houses inside the wall - 521 against 525 - and the same median block density, about 2.2 dwellings per acre. The only difference was where the empty ground sat: Nagahara's east was dense, its northwest temple quarter empty, and about 35 commoner dwellings had spilled outside the walls. So the map declares its quarters - each a named region with a role, residential, civic, mixed or reserve - and density is judged one quarter at a time.
  • How densely is a residential quarter built?
    Between about 1.5 and 11 dwellings per acre. The sevenfold span is deliberate: a townsmen's quarter was packed far denser than a samurai or official ward. The numbers at the ends are calibrations against the drawn cities, not historical measurements.
  • A quarter's density leaves out the civic compounds standing in it.
    It is measured over the quarter's ground less those compounds, so that a government ward's yamen does not make the ward read as under-built.

The rule the map follows: a walled city declares quarters that cover at least 85% of its interior, overlap by no more than 5% and spill no more than 3% outside the wall, each quarter naming its role - residential, civic, mixed or reserve - and a reserve quarter naming its kind as well (drill ground, garden, agricultural district). A drill ground is drawn as bare earth inside a dashed muster perimeter and a garden as green lawn; an agricultural district draws no boundary of its own.

  • An agricultural district draws no boundary of its own.
    Its fields, in-wall farmhouses and label already carry the picture, and a dashed boundary read as a stray dotted line cutting through Tango's farmhouses and across the Imperial road above the burakumin neighborhood. The district is still declared, so the per-quarter judging is unaffected.

The rule the map follows: every residential or mixed quarter carries between about 1.5 and 11 dwellings per acre, measured over the quarter's ground less the civic compounds inside it. A purely residential quarter also holds no empty stretch: its largest pocket clear of any dwelling stays under about 450 ft across, counting wells and civic compounds as ground served, so that a half-dense, half-empty quarter fails even when its average is in band. A mixed quarter is exempt from that, because it legitimately holds a civic forecourt.

The rule the map follows: a civic quarter may be as much as 70% open, but it must actually hold its compounds. Reserve ground of every kind together stays at or under a fifth of the interior; past that the answer is a shorter wall around less ground, never more ground declared reserve.

  • Why may a civic quarter be 70% open?
    A county yamen was laid out around a run of courtyards, and a temple precinct is, in this project's reading, mostly open ground around its halls (The size of a compound and the rank of its buildings). An open quarter with no compounds in it, though, reads as merely empty.
  • Why is ground set aside for drill grounds, gardens and farming capped at a fifth of the interior?
    The government's compounds take only a few percent of a county seat, by this record's guess. The large open users are a drill ground, where a small one stood in a corner inside the wall rather than outside a gate (How our maps size and place drill grounds, archery ranges and riding grounds), and land left to farming in a seat that never grew. A fifth is the share Jinan's lake took in a provincial capital, and the maps use no higher figure, since no page read gives a county seat a larger one. Past it the wall encloses more open ground than a provincial seat justifies.
  • Unclaimed ground is found against the whole area each feature covers, not its center.
    A coarse grid that counts a cell as used when it lies within 360 ft of any building's center misses a bare pocket like the one just inside Tango's north gate, roughly 690 by 285 ft: one building at that radius clears a disc 720 ft across, so only vast voids register. So the map is judged against every claiming feature over the whole ground it covers - buildings, compounds and groves, field and ground areas, wells, stable yards and shrine gates, the rights of way of roads, streets, alleys, the ring road and water, ward fences, the rampart with its patrol strip, and the pond.
  • How large a patch of unclaimed ground is a fault?
    About 0.83 acre. Any cell about 96 ft square that lies 60 ft clear of every claiming feature is dead ground, and a connected patch of it larger than that inside the wall is a fault. The threshold is a calibration, not a finding: the pocket inside Tango's north gate measured about 1.3 acres of dead core, while the largest legitimate open spaces anywhere else among the drawn cities measured about 0.42 and 0.21 acre, so the line sits between them with about a factor of two of headroom each way.

A pocket the map finds is built on or given a use.

  • A pocket is filled, or claimed as working ground.
    A quarter is extended or a neighborhood dropped into it, or the pocket is given a working use.
  • A stable yard is the usual working ground.
    Beaten earth, hitching rails and ground litter keep clear of roads, fields, water, the rampart and every building, and read as a real feature rather than filler. A pocket beside a gate or the stables suits it best, as at Tango's north gate, where the caravans coming from and leaving for the Phoenix border want room to marshal.
  • A thin quarter is filled, not made up for elsewhere.
    Making a quarter that is already dense denser satisfies an average and fixes nothing, so the empty quarter itself is built. At Nagahara that was a pocket of commoner housing in front of the northwest temple, since the approach to a great temple carried the shops and inns that served it - a GUESS: no page we read says so.

The rule the map follows: no connected patch of the city's interior larger than about 0.83 acre stands 60 ft clear of every claiming feature at once. Ground that would otherwise be bare is either built on or given to a drawn working feature - most often a stable yard - which is then a feature of the map with a name, not a gap.
