How our maps place country estates around a city

In this project's setting a samurai family may keep a country estate close to some farmland, but never as part of a village. That departs from the Edo period, when the samurai were gathered into the castle town; the setting's estate is closer to the medieval form, a local lord's seat with its paddy spread before its gate. So an estate is drawn with farmland at its door, and how far from the city it stands is the setting's choice, not the history's.

A city map is under a mile across, about 3,800 ft at a provincial city's scale, so the country estates proper, which lay miles out in the villages around a city, fall off it altogether; only a map of the district around a city, a kind not yet built, would show their full spread. The map draws only the nearest two or three, spread apart on the open approaches toward the capital, each a walled compound on its own ground, and leaves the rest implied. That near estate is a deliberate deviation: it is on the map so that a reader learns the type exists, not because the ground within half a mile of a gate held several.

  • The rice paddies take the ring of farmland just outside the city before any estate is placed.
    The fields are laid out first, and the estates take the pockets of open ground the fields leave.
  • How many estates does a map draw?
    Two or three, at the map's edge, some of them cut by it - as the edge cuts the farm fields, where the cut itself says the land goes on beyond the map. A provincial city's orbit holds many more, but a map draws a sample, not a census. A few estates, even ones whose grounds run off the map, are honest; a ring of estates crowding the wall would be the error.
  • The people of an estate are not counted in the city.
    Taxed farms that stand on their own, samurai country estates among them, are tallied under a settlement in the setting's count of a domain, and the maps count an estate's household with the surrounding village districts, not among the city's own inhabitants.
  • Our maps set estates at least 600 ft apart.
    Each is its own compound on its own land, never one of a tight cluster or a row. The spacing is calibrated against the three drawn cities, not taken from a source.
  • Our maps make the estates different sizes.
    The largest is at least half again the size of the smallest, a spread calibrated against the three drawn cities, not taken from a source.
  • The wealthiest samurai set their country estates on the side of the city that faces the capital.
    In this project's setting the capital is the prestigious direction, whichever way it lies from a given city, so our maps draw a city's estates on that side.
  • Which way do an estate's gates face?
    Not all the same way. The formal gate favors the southeast, an auspicious position, as the main gate of a Beijing courtyard house does; that a country estate's gate followed the same rule is this project's reading. Others face the approach from the city, because each estate fronts its own lane, which is not drawn at this scale.
  • No estate is drawn with a driveway.
    The long, worn approach lanes read on the map as broad roads laid across the countryside, and an approach lane is not a thing drawn at this scale, so the estates stand as clean walled compounds with their approaches implied.
  • Is the estate nearest the city a lord's retreat?
    Our maps draw it as one. That a provincial seat's samurai kept retreats like a daimyo's at Edo, scaled down, is this project's reading: the cases in castle towns include senior retainers, but no page gives their sizes.
  • Which form of retreat does a map draw?
    Two forms are recorded (A lord's retreat just outside town (shimoyashiki)): a house on its own ground just outside the town, with its garden around it, and a street of the town's outer edge lined by a lower mansion and its retainers' houses, as at the castle town of Sumoto. Two attested forms call for a knob rolled per city from its seed. The street has no rule on these maps yet, so until one is written every city draws the first.

The rule the map follows: the paddy takes the farmland just outside the wall first, and a provincial city draws two or three walled country estates in the open ground it leaves, each at least partly on the map; one cut by the map's edge says its land continues beyond. They stand at least 600 ft apart and vary in size, the largest at least half again the smallest. They lie toward the capital on the open approaches the fields leave free; they overlap neither each other nor the wall and moat, and they keep off the highway out of the gate. Their gates do not all face the same way: the formal gate favors the southeast, others face the approach from the city. No estate is drawn with a driveway. The estate nearest the city is drawn as a lord's retreat, a house on its own ground.
