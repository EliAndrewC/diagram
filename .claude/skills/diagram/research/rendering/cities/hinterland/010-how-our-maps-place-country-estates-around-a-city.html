How our maps place country estates around a city

A city map is under a mile across, about 3,800 ft at a provincial city's scale, so the country estates proper, which lay miles out, fall off it altogether. The map draws only the nearest one to three, spread apart on the open approaches toward the capital, each a walled compound on its own ground, and leaves the rest implied. That near estate is a deliberate deviation: it is on the map so that a reader learns the type exists, not because the ground within half a mile of a gate held several.

In this project's setting a samurai family may keep a country estate close to some farmland, but never as part of a village. That too departs from the Edo period, when the samurai were gathered into the castle town; the setting's estate is closer to the medieval form, a local lord's seat with its paddy spread before its gate. So an estate is drawn with farmland at its door, and how far from the city it stands is the setting's choice, not the history's.

  • The paddy takes the ring of farmland just outside the city first.
    The fields are laid out before any country estate is placed, and the estates take the pockets of open ground the fields leave; most of a city's estates stand farther out, off the map. A map showing a single estate, even one whose grounds run off the frame, is honest; a ring of estates crowding the wall would be the error.
  • How many estates does a map draw?
    At least two, at the frame's edge, some of them cut by it - as the frame cuts the farm fields, where the cut itself says the land goes on beyond the map. A provincial city's orbit might hold some eight to fifteen estate households, but a map never claims a census, so any count of two or more is right.
  • The people of an estate are not counted in the city.
    Taxed farms that stand on their own, samurai country estates among them, are tallied under a settlement in the setting's count of a domain, and the maps count an estate's household with the surrounding village districts, not among the city's own inhabitants.
  • Estates stand at least 600 ft apart and vary in size.
    Each is its own compound on its own land, never one of a tight cluster or a row, and the largest is at least half again the size of the smallest. The spacing and the size spread are calibrated against the three drawn cities, not taken from a source.
  • The estates lie on the side of the city that faces the capital.
    In this project's setting the wealthiest samurai set their country estates in the direction of the capital, the prestigious direction, whichever way that lies from a given city. They keep to the open approaches the fields leave free, overlap neither each other nor the wall and moat, and keep off the highway out of the gate.
  • Which way do an estate's gates face?
    Not all the same way. The formal gate favors the auspicious south side, at the southeast, as the main gate of a Beijing courtyard house does; that a country estate's gate followed the same rule is this project's reading. Others face the approach from the city, because each estate fronts its own lane, which the map is cropped too tight to draw.
  • No estate is drawn with a driveway.
    The long, worn approach lanes read on the map as broad roads laid across the countryside, and an approach lane is not a thing drawn at this scale, so the estates stand as clean walled compounds with their approaches implied.
  • The nearest estate is a lord's retreat.
    Two forms of retreat are recorded (A lord's retreat just outside town (shimoyashiki)): a house on its own ground just outside the town, with its garden around it, and a street of the town's outer edge lined by a lower mansion and its retainers' houses, as at Sumoto. Two attested forms call for a knob rolled per city from its seed; the street has no rule on these maps yet, so until one is written every city draws the first. That a provincial seat's samurai kept retreats like a daimyo's at Edo, scaled down, is this project's reading: the castle-town cases include senior retainers, but no page gives their sizes.
  • The full scattered pattern would show only on a map of a district.
    Estates strewn across the whole countryside, each by its own fields and ancestral village, out to perhaps fifteen miles and most of them nowhere near the city, would be visible only on a map of the district around a city - a kind of map not yet built. The rule the map follows: a provincial city draws one to three walled country estates outside its wall, at least two of them falling at least partly inside the cropped window; one cut by the frame's edge says its land continues beyond. They stand at least 600 ft apart, each its own compound on its own land and never a tight cluster, and they vary in size, the largest at least half again the smallest. They lie toward the capital, whichever direction that is from this city, on the open approaches the fields leave free; they overlap neither each other nor the wall and moat, and they keep off the highway out of the gate. Their gates do not all face the same way: the formal gate favors the southeast, others face the approach from the city. No estate is drawn with a driveway.
