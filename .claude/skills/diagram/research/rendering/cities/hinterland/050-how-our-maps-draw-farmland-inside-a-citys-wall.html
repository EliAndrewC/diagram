How our maps draw farmland inside a city's wall

A city on our maps draws no farmland inside its wall unless it declares a farming quarter, and one provincial city does: a whole quarter of the ground inside its wall is fields and vegetable gardens, and the same quarter holds the city's burakumin neighborhood. Farmland inside a wall was common and long-standing in China, so the maps go on drawing it where a city declares it.

  • Drawing no farmland inside a wall is the maps' default, not the history's.
    The history makes farmland inside a wall a common arrangement. The default keeps a city's interior for its houses; a city that wants fields inside declares them.
  • The one city that farms inside its wall has its own reason.
    It has water sources inside the wall, and a shape that made walling its farms out save almost nothing.
  • The farming quarter is where a city's spare ground goes.
    It lets that city hold an average provincial-city population with more space than average per inhabitant inside its wall: the surplus is farmed, not built on. When rows of houses sharing party walls take up less ground than a looser scatter would, the ground they free goes to more farmland, never to more dwellings, because the share of a city's households each caste is given already fixes how many households the city holds.

The fields of the farming quarter are drawn the way fields are drawn outside a city, with their edges worked; how they should be edged inside a wall is where the maps and the history part.

  • The drawn city rings its in-wall fields densely with farmhouses.
    The whole of such a field is in view, so its whole edge must read as worked: about sixteen farmhouses for every 3,000 ft of edge, against seven for a field outside the wall, in a tight inner row with one or two denser outer bands, rather than a sparse few on one side that leave long bare edges. The drawn example runs between twenty-four and thirty-seven by that measure. Both figures are a calibration against the drawn cities and against the farmhouses round a village's fields, not taken from a source.
  • The farmhouses inside the wall are not counted in the city's population.
    The setting counts only a city's own households inside its wall.
  • The ring is a deviation from the setting and from the history.
    The setting puts no farmer household inside a city's wall (Who lives in a provincial city?), and the history has the ground inside a wall worked by the city's own households.

Following the setting and the history, an in-wall field is worked from the city's own houses, so it is bordered by them - the ordinary houses of the quarter that faces it - and not by a ring of farmhouses, which would be farmer households living inside the wall. The drawn city does not follow this rule yet: it keeps the ring above.

The rule the map follows: no farmhouse stands inside a city's wall. An in-wall field is edged by the city's own houses, laborers' and merchants' among them, facing it from the surrounding quarter, and its edge reads as worked because those houses line it (accurate for the city dwellers who farm; the setting's own rule for there being no farmer households). How densely they line it is a calibration against the drawn city.

Not all of the in-wall ground is paddy. Some of it is drawn as vegetable tracts: intensively worked garden ground, fed from wells and from night soil.

  • A vegetable tract is drawn with no surface channel.
    It is watered from wells, as the old farm manual's market garden against a city wall was, so a paddy's channel has nothing to show there. East of a city's Imperial spine a channel could not reach the tract in any case, since it would have to cross the road.
  • A vegetable tract has no farm households round it.
    It is worked by the households of the quarters around it, as Chinese city dwellers grew their own vegetables on the idle plots inside a city.
  • A vegetable tract's parcels are the smallest dry parcels on the map.
    Each is about 55 ft across and no more than 0.15 acre, against 90 to 100 ft for a strip of the grain fields beside a paddy. A kitchen garden is hand-worked ground, so its parcels are smaller than a grain field's, not larger.
  • The tract is cut into an even number of columns.
    A single cut across it would leave one fat column of about 150 ft, the biggest dry parcel on the map - backwards for a garden.
  • How wide was a garden parcel?
    The 55 ft is a calibration against the drawn cities. It is a block of beds, not one bed: it is some five to thirteen of the hand-worked beds the history gives across, and a bed is too small to draw. No page we read gives the width of a garden parcel of the size the map draws.

An in-wall field is watered from a pond, and the ground its water reaches spreads out downhill of the pond like a fan.

  • The pond that feeds an in-wall field sits at the high corner of its ground.
    Water was let into a field by gravity, so the pond stands where water enters a city from high ground, and the fan fills the ground downhill of it. That is our own reading of the gravity feed.
  • Which way does a fan widen?
    A fan has a hand: its wide side falls to the left or to the right. No page we read speaks of it, so the maps draw either hand. The ground an in-wall field sits in is rarely even - a ring road inside the wall leaves a crescent that narrows toward one end - so the maps mirror the whole field where the crescent calls for it, rather than force it into one hand.
