How our maps draw compound walls

A walled compound - a manor or estate, a governor's yamen, a merchant's estate, a mausoleum - appears on a settlement map as its wall, its gate and a blank court, because its interior is the subject of a building plan of its own. On the settlement map the wall and the gate are the whole feature, so they are drawn at their real size: the wall 1.5 to 2 ft thick, the gate 9 to 12 ft wide for a samurai residence and 18 to 24 ft for a yamen. A building plan draws the wall 3 ft thick, and treats it as a building whose ground nothing else may enter.

  • A settlement map draws a compound wall 1.5 to 2 ft thick, the lighter wall's measured range.
    A frameless neribei was perhaps 30 cm (~1 ft) thick, and a surviving late-Edo neribei measures 0.6 m (~2 ft) across its base. The two forms of wall and their sizes are at Compound walls (neribei and tsuijibei).
  • A building plan draws the compound wall 3 ft thick, the heavier of the two real forms, so its stroke reads.
    This is a map drawing convention. The real form it resembles is the rammed-earth tsuijibei, built as much as 1 m (~3 ft) thick; the ordinary lighter wall was about 1 shaku, a foot, or more, a figure given for castle walls, and a surviving neribei measures about 2 ft at its base. So the 3 ft drawn is the heavier of two real forms, not a thickening of the only one. No wall of a magistrate's post was measured. The map's own note on the wall tells its reader the same: drawn thicker than the typical wall so the stroke reads, with the tsuijibei as the real form it resembles.
  • A wall too thin for the sheet is drawn at the thinnest line the sheet can carry.
    A map drawn to scale does not enlarge a feature for legibility. Where a wall would otherwise vanish, it is drawn at the thinnest line the sheet can carry, so that a wall 1.5 ft thick still reads at 3 ft to the pixel - a map drawing convention.
  • A gate post is drawn about 2 ft square.
    The figure is this project's own, drawn under the same kind of floor as the wall - a map drawing convention.
  • The ground under a compound wall is occupied ground.
    The wall is a thick structure with its own roof, so a building plan draws the buildings ringing a court touching the wall and never crossing into it.
  • A building stands a foot or two off the wall, its eaves nearly touching it.
    The plan leaves that clearance behind each building that backs onto the wall. The arrangement is this project's reasoning, a GUESS, set out at Compound walls (neribei and tsuijibei).
  • The wall is never thinned to give a building more room.
    Where a building and the wall compete for ground, the building gives way.
  • A building drawn over the wall would seem to dissolve into it.
    The wall is painted last and covers the outline of anything beneath it, so an overlap is not a small inaccuracy: the map would show the building melting into the masonry.
  • A samurai residence's gate is drawn 9 to 12 ft wide.
    That falls within what surviving Japanese gates measure, from a gate of one bay of about 6 to 8.5 ft to a nagaya-mon opening two ken, about 12 ft; the widths are at How wide was the main gate of a magistrate's post? The official commentary on the nagaya-mon, the gate a samurai of middle rank and above was permitted, describes its servants' quarters and stable and gives no width.
  • How wide is a yamen's gatehouse drawn?
    18 to 24 ft, this project's own calibration on the drawn sheets, a GUESS. A yamen's gate was a roofed building of three bays, but the best-preserved county magistrate's office in China is described by its forty thousand square meters (~10 acres) and its hundred-meter (~328 ft) axis, not by its gate, and no page we read gives such a gate's width.
  • A gate is drawn at a real width, never a fixed distance on the sheet.
    A gap of a fixed size on the sheet opens 204 ft at city scale, which is most of a wall missing.
  • Funerary features are drawn at their real size too.
    A funerary feature drawn to the wrong size reads as a different kind of place. Each is sized in its own section: the cremation ground and its fire bed at How large is a cremation ground, and what was its fire bed?, the bone mound at How large is a bone mound drawn?, and the single grave and the burial ground at How much ground does a grave take, and how large is a burial ground drawn?
