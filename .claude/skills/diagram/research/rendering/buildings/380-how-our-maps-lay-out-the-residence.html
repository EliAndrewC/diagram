How our maps lay out the residence

Every residence our maps draw is the house of the magistrate who holds the posting, set in the compound's private court. It is sized to a district magistrate's house of 1794, its rooms run from the reception at one end to the family's rooms at the other, it is one block under one roof or a few halls stepped back one behind the next, it lodges its guests in its own rooms, and the ground behind it, the shady side, carries the household's service.

  • The house is measured against a district magistrate's house of about 49 tsubo (~1,740 sq ft).
    That is the Yokota house at Matsushiro, built in 1794 for a 150-koku man who served as district magistrate, the one firm figure from before 1868 whose household held the office a county posting holds. On the county magistracy's plan the house, its kitchen and its bath come to about 1,780 sq ft.
  • The 67-tsubo house at Matsue is not used as a measure.
    It measures a house restored from drawings of the Meiji period, after 1868, not the house as it was built.
  • How big is the house of a samurai of about a thousand koku?
    Anywhere between about 41 and 74 tsubo (~1,470-2,630 sq ft), as the two official measures of the Kuchiba house at Hagi stand; the maps take no one figure between them.
  • How big is a kitchen?
    A fraction of the house, its size a GUESS. The one kitchen measured, the Kuchiba house's east wing, is about a sixth of the house by one of its two measures, and no other house read gives its kitchen's share. A kitchen that approaches the whole house is the tell How our maps size a compound and its buildings looks for.

Inside the house, the rooms follow one of the two orders the record holds.

  • The room order is a knob with two attested forms.
    The palace order runs from the reception in front, through the master's rooms, to the family's rooms beyond; the small house's order sets the formal zashiki deepest in, the living rooms between it and the entrance. The second rests on one house, as this project reads its plan.
  • The reception room always stands at one end of the house.
    It does in both forms, so a reception room set between the master's rooms and the family's, which neither form has, is drawn nowhere.
  • Our plans follow the palace order.
    The reception stands at the end nearest the approach, the master's rooms beside it, the family's beyond, and the inner rooms at the far end. On the county magistracy's plan the house is massed in two rows, front and back, as the Kuchiba house's rooms are, with the reception at its east end, the full depth of the house, nearest the middle gate.
  • Each labeled room stands for several rooms.
    The master's rooms, the family's rooms and the inner rooms are each a suite compressed to one label, a drawing convention.

How the house is massed is the second knob.

  • The residence is one block, or two or three halls in echelon - a knob with two attested forms.
    The ordinary form is one block, its rooms under one roof: middle and lower-ranking samurai houses had almost no corridors. Halls stepped back one behind the next are Katsura's form, and each sheet takes one of the two.
  • A residence drawn in echelon reads as a house that was added to.
    At Katsura that is the form a house took as it grew hall by hall, over about half a century, so a plan drawn this way shows a house its holders have built onto.
  • Is each hall joined to the last by a corridor?
    On our maps it is, this project's reading, putting the great houses' corridors between buildings together with Katsura's echelon. No page we read says how Katsura's halls are joined.

A guest is lodged in the house.

  • A posting's guest quarters are the zashiki and the rooms beside it, inside the residence.
    That is where a samurai house kept its guest space.
  • A guest house drawn as a building of its own is a GUESS.
    None was found at a samurai house, and the nearest attested form, the honjin's suite of honor, belongs to a post town's lodging house, not to an office. The county magistracy's plan draws one, facing the garden beside the reception rooms, as its draft of the guest quarters.

Behind the house, the shady side of its grounds is working ground.

  • The garden takes the sunny south, before the reception rooms, where the plan's buildings allow.
    The formal garden lay south of the formal rooms. Where a plan's buildings do not allow it, the reception faces the court or the service ground before its block instead, short of that ideal.
  • The shady north band behind the residence is a service strip, or narrowed to an alley.
    It holds the servants' rowhouse, a utility building and the family privy attached to the house, or it is narrowed to an alley of about 6-10 ft for carts and servants. On the county magistracy's plan the house stands 10 ft off the north wall, with the family privy flush to the wall in the alley.
  • Why put the servants, the well and the privy at the rear?
    By reasoning from where the garden stood, a GUESS: with the garden in the sun to the south, the service went behind the house. The storehouse is the one service building a page places there, and no page we read places a well, the family privy or a servants' range.
  • A wide empty band behind the house is not drawn.
    It would be dead ground inside the walls, and it would leave the household's servants, about ten on the grounds by the plan's own count, a GUESS, drawn nowhere.
  • The household's storehouses stand behind the house, about 12 by 18 ft each.
    That is the common size, about 2 by 3 ken, and the ground between them and the house is a named rear yard, as the Higuchi house kept open ground there. A plan draws two, as the Yokota house kept two; the number for any one sheet is a GUESS.
