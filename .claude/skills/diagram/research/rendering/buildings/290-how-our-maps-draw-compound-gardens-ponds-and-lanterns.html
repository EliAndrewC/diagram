How our maps draw compound gardens, ponds and lanterns

Each compound on our building plans has one ornamental garden, before the reception rooms, and the private rooms look onto it too. Its water is either a small pond or a dry garden of stones and white gravel standing for water, and a stone lantern or more stands in it as one small gray mark. Where the garden lies in the lot is at How our maps lay out samurai residences and their rooms (buke yashiki).

  • The private rooms share the one garden with the formal hall, with stepping stones of their own into it.
    That is the layout at Takayama, where one garden is seen from both the great hall and the intendant's living room, with stepping stones into it from each, and the rooms were rebuilt to an Edo-period plan. It is accurate for Takayama. Carrying it to other, humbler postings is a GUESS from this one compound, since no posting read had a separate small garden for the private rooms.
  • A garden's water is a pond or a dry garden - a knob.
    Both forms are attested beside samurai rooms: the pond garden, built around real water, and the dry landscape, which shows water without using any, one pond garden and six dry ones among the samurai-house gardens of Chiran. So each map takes one of the two, rolled from its seed, and both are accurate. Each map's note says which it draws.
  • How likely is a small posting to have a pond rather than a dry garden?
    The weight between the two forms is a GUESS. A county post keeping a pond is accurate as a form, but both offices read that had one were far grander than a county post: an office of the shogunate's intendants and the seat of a domain. So the pond is the grander of the two forms for a small posting.
  • The pond is drawn small, within sight of the rooms that face the garden.
    Its form and size are a GUESS: no page we read gives the size of a residence garden's pond.
  • Every kind of stone lantern is drawn as one small glyph.
    The lantern came in many kinds, the Kasuga, the snow-viewing (yukimi), the Enshū and the Oribe among them; drawing them all alike is a map drawing convention. How many lanterns a garden holds, and where, are a GUESS.
  • A lantern drawn in a court where guests are received is a GUESS, and stands singly.
    No page we read describes a lantern in such a court. The lantern drawn there is borrowed from the older shrine and temple use, a single lantern at the center of the front, so it stands alone on the line of approach.
