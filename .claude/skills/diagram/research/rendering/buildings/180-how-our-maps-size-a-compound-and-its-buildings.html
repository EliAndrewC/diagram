How our maps size a compound and its buildings

Our maps draw an official's compound - an intendant's office (jin'ya) or a county magistrate's office - as halls, storehouses and service buildings around open courts. They give every building a ceiling on its size, and check the order of the buildings as well. A compound in which every building is plausible on its own can still show an impossible order - a kitchen as large as the house it serves, a stable larger than the barracks, a shrine hall larger than the residence - so the order has a check of its own.

Our maps also keep a compound mostly open, with buildings on about a third of its walled ground.

  • Our maps rank the office hall largest of the halls, then the residence, then the staff's rowhouse.
    That is the order of the one intendant's office measured building by building, at Takayama, so it is historically accurate. The office hall may out-size the residence because it is not a domestic building but the office's working core.
  • Our maps keep the records store and the other small storehouses well below the rowhouse.
    At Takayama the book storehouse and the kitchen storehouse are a small fraction of the rowhouse.
  • The grain storehouse is ranked by how much rice its office holds.
    At a county magistrate's office that ships its rice on, it stays below the residence. At an office that stores a wide territory's rice it may be the largest building in the compound, as Takayama's rice store was. So the maps do not hold every storage building below the residence. Tying the rank to the rice held is a GUESS from the one office measured.
  • Our maps make the residence the largest domestic building.
    No kitchen, bath or shrine hall out-sizes it, and a kitchen stays a fraction of the living quarters. The order among these buildings is this project's reading, a GUESS: no page we read ranks them.
  • Our maps keep the stable smaller than the barracks.
    Its place in the order is a GUESS: no page we read says where a stable ranked among an office's buildings.
  • How much of a compound's walled ground do its buildings cover?
    About 30-42%, the rest courtyard. The bottom is the one office measured: at Takayama the floors of all its buildings come to about 31% of its site, and the ground they cover is no more than that, since the total counts every story and a modern pump house. The top is this project's own estimate from plans, a GUESS: no other office's site and buildings are measured on any page we read.
  • Our maps keep a compound's walls at their real size even where it looks empty.
    Inside that range a compound is not oversized: its walls stay where they are, within the range of sizes our maps allow, from the small Joge branch office up to Takayama, and a sparse compound is never made smaller to fill it.
  • Our maps gather a compound's rooms into a few connected buildings, as a real jin'ya did.
    At Takayama the entrance, the examination room, the office and the great hall are parts of one office. A schematic plan tends to scatter a dozen separate labeled pavilions with loose ground between them; our maps close up that loose ground and leave the rest open.
  • Our maps set two wooden service buildings on a shared edge side by side, or about 6-8 ft apart.
    Not 12-16 ft apart. The figure is this project's, a GUESS: no page we read gives a spacing rule.
  • Our maps keep a plaster storehouse (kura) about 6-10 ft from its neighbors, as a fire gap.
    This figure is also this project's, a GUESS.
  • Our maps keep buildings a little further apart than a real jin'ya did.
    Each building stays distinct and labeled so the map can be read. This is a deliberate deviation, for legibility.
  • The forecourt, the hearing court (oshirasu), the garden and the ground between the courts stay open.
    They are features of the plan, not slack to be filled.
  • Our maps make an open stretch no wider than its use.
    A loading apron for carts and draft animals is taken as about 15-20 ft wide, a GUESS: no page we read gives an apron's width. A stretch wider than its purpose is slack, whatever it is called.
  • Our maps keep each part of a compound as closely built as the whole.
    A sparse quarter can hide inside a compound whose buildings cover 30-42% of the whole.
  • Ochiba's and Hayakawa's compounds cover 38% and 35% of their ground.
    Every building is within its ceiling, and both compounds' walls fall in size between the small branch office at Joge and the intendant's office at Takayama. Both are inside the 30-42% our maps aim for, and both are more closely built than Takayama.
  • Hayakawa's west row of service buildings stands at fire gaps.
    The archive, the clerks' room and the stables stand 9 and 6.7 ft apart.
  • Ochiba's jail cell and barracks are one guard building.
    It stands in the compound's southeast corner beside the granary, with a 16 ft loading apron beside it.
