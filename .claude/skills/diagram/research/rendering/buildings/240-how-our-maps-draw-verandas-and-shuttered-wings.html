How our maps draw verandas and shuttered wings

On our building plans a residence's veranda is drawn as a pale strip along its garden face, 3 to 6 ft wide, and some plans carry it round one or two further faces. A wing whose rooms are no longer lived in may be drawn closed behind its storm shutters in daylight, a GUESS.

  • Which faces carry the veranda is a knob between two forms.
    Both are attested at the scale of a provincial residence: the garden face alone, or, late in the period, a continuous veranda along two or three faces. Each plan takes one. The magistrate's compound our maps generate takes the first, a veranda 4 ft wide along the garden face alone.
  • A veranda round all four faces is not drawn at a provincial residence.
    That ring belongs to the great daimyo mansions.
  • How wide is a drawn veranda?
    3 to 6 ft, half a ken to one ken, chosen along that range and free to change from one face to the next.
  • Where a plan draws the garden face wider than the others, that is this project's reading.
    At Katsura the one-ken veranda on the east of the Ko-shoin's main rooms narrows to half a ken where it turns the corner, and no page we read gives a general width for the outer veranda. The garden face at the wider end is our reading of those widths, not a stated rule.
  • A plan shows a house by day, with its storm shutters stowed.
    By day the shutters stood in their boxes, closing the house only at night. So a veranda is drawn open.
  • A wing shut up behind its shutters in daylight is a GUESS.
    The shutters are real, but no page we read says a household kept the shutters of rooms it was not using shut by day. A plan draws such a wing, darker with the shutters across it, where its story has rooms no longer lived in, and whose rooms they were is the map's own story.
