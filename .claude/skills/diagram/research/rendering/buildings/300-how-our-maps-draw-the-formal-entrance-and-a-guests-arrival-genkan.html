How our maps draw the formal entrance and a guest's arrival (genkan)

A building plan stages a guest's arrival as the record shows it: the gate, then open ground a palanquin can cross, then the step of the formal entrance. A guest's door opens onto that ground, never straight from the street into a room or against the side of a building. The household's own kitchen door is drawn the other way round, into the work space.

  • A plan's formal entrance takes one of two forms the record shows, and a knob chooses between them.
    In the first, the guest comes to a genkan with a shikidai, the form of a senior retainer's house. In the second there is no genkan with a shikidai: the guest's way runs through a middle gate in a wall and along a walled garden path to the veranda of the zashiki, and the household's everyday door is the kitchen entrance. That form is attested at a middle-rank house only.
  • Where a government office, such as an intendant's jin'ya, shares a compound with its official's residence, the genkan is drawn on the office.
    The way to the residence runs on through the office, as the visitor's route does at the Takayama jin'ya. Where the residence stands alone, the genkan is drawn on the residence.
  • A guest's door opens onto open ground, never onto the street or against a building's side.
    A guest of rank stepped from the palanquin straight onto the genkan's step, and the entrance faced open ground inside the gate, so the ground before a guest's door is ground a palanquin can cross. This is historically accurate.
  • The ground before the entrance is drawn as an open court, and a garden in its place is a guess.
    No page we read puts a garden between the gate and a genkan, so a plan that draws a guest garden in place of the court draws a GUESS.
  • On the Hayakawa plan, the guests' river door opens into the guest garden.
    It opens there and not into the narrow strip beside the guest house's side. A garden there, in place of an open court, is a GUESS: no page we read puts a garden between the gate and a genkan.
  • A pond on the way from the gate to the entrance, or a forecourt fenced off from the garden, is a guess wherever a plan draws one.
    No page we read describes either.
  • The kitchen door is drawn opening into work space.
    The kitchen door, the katteguchi, is the doorway leading from outside into the kitchen. That it opens into the work space, the reverse of a guest's door, is this project's reading.
