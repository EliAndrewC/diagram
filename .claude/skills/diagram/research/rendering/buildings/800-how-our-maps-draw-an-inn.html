How our maps draw an inn

An inn is drawn in the form of the settlement's houses (How our maps choose between the courtyard house and the Japanese house): a townhouse with rooms to let where the houses are Japanese, a carters' yard where they are Chinese.

The rule the map follows:

  • On the Japanese roll an inn is a townhouse on the street.
    It has an earth-floored passage to the back and its guest rooms upstairs and behind, and is two stories on the road front.
  • A Japanese inn is 18 to 54 ft wide and up to about 140 ft deep.
    The largest is the size of the Ohashiya at Akasaka, about 54 by 138 ft; the frontages of smaller inns are at How many inns did a post town keep, and how big was one?
  • A Japanese inn keeps its bath and privy at the back, and has no yard or stable of its own.
    An inn had a bath and a privy, but where they stood, and the want of a yard or a stable, are a GUESS.
  • On the Chinese roll an inn is a carters' fenced yard on the road.
    Its lodging houses stand along the sides of the yard, with a large stable and hay stacked in its corners. The stable and the hay rest on one late account alone, which the maps take as it stands.
  • A Chinese inn's size is a GUESS.
    No page we read gives one.
