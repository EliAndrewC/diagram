How our maps draw an inn

An inn is drawn in the form of the settlement's houses (How our maps choose between the courtyard house and the Japanese house): a townhouse with rooms to let where the houses are Japanese, a carters' yard where they are Chinese.

The rule the map follows:

  • Where a settlement's houses are Japanese, our maps draw an inn as a townhouse on the street.
    It has a passage with an earth floor to the back and its guest rooms upstairs and behind, and is two stories on the road front.
  • Our maps draw a Japanese inn 18 to 54 ft wide and up to about 137 ft deep.
    The largest is the size of the Ohashiya, an inn at Akasaka, a post town on the Tokaido, about 54 by 137 ft; the frontages of smaller inns are at Inns and cheap lodging houses: how many, and where the caravan inn stands (hatagoya, kichin-yado, dian)
  • Our maps put a Japanese inn's bath and privy at the back, and give it no yard or stable.
    An inn had a bath and a privy, but where they stood, and the want of a yard or a stable, are a GUESS.
  • Where a settlement's houses are Chinese, our maps draw an inn as a carters' fenced yard on the road.
    Its lodging houses stand inside the yard, with a large stable and hay stacked in its corners. The stable and the hay rest on one account alone, a self-published one of the cart inns of Northeast China, so they are a GUESS. Our maps take only the parts of the yard from it, never a size.
  • Our maps fix no size for a Chinese inn.
    No page we read gives one.
