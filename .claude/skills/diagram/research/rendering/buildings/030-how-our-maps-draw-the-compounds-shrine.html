How our maps draw the compound's shrine

Every compound our maps draw keeps a modest shrine inside its walls, Inari's by default. A shrine in every office follows the record; which god it serves by default is a guess, and so is any garden planted around it.

  • Every compound has a modest shrine, Inari by default.
    A shrine is accurate for a magistrate's office down to the smallest: the branch post at Joge, with three officials, had shrines inside its walls, an Inari shrine among them. A Chinese county yamen kept shrines inside its walls too. Making it an Inari shrine by default is a GUESS resting on that one post.
  • The rule is that a compound has a shrine, not how many.
    Real offices kept more than one - the Joge post an Inari shrine, a Tenjin shrine and others, a Chinese county yamen a shrine to the god of the land and one to the god of the office - but what holds across them is that every office had a shrine, so a plan draws one modest shrine however many a real office of its size kept.
  • Ochiba's shrine hall has two altars.
    Ochiba's magistrate is also a priest, so its hall serves two kami, an altar to each. How a hall serving several kami sets out its altars is at When one shrine hall serves several kami, does each have its own altar?
  • A shrine in a corner of the compound's ground, with a tree beside it, follows the record.
    A household's god was kept in a corner of its lot, in a small shrine or at an old tree or a stone. The tree is the older seat of the god.
  • A garden laid out around the shrine is a GUESS.
    A plan may plant ornamental ground around its shrine, but no page we read describes a garden around a household's shrine.
