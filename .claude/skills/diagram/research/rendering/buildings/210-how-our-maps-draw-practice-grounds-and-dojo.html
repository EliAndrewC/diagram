How our maps draw practice grounds and dojo

On our maps, formal martial training belongs to the provincial city and above. A county magistracy draws an open practice ground marked by the gear that stands on it, and no hall. A provincial city draws one state martial hall and a small rolled number of private dojo, both in or against its samurai neighborhood. The halls take the form of the domain hall that survives at Hagi, the castle town of the Choshu domain.

  • Every county magistracy draws a practice ground beside its guards' quarters.
    The magistracy's samurai on duty keep daily keiko on open ground in the compound: a swept patch with a weapon rack and striking posts. A small domain's seat, the nearest recorded case, laid out a drill ground and names no hall among its buildings.
  • Why does a county town draw no hall?
    The setting's own numbers give a county town about 15 samurai, which is no body of pupils and no living for a teacher, while a provincial city holds a few hundred and a capital about 800, and it is in the cities that the setting's own dojo stand: the "many dojos" of Toshi Ranbo, the Empire's third-largest city, and the pilgrim's vow to train at a dojo in every clan.
  • A county town's samurai are pictured as schooled in the city in youth.
    That a county samurai took their formal schooling at a city school, and keeps the edge at the posting with daily keiko, is this project's picture, a GUESS.
  • How big is a practice ground?
    About 90-135 sq ft for each samurai drilling on it: a solo kata needs a square of about 8-10 ft, and paired work roughly doubles it. The figure is a GUESS; no page we read gives a floor area for each person training.
  • The ground is marked by its gear, not by a building.
    Marking the ground and its gear rather than labeling a hall is a map drawing convention for practice that left equipment rather than architecture; no page we read describes rural practice or its gear.
  • Striking posts are drawn as upright posts only.
    The upright post is Satsuma practice from the early Edo period; no page places the knee-high bundle of branches before 1868, so the maps do not draw it.
  • A striking post is drawn as a small marker of where it stands.
    That is a map drawing convention: a real post stood a little over 4 ft above the ground. Carrying the post to a practice ground outside Satsuma is a GUESS, and so is the number drawn.
  • How is the weapon rack at a practice ground's edge drawn?
    About 8 by 2 ft, a GUESS joined from two real things: the guardroom's rack for swords laid level on forked supports, and the long arrest weapons kept at a guard post, which a painting shows standing in the open at a theater's entrance. Standing weapons upright in the open at a post is itself a GUESS from that painting, and no page describes a rack for practice weapons or its size. At city scale the rack is drawn as a marker too.
  • A magistracy draws a dojo only as a special feature of that place.
    It says something about who lives there - a noted swordsman, a retired teacher - and reads as a local distinction, the kind that draws young samurai from neighboring counties. It is a knob of the plan, not part of the default program.

A provincial city is the first size of settlement that trains indoors.

  • A provincial city draws exactly one state martial hall.
    A state institution is a program item, as the domain school was one to a domain, not a count that grows with the city. The hall is its own compound, standing near the government it answers to but not inside the governor's compound.
  • The state hall's compound is drawn as its wall and three features inside - the hall, the master's house and an archery lane - the rest implied.
    It is drawn the way the governor's compound is. No page we read describes a changing room or an armory in a martial hall.
  • Private dojo are rolled at one for every full 200 resident samurai.
    A remainder gives a chance of one more, and there is always at least one, which puts a city of about 3,000 at one or two. Private halls did reach provincial towns late in the Edo period, but how many a provincial city held no page we read says, so the ratio is this project's calibration, not a historical figure.
  • How many private halls does the one-per-200 ratio give at each size of settlement?
    Roughly: a county town 0.1, which rounds to none, a provincial city about 1.5, a domain capital about 7 and the Imperial capital about 200, of which three or four would be famous. These are averages; where they disagree with the number the map draws, the map wins.
  • The one-per-200 ratio counts samurai, not the whole population.
    The map draws its halls as serving samurai. That is a map decision: some domain schools were later opened to other subjects, and townsmen and farmers trained at private halls. The samurai share of each size of settlement already carries the difference in density between them.
  • The one-per-200 ratio does not count the countryside's samurai.
    A province holds about 550 samurai in all - roughly 300 in its city, 120 across its six county towns and 90 on country estates - and only the city's are enough to support a hall. Rural samurai ride in, or make do with the magistracy's practice ground: a GUESS no page we read speaks to. Counting the countryside separately would count it twice, because a provincial city's size already scales with the countryside that feeds it.
  • State halls are drawn violet and private dojo buff, a map drawing convention.
    No page we read shows a domain school built any differently from a private hall; the color carries a difference of ownership the map could not otherwise show.

From above, the halls take the forms the record found.

  • A state martial hall is drawn about 124 by 35 ft.
    It is a long single-story building with a tiled hip-and-gable roof, split across its length into a board-floored fencing hall and an earth-floored spear hall, each with a viewing place along one long side, as the Hagi hall is.
  • A large city's state institution may draw three halls side by side, with an open bout ground before them.
    The Mito domain's school, the Kodokan, kept its fencing, spear and other arts in three halls, with a bout ground in its south garden.
  • The head of every hall is drawn with scrolls, never a shrine.
    The head is the floor from which the master watched, hung with scrolls, and the shrine shelf, absent from Edo-period halls, came into them only from about 1936.
  • How large is a private dojo drawn?
    44 by 24 ft, some 54-63 tatami mats, about the size of the Hagi spear hall. No page we read gives a private hall's size, so the size is a GUESS.

The map's rule for martial halls follows the Japanese form, whose look the maps take. The record of China, the other anchor culture, agrees in putting the institution in the town, not the countryside, but there it is a walled drill ground rather than a hall.

  • On a map drawn in the Chinese style, the state institution is a walled drill ground of about 6 acres.
    It lies outside a gate, with a drill hall of three bays at its north, a platform before it and a gate in the south wall, as the gazetteer of the county of Chongming records its ground. The map takes the ground's size in mu, not the page's other figure of one square li, and draws the three-bay hall rather than the undated nine-bay hall that stood there later.
  • How wide is the Chinese drill hall drawn?
    About 32 ft, three bays of about 3.2 m (~10 ft) each, the bay of the later nine-bay hall at Chongming; the width and the depth of a three-bay hall are a GUESS.
