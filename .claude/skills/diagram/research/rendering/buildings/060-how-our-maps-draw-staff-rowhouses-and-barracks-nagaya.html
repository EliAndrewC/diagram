How our maps draw staff rowhouses and barracks (nagaya)

An official's compound on our maps may lodge its staff on its own grounds, in a barracks drawn as a rowhouse: a long, narrow building no more than about 24 ft deep, divided into dwellings of a few men each. A compound's servants live apart from its staff, in servants' quarters of their own.

  • Our maps may lodge an office's staff on its grounds or in a district of their own, as offices of the time did.
    A rural intendant's office kept the huts and rowhouses of its junior officials on its grounds, and Edo's great town magistracy housed its constables in a district of their own.
  • Where does a county magistrate's office on our maps lodge its staff?
    On its grounds, by default. Its staff of about fifteen men are mostly unmarried, with no families to house. That the whole staff of a rural office lived on the grounds is a reconstruction: the sources name the junior officials' quarters and a storehouse keepers' rowhouse, but not who else lived inside.
  • The barracks is a long, narrow building, never a square or squat block.
    It is drawn 12 to 24 ft deep, built of bays about 12 to 18 ft wide, and up to about 145 ft long. That form and those limits are accurate: every rowhouse measured is long and narrow, from the gate rowhouse of the intendant's office at Omori, about 108 by 13 ft, to the foot soldiers' rowhouse at Shibata, about 143 by 24 ft, and a foot soldier's dwelling at Obata is 2 ken (~12 ft) wide.
  • The barracks is divided into dwellings, each an earth-floored entry and a raised floor.
    That is the warrior's rowhouse dwelling the historian Mitamura Engyo (mitamura-nagaya-tcpip) describes.
  • How many men share a dwelling?
    A few, which is a GUESS from one case: in 1860, three retainers on duty at their domain's Edo residence shared one.
  • How long is the barracks drawn?
    As long as the bays its staff of about fifteen men needs, and that number of bays is a GUESS: apart from the one case of three retainers sharing a dwelling, no page we read says how many unmarried men usually shared one.

The servants' quarters is a building of its own, and how it is entered follows how its servants lived: together in one room, or in dwellings of their own.

  • A compound's servants' quarters takes one of two historical forms, a knob set per compound.
    Either a common room entered by one door, like the large rooms where the chugen, servants below the foot soldiers, lived together in their lord's residence, or a row of dwellings of one or two bays each, every dwelling with a door of its own, as in the warriors' and servants' rowhouses. Both forms are accurate. The share of compounds that takes each is a GUESS.
  • How many bays does a common room run to?
    A GUESS: the one room described is two rooms of six tatami mats, each about 9 by 12 ft.
  • Are a common room's bays open to one another, or divided by sliding partitions?
    Our maps draw either. The sliding partitions are a GUESS: the room described names its two six-mat rooms, not what stood between them.
  • A row of bays walled off from one another behind one shared door is never drawn.
    No page we read shows one.
  • Ubame's servants' quarters, four bays behind one door, is the common-room form.
    It is accurate as drawn, so long as its bay divisions read as sliding partitions within one room and not as walls between dwellings. Drawn in the other form, it takes a door to each dwelling.
  • Nothing in the barracks is divided, labeled or drawn as a bunk room or a bunk.
    No page we read puts a bed or a bunk in a rowhouse before modern times.
