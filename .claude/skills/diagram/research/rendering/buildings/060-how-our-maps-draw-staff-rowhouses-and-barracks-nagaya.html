How our maps draw staff rowhouses and barracks (nagaya)

An official's compound on our maps houses its staff in a barracks drawn as a rowhouse: a long, narrow range divided into dwellings, with a few men on duty to each.

  • Staff may live on the grounds or in a district apart, and both are historical.
    A rural intendant's office kept the huts and rowhouses of its junior officials on its grounds, and Edo's great town magistracy housed its constables in a district of their own. A county magistrate's office on our maps lodges its staff on the grounds by default: the setting's working platoon is mostly men without dependents. That the whole staff of a rural office lived on the grounds is a reconstruction: the sources name the junior officials' quarters and a storehouse keepers' rowhouse, but not who else lived inside.
  • The barracks is a long, narrow range, never a square or squat block.
    It is drawn 12 to 24 ft deep, built of bays about 12 to 18 ft wide, and up to about 145 ft long. That form and those limits are accurate: every rowhouse measured is long and narrow, from the Omori gate range at about 108 by 13 ft to the Shibata foot soldiers' row at about 143 by 24 ft, and the Obata dwelling is 2 ken (~12 ft) wide.
  • How long is the barracks drawn?
    As long as the bays a platoon of about fifteen needs, and that number of bays is a GUESS: no page we read says how many unmarried men shared one dwelling.
  • The barracks is divided into dwellings, each an earth-floored entry and a raised floor, with a few men on duty to a dwelling.
    That is the warrior's rowhouse dwelling the historian Mitamura Engyo (mitamura-nagaya-tcpip) describes, and in 1860 three retainers on duty in Edo shared one.
  • The servants of the lowest rank may instead share one large room.
    The chugen lived communally in large rooms in their lord's residence.
  • Nothing in the barracks is divided, labeled or drawn as a bunk room or a bunk.
    No page we read puts a bed or a bunk in a rowhouse before modern times.
