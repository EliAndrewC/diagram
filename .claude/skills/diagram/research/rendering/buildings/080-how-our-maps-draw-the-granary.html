How our maps draw the granary and the tax rice (gokura)

Our maps give a county magistrate's compound a granary, a vented storehouse where the tax paid in grain waits on its way out: rice in straw bales above all, with a share of other grain beside it. It is a single storehouse of about 45 by 25 ft, the next store after the villages' own storehouses for the tax rice (gokura), which held it before it reached the office. Its form, where a county's rice waits, and where a provincial city keeps its own are each a knob, rolled per settlement from its seed, because the record shows more than one form of each.

  • A granary takes one of two forms, a knob.
    Either a timber storehouse raised on posts, the takakura, or an earth-walled kura set with ventilation gaps on the model of the Takayama intendancy's rice store; each map takes one. Where the posts are drawn, the reason is rats and damp, which holds away from a river as well as beside one.
  • Our maps draw the earth-walled kura with no posts.
    No page we read says how an earth-walled grain storehouse's floor was raised, so posts under one would be a guess, and the maps do not draw them.
  • The granary is labeled "granary", never "rice granary".
    A wet-rice county's granary is rice-dominant, since the paddy tax was rice received into the lord's storehouses, with a visible share of other grain beside it.
  • More of the tax arrives in kind than it did in Edo Japan: a deliberate deviation.
    Where the Edo dry-field tax was often paid in coin, this setting is rich in goods and poor in coin, so the dry-field share of a county's tax arrives in soybean and barley bales beside the rice more often than its Japanese model.
  • What does an upland county's granary hold?
    Goods as much as grain. An upland county's tax arrives as goods, as some of the shogunate's villages in Ina county paid in split timber, so its granary is part warehouse of goods - this project's reading.
  • The famine reserve is a corner of the granary, not a granary of its own: a deliberate simplification.
    In the Edo period the shogunate, the domains, and the towns and villages kept emergency reserves of rice and other grain in community and charity granaries, and our maps draw one granary instead of two, with a corner of unhulled rice kept against famine. That unhulled rice was kept because it stores longer than hulled is this project's reading; no page we read ranks the two.
  • What is the rice in an office's granary for?
    Some waits to be shipped and some is held as a local reserve. This is this project's reading, a reconstruction: the pages we read make the villages' own storehouses a temporary store for shipment and, in places, a reserve, and say nothing of the rice passing through an office. The maps in their hand-drawn style caption the granary as a staging store for tax grain.
  • How big is a county office's granary on our maps?
    One storehouse of about 43 to 50 by 25 to 27 ft, some 1,100 to 1,350 sq ft - a GUESS, since no page we read gives the size of a county office's grain storehouse or how many it kept. It is set above the 440 to 740 sq ft of the storehouses at a three-village store site and at about a tenth of the 11,222 sq ft of the Takayama intendancy's rice store.
  • Where a county's tax rice waits is a knob.
    In the office's own grain storehouse, or in a row of storehouses at the town's river landing, beside the loading place. The office's storehouse is attested; the landing row is this project's reading of the rice being carried to a landing to be shipped.
  • Which counties keep their rice at the landing, and which at the office?
    A county on navigable water leans to the landing, and its rice moves through; an isolated one leans to the office's storehouse and keeps it, so a county's map shows which kind of county it is. The lean is this project's reading.
  • How many storehouses stand in a landing's row, and how big is each?
    Several, each about the 440 to 740 sq ft of a storehouse at a three-village store site; their number is a GUESS.
  • How does a county's rice travel downriver?
    On boats hired for the run; the office owns none. The shogunate's tax rice went by sea on ships it hired directly, under an official pennant, inspected at the ports of call; carrying that to a county's river is this project's own. The barge at the dock is one taken on for the run.
  • How does the office keep hold of rice it does not carry?
    By counts and records - this project's reading - drawn as the tally office at Hayakawa's river landing.
  • Where a provincial city keeps its tax rice is a knob.
    Both forms are attested: a rice storehouse inside the castle's outer ward, or, as at a great domain's river port, a storehouse ground with its own quay and the domain's office beside it. Our maps put that ground at the city's river landing, with a small office.
  • How many storehouses does a provincial city's store hold?
    Up to about nine, the count of a great domain's port store, the top of the range for a provincial city. The count for a smaller city, and every storehouse's size, are a GUESS.
