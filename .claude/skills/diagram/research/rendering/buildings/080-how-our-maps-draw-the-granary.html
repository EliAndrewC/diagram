How our maps draw the granary and the tax rice (gokura)

Our maps give a county magistrate's compound a granary, a vented storehouse where the tax paid in grain waits on its way out: rice in straw bales above all, with a share of other grain beside it. It stands inside the compound, drawn about 43 to 50 by 25 to 27 ft. Its form, where a county's rice waits, and where a provincial city keeps its own are each a knob, rolled per settlement from its seed, because the record shows more than one form of each.

  • A granary takes one of two forms, a knob.
    Either a timber storehouse raised on posts, the takakura, or an earth-walled kura set with ventilation gaps on the model of the Takayama intendancy's rice store; each map takes one. Where the posts are drawn, the reason is rats and damp, which holds away from a river as well as beside one. Posts under an earth-walled kura would be a GUESS, since no page we read says how its floor was raised.
  • Where a county's tax rice waits is a knob.
    In the office's own grain storehouse, or in a row of storehouses at the town's river landing, beside the loading place. The office's storehouse is attested; the landing row is this project's reading of the rice being carried to a landing to be shipped. A county on navigable water leans to the landing and its rice moves through; an isolated one leans to the office's storehouse and keeps it - so which a county is can be read from its map. That lean is this project's reading.
  • An office's granary holds rice in transit beside a local reserve.
    This project's reading, a reconstruction: the pages we read make the village store a temporary store for shipment and, in places, a reserve, and say nothing of the rice passing through an office. The hand-drawn sheets caption the granary as a staging store for tax grain.
  • How big is a county office's granary on our maps?
    One storehouse of about 43 to 50 by 25 to 27 ft, some 1,100 to 1,350 sq ft - a GUESS, since no page we read gives the size of a county office's grain storehouse or how many it kept. It is set above a three-village storehouse's 440 to 750 sq ft and at about a tenth of the 11,222 sq ft of the store that served a whole province.
  • A landing's storehouse row is several storehouses of a village store's size.
    Each about the size of the three-village site's; their number is a GUESS.
  • The rice goes downriver on hired boats, and the office owns none.
    The shogunate's tax rice went on ships it hired directly, under an official pennant, inspected at the ports of call; carrying that to a county's river is this project's own. So the office's hold on the rice is a matter of counts and records - this project's reading - drawn as the tally office at Hayakawa's river landing, and the barge at its dock is one taken on for the run.
  • Where a provincial city keeps its tax rice is a knob.
    Both forms are attested: a rice storehouse inside the castle's outer ward, or a storehouse ground at the city's river landing with its own quay and a small office beside it. A great domain's port store ran to about nine storehouse buildings, the top of the range for a provincial city; the count for a smaller city, and every storehouse's size, are a GUESS.

In the setting, most of the Emperor's share of the taxes does not travel to the capital: it is stored in Imperial granaries in each domain's capital and spent there. Those are at The Emperor's granaries are separate.

  • The granary is labeled "granary", never "rice granary".
    A wet-rice county's granary is rice-dominant, since the paddy tax was rice received into the lord's storehouses, with a visible share of other grain beside it.
  • More of the tax arrives in kind than it did in Edo Japan: a deliberate deviation.
    Where the Edo dry-field tax was often paid in coin, this setting is rich in goods and poor in coin, so the dry-field share of a county's tax arrives in soybean and barley bales beside the rice more often than its Japanese model. An upland county's tax arrives as goods, as the upland Ina villages paid theirs in split timber, so its granary is part warehouse of goods, part strongbox - this project's reading.
  • The famine reserve is a corner of the granary, not a granary of its own: a deliberate simplification.
    Edo kept its emergency reserve of rice and other grain in community and charity granaries, and our maps draw one granary instead of two, with a corner of unhulled rice kept against famine. That unhulled rice was kept because it stores longer than hulled is this project's reading; no page we read ranks the two.
