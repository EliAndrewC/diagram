How our maps place the vegetable garden in the sun

Every residence and walled compound on our building plans has a vegetable garden. Its size and its seat are both knobs, each value one the record attests, and the sun takes out any seat where the garden's bed would lie in shade. The shadows are counted by the same rule as a farmhouse's kitchen bed, at How our maps keep yards and gardens in the sun, and where the rest of the house's service stands is at How our maps lay out samurai residences and their rooms (buke yashiki).

  • A garden's size is a knob between two attested forms.
    At the low end it is a soup-greens plot of about 100 m² (~1,076 sq ft), like the Takei house's; at the high end it is a field over about half the grounds, like the Yokota house's. Each settlement rolls its own, and each hand-drawn sheet takes its own.
  • A garden's seat is a knob among four attested ones.
    West of the residence, as at the Takei house; south, beside the formal garden, as the Higuchi house's field lay south of its pond; in the rear service ground, as a Chinese county office listed its plot behind the residence gate; or on a parcel of its own, as a daikan's office kept its field. No page we read seats a Japanese residence's vegetable garden north of the house, so the rear seat rests on the Chinese list.
  • The sun takes out a seat where a full-sun bed gets under six hours of direct sun in autumn.
    Among the seats left, a scripted plan rolls one and a hand-drawn plan takes one. That a household chose among its seats by the sun is a GUESS: no page we read gives a household's reason for putting its plot where it did.
  • On the three hand-drawn magistracy plans, the bed stands south, in the inner court's garden.
    At Ochiba, Hayakawa and Ubame the west and the rear lie in the shade of the kitchen and the residence, so the only seat left inside the walls is the south. Each bed takes sunny ground in the inner court's garden, south of the house, as the Higuchi house's field lay south of its pond.
  • At Ubame, the bed is a soup-greens plot.
    The inner court there has room for a bed only at the size knob's low end.
  • A plot beside the formal garden is drawn unscreened, as its own ground in rows.
    It is set beyond the ornamental ground where the court allows. Both are a GUESS: no page we read names a fence or hedge between the two, or says which side of the formal garden the plot lay on.
  • No bed is fenced on its own.
    This is a GUESS: the walls against boar and deer that were found ring a village's farmland, not a single plot.
  • At a country shrine, the resident keeper's plot stands near the hall, on open ground that gets its sun.
    The keeper lives in the hall. The plot's place and its size are a GUESS: no page we read says where a keeper living in a precinct grew their vegetables.
