How our maps draw the main gate and gatehouse

A building plan draws its compound's main gate in one of the forms the record shows, and its gatekeepers in one of the two places they sat. Where two forms are both attested, each plan takes one, a knob rolled per plan. A Japanese post's gate is drawn from about 6 to 12 ft wide; a yamen's is a roofed building of three bays, drawn 18 to 24 ft wide.

  • A Japanese post's main gate is a gate of one bay or a nagaya-mon, a knob.
    A one-bay gate is drawn about 6 to 8.5 ft wide, the frontage registered examples measure, and a nagaya-mon's opening about 12 ft, the two ken a registered one opens in.
  • A samurai residence's gate on the town and city maps is drawn within that span.
    Its 9 to 12 ft, set at How our maps draw compound walls (neribei and tsuijibei), falls between the one-bay gate and the nagaya-mon.
  • A yamen's main gate is drawn as a roofed building of three bays.
    That is its form in the record: a building with a roof, not an opening in the wall, three bays wide by law.
  • How wide is a yamen's gate drawn?
    18 to 24 ft. The width is a GUESS: no page we read gives such a gate's width.
  • The gatekeepers sit in a gatehouse beside the opening, or in a room of the gate range, a knob.
    Both are attested: the gatehouse is the Takayama jin'ya's form, the guardroom inside the gate range the Kaibara jin'ya's.
  • Both are drawn about 2 ken (~12 ft) deep.
    That depth is measured for both forms: the Kaibara gate range and the Kita-in guardroom.
  • How long is a gatehouse of its own drawn?
    About 40 ft, a GUESS. The one freestanding guardroom measured is about 18 ft long, and no page we read gives the size of the Takayama gatehouse; a building of 40 ft or more beside a gate is the scale of a gate range.
