How our maps choose between the courtyard house and the Japanese house

A Japanese townhouse and a Chinese courtyard house are not one house in two styles: the townhouse is one building on the street with its rooms in a row along a passage, and even a rich townsman's dwelling behind the shop was joined to it by a veranda and a garden rather than closing a court; the courtyard house closes a yard with buildings on all four sides. So the maps do not choose house by house, by rank or class: a settlement follows one tradition or the other.

Inside an administrative compound the buildings are always Japanese (How our maps draw a magistrate's compound).

The rule the map follows:

  • Each settlement rolls, from its seed, whether its houses follow the Japanese forms or the Chinese - a knob.
    The same roll governs the town's shops (the frontage knob at How wide is a merchant's shophouse, and how does wealth change it?) and its inns (How our maps draw an inn).
  • On the Japanese roll a townsman's house is one building on the street.
    Its rooms stand in a row along a passage, as How our maps draw a merchant's townhouse draws them.
  • On the Chinese roll a house is a court closed by buildings on all four sides.
    An ordinary household has one court, and a large or rich one two or three in a row, never deeper than about 250 ft, the 77 m (~253 ft) of the deepest Beijing courtyard house.
  • Which households get more than one court is a GUESS.
    No page we read says which households of a Chinese town lived in a one-court house and which in more.
  • On the Chinese roll a shop is the two-story street building of a courtyard house of one court or more.
    That is the shop of the old county seat of Qixian.
