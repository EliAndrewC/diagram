How our maps count and draw households (ie)

A map's stated population is its dwellings times five, the setting's household, and never its buildings. A hamlet or village draws one occupied farmhouse for each household it declares, and each settlement rolls from its seed whether its families keep their old couple under one roof or in a retirement house in the same yard - both forms are attested.

  • Shops, offices and storehouses are never counted as homes.
    A town or a city draws a great deal that houses nobody: shops, the ministries and the governor's offices, flophouses, storehouses, the furniture inside a gate. Count those and the place looks half again as populous as it is.
  • Ten kinds of building on our maps are homes.
    A laborer's dwelling and the larger house of a master laborer, a servant household, a burakumin household, a samurai house and a senior samurai's larger one, a merchant's small house, the merchant's house-over-shop and a rich merchant's large house, and a monk's household by a temple precinct. The merchant's house-over-shop is the one building that is both: it counts as a home and as a shop.
  • A monk's household counts as a home but is left out of the card's count by caste.
    A map's card gives its population caste by caste. The monks' households are real resident families, so they count among the dwellings, but clergy are not a lay caste.
  • A town counts its drawn farmhouses among its dwellings.
    Those farmhouses are part of its own farming households.
  • A city counts no farmhouse at all.
    A provincial city's figure is its urban castes. The farmers who feed it live in the villages and hamlets of the surrounding county and are counted there. So a city of about 3,000 places about 600 dwellings.

At hamlet and village scale, a declared household is expected to have a roof on the sheet.

  • A hamlet or village map places close to one occupied farmhouse for each household it declares.
    The placer is held to between 0.85 and 1.05 occupied farmhouses for every household declared, and aims at one apiece. A hamlet that declares fifteen and places twelve is a failure to be fixed, not a map to be published.
  • Sixteen of the seventeen maps published with this project draw exactly one house per household.
    The seventeenth draws 0.94.
  • When the placer cannot fit enough farmhouses, it searches wider ground rather than starting over.
    It keeps widening the ground it draws candidate sites from instead of re-rolling the map, so the houses already placed stay where they are.
  • The setting puts a floor of fifty houses under a village of about seventy households.
    That figure is a floor on what a map may show, not a ratio: it does not say that three households in ten share a roof with another. Our band is tighter, so a map of seventy households draws at least sixty.
  • Abandoned houses are not counted toward the households.
    They are former homes.
  • Where drawn houses and declared households differ, the map's card states both.
    One sheet drawing 66 farmhouses for 70 households names both, because "66 farmhouses, population ~350" invites the reader to divide and get 5.3 to a house.
  • Every household on a hamlet or village map is a farm family with its own farmhouse, as in the later Edo period.
    Early in the Edo period a registered house plot could hold a master's family with dependent families in huts around it, counted as one hearth; those dependents later split off into households of their own. Our villages are drawn as the settled villages that came after, the villages of the later registers, and no plot is drawn as a master's yard full of dependents' huts.

Two roofs can house one household.

  • Each settlement rolls one of two family forms from its seed.
    The generations share one roof, or a farmhouse has a retirement house in the same yard. Both are attested, so neither is treated as the only right one.
  • A retirement house is not a household.
    It is a second roof of its farmhouse's family, so it is neither counted toward the households nor against the band of occupied farmhouses.
  • The share of homesteads with a retirement house, and the weights of the two family forms, are our own guess.
    No page we read gives a share of homesteads that had one, so both the share where the custom is kept and the weights of the roll are a GUESS.
