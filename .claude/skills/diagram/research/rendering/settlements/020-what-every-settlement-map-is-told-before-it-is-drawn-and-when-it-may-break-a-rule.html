What every settlement map is told before it is drawn, and when it may break a rule

Three facts about a settlement cannot be worked out from its own ground, and each decides where a whole family of things goes, so all three are declared with the settlement before anything is drawn, and everything placed later is held to them. A map may break one of the rules that follow from them only for a reason in its own history, written down.

  • Which way the water flows is declared first, at every size.
    Everything downstream follows it: which end of the river takes the wharf, which way the drainage ditches run out, which way a side channel leaves the river so that the current turns into it, and where the nuisance trades stand against the drinking water.
  • The clan that holds the settlement is declared, and its two patron Fortunes take the principal temples.
    The setting gives each clan two patron Fortunes and every city of the clan a temple to each, and one of the two is drawn larger than every other temple on the map.
  • The bearing of the Imperial capital is declared, and the samurai country estates face it.
    On this project's reading, a clan's senior houses want their land on the side of the settlement that faces the capital, on the road their lord and their peers travel, and nothing in the map's own ground says which side that is.

Some settlements are not ordinary, and a rule right for every planned town and city can be wrong for one of them.

  • A map may be excused a rule only in writing, for a reason in its own history.
    The written reason names the history and what it cost, and the excuse lapses when it stops excusing anything. A rule is never quietly relaxed for every map.
  • One walled town is excused from housing its declared population, because its wall was built in haste around a town never planned for one.
    It stood unwalled, changed hands in a war between two Great Clans, and was walled in haste around the town already there, so its wall was never sized to a population. A chrysanthemum field stands inside the wall for symbolic reasons, and it has no burakumin quarter, for logistical ones; both cost it dwellings a planned town of the same size would house. So its housing cannot reach its declared population, and it is excused that arithmetic on that ground and no other.
  • A map that is merely hard to fit is re-planned, most often by growing its wall.
    From outside, a settlement whose history conflicts with a rule and one that is merely hard to fit look alike. Another city could not reach its declared population at first, but its oddity - eight temple precincts scattered through it by trade - takes a known amount of ground that can be allowed for in advance, so it was given a bigger wall, not an excuse.
  • New rules are set against ordinary settlements first.
    Defaults set against unusual settlements bend to fit their exceptions, and the first city and the first walled town are both unusual. Ordinary settlements are built until the rules settle, and the unusual ones then earn their exceptions.
