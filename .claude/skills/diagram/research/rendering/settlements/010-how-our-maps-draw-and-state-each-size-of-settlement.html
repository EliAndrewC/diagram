How our maps draw and state each size of settlement

Each size of settlement is drawn to its own count of households, at its own round scale, and the card beside the map states its size in terms a reader can check against the drawing. Where a place is bigger than its sheet - a town ringed by farms, a city fed by the villages around it - the card says what the figure counts and what is left undrawn.

  • A hamlet draws 10 to 20 households, and no headman's house, shrine or tax-free plot.
    These are the setting's hamlet and its institutions (The five sizes of settlement).
  • A village draws 40 to 100 households, with a headman's house, a shrine and two or three tax-free plots.
    The headman's house is the largest on the sheet.
  • A town draws all 82 of its non-farm households, and as much of its farming households as the sheet holds.
    Its 24 merchant, 29 laborer, 13 servant, 12 burakumin and 4 samurai households are drawn at those counts, with a magistrate's walled manor; of its roughly 156 farming households, only a sample fits.
  • No samurai house stands in a hamlet or a village.
    Both are peasant-only in the setting, and a samurai family's country estate stands outside them.
  • A provincial city draws about 600 households and no farmers.
    They are placed at roughly 40% laborer, 20% servant, 25% merchant, 10% samurai and 5% burakumin, each caste within about 30% of its share.
  • The city's mix is held as firmly as its total.
    A city that meets its population with the wrong castes reads as the wrong kind of place. When households are packed one caste after another, the last caste in the list gets whatever ground is left, which can be nothing, so the servants, who live among the households they serve, are placed among the laborers and merchants rather than after them.
  • A walled samurai estate counts as a household like any other.
    So the estates outside a city's wall and the merchant estates inside it count toward their castes, and the ordinary houses make up only the remainder.
  • A domain capital draws about 12,360 inhabitants' worth of households.
    That is the setting's settled 12,000 or so, with the samurai families who move to the capital.

Each size is drawn at its own scale, so that a sheet fits its place.

  • Each size is drawn at a round number of feet to the pixel.
    One foot per pixel for a hamlet and a town, two for a village, three for a provincial city and a capital, and a third of a foot for a building plan, the finest of the ladder. The numbers are round so that a reader can measure a distance off the sheet in their head. Why the ladder is what it is, and the real building complexes that bracket the building-plan figure, are at The building-plan scale is 3 px = 1 ft.
  • Every size is declared in real feet and converted at the map's own scale.
    So a feature thirty feet across is thirty feet across on every size of map. An audit of the drawn maps found the dwellings already consistent with each other, but town fields four to eight times under their real area, and roads, streets, an inn, a stable yard and a storehouse changing their real size from one size of map to the next, because they were written in pixels.
  • A thin line is drawn at least 4 pixels wide - a map drawing convention.
    A five-foot alley at three feet to the pixel would be 1.7 pixels wide, which cannot be seen. The floor only raises a line to where it can be read: nothing is drawn wider than the floor, and anything truly wider than it is drawn at its true width.

The card beside a map states the settlement's size, and what the figure counts differs by size.

  • A hamlet's or a village's card states the exact number of farmhouses drawn, with no tilde.
    Every household is drawn, so the count is a statement about the sheet, which a reader could sit and count. The population that follows it is five to a household and carries the tilde.
  • A town's or a city's card states the exact number of non-farm dwellings drawn, and no farmhouse count.
    Both are surrounded by fields and farmers that are deliberately not all drawn, so a farmhouse count would describe nothing true. Their populations are explained rather than left bare, because the two figures mean different things.
  • A city's population excludes farmers altogether.
    Its figure is its urban dwellings times five; the farms on its sheet belong to village districts and counties counted separately.
  • A town's card states about 1,200, and says it includes farmers who are not all drawn.
    The setting counts a town's figure in and around it, farmers of its own fields included, so the card gives about 1,200 while the sheet draws every one of the 82 non-farm dwellings and only a sample of the 156 farmhouses.
  • A town's 1,200 is the town's own, not its county's.
    The town's breakdown adds up on its own, one town map draws exactly its 82 non-farm dwellings, and the setting's table lists towns, villages and hamlets as separate lines that sum to the domain. Reading a town's figure as taking in the farmers of its county's villages would count about 172,000 inhabitants twice across the domain.
  • The town's figure describes the place, not the drawing - a disclosed departure.
    Everywhere else the card describes what is drawn; here it describes the place the drawing is a sample of, and says so.
  • A card whose drawn houses and declared households differ names both.
    So a reader who counts the houses on the sheet is never left to wonder why the count and the stated households disagree.
  • A village's card names its county, and never a village district.
    A village and the district it heads share one name, so naming the district would say nothing, and the card names the county every village belongs to instead.
  • A hamlet's card names the district it belongs to, and the direction of the district's village.
    A hamlet belongs to a district named for another place, the village at its head, so one name tells the reader both its district and which village its lanes lead to.
  • The shared name is not flagged on the map.
    It departs from how real places were named, but it is set out in the setting's own core materials, which the players of this campaign already know.
  • The card ranks no kind of settlement as commoner than another.
    No page we read gives a count or share of any form, so the card makes no historical ranking. It states the setting's arithmetic instead: about 1,296 hamlets to 216 villages, and about 40% of a domain's inhabitants in a hamlet.
  • The card says a hamlet farms rice because its map draws rice.
    It claims no dominance for wet rice, which no source we read measures, and does not take the kokudaka for a measure of it.
  • The card states that a hamlet has no headman, and says this is a simplification.
    The rule is the setting's; history had branch hamlets with officials of their own. The card says so in the paragraph that gives its basis, which is what makes the rule a deliberate deviation - one the reader is told about rather than discovers.
  • The card multiplies households by the setting's five, and claims no historical support for it.
    No historical household size could be read.

Three facts about a settlement cannot be worked out from its own ground, and each fixes a whole family of placements, so all three are declared with the settlement before anything is drawn, and every later placement is held to them.

  • Which way the water flows is declared first, at every size.
    Everything downstream follows it: which end of the river takes the wharf, which way the drainage ditches run out, which way a feeder is tapped so that the current turns into it, and where the nuisance trades stand against the drinking water.
  • The clan that holds the settlement is declared, and its two patron Fortunes take the principal temples.
    The setting gives each clan two patron Fortunes and every city of the clan a temple to each, and one of the two is drawn larger than every other temple on the map.
  • The bearing of the Imperial capital is declared, and the samurai country estates face it.
    A clan's senior houses want their land on the side of the settlement that faces the capital, on the road their lord and their peers travel, and nothing in the map's own ground says which side that is.

Some settlements are not ordinary, and a rule right for every planned seat can be wrong for one of them.

  • A map may be excused a rule only in writing, for a reason in its own history.
    The written reason names the history and what it cost, and the excuse lapses when it stops excusing anything. A rule is never quietly relaxed for every map.
  • The worked case is a walled town that was never planned as one.
    It stood unwalled, changed hands in a war between two Great Clans, and was walled in haste around the town already there, so its wall was never sized to a population. A chrysanthemum field stands inside the wall for symbolic reasons, and it has no burakumin quarter, for logistical ones; both cost it dwellings a planned town of the same size would house. So its housing cannot reach its declared population, and it is excused that arithmetic on that ground and no other.
  • A map that is merely hard to fit is re-planned, most often by growing its wall.
    From outside, a settlement whose premise conflicts with a rule and one that is merely hard to tune look alike. Another city could not reach its declared population at first, but its oddity - eight temple precincts scattered through it by trade - was something the space budget could price honestly, so it was given a bigger wall, not an excuse.
  • New rules are set against ordinary settlements first.
    The first city and the first walled town were both drawn early and both are unusual, so for a long time the defaults were bent to fit the exceptions. Ordinary settlements are built until the rules settle, and the unusual ones then earn their exceptions.
