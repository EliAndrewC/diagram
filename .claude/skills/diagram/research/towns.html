The town tier: the research behind the layout, wall and market rules

The findings behind the layout, wall and market of a town on these maps, the decisions they drove, and - where no generator draws the feature yet - the rules a map follows.

Chinese towns were PLANNED - the gate-to-yamen axis

Sources: song-architecture-enwiki (Tang walled wards -> Song shop-lined streets), guanxiang-zdic (the term; commerce outside the gates is a GUESS - no readable page supports it), jokamachi-jawiki (zoning and kinked streets), changan-enwiki (Nara and Kyoto modeled after Chang'an). The gate-to-yamen axis for an ordinary county seat is a GUESS - no readable page supports it

Chinese towns - Rokugan's geographic model - were planned, not organic: a main avenue ran from the principal (usually south) gate straight to the government office (yamen), which sat on that axis facing south, with the grid divided into blocks by cross streets27. Early (Tang) cities walled those blocks into curfew wards1; by the Song the wards opened into shop-lined streets (merchant shophouses fronting the roadbed),2 and commerce spilled outside the gates along the approach19 road4 (the guan-xiang suburb).3 Japan copied the Tang grid for its imperial capitals (Nara/Kyoto)5 but laid out castle towns (jokamachi) as zoned districts - samurai by the castle,6 merchants in trade-blocks,7 temples at the edge8 - with deliberately kinked, defensive streets.9

The rule the map follows: a town reads as blocks fronting streets, never as a scatter of houses. An unwalled town gets that from the road running through it, which is its spine. A walled town has its streets drawn first, before any building is placed: one main avenue about 26 ft wide running inward from the gate to the magistrate's compound, carried on through the gate so that the market outside fronts the same avenue, and one or two market cross-streets about 20 ft wide to carve the interior into blocks. Businesses are then lined along the street two deep, each turned to face it, and the block interiors behind them are filled with housing, each building turned toward the nearest street and any spot that would front no street left empty. The gate-to-compound axis is a guess rather than a finding for an ordinary county seat: the planned axis is attested for capitals, and no readable source was found putting it in a small seat.

Who lives in a town, and in how many houses?

Sources: the setting's own budget notes. This entry rests on the setting's canon rather than on historical work, and is stated as canon

A county seat holds about 1,200 inhabitants in about 238 households, and unlike a hamlet it is not one caste. The campaign notes give a town roughly 24 merchant households, 29 laborer dwellings, 13 servant households, 12 burakumin households and 4 samurai families beside its ~156 farming households. Because those numbers are documented rather than invented, the map places the actual count of each, one building to a household, instead of a token sample of each caste - a town whose merchants are a picturesque half-dozen is a town that has quietly lost two thirds of its trade. There is no peasant headman at this tier: the state is present in person, as the magistrate.

Three map drawing conventions stand between that census and what is actually drawn, and they are the same reasoning as the manor glyph below - the sheet draws a household where it stands, and a household that lives inside someone else's walls is presumed to be there rather than drawn twice. Servants mostly live inside the samurai and merchant compounds they serve, so only the five or so miscellaneous servant households stand alone and the rest are not drawn. Shops are business premises counted separately from the merchant dwellings: they house nobody, so adding one perturbs no population figure. Samurai show as five to ten houses, the roughly fifteen-strong working platoon being barracked inside the manor and not drawn.

Wealth is not flat either. The town wealth tiers put a minority of merchants in the very-rich and rich brackets and a few laborers at master's rank, so a town is never one uniform house size - a row of identical boxes is the tell that the tiers were ignored.

The rule the map follows: a town map draws about 24 merchant houses, 29 laborer dwellings, 5 free-standing servant households, 12 burakumin dwellings, 5 to 10 samurai houses and about 156 farmhouses; shops are extra and count against no household. The farmhouses must remain the largest single group on the sheet, which caps the laborers - small and large together - below them. Four or five of the 24 merchants live in a large house and two or three of the 29 laborers in a master's, the rest in small or standard dwellings; the large footprints do not fit the tight frontage and packing grids, so they are set by hand into cleared gaps after the packed rows are seated, just behind the frontage.

How is a town zoned - what fronts the street, and what sits behind it?

Sources: kyoto-machiya-isan (the shop and the dwelling in one building); the distances are calibrated against the three drawn towns

A merchant family in a shop-house town lives over or directly behind its own premises: the Kyoto townhouse is built on the coexistence of work and dwelling, its street front the place where customers are received and work is done, the living space unfolding toward the back along the passage;21 and the trades that need no frontage live where frontage is cheapest. So the ground between the road and the fields reads in bands: the storefronts on the roadbed, the merchant families' own houses in a tight rank directly behind them, a clear gap, then the laborers' warren, and off at an edge on marginal ground the burakumin quarter. What the reader should never see is the two house types intermixed, which reads as a village that happens to have shops in it rather than as a town whose commerce is its street.

The rule the map follows: on a town built along one road, no merchant home stands within the band the shops themselves occupy, and the nearest laborer dwelling lies at least 35 ft deeper from the road than the deepest merchant house (on the drawn unwalled town the merchant houses sit 106-116 ft from the road and the laborers begin at 156 ft ). A home tucked directly behind a storefront - in the shop's shadow along the road and one building deeper, 15 to 74 ft - lies parallel to that shopfront, so the pair reads as one unit; where the road runs at an angle the storefronts tilt to it and the homes behind them tilt to the same angle, a half-turn counting as parallel. Housing stays off the main commercial frontage, set back at least 92 ft from the main street. The burakumin quarter is packed as its own group at an edge and is no part of the commercial frontage. A walled town is laid around cross-streets rather than one radial axis, so its banding is read street by street rather than by depth from a single spine.

The market-day flophouse - who actually stays over

Sources: kichinyado-jawiki, kichinyado-kotobank (3 mon in 1611, 6 mon in 1658, 24 + 16-24 mon in 1843)13

A county seat exists partly as the periodic-market center for its hinterland: most peasants come from within a day's round-trip, but those at the far edge of the catchment stay over on market eve28 in cheap communal lodging - a kichin-yado ("firewood-fee inn"),10 where you sleep11 on straw29 under a roof for a few mon a night.12

The rule the map follows: every town draws one market-day flophouse unless it declares otherwise, and a busy hub draws more. It is deliberately large, plain and barn-like - no awning, a long row of plain doorways - and so reads as nothing like a shophouse, because its whole value is volume at rock-bottom price: it beds dozens on market eve. It stands where travelers arrive: in the gate market outside a walled town's gate, since the gate shuts at dusk and a peasant arriving late cannot get in at all, or along the road of an unwalled one.

Where does a caravan inn stand, and what does it need beside it?

Sources: dachedian-jilin-daily (the north-Chinese wagon inn: where it stood, its yard, its stable, and its single story), okabe-hatago-jawiki (a surviving Japanese post-station inn, two-story, and what a post-station inn does not have); the trade-route premise rests on general reading and is not cited; the distances are calibrated against the drawn towns

A county seat sits on the trade route that made it worth administering, so it keeps lodging of a second kind, above the flophouse: one prominent caravan inn for the wagon-train merchants, their drivers and their guards, with a large stable beside it and open ground beside that for the oxen and horses. The closest attested analogue is the north-Chinese wagon inn, which stood on the main thoroughfare or in the belt around a city's outskirts and kept exactly that arrangement: open ground in the back yard for parking the carts, a long stable with one very long feeding trough and a tying rail above it, a lean-to beside the stable for the grooms, and a well in the yard.22 It is drawn single-story, because that account puts the buildings in its yard uniformly single-story, of mud brick and reed thatch.25 A two-story highway inn is attested in Japan - a Tokaido post-station inn of about 1836 still stands, two stories, its upper floor the guest rooms26 - but nothing read describes a cart yard, a long stable, a feeding trough or a grooms' lean-to at a lodging house of that kind, so whether it is the better analogue for a wagon inn is an open question and not a settled one. The city tier keeps several of these at its gates; a town keeps one. The failure the rule guards against is an inn drawn correctly and then buried: a caravan pulls up to the road it is already on, so an inn behind two ranks of shops is an inn nobody can reach with a wagon.

The rule the map follows: one inn with its stable within about 150 ft , and open ground beside the stable - no more than four dwellings within about 75 ft of it. The inn fronts the route it serves: within about 115 ft of the roadbed with no building between it and the road, its entrance turned to the nearest point of the road and its long edge running along it, so a diagonal road gets a tilted inn. An unwalled town sites it along the road where the caravans pass; a walled town keeps inn and stable inside the rampart, fronting the main street, because the caravans come in through the gate.

Where does a town's theater stage stand, and which way does it open?

Sources: the tier-scaling finding on how a capital's institutions scale down, where a permanent theater house is capital grammar and a provincial town's playing place stands instead on shrine ground; the distances are calibrated against the drawn towns

The town's playing place is not a building of its own trade: a permanent roofed theater house belongs to the capital, and below that tier the stage belongs to a religious precinct, where the festival crowd already gathers. So what a town draws is a roofed stage facing an open viewing ground within its temple or monastery precinct - the East Asian temple opera stage or shrine kagura stage, never a Greco-Roman seating bowl - with the audience standing on the ground between the stage and the hall. That is also why the stage's orientation is not free: a stage whose open side is turned away from the hall puts its audience outside the precinct, with its back to the thing that owns it.

The rule the map follows: every town draws one theater stage unless it declares otherwise, adjacent to a religious hall - within about 260 ft of the nearest one - with its open viewing side turned toward that hall. A walled town keeps the stage inside the walls unless it declares otherwise.

Does a town keep a fire-watch tower?

Sources: no work of record - the fire argument rests on general reading and is not cited; the walled-only scoping is the 2026-07-24 decision below

Only a walled town stands a fire-watch tower. The reasoning is the wall: a packed wooden core is where a fire is catastrophic, and the rampart that keeps raiders out also traps the blaze inside with the town, so the seat pays for a watch. An open town - a road town strung along the highway, or an unwalled county seat whose houses sit at detached village grain - has field gaps between its blocks that act as natural fire breaks, and gets none. That the gaps do that work is a guess: it rests on general reading, and no source is cited for it.23 The walled-only scoping is the decision of 2026-07-24. The tower itself is a hinomi-yagura: an open braced-timber frame with an alarm bell, and it is the magistrate's watch rather than the neighborhood's.

The rule the map follows: a walled town stands one fire-watch tower among the laborer and merchant blocks - in the dense commoner quarter, not the samurai one - standing clear of the rampart, and distinct from the guard towers that sit on the rampart itself. An open town draws none. The tower is placed last, after the housing and the farmsteads, onto a seam the finished map actually has, so that adding it moves nothing else.

The gate market exists for TRAFFIC, not taxes

Sources: the setting's own budget notes (tariffs at city and capital gates only; the yoriki audit), which are the setting's canon rather than historical work; the traffic drivers from guanxiang-zdic

The why is TRAFFIC, not taxes (the GM's ruling, 2026-07-24): towns levy NO import tariffs - the setting's own budget notes put the entire tariff apparatus (the Yasuki Taka gate collection, the tariff-audit yoriki) at provincial-city and capital gates only, ~2,700 manageable collection points instead of an impossible ~14,400 town gates - and the county magistrate's jurisdiction is the whole COUNTY, so standing outside the gate crosses no tax or regulatory line at all. The honest drivers are the ones the city-tier research validated: through-road travelers (carters, peddlers) buying services without detouring inside20, the market-day chokepoint where the rural catchment trades, and late arrivals who find the gate shut at dusk.

The rule the map follows: a walled town draws a small gate market outside its gate unless it is a real exception - a purely military fort, a depopulated or suppressed gate. It scales with the gate's traffic rather than with the town's population: typically four to eight permanent premises, never fewer than three, strung along the approach road within about 420 ft of the gate and fronting the same avenue as the main street inside. Like the farmland, it is a slice of something larger. At city scale the slice shows by running off the cropped frame; a town canvas is the view and keeps its buildings clear of the frame edge, so a town's market sits whole inside the frame and the approach road running off the edge carries the "more beyond the map" signal by itself. Premises beyond the merchant dwellings are shops, which house nobody, so a larger market never disturbs the population count. The laborers and servants are never pushed outside to make room: that inverts the real pattern, in which the wall is what the commoners are inside of.

A street is access infrastructure for the buildings it serves

Sources: hutong-enwiki

The why is historical: a street is access infrastructure for the buildings it serves, and it is paved or worn into the ground by the foot traffic to and from them - Beijing's hutong alleys14 "emerged as access routes lined by contiguous courtyard residences," and a desire path forms only between real destinations. A planned grid line that never gets built up simply isn't drawn (an undeveloped block).

Note the asymmetry the rule inherits from that: a building with no street is perfectly fine - the poor cannot afford street frontage and sit in the deep cores of the blocks - while a street with no buildings is not. So an empty lane is fixed by demand rather than by deletion first: if there are dwellings the lane could serve, or households the census still owes, the lane is routed to them or they are moved onto it, lower-status dwellings readily lining lesser back lanes; only when the population is already fully housed does the street have no reason to exist, and then it is trimmed back to its last building or removed.

The threshold was a red herring, and the real defect was what counted as frontage. A government avenue on the drawn provincial city was passing the emptiness test while having literally no houses on either side of an approach some 450 ft long : the buildings crediting it were a band of laborers on the far side of a samurai ward fence, serving their own side of the fence and unable to reach the avenue at all. Tuning the length threshold would have hidden that; excluding frontage counted across a fence or a wall surfaced it,

The rule the map follows: inside the walls, no stretch of street longer than about 130 ft is empty of buildings that front that street - the street each was turned to face, not merely one it stands near - and a building with a fence or a wall between it and the point it would front serves its own side instead. A town's lesser lanes exist to be fronted: the laborers' quarters are given their own back lanes off the avenue, so their dwellings front a street without standing on the main commercial frontage.

A rampart's cost scales with its LENGTH

Sources: chinese-city-wall-enwiki, thepaper-city-walls (irregular plans set by topography and by taking high points in). The gully-and-quarter imagery is illustration; the cost argument is reasoning

The why is pure economics: a rampart is the single most expensive thing a town builds (rammed earth or stone, maintained for centuries),1615 and its cost scales with its length, so a town walls in exactly what it must defend and no more - the line is drawn to skirt the built-up area, not to inscribe a tidy circle around a lot of empty ground. Historically this is why town walls are irregular1817: they kink in to exclude a gully and bulge out around a quarter, following the settlement's actual footprint (and the terrain).

The rule the map follows: a walled town's rampart is drawn as an irregular thick line with a gate in it, and the irregularity is required: at least five sections, with the section lengths genuinely spread rather than merely unequal - a coefficient of variation of a quarter or more . That is a spread test on purpose, so a wall may hug a feature with several short segments while a lazily near-regular polygon still fails. Nothing is built on the rampart: it carries a no-build corridor about 46 ft wide , so even a large building's corner stays off it. A field may abut the wall but never crosses it. And the wall need not close a full ring - it may be an open arc whose two ends run partway up the flanks of a steep hill, the slope closing each side in place of a blunt wall end, with the magistrate's compound on the summit.

The rule the map follows: the wall hugs the built town. No continuous stretch of rampart longer than about 280 ft may run more than about 140 ft from the nearest building, feature or piece of terrain ; the two thresholds are calibrated against the drawn walled town rather than derived from a source. Two kinds of slack are legitimate and expected: terrain, since a wall climbs or skirts a hill rather than leveling it and the hill is itself what the rampart is standing on, and a modest gate forecourt, the open assembly square just inside the gate. A long empty margin running the whole length of one face is neither, and the fix is almost always to pull that face inward to ride just outside the last buildings, redrawing the cross-street and the quarter behind it to match, rather than to scatter buildings into the margin. This composes with the street rule above: a street earns its length from the buildings along it, and the wall earns its length from the whole built area - both reject infrastructure drawn around emptiness.

Why is the magistrate's manor drawn as a plain walled box?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-27

On a settlement map a walled compound is drawn as walls, a gate and an empty court, and the GM ruled on 2026-07-27 that this box is a simplification of the compound, not a scale reduction of it. It is a map drawing convention, and it has three consequences, each of which has been mistaken for a defect at least once. First, the footprint on the settlement map need not match the compound's own detailed sheet in shape, proportion or size - the real compound may not even be rectangular. Second, features the detailed sheet draws outside the walls - gate boards, a bounty board, an approach fork to a cart gate or a parley door - need not appear on the settlement map at all. Third, the glyph is presumed to contain everything the detailed sheet shows, drawn or not; that is the same reasoning that puts a county seat's tax-rice granary inside the magistrate's compound rather than beside it as a separate building. A difference between the two artifacts is therefore the convention working rather than an error, so a difference between the two is the convention working rather than an error.

The rule the map follows: a town draws the magistrate's compound as walls, a gate and an empty court, and nothing it is presumed to contain is drawn a second time beside it - which is why a county seat draws no separate granary unless it is one of the rice-transit towns that keeps a storehouse row of its own. Neither the checks nor a reviewer treats a difference between this glyph and the compound's own detailed sheet as a fault.

Where does a magistrate's manor stand, and which way does its gate face?

Sources: neixiang-xianya-zhwiki (the surviving Qing county yamen, and the regulation that puts its gate to the south); the siting is calibrated against the drawn maps

A magistrate's manor stands at the edge of the settlement it administers rather than in the middle of it, and its gate faces what it fronts: the town itself, or the road it sits beside. There is no fixed compass default, because the answer depends on where the town is - one drawn manor faces west to the hamlet below it, another south down its hill to the town, and the third is tilted so that its front wall runs parallel to the diagonal road it fronts, its gate opening onto the roadbed. The fallback when nothing is said is south - the formal orientation, laid down for a Chinese local government office by regulation and by ritual propriety: the seat sits to the north and faces south, civil on the left and military on the right, the court in front and the sleeping quarters behind24 - never a blind west.

Tilting the compound is cheap to draw and expensive downstream, and the way that was chosen to deal with it is worth recording because the obvious alternative is wrong. A tilt changes the manor's footprint and therefore the ground it blocks, which reshuffles the seeded blocks of housing around it and can knock a caste count off its band. The fix used is to re-roll the map's seed until the counts land back in band; each of the dense towns and the drawn city carries a hand-picked seed for exactly this reason. Hand-correcting the individual block counts instead is whack-a-mole: each correction perturbs the next block, and the map stops being the output of its own seed.

The rule the map follows: the manor stands at the edge of the town, its gate set deliberately to face either the dwellings it administers or the road it fronts, and the whole compound is turned where it fronts a road at an angle so that its front wall lies parallel to the roadbed.

How is a town farmstead laid out?

Sources: the GM's decision of 2026-07; the homestead bundle's own grounding is on the homesteads page

A town's farmhouses are not a looser version of a hamlet's. They stand in a nucleus and run the same to-scale homestead bundle the villages do - house, threshing yard strictly to its south, an adaptive sunny garden, a storehouse plot reserved to the north - and the whole bundle is tested against the paddies by its footprint.

That footprint test is the GM's direction of 2026-07, and what it guards against is the reason to keep it. Placing the house first and testing the fields by their centers is a 14 ft error in the wrong direction at a town's grain: a 44 ft town house passes such a test with a corner already sunk into the crop. Testing what the building actually occupies, against what the field actually occupies, is the only version of the rule that holds at this scale.

The nucleus also changes the greenery. A dispersed farmstead is sheltered by its own grove; a nucleus shelters itself, so the town's farms get no per-farm groves at all and the green instead comes as one communal windbreak on the windward side, with a leafy copse scatter through the rows.

The rule the map follows: town farmsteads use the nucleated to-scale homestead bundle, footprint-tested against the fields, with no per-farm grove; the shelter is communal. They stand outside the rampart, ringing the paddies at roughly village density, because a farmer builds close to the ground they work; a walled town does not farm inside its walls, where the land is too dear and the rampart too costly to throw around fields.

Which way does a shelter belt lie, and does its compass bearing matter?

Sources: this project's measurements across every nucleated map in the pool (2026-07); the shelter-belt agronomy itself is on the vegetation page

The belt nestles against the windward fringe of each farm cluster, and it has to be wide enough to reach open ground: clumps are refused wherever they would fall on a house, a yard or a garden, so a band drawn only over the packed rows thins to a scatter, and a band has to bridge from the rows out into clear ground to take at all. Corner masses of forest by themselves are decoration rather than a wind wall, and a town with more than one farm cluster needs a hugging band for each, merging into the corner woods where they meet.

Two attempts to turn the rest of that into a measurable property both failed. The first was a canopy-fraction metric - how much of the windward arc within some radius is under canopy - calibrated across every nucleated map in 2026-07. It cannot separate the approved forms from the bad ones, and it ranks them backwards: the approved ribbon-form belt scores 4 to 18 per cent while the corner-blob layouts it was meant to reject score 29 to 45. So the form-aware adjacency measure replaced it - is there a substantial belt actually beside a farmhouse - and the canopy fraction was dropped.

The second was the belt's bearing, and here the measurement was taken before the finding was believed. A review reported the border town's belt as facing the wrong quarter: a nearly north-south belt against a declared northwest wind, where the textbook shelter belt lies northeast-southwest across the windward face. Two metrics were calibrated across every nucleated map in the pool before anything was touched, and both refuted the report. Measured as the face presented to the wind - the clumps' extent across the wind over their extent along it - the accused belt scores 1.46 against 1.02, 0.90 to 1.13, 1.04, 1.25 and 1.33 for the approved maps: a wider face than most of the pool, so any threshold that failed it would fail half the approved maps too. Measured as the principal axis of the clumps against the ideal across-wind axis, it is 31 degrees off, the second-best alignment in the whole pool - only a ribbon-form belt at 5 degrees and another at 14 beat it - while every approved map runs 33 to 49 degrees off. The pool's real convention is therefore that a belt follows the cluster's windward fringe, and the wind decides which SIDE it lies on, not which bearing it runs at. No bearing rule is drawn from this, and a belt whose only fault is its compass angle is not a fault at all.

The rule the map follows: each farm cluster carries its own band of trees along its windward fringe, deep enough to stand in open ground rather than only over the rows. At least one substantial grove - a dozen clumps or more - stands within 150 ft of a farmhouse ; on the approved maps the belt stands much closer than that limit, between about 37 and 262 ft from the nearest farmhouse depending on the tier. A town whose shelter is a real wood is exempt: one drawn settlement nestles against the forest itself, which is the same property arrived at by other means.

How big is a town's paddy plot?

Sources: the plot-size finding on the fields page (pre-modern plots 0.02-0.25 acre, irregular) and its in-field features finding

A town's paddies are drawn on a fine comb, and the grain is set by what the town's own canals can feed rather than by the look of the quilt. The comb is set to plots about 58 ft across on rows 52 to 72 ft apart , which is a bunded paddy of roughly 0.08 acre - inside the attested pre-modern band and at the small end of what these maps otherwise draw. A coarser grain was tried and declined: at about 66 ft across, the comb's own rule that an offtake must stand at least two plot-widths along its canal put the minimum offtake spacing above the length of the short canals a town has room for, and the delivery network starved.

The small in-field flourishes that a hamlet's paddies carry - a pocket pond, a rock, a grave island - are switched off at town scale and above. That is the GM's reading of the sheet: at a foot to the pixel the glyphs stop reading as flourishes and start reading as a pond and an ossuary standing in the middle of the rice. It is a map drawing convention, not a claim that town paddies had none.

The rule the map follows: a town's paddy comb is drawn at about 58 ft across the plot with rows 52 to 72 ft apart, about 0.08 acre to the plot, and the in-field flourishes are off at this tier and above.
