The town tier: the research behind the layout, wall and market rules

The research behind the rules in ../settlements/towns.md.

Chinese towns were PLANNED - the gate-to-yamen axis

Sources: song-architecture-enwiki (Tang walled wards -> Song shop-lined streets; "curfew" is the entry's gloss), guanxiang-zdic (the term; commerce outside the gates is a GUESS - no readable page supports it), jokamachi-jawiki (zoning and kinked streets), changan-enwiki (Nara and Kyoto modeled after Chang'an). The gate-to-yamen axis for an ordinary county seat is a GUESS - no readable page supports it

Chinese towns - Rokugan's geographic model - were planned, not organic: a main avenue ran from the principal (usually south) gate straight to the government office (yamen), which sat on that axis facing south, with the grid divided into blocks by cross streets (unsourced). Early (Tang) cities walled those blocks into curfew wards1; by the Song the wards opened into shop-lined streets (merchant shophouses fronting the roadbed),2 and commerce spilled outside the gates along the approach19 road4 (the guan-xiang suburb).3 Japan copied the Tang grid for its imperial capitals (Nara/Kyoto)5 but laid out castle towns (jokamachi) as zoned districts - samurai by the castle,6 merchants in trade-blocks,7 temples at the edge8 - with deliberately kinked, defensive streets.9

The market-day flophouse - who actually stays over

Sources: kichinyado-jawiki, kichinyado-kotobank (3 mon in 1611, 6 mon in 1658, 24 + 16-24 mon in 1843)13

A county seat exists partly as the periodic-market center for its hinterland: most peasants come from within a day's round-trip, but those at the far edge of the catchment stay over on market eve (unsourced) in cheap communal lodging - a kichin-yado ("firewood-fee inn"),10 where you sleep11 on straw (unsourced) under a roof for a few mon a night.12

The gate market exists for TRAFFIC, not taxes

Sources: setting-canon - budgets.md (tariffs at city and capital gates only; the yoriki audit); the traffic drivers from guanxiang-zdic

The why is TRAFFIC, not taxes (the GM's ruling, 2026-07-24): towns levy NO import tariffs - budgets.md puts the entire tariff apparatus (the Yasuki Taka gate collection, the tariff-audit yoriki) at provincial-city and capital gates only, ~2,700 manageable collection points instead of an impossible ~14,400 town gates - and the county magistrate's jurisdiction is the whole COUNTY, so standing outside the gate crosses no tax or regulatory line at all. The honest drivers are the ones the city-tier research validated: through-road travelers (carters, peddlers) buying services without detouring inside20, the market-day chokepoint where the rural catchment trades, and late arrivals who find the gate shut at dusk.

A street is access infrastructure for the buildings it serves

Sources: hutong-enwiki

The why is historical: a street is access infrastructure for the buildings it serves, and it is paved or worn into the ground by the foot traffic to and from them - Beijing's hutong alleys14 "emerged as access routes lined by contiguous courtyard residences," and a desire path forms only between real destinations. A planned grid line that never gets built up simply isn't drawn (an undeveloped block).

A rampart's cost scales with its LENGTH

Sources: chinese-city-wall-enwiki, thepaper-city-walls (irregular plans set by topography and by taking high points in). The gully-and-quarter imagery is illustration; the cost argument is reasoning

The why is pure economics: a rampart is the single most expensive thing a town builds (rammed earth or stone, maintained for centuries),1615 and its cost scales with its length, so a town walls in exactly what it must defend and no more - the line is drawn to skirt the built-up area, not to inscribe a tidy circle around a lot of empty ground. Historically this is why town walls are irregular1817: they kink in to exclude a gully and bulge out around a quarter, following the settlement's actual footprint (and the terrain).
