What a label may cover on town and city maps

A town or a city carries far more captions than a hamlet does - districts, ministries, temples, markets, gates, roads - and on a crowded sheet what matters is less where a caption looks best than what it is allowed to lie across. How a caption is placed, turned and wrapped is at Labels on maps (cartographic label placement). These rules are this project's conventions, checked against the three drawn towns and the three drawn cities; none of them is a historical finding.

  • A caption lying across a building tells the reader that building is the thing named.
    That is right when it lies on its own subject and wrong one door along. A "flophouse" caption across a flophouse says something true; the same caption a few feet over, across a merchant's estate, has silently relabeled the estate. So would a gate guard-house's caption resting on a flophouse, or a temple's name over a merchant house.
  • Buildings of one kind are the subtler case.
    Two ministries are the same kind of thing, so a rule that only knows kinds will let one ministry's name sit on the office next door: a Ministry of Justice caption standing on the Ministry of Works.

The rule the map follows, in a town or a city:

  • No two captions overlap, and no caption runs off the edge of the picture.
    Plenty of things rightly run off the edge - fields, roads, country estates, farmhouses, the countryside continuing - but a caption that spills past it is clipped and unreadable, so every caption's block sits wholly inside the framed window.
  • A caption may lie on the feature it names, and on no building of a different identity.
    Its own building or compound, or for a district caption any building of that district. It may also clip a street-fronting shop, or a servant house among the households it serves, since those line every quarter and are never mistaken for the subject.
  • A caption's own wording says which groups of buildings it may cover.
    A flophouse caption the flophouses; a guard-house or inspection caption the gate furniture; a ministry's name the ministries; a temple's or shrine's name the temples; a samurai caption the samurai houses and estates; a graveyard, cremation-ground, mausoleum or ossuary caption any of the funerary structures, but never a temple; a street's or road's name the frontage it runs along.
  • A caption that names no building may clip nothing.
    Farmland, a market, the place name itself.
  • Funerary ground is a subject in its own right.
    A temple's caption may not lie on a graveyard any more than a graveyard's caption may lie on a temple; where two such subjects stand together, one caption goes above its building and the other below.

The rule the map follows, in a city:

  • A named civic building's caption may not touch any other named civic building.
    A ministry by name, the governor's yamen, a temple by name: the group rule alone would let one ministry's name sit on the next ministry. The remedy is to separate the two offices, which the rule against civic buildings standing wall to wall already wants, or to move the caption onto its own building.
  • A district caption sits with the cluster it names.
    "Samurai neighborhood", "merchant district", "burakumin neighborhood", "samurai estates": each stands entirely on the same side of the wall as its subject (the laborers', outcaste, samurai, merchant and in-wall farming captions inside the rampart, the outlying estates' caption outside), among its own buildings - the nearest of them within about 390 ft for a cluster of houses, further for sparse manors, temples or fields - and never floating over a foreign field or another compound.
  • A misplaced district caption is moved onto its own cluster.
    Never onto the district's edge over a neighbor, and never out over the moat.

The rule the map follows, for naming districts in a town or a city:

  • Each named district has one caption.
    A neighborhood that already carries its name never gets a second caption for a pocket of itself: the commerce strip outside a temple's gate is part of the temple neighborhood, and naming it again is clutter. A feature inside a district that needs naming is named by its own caption on its own glyph - a particular temple, a graveyard - not by a second district-scale caption.
  • "Quarter" names a genuine quarter of the settlement.
    In practice a city has quarters and a town does not. A small part of a town is a neighborhood, an enclave or a row: an outcaste neighborhood, a samurai neighborhood.
