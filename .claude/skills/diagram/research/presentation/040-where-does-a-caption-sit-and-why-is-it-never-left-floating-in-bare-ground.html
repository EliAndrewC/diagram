Where does a caption sit, and why is it never left floating in bare ground?

Where mapmakers have long put it. Placing names on maps is one of the best-studied parts of cartography, and the rules for a caption naming a single feature are settled enough that the mapping programs cited below build them in. A point's caption has two jobs, in this order: it must be easy to read, and it must be plainly the name of this feature and no other. Everything below serves those two jobs, and the maps follow the standard rather than a rule of their own.

Which side. A caption that cannot stand inside what it names - a notice board, a well, a small shrine too small for its words - stands beside it, at the first free place in a fixed list: directly above, directly below, to its right, upper right, lower right, to its left, upper left, lower left. That is the order readers themselves chose in the one study found that asked them - nearly 800 of them - which found they significantly prefer a name directly above its point to the conventional upper right, and ranked the places in that order. The cartography textbooks give a different order, upper right, upper left, lower right, lower left, straight right, straight left, above and slightly right, below and slightly left. A set of eight positions like it is the typical way the problem of placing names is framed. A caption set level with its feature, like an item in a list, is held to read worse than one lifted above or dropped below it. The study leaves out the two "slightly" places as a relic of older printing. Authorities differ on the order, and several rank a place beside the point higher than the textbooks do. A German reference dictionary of cartography counts the place to the right of a point as ideal, though without saying whether level with it or at its upper right. A series of schemes for labeling oil wells puts directly above higher than other authors do. A mapping library's own example tries only the four places beside the point, and no corner at all. The maps follow the readers' order, so that every caption on every sheet follows the same pattern.

How far. The gap is measured from the feature's drawn edge to the caption's nearest edge, and it is the same for every caption of a size. No source found fixes the number: the cartography course says only that it depends on the map and that consistency is what matters most. These maps use half the height of the caption's lettering, which for a notice board's 8 ft lettering is 4 ft of air: close enough to read as one thing, far enough that the words do not touch the glyph. That figure is our calibration, not a finding.

When something is in the way. A caption goes on empty ground if it can, and only when no empty ground is left does it cover something - and then the least important thing it can. Another caption, the feature itself, a house or any other building, a well or a small fixture is never covered while any other choice exists. A lane or a ditch may be crossed, but crossing one is better than crossing two. Fields, scrub, groves and open ground are not in the way at all - they are what the mapping programs call available space - so a name lies on the land the way a name lies on any map. A seat that would run off the edge of the picture is never taken, because a clipped caption cannot be read. Put together, a caption's quality is what it covers plus how far down the ranked positions it had to go, which is how the standard itself scores a labeling.

Moving further out, and the line that ties it back. When every position at the usual gap is taken, the caption tries the same positions a step further out, then another, and the nearer free seat always wins. It may go as far as twice the usual gap without anything more, which is the kind of allowance a mapping program lets a mapmaker set for a label that cannot keep its preferred distance. Beyond that it is joined to its feature by a thin leader line, the device mapmakers use for a caption displaced from the thing it names. And a caption is never left off: the GM ruled that "for the time being we'll treat labels as mandatory when the thing is marked as needing a label", so on a sheet with no free ground at all the caption still goes down, where it covers the least.

What a caption names decides which kind of placement it gets. A small feature - a notice board, a well, a privy - is labeled beside it as above. A larger feature named on itself - a building's name written across its roof on a plan, a field's name across the field - is an area caption and is set inside the area it names, over its middle when that is free; and a road's name runs along the road, above it rather than below (see why a caption tilts). Captions are seated last, once the map is finished, because where a caption can go depends on everything else already being there.
