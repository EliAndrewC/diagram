Where does a caption sit, and why is it never left floating in bare ground?

Sources: the GM's rulings of 2026-07, 2026-07-26 and 2026-08-27; this project's own measurements of the shipped maps

Two things are being traded against each other, and the order between them is the whole rule. First, empty ground wins: if a caption can sit somewhere that still plainly names its subject and covers nothing else, it must, and only when every candidate covers something does it take the least damaging one. The defects that forced this were both of the same kind - a ministry's name lying over samurai houses while the ground just below the office was bare, and an "Imperial Road" caption over a farmstead's trees while the road's other flank was empty.

But empty ground is plentiful, which is the trap the first version fell into (GM, 2026-07-26). Counting overlaps alone made every clear candidate tie at nothing, and the winner then fell out of the order things happened to be drawn in - so a caption could float 120 to 165 ft out in bare ground and score exactly as well as one tucked against the thing it names. One city shipped both halves of that failure: an "Imperial Road" caption some 165 ft clear of the roadway, and a "gate market" caption about 126 ft east of its own stall rows, close enough to the execution ground that it read as naming the execution ground. So nearness is the second term, and a caption that names a thing - a market row, a works, a road - is seated against that thing rather than at coordinates somebody picked.

The seat is chosen by stepping outward from the subject: about five feet of air at a hamlet's scale, in steps of about six, nine rungs deep, taking the nearest seat that covers nothing. Only when the whole ladder is blocked does it fall back to the least-covered seat. Within a rung, a tie goes to the seat least crowded by other captions - two seats equally tight against the subject are not equally good if one of them is also up against a neighbor's words, which is precisely how "gate market" came to read as a second line of "execution ground". A caption may sit anywhere along its subject, so a long subject is also tried at slides along its own length; without that freedom the ladder has one seat per flank per rung and abandons a whole flank whenever a neighbor's caption happens to hold that latitude. Two constraints are honored while the seat is being chosen rather than left for a later check: captions already placed and the placard count as obstacles, and any candidate outside the cropped window is thrown away outright, because a clipped caption is unreadable.

Captions are seated last, once the map is finished. Seating one when its subject is drawn judges it against half a map: a city places its gate markets before its execution ground exists, so the north market's caption took the flank that later filled up. The deferred captions seat first and the road's caption last - most constrained first, since a road caption can slide along a long roadway while a market row's frontage is short. A consequence, and a welcome one: a caption seated after the frame is taken cannot hold a side of the frame open, which is what the GM wanted anyway.

A road caption is seated against the roadway's cross-section and its tangent, not against the bounding box of the road segment. For one town's diagonal Imperial road that box is a square some 486 by 256 ft whose edge is about 280 ft from the road itself, so a caption seated a few feet off the box sat nowhere near the road. An anchor written into a map by hand is therefore only a hint - which flank, and where along the road - and can no longer set the distance, which is what had let a hand anchor 102 ft out survive as a 55 ft gap. And a caption lying along a road needs a stretch of roadside that is actually bare: the natural anchor is the road's midpoint, which on that town is the middle of a core lined two rows deep on both flanks, so the honest answer is to name the road where the road is clear - out past the last shopfront on the approach - rather than to widen what counts as near.

How close is close enough? A backstop catches a caption that ran the whole ladder and still landed nowhere near its subject: the finished gap is measured from the subject's own box and capped at three times the size of the lettering. On 2026-08-27 the GM asked for the opposite move as well - "move the label fifty percent of the way toward the thing that it is labeling. Let's try that and see how it looks" - so a seated caption is measured against its subject's true footprint and pulled half that gap toward it, and the pull is refused whenever the moved block would touch the subject or anything else. It is applied today to one hamlet's notice-board caption, which came in from about 11 ft to 5.5 ft, and it is provisional: a general rule for every caption seat if the GM likes it on the sheet, and until then the one worked example.

One lesson underlies all of this and is worth stating on its own: the shape that decides where a caption may sit must be the same shape that judges it afterward. While captions were probed generously - measured by an upright box drawn around a tilted block - the two disagreed invisibly, and the moment the probe became honest about a caption's real reach, a caravan-inn caption settled into the corner slack of a tilted stable and was caught as covering it. The overstatement is large for a long caption: the upright box around a 97 ft caption lying at some 27 degrees claims about 3.3 times the text's real thickness, which is enough to make the ladder reject the one seat a road caption wants - lying along the roadway, in the lane between the roadbed and the shopfront setback - and walk out to 64 ft of bare ground instead. The placement and the verdict now measure the same drawn shape.

The rule the map follows: a caption naming a particular feature is seated against that feature, at the nearest position that covers nothing else, preferring a position that is not crowded by another caption; a caption naming an area is seated among the things it names. Captions are seated after the map is complete and inside the finished frame. A caption ends up within three times its own lettering height of the thing it names, or the map is wrong.
