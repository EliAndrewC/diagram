The map sheet: its title, legend, frame and margins

A settlement map is a sheet drawn for a reader to look at, and nearly everything about the sheet itself is a map drawing convention rather than a finding about the past: no village had its name lettered across its fields or a bar showing a hundred feet. The sheet carries the place name with a scale bar under it, and nothing else that explains itself - no legend, no compass, no descriptive subtitle, and no caption on anything the drawing already shows. A map earns more by being self-evident than by being annotated.

The picture is cut down to what it shows. Rather than a fixed canvas, the frame is the box around the settlement's solid features - its houses, fields, water and groves - plus a narrow margin: about 30 ft on a hamlet sheet, 60 ft on a village one, about 105 ft on a provincial city's. So there is very little bare ground at the edge, and the wild ground, streams and lanes run off it.

The sheet carries a name and a scale bar, and leaves out whatever the drawing can say for itself.

  • The sheet has no legend and no descriptive subtitle.
    There is no key box pairing swatches to meanings, and no line under the title telling the reader this is "an average farming village".
  • No caption states what the drawing already shows.
    A reader can count the torii, so the map never states how many there are; a reader can see that a building stands on a hill, so no subtitle says "atop the hill". Captions are spent on the few things the ink cannot carry - which plots lie fallow, which are tax-free, what a particular building is - and the rest of the sheet is left to be looked at.
  • A building's subtitle adds information or is dropped.
    A subtitle that restates the name is cut - a monastery named for its patron needs no "(town monastery)" under it - and so is one that describes what the drawing shows. One that says something the sheet cannot is kept: that a shrine is still tended, that a compound is a hunting lodge by a named forest. A building is never subtitled with the settlement's own rank: "county seat" names the town, not the building standing in it.
  • There is no compass.
    These maps are always drawn north up, so a compass rose would repeat a fact that never varies, adding a mark that belongs to the map rather than to the place on a sheet that shows nothing else of that kind.
  • A scale bar sits under the place name.
    It is a ruled bar with a tick at each end and one in the middle, the real distance written beneath it, and a line of fine print giving how many feet one unit of the drawing stands for, as on the building plans.
  • The scale bar is a fixed length on the page, not a fixed distance on the ground.
    That is what makes it cheap: the same bar reads 100 ft on a hamlet or town sheet, 200 ft on a village one and 300 ft on a provincial city's, all round numbers, so no rank of settlement needs the bar tuned by hand.
  • The title is set in blank ground.
    The title placard - the name and the bar together - is placed by scanning the framed window from the top for a box that clears every feature on the map, so the name never lies across buildings, fields, water or a grove. The search runs over the final window, after the frame is fixed, or it would seat the name against an edge that then moves.

The rule the map follows, for what the sheet carries:

  • Every settlement sheet, at every rank, carries the place name and a scale bar beneath it, and nothing else of that kind.
    No legend, no key, no compass, no descriptive subtitle, and no caption stating a count or a fact the drawing already shows.
  • The bar's stated distance agrees with the scale the map declares.
    A bar that reads 100 ft spans 100 ft of the drawn ground.
  • The title placard stands clear of every drawn feature.
    The name and its bar lie across no building, field, water or grove.
  • Caption text is drawn above all other ink.
    No part of a caption is ever covered by something drawn later.
  • A building's subtitle adds information or is dropped.
    A subtitle that restates the name, describes what the drawing shows or gives the settlement's own rank is cut.

A fixed canvas leaves dead margins on a map whose settlement sits in one corner and whose paddy runs off to one side, so the frame is computed from what is drawn.

  • The frame is the box around the solid features, plus a margin.
    The houses, the fields, the water, the groves and the pond set the box; a hamlet sheet adds about 30 ft and a village one about 60 ft. The image size that falls out is not a round number, and that is expected.
  • A field counts only its drawn plots.
    The larger keep-out area the generator uses to keep houses off a field can run on past the last plot with nothing in it, and framing to it puts dead ground back: on one sheet it dropped the south margin some 210 ft below anything drawn, and twice that on a village map.
  • Wild ground, streams, channels and lanes are cut off at the edge and never widen it.
    The scrub commons, the streams, the channels and the lanes are drawn up to the frame and cut there, reading as "more wild ground, more map, this way" - which is true, and is what the reader should take from it.
  • The frame does not open to show more of the commons.
    Opening it far enough to keep about two thirds of a trailing commons holds the edge out over empty back-slope grazing - past a graveyard on one flank, some 130 ft past a lone pond on the other, more on a village sheet - which is the dead space the crop exists to remove. So the commons is cut off like the rest of the wild ground. If a map ever wants a wild feature to widen its frame, the honest change is to count that feature as solid, not to open the frame to the commons.
  • The village windbreak is framed on its edge toward the houses, and its depth runs off the sheet.
    The canopy edge facing the houses holds the frame open at the usual margin, and the trees behind it are cut off at the edge. The measurement behind it and the alternatives declined are at How our maps keep yards and gardens in the sun.
  • What the frame must hold is drawn before the frame is taken.
    Because the frame is computed from what has been drawn, the order decides what survives. The large features, and above all an outlying one well away from the cluster - a back-slope graveyard, a shrine set apart - come first. Only things that drop into the framed space afterward, the wells among the houses, the monk's plots and the title placard, come later. A solid feature added after the frame is fixed is silently sliced by it: a graveyard set apart to the west of a village is cut in two if it is drawn after the frame.
  • A caption that alone holds a side of the frame open is moved inward.
    It is moved in among the things it names, rather than the crop padded around it: one city's "samurai estates" caption stands among its manors for this reason.

A provincial city is framed more tightly, on the settlement itself.

  • A provincial city is framed on its wall.
    A town map is as much about its surroundings as about the town; a city map is about the city, and the countryside around it matters little. So the window just takes in the walled place - the rampart, its moat, and the clusters kept outside it (the gate markets, the flophouses, the funerary grounds, the wharf stalls) - with the farmland, the outlying estates and the far rows of a gate market running off the edge. The margin is about 105 ft past the outermost thing the frame is taken on. Farms cut off at the edge are fine: the point is to show they are there, not the whole field.
  • A city framed on its wall with no margin loses its farmland from view, so the margin is a stated figure.
    Without it, the irrigated rice fields spreading out from the wall are cut to a sliver about 138 ft wide, and the ring of farmland reads as bare though the fields are drawn. The margin of about 105 ft shows them without paying for empty ground.

Even with a narrow margin, a single feature far outside everything else can by itself force a bigger picture, and a sheet can come out 15 percent taller than it needs to be. Moved inward, that one feature lets the whole map crop tighter.

  • One feature may hold the frame open by up to three times its own size, or by about 60 ft if that is more.
    The 60 ft is on a hamlet or town sheet: about 120 ft on a village one and 180 ft on a city's. Only a feature past both limits fails.
  • The limit is a ratio because a legitimate far feature is about as far out as it is big.
    This project's census of the shipped maps showed it. A pond or a forest that pushes the frame out is large and meant to be out there: every legitimate case has a gap about equal to its own size - ponds at 1.03 to 1.35 times their own extent, one hamlet's forest at 1.14. A city's tanning-yard caption stood some 534 ft past everything else on a box 30 ft tall: 17.8 times its own size. A flat distance limit, the obvious first try, would have condemned every hamlet's irrigation pond at the head of its fields.
  • The far-feature limit is tested on the same list of features the frame was computed from.
    The two crops take different sets on purpose - a city frames on its moat ring, its outlying clusters and its captions while its rice fields and farmhouses are cut off - so the frame and the test read one list, and a check that re-derived the list would drift from it.
  • A city's trade works outside the wall are framed by their caption, not by the works.
    The list a city's frame is computed from carries every placed caption box but not the tanning yards themselves, which is why one city's frame is set by the words "tanning yard" rather than by the yard.
  • A city's kiln works count toward the frame themselves.
    A kiln works is some 140 by 120 ft with its own housing - wider than any caption of it - and an outlying cluster whose point is where it stands, which must not be cut off at the frame.
  • A legitimate outlier is declared on its map, with the reason.
    The rule is not relaxed for it. In one city two rules conflict: the tanning yard must stand below every irrigation intake, and the clear corridor kept along the channel that draws off the fields' water blocks everything above a line, so the shallowest legal place saves only about 24 ft of a sheet some 4,200 ft tall, and is flagged anyway. The declaration turns silent bloat into a cost somebody chose.
  • A pond at the head of a valley that feeds a field may stretch the frame past that limit.
    It is the commonest far feature on the maps, and it is exempt on physical grounds: the feed is gravity, so the pond has to stand above the field's water entry; pulling it inside a tighter frame would put it downhill of the field it waters.
  • A valley-head pond is slid sideways until it no longer pokes past the fields, rather than moved.
    It only has to contain its own sluice to feed the field, so the drawn water slides, apart from the sluice and the field, until its far edge is flush with the dry plots instead of poking past them. One village's pond is nudged about 314 ft west, its east edge flush with the dry-field strip, for an image about 9 percent smaller. The poke left on its uphill side is the cost of a valley head, and is accepted.
  • A village's fields are not mirrored to stop its valley-head pond poking out past them.
    A mirrored layout only flips which side the pond pokes out of, and it erases the deliberate difference between two neighboring villages, which is worth more than the space saved.

The rule the map follows, at hamlet and village scale:

  • The frame is the box around the solid features plus a margin.
    About 30 ft on a hamlet sheet, 60 ft on a village one.
  • A field contributes its drawn plots and not its keep-out area.
    Empty ground past the last plot never widens the frame.
  • The commons, the streams, the channels and the lanes are cut off at the edge.
    They are drawn up to the frame and never widen it.
  • Every solid feature lies wholly inside the finished window.
    The houses, the groves, the graveyard, the shrine, the wells, the torii: a feature placed too late and quietly sliced is caught rather than shipped.
  • No edge stands more than about 56 ft beyond the outermost feature the frame was taken on, on a hamlet sheet, or about 112 ft on a village one.
    That leaves the working margins, 30 to 44 ft on a hamlet sheet, a little slack, and fails an edge held open by something that should have been cut off.

The rule the map follows, for a provincial city:

  • The map is framed on the settlement itself.
    For a walled city: the wall, the moat and the clusters kept outside it, plus about 105 ft of margin at the city scale and room above for the title placard.
  • The countryside runs off the edge and is not drawn whole.
    The outlying estates, the surrounding farmland and the far rows of a gate market are cut off by the frame.
  • A single side may be widened for farmland.
    It keeps a representative band of farmland on a flank with no outlying cluster to anchor it.
  • The edge of the map is the cropped window.
    Every check that asks whether something runs off the edge reads the cropped window, not the full drawing area.

The rule the map follows, for one feature far out:

  • No single feature holds the frame open by more than three times its own size and more than about 60 ft on a hamlet or town sheet (120 ft on a village one, 180 ft on a city's), unless the map says why.
    A feature that does is moved inward, or the map records the reason it cannot be - a conflict with another rule, a gravity feed that needs the high ground - as a declaration a reader can be shown, not as a loosened limit.
  • A pond that feeds a field is exempt, and is nudged rather than moved.
    It slides until its far edge is flush with the plots, keeping its sluice inside the water.
