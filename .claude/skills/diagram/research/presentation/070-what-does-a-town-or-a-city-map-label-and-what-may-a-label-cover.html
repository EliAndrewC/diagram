What does a town or a city map label, and what may a label cover?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-21; this project's own review of the drawn towns and cities

A town or a city carries far more captions than a hamlet does - districts, ministries, temples, markets, gates, roads - and on a dense sheet the question stops being where a caption looks best and becomes what a caption is allowed to touch. A caption lying across a building tells the reader that building is the thing named. That is exactly right when it lies on its own subject and exactly wrong one door along, which is the whole of the rule below; nothing in it is a historical finding, and none of it is drawn by a generator yet.

The judgment that keeps recurring is about identity rather than about ink. A "flophouse" caption sitting across a flophouse says something true; the same caption a few feet over, across a merchant's estate, has silently relabeled the estate. The same went for a gate guard-house caption that came to rest on a flophouse, and for a temple's name that ended up over a merchant house. Sibling buildings of one kind are the subtler case: two ministries are the same kind of thing, so a rule that only knows kinds will happily let one ministry's name sit on the office next door - which is how a Ministry of Justice caption came to stand on the Ministry of Works.

The rule the map follows (town and city): no two captions overlap, and no caption runs off the edge of the picture. Plenty of things rightly run off the edge - fields, roads, country estates, farmhouses, the countryside continuing - but a caption that spills past it is clipped and unreadable, so every caption's block sits wholly inside the framed window.

The rule the map follows (town and city): a caption may lie on the feature it names - its own building or compound, or for a district caption any building of that district - and may clip a street-fronting shop or a servant house interleaved among the households it serves, since those line every quarter and are never mistaken for the subject. It may not lie on a building of a different identity. Each captioned structure belongs to a group, and a caption's own wording says which groups it may cover: a flophouse caption the flophouses, a guard-house or inspection caption the gate furniture, a ministry's name the ministries, a temple's or shrine's name the temples, a samurai caption the samurai houses and estates, a graveyard, cremation-ground, mausoleum or ossuary caption any of the funerary structures but never a temple, a street's or road's name the frontage it runs along. A caption that names no building at all - farmland, a market, the place name itself - may clip nothing. Funerary ground is a subject in its own right, so a temple's caption may not lie on a graveyard any more than a graveyard's caption may lie on a temple; where two such subjects stand together, one caption goes above its building and the other below.

The rule the map follows (city): a named civic building's caption - a ministry by name, the governor's yamen, a temple by name - additionally may not touch any other named civic building, because the group rule alone would permit one ministry's name to sit on the next ministry. The remedy is to separate the two offices, which the rule against civic buildings abutting already wants, or to move the caption onto its own building.

The rule the map follows (city): a district caption - "samurai neighborhood", "merchant district", "burakumin neighborhood", "samurai estates" - sits with the cluster it names. It stands entirely on the same side of the wall as its subject (the laborers', outcaste, samurai, merchant and in-wall farming captions inside the rampart, the extramural estates' caption outside), among its own buildings - the nearest of them within about 390 ft for a cluster of houses, further for sparse manors, temples or fields - and never floating over a foreign field or another compound. The remedy for a misplaced district caption is always to move it onto its own cluster, never onto the district's edge over a neighbor and never out over the moat.

The rule the map follows (town and city): one caption per named district (GM, 2026-07-21). A neighborhood that already carries its name never gets a second caption for a pocket of itself - the commerce strip outside a temple's gate is part of the temple neighborhood, and naming it again is clutter. A feature inside a district that needs naming is named by its own caption on its own glyph - a particular temple, a graveyard - not by a second district-scale caption.

The rule the map follows (town and city): "quarter" is reserved for a genuine quarter of the settlement, which in practice means a city has quarters and a town does not. A small sub-area of a town is a neighborhood, an enclave or a row: an outcaste neighborhood, a samurai neighborhood.
