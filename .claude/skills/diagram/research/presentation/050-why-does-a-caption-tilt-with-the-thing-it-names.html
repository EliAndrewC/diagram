Why does a caption tilt with the thing it names?

Sources: esri-point-label-rotation (a rotated feature's label takes its angle); krygier-geog353-type (horizontal type reads easiest); psu-geog486-point-labels (a line's label above it, never upside down); the GM's rulings of 2026-08-27 and 2026-09-27; this project's measurements of the three drawn cities

So that the words read as part of the thing, the way a level caption reads as part of a level building. The GM stated it as a general rule on 2026-08-27: "the notice board is at an angle, therefore, the notice board label should be at exactly the same angle". A caption takes its subject's own rotation - exactly, not approximately - normalized only so far as is needed to keep the text from reading upside down, and it is seated off the subject's rotated edge rather than off an upright box around it. A road's caption takes its bearing from the stretch of roadway it is seated against; a row of shopfronts takes the axis of the street it fronts.

Mapping programs allow the same. Where a feature carries a direction of its own, its caption can be turned to that angle, and then the angle - not the usual positions around the feature - decides where the caption goes; so on these maps the ranked positions of the previous question are read in the feature's own turned frame, "upper right" meaning upper right as the words read. A road's name follows the road and sits above it rather than below, and is never written upside down. Everything else is level, because horizontal lettering is the easiest to find and read. The GM confirmed the tilt again when the standard was adopted: "Tilted or horizontal text is okay for small point features like the board, and in general is fine if the standard says it might be preferred for things which are themselves tilted".

A subject at a square rotation - a quarter, a half or three quarters of a turn - has a horizontal edge of its own, so its level caption is aligned with it and stays level.

What does not tilt is everything with no axis to follow: a caption over an upright block of packed houses ("laborers' dwellings", "samurai houses", "barns"), a caption on a point fixture such as a boundary stone, and a city district's caption. The fabric under all three drawn cities' district captions was measured and is level, so tilting them would align them with nothing on the map. Tilt follows the subject's axis; it is not decoration.

A tilted caption takes up a taller block than the same words set level, so where the picture's edges are drawn to take in its captions, a tilted one near the edge can widen the picture by a few feet - one city's grew about 45 ft for its tanning yard's caption.

The rule the map follows: a caption naming a rotated feature is drawn at that feature's own angle and seated off its rotated edge; a caption naming a road or a row laid along one runs along that line at its bearing; a caption naming an area, an upright block or a point fixture is level. The text never reads upside down.
