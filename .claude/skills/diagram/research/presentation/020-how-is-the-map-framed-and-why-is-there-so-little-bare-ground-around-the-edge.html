How is the map framed, and why is there so little bare ground around the edge?

Sources: the GM's rulings of 2026-07-23; this project's own measurements of the shipped maps

Because the image is cut down to what it shows. A fixed canvas leaves dead margins on a map whose settlement sits in one corner and whose paddy runs off to one side, so the frame is computed instead: take the bounding box of the hard content - the houses, the fields, the water, the groves, the pond - add a margin, and make that the picture. A hamlet sheet takes a margin of about 30 ft and a village one about 60 ft. The image size that falls out is not a round number, and that is expected.

Only what is really drawn counts toward the box. A field contributes the extent of its visible plots, never the larger envelope the placer uses to keep houses off it: that envelope can run on past the last plot with nothing in it, and framing to it puts dead ground back - on one sheet it dropped the south margin some 210 ft below anything drawn, and twice that on a village map.

Everything that is not hard content clips at the edge and never extends it. The scrub commons, the streams, the channels and the lanes are drawn right up to the frame and cut there, reading as "more wild ground, more map, this way" - which is true, and is what the reader should take from it. A bleed rule was tried and is not kept: the frame used to open far enough to preserve about two thirds of a trailing commons, and in practice that held the edge out over empty back-slope grazing - past a graveyard on one flank, some 130 ft past a lone pond on the other, more on a village sheet - which is exactly the dead space the crop exists to remove. So the commons clips like the marsh, and the frame hugs the real settlement rather than the scrub around it. If a map ever wants a wilderness feature to widen its frame, the honest change is to count that feature as hard content, not to bring a bleed back.

One frame-setting rule lives with the homestead grove rather than here: the communal windbreak's depth clips at the edge while its inner face - the canopy edge toward the houses - holds the frame open at the standard margin. The ruling, the measurement behind it and the alternatives declined are at The garden's sun, and how far the windbreak shades.

Because the frame is computed from what has been drawn, order decides what survives. Anything the picture must contain has to exist before the frame is taken: the large features, and in particular an outlying one that sits well away from the cluster - a back-slope graveyard, a shrine set apart. Only things that drop into the framed space afterward - the wells among the houses, the monk's plots - and the placard itself come later. A hard feature added after the frame is fixed is silently sliced by it, which is how one village's west-set graveyard came to be cut once the surrounding scrub stopped padding the edge.

A provincial city is framed on its wall rather than on its scattered belongings. A town map is as much about its surroundings as about the town; a city map is about the city, and the countryside around it matters little, so the window just barely encompasses the walled place - the rampart, its moat, and the satellites that are the point of being outside (the gate markets, the flophouses, the funerary grounds, the wharf stalls) - with the farmland, the outlying estates and the far rows of a gate market simply running off the edge. The GM settled the margin on 2026-07-23: "the aggressive crop [is] the default for all cities unless I state otherwise", about 105 ft of air past the outermost thing the frame is taken on. The reason the margin is stated rather than left to taste is that the first cities were framed the other way, on the principle that a city map is about the city and little else, and the result cut every irrigation fan outside the wall to a sliver about 138 ft wide. The fans had been drawn all along; they were invisible, and the ring of farmland read as bare through two features of work spent filling ground that was already full. A frame widened by hand to some 750 to 780 ft past the moat proved they were there, and the settled answer is the content crop at the aggressive margin, which shows them without paying for empty ground. The GM's rule for what clips is the same one the villages follow: "it's okay if farms get cut off... the point is to show they're there, not to show the entire field".

A caption never pins the frame (GM, 2026-07-23). Where a caption was the only thing holding a side of the picture open, the answer is to move the caption inward, among the things it names, rather than to pad the crop around it - one city's "samurai estates" caption was moved in among its manors for exactly this reason.

The rule the map follows: at hamlet and village scale the frame is the bounding box of the hard content plus a margin of about 30 ft on a hamlet sheet, 60 ft on a village one; a field contributes its drawn plots and not its keep-out envelope; the commons, the streams, the channels and the lanes clip at the edge; and every hard feature - the houses, the groves, the graveyard, the shrine, the wells, the torii - lies wholly inside the finished window, so a feature placed too late and quietly sliced is caught rather than shipped. No view edge stands more than about 56 ft of hamlet ground beyond the outermost feature the frame was taken on, which leaves the working margins of 30 to 44 ft a little slack and fails an edge held open by something that should have clipped.

The rule the map follows: a town or provincial-city map is framed on the settlement itself - for a walled city the wall, the moat and the satellites kept outside it, plus about 105 ft of margin at the city scale and room above for the placard. The outlying estates, the surrounding farmland and the far rows of a gate market run off the edge and are not drawn whole. A single side may be widened to keep a representative band of farmland on a flank that has no satellite to anchor it. Every check that asks whether something "runs off the edge" reads the cropped window, not the full drawing area, as the edge of the map.
