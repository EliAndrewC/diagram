May one outlying feature make the whole map bigger?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25; this project's census of the shipped maps

Not silently. The previous question asks whether the margin is generous; this one asks the opposite - whether a single element, standing far outside everything else, is by itself forcing a bigger image. Move that one thing inward and the whole map crops tighter, which is worth knowing before shipping a sheet fifteen per cent taller than it needs to be.

The hard part is telling a legitimate outlier from an accident, and the discriminator is the ratio, not the gap - which only measuring the pool revealed. A pond or a forest that pushes the frame out is the outlying content: it is large, and it is meant to be out there. Every legitimate case on the shipped maps has a gap roughly equal to the feature's own size - ponds at 1.03 to 1.35 times their own extent, one hamlet's forest at 1.14. Against that, a city's tanning-yard caption stood some 534 ft past everything else on a box 30 ft tall: 17.8 times its own size. So the test is that the gap must exceed both a floor of about 60 ft of hamlet or town ground and three times the feature's own extent - "it drags the frame further than its own size". A flat distance threshold, which is the obvious first guess, would have condemned every hamlet's source tameike.

The test reads the same list of contributors the frame itself was computed from, rather than a second list written to resemble it. The two crops deliberately take different sets - a city frames on its moat ring, its satellites and its captions while its paddy fans and farmhouses clip - so the one function answers for both, and a check that re-derived what the generator had already computed would drift from it feature by feature. Folding both crops onto it was proved to change nothing: all seventeen maps were re-framed and every window came back identical to the byte.

One consequence is worth knowing before chasing a footprint that is not in the set: a city's extramural trade works are framed by their caption, not by the works. The city's contributor list carries every placed caption box but not the tanning yards themselves, which is why one city's frame is set by the words "tanning yard" rather than by the yard. The kiln works left that exclusion on 2026-07-27, and both directions are worth recording: the exclusion was right while a kiln was a mound of about 28 by 18 ft whose caption was wider than it was, and wrong once the kiln became a works of 140 by 120 ft with its own housing - wider than any caption of it, and a satellite whose entire point is where it stands must not clip at the frame.

Where the outlier is legitimate, the map declares it, with the reason, rather than the rule being relaxed. One city is the worked example, and it is a genuine conflict between two rules rather than a slack placement: the tanning yard must stand below every irrigation intake, and the tap channel's corridor blocks everything above a line, so the shallowest legal seat saves about 24 ft of a sheet some 4,200 ft tall and is flagged anyway. The declaration turns silent bloat into a cost somebody chose.

The commonest outlier of all is exempt on physical grounds. A valley-head pond that feeds a field has to stand above the field's water entry, because the feed is gravity; pulling it inside a tighter frame would put it downhill of the field it waters, which is backwards. So it is not relocated - but the cheap part of the slack is still reclaimed with a nudge. A pond only has to contain its own sluice to feed the field, so the drawn water can slide a little, independently of the sluice and the field it serves, until its far edge sits flush with the dry plots instead of poking past them. One village's pond is nudged about 314 ft west, its east edge flush with the dry-field strip, for an image about nine per cent smaller; the poke that remains on its uphill side is the irreducible cost of a valley head and is simply accepted. Re-orienting the whole field to dodge the poke was declined: a mirrored layout only flips which side the pond pokes out of, and it erases the deliberate difference between two neighboring villages, which is worth more than the pixels.

The rule the map follows: no single feature holds the frame open by more than three times its own size, and by more than about 60 ft of hamlet or town ground, unless the map says why. A feature that does is moved inward, or the map records the reason it cannot be - a conflict with another rule, a gravity feed that requires the high ground - as a declaration a reader can be shown, not as a loosened threshold. A pond that sources a field is exempt by construction and is nudged rather than moved: it slides until its far edge is flush with the plots, keeping its sluice inside the water.
