Why is there no legend, no compass and no caption on the obvious?

Sources: the GM's rulings of 2026-07-20; this project's own measurements of the shipped maps

Because a settlement map earns more by being self-evident than by being annotated. The sheet carries the place name and nothing else that explains itself: no key box pairing swatches to meanings, no descriptive line under the title telling the reader this is "an average farming village", and no caption on anything the drawing already says. A reader can count the torii, so the map never states how many there are; it can see that a building stands on a hill, so no subtitle says "atop the hill". Captions are spent on the few things the ink cannot carry - which plots lie fallow, which are tax-free, what a particular building is - and the rest of the sheet is left to be looked at.

There is no compass either. These maps are always drawn north up, so a rose would repeat a fact that never varies while adding a mark that belongs to the map rather than to the place, on a sheet that otherwise shows nothing else; it was removed everywhere. What the sheet does carry, since the GM's ruling of 2026-07-20 and matching the building sheets, is a scale bar drawn directly under the place name: a ruled bar with a tick at each end and one in the middle, the real distance written beneath it, and a line of fine print giving the feet in one drawn unit. The bar is a fixed length on the page rather than a fixed distance on the ground, which is what makes it cheap: the same bar reads 100 ft on a hamlet or town sheet, 200 ft on a village one and 300 ft on a provincial city's, all of them round numbers, so no tier needs the bar tuned by hand.

The name itself is dropped into blank ground. The placard - title and bar together - is seated by scanning the framed window from the top for a text box that clears every feature on the map, so the place name never lies across buildings, fields, water or a grove. The search runs over the final window, after the frame is fixed, or it would seat the name against an edge that then moves.

The rule the map follows: every settlement sheet, at every tier, carries the place name and a scale bar beneath it, and nothing else of that kind - no legend, no key, no compass, no descriptive subtitle, and no caption stating a count or a fact the drawing already shows. The bar's stated distance agrees with the scale the map declares. The placard stands clear of every drawn feature. Caption text renders above all other ink, so no part of a caption is ever covered by something drawn later.

The rule the map follows: a building's subtitle adds information or is dropped. A subtitle that restates the name is cut - a monastery named for its patron needs no "(town monastery)" under it - and so is one that describes what the drawing shows. Subtitles that say something the sheet cannot are kept: that a shrine is still tended, that a compound is a hunting lodge by a named forest. A building is never subtitled with the settlement's own tier: "county seat" names the town, not the building standing in it.
