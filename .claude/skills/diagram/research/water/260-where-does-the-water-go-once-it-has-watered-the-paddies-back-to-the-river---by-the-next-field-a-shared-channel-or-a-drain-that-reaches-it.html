Where does the water go once it has watered the paddies? Back to the river - by the next field, a shared channel or a drain that reaches it

Sources: maff-nogyoyosui-suiden (the three layouts; separation as the consolidation standard; the reuse of return flow), tabayashi-1987 (one network supplying and receiving; separation dated to the postwar projects), jawiki-yosuiro (the supply channel set apart from a channel for drainage alone), asaza-yatsuda (the used water flowing on to the river)

The question. The ditches that feed the paddies and the ditch that collects what runs out of them are both dug channels. Are they one kind of thing, and where does the collected water go?

What the record found. Japanese planning names three layouts of a paddy's water: field-to-field irrigation, in which water passes over the bunds from one plot to the next; the combined type, in which one channel both supplies and receives; and the separated type, with a supply channel above and a drainage channel below.100 The older network did both jobs at once - "the canal network extending widely over the area of paddy fields supplied irrigation water to paddies and received discharge from them"103 - and combined channels were sometimes an old river course, so that reuse of the water happened by itself; where it did not, works were built to return drainage to the supply channel or use it directly, until "within all paddy-irrigation districts a thoroughgoing irrigation-drainage reuse system was completed that lets out no wasted water at all".101 Separating the supply channel from the drainage channel, giving every plot its own inlet and outlet and cutting the drain about a meter below the field, is the standard of field consolidation,102 which came with the postwar projects: "irrigation and drainage canals were separated and arranged along farm roads", in blocks laid out from 1963 "with a road, an irrigation channel, and a drainage channel adjacent to each block".104 Separating them raises the water a district needs, because "residual water is drained directly into the drainage canal and the opportunity for repeated use decreases".107 The language keeps the two apart all the same: a supply channel is a channel made to draw water in, and a channel whose purpose is drainage alone is another thing, not counted among them.105 And the water that leaves the paddies goes on: in a valley paddy "the water, after being used carefully, then flows into the river and eventually into the lake".106

What it means on the map. The two kinds are two things a reader can hover: the irrigation ditch - the head race, the supply canals and the delivery ditches that bring water to the paddies - and the drainage ditch - the collector along the low line and its run onward. A hamlet's drain is a dug channel that reaches a watercourse; the maps never give one a natural brook of its own, which is the map's rule rather than the record's: where the passing brook comes near the collector's outfall the drain joins it, as a district's drainage returned to the river to be taken up below;108 where it does not, the drain runs on as a ditch to the edge of the map or to the pond at the field's foot, which is the map's earlier decision about where that pond stands (the fields record). The ANGLE at which the drain meets the brook is whatever the ground gives it, and nothing here rules on it: the record's canal-junction rule is about an offtake leaving its parent, and what angle a made outfall arriving at a watercourse was cut to was not asked in this pass. The separated layout the map draws - a supply net above, one collector below - is the form the record dates to modern consolidation; the older field-to-field and combined forms are attested and are not drawn, because a hamlet drawn in the field-to-field form would have no ditch between its plots for a reader to hover, and that trade is recorded here as a deliberate one.
