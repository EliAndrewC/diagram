The wet toe is as wide as the FAN, not as wide as the valley

Sources: senjochi-jawiki (the fan's three zones and the spring line at its toe), mlit-senjochi-kurashi (paddy and settlement at the toe, mulberry and orchard at mid-fan), woods-westbrook-2006 (slope wetlands at fan perimeters), kohai-shicchi-jawiki, shizen-teibo-jawiki (backswamp formation and land use), maff-drain-shape, environmental-impact-irrigation-enwiki, soil-salinity-control-enwiki

What prompted it (GM 2026-08-12): a map whose reed toe ran from one edge of the sheet to the other, so that a settlement whose fall pointed at its own frame had no dry exit anywhere - every road out had to turn away over the settlement's back, which contradicts the water-mouth doctrine. The GM asked where that wall-to-wall wetness had been established, and the honest answer was: nowhere - the only stated rule was that the marsh lies downhill of the field. The GM's instinct was to derive the width from what the fan waters, and asked for it to be checked against real farmland first.

What the research found: real wet toes are FEATURE-bounded, not valley-bounded, and two separate landforms say so.

  • The alluvial fan (扇状地), which is the landform our comb fans actually depict. Its three zones are canonical in Japanese physical geography: at the 扇頂 (apex) the gradient is steep; across the 扇央 (mid-fan) the river water sinks into coarse gravels, so streams run dry and the ground is poor for paddy - historically woodland, then mulberry (桑畑), now orchards; and at the 扇端 (toe) that water re-emerges in a spring line, 湧水帯, which is why settlements and paddy have clustered there since antiquity. The decisive point for us is WHERE that line runs: it emerges where the fan's gravels, which pass water freely, give their underflow back as a spring belt at the fan's toe, so it follows the fan's own geometry rather than the width of the valley the fan sits in. The wetland literature says the same from the other direction - slope wetlands form at breaks in slope where groundwater discharges, and are typically found on the perimeter of permeable alluvial fans.
  • And the mechanism is fed by the command area itself. In fan-country paddy districts, river infiltration and seepage from the rice fields are the significant sources of groundwater recharge - the water that emerges below is largely the water the fan put in. Canal and field seepage causing waterlogging below an irrigated command is the same relationship in the modern engineering literature. So the wet ground below a fan is downstream of what the fan waters in a causal sense, not merely a coincidence of elevation.
  • The one landform that could have contradicted it does not. On a floodplain the wet ground is the 後背湿地 (backswamp) - but it is bounded by the 自然堤防 (natural levees) that make it, which is again a feature boundary rather than the valley's width, and in Japan most backswamp was itself converted to paddy rather than left as reed waste. Neither landform gives us wetness from valley wall to valley wall.

The decision: the toe band takes its cross-slope extent from the CULTIVATED extent (the fan's plots plus the dry hem) with a 90 ft shoulder each side for the seepage spreading a little past the watered ground, clamped to the canvas. The shoulder is the same 90 ft the band already used for its uphill overlap.

The departure we are taking knowingly: a real spring line is an ARC following the fan's toe, and ours is a straight contour band with square lateral ends. Curving it would be more faithful; the band is a ground-cover region whose reeds are scattered and feathered to nothing at the margin, so the square end is not visible as an edge.

The GM, on Inashiro (2026-08-26): "I would have expected that the marshland would run parallel to the drainage ditch, but what we actually have is a drainage ditch which runs at an angle ... and then marshland where the demarcation ... appears to run exactly east to west parallel to the edge of the map. that makes it look like a mistake rather than a feature of the geography." The research pass: MAFF's field-drainage design standard places the field-toe interceptor drain (承水路) "nearly parallel to the contours, with a gentle gradient", and the collectors (集水路/排水路) perpendicular to them at 200-300 m spacing (MAFF 排水路の形状・構造と適用条件); the fan-toe spring line is an arc along the fan's foot (above). So in reality the toe drain and the wet edge run TOGETHER, and our collector's drawn grade is steeper than a lowland collector's fall (~0.1% in this page's estimate; the upland-field standard read gives far steeper grades) (convention - a map drawing convention, recorded: the drain is drawn steep so its flow reads; the marsh must not inherit that fiction). The decision on the wet edge: the toe band's inner edge is sampled across the slope - at each station the shoulder above the LOCAL lowest crop point, smoothed over three stations - so the reeds begin at the fan's foot everywhere along the collector.

Two ways the wet edge is set, and the rule for choosing between them. Some maps in the pool draw their toe by hand, with literal coordinates, and deliberately claim a generous region that OVERLAPS the crop - the reeds then fill up to the field's true low edge whatever its shape, because the scatter skips any point standing on a paddy. Kikuta's hand-drawn marsh reaches well above its entire collector, so it claims ground at rice height as wet, and that is invisible only because of the skip. A computed toe takes its inner edge from the cultivated extent instead.

What it means on the maps: dry ground appears at both lateral ends of every toe, and with it every settlement keeps a downslope exit: Ikegami's and Akagahara's roads run south over ground that is dry for their whole length, while Moritono's, whose fall is west and whose straight track would run down the wet side, keeps its diverted course.
