The wet toe is as wide as the FAN, not as wide as the valley

Sources: senjochi-jawiki (the fan's three zones and the spring line at its toe), mlit-senjochi-kurashi (paddy and settlement at the toe, mulberry and orchard at mid-fan), woods-westbrook-2006 (slope wetlands at fan perimeters), tedori-isotope-jagh (paddy water recharging a Japanese fan's groundwater),kohai-shicchi-jawiki, shizen-teibo-jawiki (backswamp formation and land use), environmental-impact-irrigation-enwiki, soil-salinity-control-enwiki

What prompted it (GM 2026-08-12): a map whose reed toe ran from one edge of the sheet to the other, so that a settlement whose fall pointed at its own frame had no dry exit anywhere - every road out had to turn away over the settlement's back, which contradicts the water-mouth doctrine. The GM asked where that wall-to-wall wetness had been established, and the honest answer was: nowhere - the only stated rule was that the marsh lies downhill of the field. The GM's instinct was to derive the width from what the fan waters, and asked for it to be checked against real farmland first.

What the research found: real wet toes are FEATURE-bounded, not valley-bounded, and two separate landforms say so.

  • The alluvial fan (扇状地), which is the landform our comb fans actually depict. Its three zones are canonical in Japanese physical geography: at the 扇頂 (apex) the gradient is steep; across the 扇央 (mid-fan) the river water sinks into coarse gravels, so streams run dry and the ground is poor for paddy - historically woodland, then mulberry (桑畑), now orchards; and at the 扇端 (toe) that water re-emerges in a spring line, 湧水帯, which is why settlements and paddy have clustered there since antiquity. The decisive point for us is WHERE that line runs: it emerges where the fan's gravels, which pass water freely, give their underflow back as a spring line at the fan's toe, so it follows the fan's own geometry rather than the width of the valley the fan sits in. The wetland literature says the same from the other direction - slope wetlands form at breaks in slope where groundwater discharges, and are often found on the perimeter of highly permeable alluvial fans.
  • And the mechanism is fed by the command area itself. A stream sinking into a fan's gravels was the primary source of groundwater on a Rocky Mountain fan studied for it, and on the Tedori fan, a Japanese paddy fan, the water standing on the paddies recharges it beside the river's water - so the water that emerges below is, as this record reads it, in good part the water the fan put in. Deep percolation losses from an irrigation scheme raising the water table until the ground waterlogs is the same relationship in the modern engineering literature. So the wet ground below a fan is downstream of what the fan waters in a causal sense, not merely a coincidence of elevation.
  • The one landform that could have contradicted it does not. On a floodplain the wet ground is the 後背湿地 (backswamp) - but it is bounded by the 自然堤防 (natural levees) that make it, which is again a feature boundary rather than the valley's width, and in Japan backswamp was traditionally cleared for paddy rather than left as reed waste. Neither landform gives us wetness from valley wall to valley wall.

Some low fields sit inside dikes that keep the river out; others lie open at the foot of a valley slope. Whether the wet edge of such a diked field carried different plants from the wet foot of an undiked valley, no page we read compares.

The decision: the toe band takes its cross-slope extent from the CULTIVATED extent (the fan's plots plus the dry hem) with a 90 ft shoulder each side for the seepage spreading a little past the watered ground, stopping at the map's edge. The shoulder is the same 90 ft the band already used for its uphill overlap.

The departure we are taking knowingly: a fan is laid down in a fan shape with the valley mouth as its apex, so a real spring line at its toe is an ARC, and ours is a straight contour band with square lateral ends. Curving it would be more faithful; the band is a ground-cover region whose reeds are scattered and feathered to nothing at the margin, so the square end is not visible as an edge.

Where the toe's inner edge runs against the drain, and whether a reed edge along a drain is older than modern drainage standards, is its own question.

What it means on the maps: dry ground appears at both lateral ends of every toe, and with it every settlement keeps a downslope exit: Ikegami's and Akagahara's roads run south over ground that is dry for their whole length, while Moritono's, whose fall is west and whose straight track would run down the wet side, keeps its diverted course.
