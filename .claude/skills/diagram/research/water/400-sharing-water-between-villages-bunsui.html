Sharing water between villages (bunsui)

Where a ditch forked between two villages, they shared one supply, and the share was set mostly by rule rather than by any structure. The width of each channel and the hours its gate stood open were fixed place by place, and in a dry season the villages took the water in turns. On one canal, dug in 1110 for ten villages and serving 118 by the Edo period, the rules of 1537 fixed a channel's width and depth down to the inch.

The rules were exact because a split is so easily cheated: a slight difference where two channels part, or one stone thrown into the fork, changes each village's share. The village upstream had the advantage, and quarrels over water ran through village life, some of them deadly.

  • In the medieval period the management of water passed to the villages.
    Control passed from the estates to local lords, and the management of the water on to leading farmers and village organizations; in the same period the quarrels between areas over how the water was divided grew fierce.
  • The answer was rotation (bansui).
    The width of each channel and the hours its weir or gate could stand open were fixed area by area, and in seasons and places short of water the flow was measured strictly and each intake adjusted.
  • The village upstream was favored, and the village downstream quarreled with it.
    Even under the rules, the reach upstream, which the split favored, and the disadvantaged reach downstream often came into conflict.
  • A split is easily cheated, because water runs toward ground lower by a few millimeters.
    A slight difference in the cross-section where two channels part changes how much water each one takes, and a single rock thrown into the fork changes it sharply; the village upstream has more water and the advantage.
  • The rules were written down to the inch.
    The Juugou canal off the Kuzuryu River was dug in 1110 for the ten villages of one estate. Its regulations of 1537 fixed the channel at 6.1 shaku wide and 3.21 shaku deep, under supervising officials, and by the Edo period the canal served 118 villages.
  • Water disputes could kill.
    On one river, the disputes that remain in the record alone number 36, and some were fierce enough to cause deaths.
  • In a drought, villages took the water in fixed turns.
    In one district the whole area is split in two in a drought, the three upstream main canals against the four downstream, and each is given the water for 24 or 48 hours at a time. Records from 1666 give one village three days, another two and three more one day each, the cycle then repeated. Ponds, too, were shared in turns when the water ran low.
  • Farmers elected water heads (mizugashira) to run the flow.
    In one district four of them, chosen from among the farmers who owned at least a set area of land, manage the water.
  • A turn could be timed by burning incense sticks (senkomizu).
    The length of a turn was measured by the time a fixed number of incense sticks took to burn. It was found where, in a severe drought, some paddies were barred from rice and their farmers made to plant dry-field crops.
  • An upstream village could turn its position into authority over a shared pond.
    One village gradually enlarged its authority over a pond by its advantage of lying farthest upstream.
  • A village could trade the work of managing a pond for the right to its water.
    A village lying far from the pond asked the upstream village to manage it, in exchange for the right to draw water from it, and the arrangement has lasted to the present.
  • Inside one village, the smallest ditches were kept by the farmers they served.
    A 1987 survey found the smallest ditches run by small groups of farmers and counted as part of the paddies they fed. Under rotation the head of the village directed the water to every paddy.

At the split itself, the record shows four kinds of work, from a few sandbags to a pavilion of iron pillars.

  • Some splits were a mouth cut to an agreed width, rebuilt by its users every year.
    On one Nagano canal two quarreling villages set their split each spring by unsealing two wisteria vines of different lengths, cutting measuring sticks to them, and building water mouths of sandbags to those widths at a pool on the channel. The custom lasted until a circular divider replaced it in 1953.
  • Some splits were set by a graduated stake (bungi).
    Where one channel was divided between two areas or villages, a marked post was set at the division point, and the split was made by its height.
  • Some splits were a proportional flume.
    At Kuji, on the Nikaryo canal, a flume built in the mid-Edo period divided the canal four ways in proportion to the cultivated land of each district, and the quarrels went on.
  • In China, one split between two counties was made by counted openings.
    At Huo Spring in Shanxi, iron pillars under a pavilion part the outflow into ten openings, three to the southern county and seven to the northern, and a stele records the split with a diagram on its back. That is a split between two counties, far above a village ditch.
  • The round concrete divider that splits a canal exactly is modern.
    Dividers able to share water accurately were devised from the Taisho era, and the first round divider was built in 1914.

    No page we read gives the size of either.
