Which way water flows, and how channels bend and join

Water runs downhill, and in a farming landscape most of it runs in channels someone dug. A ditch may be laid across a slope rather than down it, to catch the water running off the ground above or to carry water along a hillside at a gentle fall, and a canal for boats is held level, joined to water at another level by a gated lock. Before the early modern period Japan's irrigation ditches were mostly earth dug out and trodden firm, and an earth channel turns on a sweep rather than a sharp corner.

Where one channel leaves another, modern engineering points the new channel downstream: a canal leaves a river at 30 to 45 degrees to its flow, a branch leaves a canal at 60 to 80. The Edo-period weir that slanted with the river put its intake on the downstream side, the same lean, but no source from before modern times gives an angle. Where two channels had to pass at different levels, the builders carried one over on an aqueduct or under in an inverted siphon.

  • A ditch dug along a slope is deliberate work, not a mistake.
    A contour canal is dug to follow closely the contour line of the land it crosses, and a trench dug along the contour, across the slope, holds the water running down the hill. The drainage standard quoted at The wet toe is as wide as the FAN lays a field-toe drain nearly along the contours for the same reason.
  • A canal for boats is held level.
    Canals need to be level, and where one meets a river or the sea at another level, a gated chamber, the pound lock, raises or lowers the water between them. The double-gated pound lock dates from tenth-century China. So a cargo canal has no downstream: a barge is poled both ways along it, and a dead-end dock basin has no through-flow at all - this project's reading of a level cut.

A channel that changes direction has to bend, and how it bends depends on what it is made of.

  • An earth ditch turns on a sweep, not a sharp corner.
    Water scours the outside of a bend and lays silt on its inside, so this project reads a sharp corner cut in earth as one the water would round off in time. Sharp corners belong to stone-lined work - an aqueduct, a sluice box, a revetted canal - and Japan's ditches before the early modern period were mostly dug earth, trodden firm.
  • How tight is the sweep of a bend?
    A meandering river turns on a radius of two to three times its own width. That a dug farm ditch turns no tighter is this project's own step from a river's bend to a dug one, a GUESS, not the article's; on that reading the sweep grows with the channel, wide for a wide canal and tight for a narrow ditch.
  • Chinese canal design today follows the large bends and straightens the small ones.
    A Jiangsu design guideline tells field canals in hill country to follow the slope: "let the large bends take the lie of the land, and straighten the small bends" - an alignment of sweeps and straight runs with no corner anywhere. The guideline is modern, and cannot say how old the rule is.

Where a channel leaves a river or another channel, the angle of the join governs how much silt it draws in and how smoothly the water turns into it. Almost everything known of that angle comes from modern engineering.

  • At what angle does a modern engineer take a canal off a river?
    At 30 to 45 degrees to the river's flow, pointing downstream. An Indian open-university course unit on silt control calls a canal leaving at 90 degrees "the most objectionable orientation", and recommends the acute angle so that the flow lines curve into the regulator instead of striking it. For a branch leaving a canal, the same unit prescribes 60 to 80 degrees to the parent's center line, in the direction of flow, to keep excess silt out. The near-square offtake belongs to a canal's branch, not to a headworks on a river.
  • Modern engineering sites an intake to skim the cleaner upper water.
    The slow lower layers of a flow, heavy with sediment, are the ones a diversion draws most easily. So the same unit puts the intake on the outer bank of a bend, toward its downstream end, because a bend sweeps its bottom layers to the inside, and closes the mouth with a raised sill and a skimmer wall against bedload and floating matter.
  • Is there a best angle for an intake?
    The engineers disagree. A laboratory-channel study found that the share of sediment an intake draws falls as it is turned toward the main flow, to its least at about 16 degrees off it. It takes the large share a right-angle intake draws from the earlier work it reviews, and cites two early investigators who held that there is no best angle, since it moves with the share of the flow diverted and with where the intake stands. And a headworks rule of 90 to 120 degrees is measured from the weir's axis, not from the river's own line.
  • What does the angle do where a channel rejoins a river?
    A numerical study of a confluence in a river bend found that widening the angle at which the side channel enters enlarges the zone of separated flow in the junction, and raises the greatest bed shear stress there by 59% between 30 and 115 degrees. By those figures a small angle, pointing downstream, disturbs the junction less than a large one. That the water behind a wide mouth goes slack, and that the far bank is attacked, is this project's extension of the study, which is of a bend and reports neither.
  • Do tributaries and drainage ditches join a river pointing downstream?
    This project's reading is that natural tributaries curve to join pointing downstream, and that dug drainage returns were cut to do the same - a GUESS. The articles on confluences and tributaries say nothing of the angle, and nothing we read says that drainage returns were cut to point downstream.

The older record shows the lean, and where an intake was put, but not the angle.

  • The Edo-period weir that slanted with the river put its intake on the downstream side.
    Weirs in the Edo period were classed by the main direction of the river's flow and the weir's angle to it, and the kind built slanting with the flow had its intake "on the side toward which the water flows". This project reads that as the same downstream lean the modern figures give: the water reaches the intake travelling along the weir with the current, not across it. Slanting weirs were the common kind before the Meiji period.
  • Did anyone set the angle before modern engineering?
    No source we read does. Nothing we read from before 1868 in Japan, or before the end of the Qing in China, gives the angle - in degrees or as a rule - at which a canal left its river, a ditch its parent, or a moat or an intake met a river.
  • The intake of the Tamagawa aqueduct is thought to have been sited where the river's water gathers toward its bank.
    The aqueduct that carried water to Edo was built in 1653. Its intake at Hamura is thought to have been placed there because the ground is high, and because the Tama river strikes the ridge of the Kusabana hills on its south bank, so that the water gathers toward the opposite bank. At the weir today, gabions, frames and a throw-across weir guide the river's natural flow into the intake; the city's page does not say the weir is as it was in the Edo period.

A castle moat fed from a river joins it at two ends, but it is not a canal.

  • A castle moat is defined by the water it holds.
    A Japanese dictionary defines a moat as ground dug around a castle and filled with water, to keep out an enemy. Xi'an's moat held water the year round, and a Japanese castle moat could draw its water in from a river.
  • Did a moat want a current through it?
    This project's reading, a GUESS, is that a moat wants its water level more than a flow: its job is to stay full, not to pass water, so it has no need of the mouth an irrigation canal cuts to catch the current. No work we read draws that distinction. The one moat we read of whose water moved was Shanghai's, which rose and fell with the tides of the Huangpu River.
  • A moat that silted up was dredged.
    Shanghai's moat silted up in the Qianlong reign, and the county magistrate ordered it dredged so that boats could pass.

Two channels that meet need not share their water.

  • Where two channels had to cross at different levels, the builders put in an aqueduct or an inverted siphon.
    Where an open irrigation or drainage channel crosses a river and the banks differ much in height, the water is usually passed under the river by gravity in an inverted siphon, a method found on old canals such as the Shibayama siphon of the Minumadai canal. An aqueduct bridge carries water across a river or a valley instead. Short of such a structure, the ditches of a rice plain join rather than cross: one feeds another or is fed by it.
