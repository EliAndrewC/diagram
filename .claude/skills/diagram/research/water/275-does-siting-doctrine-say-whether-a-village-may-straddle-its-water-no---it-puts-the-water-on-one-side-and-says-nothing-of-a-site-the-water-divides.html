What the siting doctrine adds, and its limits. The four-gods formulation wants "flowing water on the left" of a site, with hills behind and a pond in front; a Chinese village-landscape study describes Shanggantang as "mountains on three sides, water on one side"; and a survey of Shaanxi villages describes one village with a stream on each side, east and west, that "flow around and past the village". None of the three passages contemplates a site divided by its water, and no siting text read states a rule either way (searched 2026-09-12: the Japanese and Chinese village-siting literature listed above). Its limit: this is a general siting formula and village-landscape scholarship, not a rule anyone states for a farming hamlet's brook. One trap is worth naming: a LINEAR settlement is one whose buildings, in most cases, are "lined up on both sides of a single street", and Yajima Jinkichi's definition of the farming linear village allows houses "on one side of it or on both" - but the thing with two sides there is a street (the Japanese article lets a linear settlement follow "a road, a river or a canal", yet gives its two sides only to a street), and neither says a settlement straddles a stream.
