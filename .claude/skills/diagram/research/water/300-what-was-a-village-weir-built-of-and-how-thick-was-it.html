What was a village weir built of, and how thick was it?

Sources: suido-ishizue-iseki (the first weirs on small streams; the grass weir; the stake-and-log weir of the Kodera site), hrr-agagawa-dento (Edo-period weirs of timber frames and stone; what a frame was), kotobank-jakago (the gabion and its size)

The question. On a hamlet that builds a weir, the maps draw a bar of stone-packed timber crib across the brook at the intake, five feet thick (above, where the crib and its oblique line are sourced). What else a weir on water this small was built of, and how thick it stood, decides what that bar should look like.

The weir on small water was built of what lay to hand. "The first weirs, such as ones that held the river back with nearby wood and stone, were fragile, so they could be built only on mountain streams with small flow or on small tributaries" - which is to say that the small stream is where the simplest weir belongs. Four forms are read:

  • Stakes and woven reed. The grass weir: "where an agreement with downstream users prevents building a permanent weir, a simple weir of driven stakes with kaya woven between them is still used today". It is a fence across the water, as thick as its row of stakes.
  • Stakes and logs packed with clay. A weir excavated at the Kodera site in Matsuyama was stakes driven at a slant with logs laid along them and more stakes driven to hold the logs, built up into A-frames, tied with wisteria vine, its gaps "packed with clay, gravel and the like and held with woven grass".
  • Timber frames weighted with stone. Edo-period weirs were built of timber frames, of stone and of reed facing, the square frame being "timber joined with mortise and tenon ... set in the river and fixed by throwing stones in from above". That passage is about the diversion weirs of large rivers, but it is the same crib of timber and stone the record already gives for the old village intakes.
  • Gabions. A gabion is "a cylindrical basket woven of bamboo, wire or the like, packed with cobbles or rubble", and "the baskets are mostly about 40-60 cm in diameter and 4-10 m long". The word is in the Japanese-Portuguese dictionary of 1603.

How thick. No source read gives the thickness or cross-section of a village weir; the only dimensions read are of river works, frames many meters long. What the forms do fix is a range: a stake-and-reed weir is a fence, as thick as one row of stakes; a single course of gabions is one basket, about 1.3 to 2 ft; a crib is as thick as its frame, which at village scale is not recorded.

What this means for the maps. A weir on a hamlet's brook was built in more than one way, so the weir's form is a knob, rolled per settlement among the four above, and each is drawn at its own thickness: the gabion course at the basket's read diameter, about 2 ft; the stake fence at the finest width the map can draw, since its real thickness is below it (a convention); the stake-and-log frame and the stone-packed crib at 5 ft, which is a guess, since no village figure was found. The even chance among the four is a guess as well. The drawn length, a full closure where half closures were common, stays the convention recorded above.

The rule the map follows: a weir hamlet's weir takes one of four forms, rolled per map with an even chance - a fence of stakes and woven reed, a frame of stakes and logs packed with clay, a crib of timber packed with stone, or a course of stone-filled baskets - drawn about 1.5 ft thick for the fence, about 2 ft for the baskets, and 5 ft for the frame and the crib.
