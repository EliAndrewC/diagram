Lifting water onto fields: treadle wheels and chain pumps (fumiguruma)

Most paddies took their water by gravity, from a ditch that left a stream above them. Where the water lay below the field a farmer had to lift it, and in Japan that was seldom needed: the paddies were generally watered easily by channels or simple weirs. Where it was, a farmer carried a treadle wheel (fumiguruma) out to the paddy's edge and trod it, lifting the water about a yard at most.

China lifted far more. Its chain pump, trodden by people or turned by an ox, raised water from a canal onto the fields, and by a river a noria turned by the current watered a hundred mu day and night.

  • Japanese paddies depended little on lifting.
    They were generally watered by channels or a simple weir. The chain pump of the seventeenth century was hard to make and did not spread, and the treadle wheel that took its place spread only in the reclaimed lowlands and new-field districts downstream, where new channels were hard to dig.
  • The chain pump came to Japan from China, and the treadle wheel drove it out.
    The "dragon-bone wheel" is said to have been invented in China. Two people facing each other trod it round; it was used mainly around the Kinki region early in the Edo period, and was driven out by the spread of the treadle wheel.
  • The treadle wheel was made in Osaka in the 1660s, early in the Edo period, and had spread across Japan by the late eighteenth century.
    An agricultural tool book of 1822 says it was made in the Kanbun era (1661-72) by two craftsmen of Osaka, and had spread through the provinces by 1751-1780.
  • The one-person wheel came in three sizes.
    It was made 4.5, 5 and 5.5 shaku across, beside a larger two-person wheel and a small hand-turned one.
  • A treadle wheel was carried to the field, not fixed in place.
    Used almost only for paddies, it was taken wherever it was needed. It lifted water about 1 m (~3 ft) or less from the ditch to the paddy, and for a higher paddy two or three were set in a row. One person worked it, where the chain pump had needed two.
  • A surviving wheel in Settsu is about 5 ft across.
    It measures 164 cm (~5 ft) across with 17 blades, lifts the water a few tens of centimeters (about a foot or two), and is light enough to be taken apart and carried to the paddy by one person. For draining floodwater, two-person wheels were used, and a rig of three tiers trodden by six people survived there until after the war.
  • Without a wheel, two people swung the water up in a bucket.
    In regions and on farms with no treadle wheel, a bucket hung on two pairs of ropes was swung by two people to scoop the ditch water up into the paddy.

In China the same work was older and done on a larger scale.

  • The Chinese chain pump goes back to about the Eastern Han.
    It improved and spread further in the Song dynasty. It suits the plains, lifting water about 1 to 2 m (~3-7 ft), or straight from a supply canal onto the fields, by human or animal power.
  • By a river, a noria watered a hundred mu day and night.
    A Ming encyclopedia of technology describes a weir turning the current beneath the wheel, which fills its tubes and tips them into a trough that runs to the fields.
  • At a still pond, a chain pump was turned by an ox or trodden by several people.
    The same encyclopedia gives the pump's body as two zhang long at most and half that at least.
  • How much ground could one pump water in a day?
    The encyclopedia reckons a person's full day at 5 mu and an ox's at twice that; a small hand pump a few chi long waters 2 mu a day, and the well-sweep and the windlass less still.
  • How many lifting devices did a village own?
    No page we read counts them.
