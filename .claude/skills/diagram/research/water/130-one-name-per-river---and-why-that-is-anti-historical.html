One name per river - and why that is anti-historical

Sources: chang-jiang-jawiki, shinano-gawa-jawiki, sumida-gawa-jawiki, chang-jiang-zhwiki, kasenho-jawiki, yodogawa-jawiki

What the research found: one-river-many-names was the NORM in the pre-modern world, in both reference cultures. China: the Yangtze has no single name along its course, which this page reads as holding before the modern era too (the pages quoted do not date most reach names) - its course runs Tuotuo He / Tongtian He / Jinsha Jiang, then regionally Chuan Jiang (Sichuan) / Jing Jiang (Hubei) / Xunyang Jiang / Yangzi Jiang (the reach below Nanjing, named for a ferry crossing; the name came to be used abroad for the whole river). Japan: per-stretch renaming was standard practice - the Chikuma-gawa becomes the Shinano-gawa at the Nagano/Niigata prefectural border, the Seta/Uji/Yodo system renames at a prefectural border and again at a confluence, and within Edo itself one river carried different names reach by reach (Miyato-gawa around Asakusa, also Asakusa-gawa, Okawa below Azuma Bridge, Sumida-gawa, all the same water). The mechanism: pre-modern river names were LOCAL (this page's reading of the cases above) - people named the reach they lived on and used, and nobody needed a whole-course name because nobody administered or traveled the whole course; names broke at both political borders and confluences. One-name-per-river is a modern bureaucratic artifact (Japan's first River Law of 1896, and the River Law in force from 1965, under which the Sumida-gawa's reach was renamed by statute; the mapping agencies and the People's Republic of China's choice of Chang Jiang are this page's reading).
