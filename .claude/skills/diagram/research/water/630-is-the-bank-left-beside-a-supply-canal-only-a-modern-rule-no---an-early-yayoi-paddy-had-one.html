Is the bank left beside a supply canal only a modern rule? No - an early Yayoi paddy had one

Sources: hattori-site-yayoiken (an early paddy's wide bank bund along its canal, and its small bunds), zhouli-zhushu-suiren (a path on the bank of every grade of field channel), tamagawa-josui-jawiki (a protected strip on each side of an Edo aqueduct), kotobank-azebiki (the Edo survey's width for a bund)

The question. Where dry plots lie beside a supply canal, the maps leave a bare bank about 5 ft wide between the water and the first plot, and they draw a paddy's bund 1.5 ft wide (What drawing at true size left open). The bank's width was set from a modern Chinese design guideline's minimum bank top, and the bund's was checked against a modern prefectural drawing. Under the GM's ruling of 2026-09-28 that nothing attested only in modern times is drawn, do the bank and the bund have a record from before modern times?

What the record found. Both do.

  • A walkable bank along the canal. At the Hattori site on the Yasu River, an excavated paddy from the first years of the Yayoi period, the large bunds were 80-150 cm wide (about 2.6 to 4.9 ft) with flat tops, one of them ran along the central canal, and they seem to have served as paths for the farm work. The Rites of Zhou put a path on the bank of every grade of field channel, from the smallest up, and its Tang sub-commentary says the path on the smallest takes no cart but oxen, horses and people on foot. In Edo Japan a notice board of 1739 forbade cutting the trees, the undergrowth or the grass within 3 ken (about 18 ft) on each side of the Tamagawa aqueduct canal, a strip kept from cutting along the water.
  • The bund's width. The same Yayoi site's small bunds, the ones between plots, were 20-60 cm wide (about 0.7 to 2 ft) and not meant to be walked on, and an Edo land survey reckoned a bund at one shaku, about 1 ft.

The limits are plain. The Yayoi paddy is one site of tiny plots, far older than the setting, and its bank's use as a path is the excavators' "seems". The classical paths are part of an ideal scheme, said to carry carts and walkers to the capital, and the Rites give the smallest path no width. The Edo strip belongs to a city aqueduct, not a farm canal, and it is a strip of trees and grass kept from cutting, not a bare bank top, so it shows a margin kept along the water rather than the bare bank the maps draw. No record from before modern times was found that gives the width of the bank along a village's own supply canal.

What this means for the maps. The bank stays: a strip of walkable earth beside the canal is as old as the early Yayoi paddies of Japan and as old as the classical Chinese field layout. The premodern figure it is calibrated to is the Yayoi bank bund's 2.6 to 4.9 ft; the drawn 5 ft is at the top of that range. The bund's 1.5 ft lies inside the Yayoi small bunds' 0.7 to 2 ft and near the Edo survey's 1 ft, so a narrower bund, if one is ever wanted, stays inside the premodern range as well.
