River names: one river, many names

In the cases we read, a river did not have one name from its source to the sea. In China and in Japan alike, a river changed its name at a province border or where another river joined it: the Yangtze has a string of names along its course, and the river that runs through Edo had several within the one city. That this held for rivers generally, and not just for the ones traced below, is this project's reading, a GUESS.

A single name for a whole river looks, in this project's reading, like a modern habit that came with national river laws.

  • The Yangtze changes its name reach by reach.
    Its headwater is the Tuotuo He (he and jiang are both Chinese for river), which becomes the Tongtian He where another stream joins it, and its upper course is the Jinsha Jiang. Through Sichuan it is popularly called the Chuan Jiang, and through the old province of Jingzhou, in Hubei, the Jing Jiang. In Jiangxi it is also called the Xunyang Jiang, in Anhui the Wan Jiang, and below Nanjing the Yangzi Jiang.
  • "Yangtze" is the name of one reach, used abroad for the whole river.
    The reach below Nanjing, the Yangzi Jiang, took its name from a ferry crossing, or in the same page's other account a bridge, and outside China the name came to be used for the whole river.
  • In Japan, the Shinano is the Chikuma upstream.
    The river is called the Shinano in Niigata, and going upstream across the border into Nagano its name changes to the Chikuma.
  • The Shinano was named for the province its water came from.
    In old times it was called the Okawa, "the big river", and only later did its lower reaches come to be called the Shinano, as the river that flowed down from Shinano Province.
  • The Yodo has three names along its course.
    It leaves Lake Biwa as the Seta, becomes the Uji about where it enters Kyoto Prefecture, and is the Yodo only below the place where the Katsura and the Kizu join it. One change comes at a border, the other at a confluence.
  • Within Edo, one river carried several names.
    An edict of 835 records it as the Sumida, and it was also called the Miyato-gawa (gawa, river), its name around Asakusa, and the Asakusa-gawa; in the Edo period the stretch below Azuma Bridge was also called the Okawa.
  • How old are the Yangtze's reach names?
    The pages we read do not date most of them. That they held before the modern era, as the Japanese names above did, is this project's reading of the cases, a GUESS.
  • Why did a river have many names?
    That the names were local is this project's reading of these cases, a GUESS: the Shinano's lower reaches were named for the province the water came from, and the names broke at borders and at confluences. That no one needed a name for the whole course, because no one governed or traveled the whole of it, is this project's reading of these cases, a GUESS.
  • Is one name per river a modern habit?
    Japan's first River Law was passed in 1896. Under the River Law in force from 1965, a new floodway became the main course of the Arakawa, and the river's old channel below the Iwabuchi sluice was renamed the Sumida by statute, one name for the whole channel. That such laws are what gave rivers one name end to end is this project's reading, a GUESS.
