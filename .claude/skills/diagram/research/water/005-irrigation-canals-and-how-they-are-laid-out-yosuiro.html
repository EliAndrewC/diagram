Irrigation canals and how they are laid out (yosuiro)

An irrigation canal carried water by gravity from its source - a river, or a reservoir pond (tameike) set above the fields - down a head race that split at division points into smaller and smaller canals, the main canal narrowing as each branch drew off its share. The smallest ditches, which brought the water to the paddies' inlets, were looked on as part of the paddies they served, and in the old form the water went on from one paddy to the next rather than down a ditch to each.

Because water runs only downhill, a canal waters only the ground below it, so canals were laid on the high ground and the water left the fields by a drain along the lowest line. When the Minuma reservoir was reclaimed in 1728, in the Edo period, its supply canals were set along the edge of the old reservoir and a drainage channel down its center. On flat ground each smaller canal left the one that fed it at right angles, a rule as old as the classical Chinese commentaries and Japan's ancient land grid.

  • A reservoir pond sits above the fields it waters.
    A Japanese irrigation pond is set higher than the paddies it serves, so that its water reaches them by gravity. One kind stands at the head of a valley, made by closing the valley mouth with an earthen dike. More on the ponds themselves is at the pond's outlet and the canals it feeds.
  • How did the water leave a pond?
    Through intake holes in an inclined pipe (shahi) laid along the dam's upstream face, which lead the water down to a bottom pipe (sokohi) through the base of the dam; a separate works passes the inflow of heavy rain safely downstream, for the pond's own safety. That the intake is the pond's one outlet to the fields, and that the flood works plays no part in sharing the water out, is this project's reading of that description, a GUESS: the page does not itself say so.
  • A large pond could feed smaller ponds below it.
    Japanese pond irrigation includes parent-child systems, in which a large pond distributes water to smaller ponds. Manno-ike in northeast Shikoku, the largest irrigation pond in Japan, serves some 4,600 ha (~11,000 acres), and some 60 child ponds took water from it. The Japanese names parent pond and child pond (oyaike, koike) are this project's, not the source's.
  • In China, a long canal linking many ponds is called a melon-on-the-vine system.
    The name is literally "long vine bearing melons": the canal's head is likened to the root, the canals to the vine, and the reservoirs and ponds along them to melons on the vine.
  • Such systems go back to the Warring States period.
    The Baiqi canal of the Warring States period is named as the earliest melon-on-the-vine system.
  • Small pond systems once watered over a third of China's irrigated land.
    The Chinese beitang system of small ponds is described as more than 2,500 years old, and in the 1950s some 8.3 million such systems served 39% of China's irrigated land. In this project's reading they are the closest Chinese counterpart of the Japanese reservoir pond.

From its source the water went down a branching network, much like a tree.

  • A main canal narrows as its branches draw off water.
    In the large river systems the main canals gradually decrease in size as they are tapped by branch canals, and a part of one branch may serve most of a village's land. The canal networks of the large ponds are long and widespread, like those of the rivers.
  • The smallest ditches were often counted as part of the paddies they served.
    They were run by small groups of farmers, and often considered part of the paddy fields they brought water to.
  • In the old form, water passed from paddy to paddy (tagoshi kangai).
    Modern paddies draw water from a supply canal and let it out into a drain, while a hill village in Oita still keeps the old irrigation that passes water from one paddy to the next.
  • Was the old network of ditches sparse?
    Where water passed from paddy to paddy, a village needed fewer ditches, and this project reads the old network as sparse, a village digging no more than it needed - a GUESS. The pages we read describe the paddy-to-paddy flow without counting the ditches.
  • When did a ditch beside every paddy come in?
    With the land reorganization of the Meiji era, on this project's reading: a ditch to fill every paddy and another to empty it (yohaisui bunri, "separate supply and drainage", a name this project uses) is a form of that time. The Ishikawa method of 1887 squared the paddy parcels so that every one of them adjoined a branch road and a supply or drainage channel, and the land reorganization law of 1899 set out to regularize field shapes and build irrigation and drainage works, though it does not say in so many words a ditch to every paddy. The Edo sources we read describe trunk canals and drains, and none of them a supply ditch reaching every plot with its own outlet to a drain.

Keeping the water that came in apart from the water that went out was an Edo-period change on the Kanto plain.

  • The older Kanto school ran irrigation and drainage in the same channels.
    Under it, securing water for the villages downstream always made drainage difficult upstream, and as new land was opened upstream and downstream came into conflict.
  • The Kishu school separated the drains from the supply.
    The shogun Yoshimune summoned its engineer Izawa Yasobei from Kishu in 1722 to open new fields, and his school built large canals drawing straight from the major rivers and separated the drainage rivers. Izawa led the building of the Minuma-dai canal on the shogun's orders.
  • The Kishu school was a method of river engineering.
    It is described as river training - high continuous dikes, straightened meanders, more intakes on the major rivers and wider irrigation canals - with the Minuma-dai canal among its achievements.
  • At Minuma, the supply ran in two canals along the edges and the drain down the middle.
    The reclamation of 1728 set supply canals along the rim of the old reservoir and a drainage channel down its center. The two were the east-edge and west-edge canals, dug from the reservoir after it was made an irrigation reservoir in 1629, and they flow along the rim of the plateau, while the Shiba River runs down the center of the lowland. So water could enter the paddies from both sides, on this project's reading.
  • How cleanly were supply and drain kept apart?
    At the scale of the trunk canals, cleanly. A historian of the district finds its irrigation and drainage basically separated after Minuma, though, as the same study adds, not everything was neatly separated.
  • A drain's water was used again downstream.
    The surplus water of the Minuma-dai canal ran out to the old Tone River in the low ground and became the source of the Kasai canal. At the Konuma marsh, reclaimed from 1729, the drainage was gathered down the center and used as irrigation water by the villages below.

The right angle between a canal and the smaller one it feeds is far older than any modern standard, in China and in Japan alike.

  • The classical Chinese commentaries lay each smaller channel across the one that feeds it.
    The Rites of Zhou lays the countryside out as a ladder of channels, and its standard commentary, by Zheng Xuan of the Han dynasty, says that with the fields running south the smallest channel runs lengthwise and the next crosswise, and so on up the ladder. The sub-commentary of Jia Gongyan, of the Tang dynasty, spells it out: a crosswise channel at the head of every ten allotments, a north-south one on the east side of every ten of those, a crosswise one on the south side of every ten of those.
  • The Lake Tai polders of the Song dynasty were ruled by crossing channels.
    On the Lake Tai plain of the lower Yangtze, in the Five Dynasties and the Song, the polder country took the shape of crosswise and lengthwise channels, one every few li each way.
  • Japan's ancient jori grid put a road and a waterway beside every square.
    The jori land division is thought to have ruled the plains into squares of one cho, about 109 m (~358 ft), each beside a road and a waterway. The squares were grouped six by six, and each was cut into ten equal tan. The grid ran north-south and east-west but was tilted in many regions, and where the ground tilted, as in the Omi basin, its axes ran with the main slope and at right angles to it.
  • The jori grid goes back to the eighth century.
    Land described by it first appears in 743, in the Nara period, and it was at its height around the ninth century, when it is thought to have spread across all the plains.
  • Excavated Heian paddies sit inside a right-angled frame.
    Paddies of the Heian period dug up in Gunma lie inside large bunds and waterways facing east-west and north-south, in plots 5 to 20 m (~16-66 ft) on a side, though many of the small plots inside are out of true.
  • Where did the jori grid survive?
    In western Japan its regular parcels are widespread - so widespread that later land readjustment was seldom needed there. That a long-settled plains village there would show semi-regular gridded paddies, not an irregular patchwork, is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • A modern Chinese guideline makes the high-ground siting and the right angle design law.
    It puts a canal of every grade, down to the field canal, on the higher ground of the area it commands, so that gravity irrigation works at all. Its main and branch canals run along the contours or the watershed divides, and the laterals below them across the contours. On a flat polder plain it sets each field canal at right angles to the one that feeds it. The wording is modern, but the Edo reclamations and the older record above follow the same rules.
  • How closely did real fields follow the right angle?
    Only loosely, as far as the record shows. The classical rule is an ideal and says nothing of how far real fields kept to it; the Lake Tai channels are district waterways a few li apart, not a village's ditches; and the jori grid is a frame of 109 m (~358 ft) squares, inside which the smaller plots and ditches went their own way. What the three share is that on flat ground a channel leaves the one that feeds it square.

On an alluvial fan, where a river spreads out of the hills, the canals took a different shape.

  • Whole alluvial fans were irrigated for rice.
    The Tedori fan on the Sea of Japan coast, some 150 square kilometers (~58 square miles), is almost completely covered with paddy fields, and the irrigation system on it is given as representative of its type. The Isawa fan, about 20,000 ha (~49,000 acres) and one of the largest in Japan, was opened for fields by Goto Juan, who drew water from the Isawa River by order of the Sendai domain; the canals of his weir came to some 43 km (~27 miles).
  • Did a fan's canals radiate from its head?
    This project reads them so: gently descending canals leaving the river where it comes out of the valley and spreading out over the fan, as the Isawa canals spread over their plain. That they leave at the fan's head and radiate, and the likeness to China's Dujiangyan, are a GUESS; the pages we read give the fans and their canals, not the canals' plan.
