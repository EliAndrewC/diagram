Villages beside their stream: one bank or both

Whether a settlement's houses, a farming hamlet's among them, stand on one bank of its water or on both depends on the size of the water. Against a river the record shows a settlement on one bank, and the river could be a boundary between villages. A settlement's own small channel runs through the middle of the place. Between the two, at the size of a stream, both forms occur: villages built along both banks, and villages beside the water.

The siting doctrine of China and Japan, which says where a good site has its hills and its water, puts the water on one side of a site, or on each side, and says nothing of a site the water divides. At the scale of one farm, nothing we read sets a household's own garden or storehouse across a channel from its house.

  • Harie, in Shiga, stands on one bank of the Ado, its region's largest river.
    Harie lies on the left bank of the alluvial fan at the lower reaches of the Ado River, the largest river of its region.
  • A hamlet on a river terrace stood on one bank, and called the hill across the river "the facing mountain".
    A settlement-geography study of the hamlet records the hill behind it, on its own side of the river, as the Urayama, "the back mountain", and the hill across the river as the Mukoyama, "the facing mountain". The river ran past the hamlet, not through it.
  • On the Tenryu, the boundary between villages ran wherever the river's main current flowed.
    It was a moving boundary, and an Edo-period suit over the river's islands, heard in 1739, was fought between villages lying on opposite banks.
  • At Harie, the settlement's own stream runs through the middle of the place.
    The same page that puts Harie on one bank of the Ado says the Harie Okawa flows through roughly the center of the district. The waterways within the settlement feed it, and an account of the kabata describes the channel that flows alongside the house.
  • Was Harie's channel through the middle an oddity?
    Probably not. Harie's spring water has been selected as a national Important Cultural Landscape, together with the neighboring Shimofuri district, which suggests a form that is valued.
  • Harie's channels are known from three accounts of the one place, and none of them dates the channels.
    The district's own site, the encyclopedia article and a magazine visit are three accounts of Harie rather than three settlements, and none of them dates the layout of the channels.

Four villages in the hills of southern China show both forms at the scale of a stream.

  • Two villages of one county in Anhui show both forms: Xidi built between its streams, Hongcun beside one.
    The World Heritage listing of the two villages, in southern Anhui, sets them side by side: Xidi built along and between three streams that converge to its south, Hongcun at the foot of a hill next to a stream.
  • Hongcun's rivers flow past it, and its villagers led a channel through it to every household.
    The rivers flow past on the west, and the villagers cut a winding channel nearly 1 m (~3 ft) wide to bring their water into the village. So Hongcun pairs the two forms the way Harie does.
  • Does Chengkan stand beside a stream, or beside a river?
    Chengkan is a traditional village of Huizhou, with its river, the Zongchuan, flowing past the village's east side. Both Hongcun's and Chengkan's watercourses are rivers by name, but only Hongcun's has a source that gives its size, the listing's "stream"; no page we read gives the Zongchuan's width, so Chengkan may belong with the rivers above.
  • A visitor's account of Likeng has its houses facing each other across the stream, joined by bridges.
    Likeng, in Wuyuan, was first built in the Northern Song period. A visitor's account has its houses facing each other across the water, with bridges linking the households on the two banks - ten-odd of them, of stone, timber and brick.
  • How far do these villages speak for a farming hamlet?
    Only partly. Xidi and Hongcun are clan villages, built by officials and merchants coming home and living by trade rather than by farming, and Chengkan's account speaks of the same clan society and merchant culture. All of them are described as they survive, and the Likeng account is one traveler's diary, the weakest source here, and says nothing of who built the village or how it lived.
  • Which form does a village take at a stream's size?
    Either, and the record prefers neither: Xidi and Likeng are built along both banks, Hongcun beside its stream, and Chengkan beside a river of unknown size.

The siting doctrine adds little. It is a general formula for a good site, and village-landscape scholarship that applies it, not a rule anyone states for a farming hamlet's brook.

  • The four gods formulation wants flowing water on one side of a site.
    It wants flowing water on the left, a long road on the right, a pond in front and hills behind.
  • A Chinese village-landscape study describes Shanggantang as "mountains on three sides, water on one side".
    The village has a named hill to its left, its right and its back, and faces a river in front.
  • A survey of Shaanxi villages names a stream on each side of a village as a fengshui configuration.
    It describes one village with a small stream on its east and one on its west, flowing around and past it, as the configuration of "twin streams embracing".
  • Does any siting text say whether a village may straddle its water?
    No text we read states a rule either way, and none of these passages contemplates a site divided by its water.
  • A linear settlement's two sides are a street's, not a stream's.
    A settlement described as having houses "on both sides" might be taken for one built astride its water. In most cases a linear settlement's buildings are lined up on both sides of a single street. The Japanese encyclopedia article lets one follow a road, a river or a canal, but gives its two sides only to a street. The geographer Yajima Jinkichi's definition of the farming linear village puts its houses along a street, on one side of it or on both. Neither says a settlement straddles a stream.

The same holds at the scale of one farm: whether a household's own house, yard, garden and storehouse stood together on one bank of a channel.

  • Did a farmstead stand whole on one bank of its channel?
    Nothing we read places a household's own garden, privy, yard or storehouse across a channel from its house, and nothing we read says that never happened. What does turn up is a channel running along the houses: at Harie, near the northwest shore of Lake Biwa, the channel flows alongside the house and its kabata. That a household's own buildings stood together on one side of such a channel is this project's reading of that picture, not something the page says, and so a GUESS.
