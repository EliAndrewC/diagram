Were a pond's reeded shore and reed-free bank only modern? No - a Chinese classic puts the cattails inside the bank, not on it

Sources: maoshi-zhengyi-juan7 (the poem of cattail and lotus at a marsh's embankment, and its commentary: the plants inside the bank, not on it), classic-of-poetry-enwiki (the date of the poem's collection), chenfu-nongshu-juanshang (a Song pond's bank, planted with a few trees and trodden firm by cattle), nagano-shishi-kawayoke (an Edo embankment's slopes turfed)

The question. Every map with a reservoir draws a fringe of reeds on wet ground round its water, and keeps the reeds off its embankment (A reservoir's shore is reeded, and its EMBANKMENT is mown). Every source that question quotes describes the ponds of today, and the reason it gives for the bare bank, mowing and burning to keep it strong, is present-day management. Under the GM's ruling of 2026-09-28 that nothing attested only in modern times is drawn, are the reeded shore and the reed-free bank attested before modern times?

What the record found. Both are, in China; the mowing and burning are not.

  • Water plants inside the bank, not on it. The Book of Songs, whose poems date from the 11th to the 7th centuries BC, has a poem that opens "by the embankment of that marsh there are cattail and lotus". The commentary printed with it glosses the embankment as the bank at the marsh's edge that holds back the water, and says the poem names the bank's edge because the two plants stand inside the embankment: they do not grow on the embankment itself. Cattail is one of the tall water plants the reservoir question finds in a pond's shallow shore.
  • A pond bank planted and grazed, not reeded. Chen Fu's farming manual of the Song dynasty, which sizes the pond a high field needs, has its bank raised high and planted with a few mulberry and cudrania trees to tether cattle: the cattle get cool shade, and the bank, trodden by them, grows firm.
  • An embankment's slopes turfed. In the Edo period the Matsushiro domain turfed the slopes of its river embankments to stop the soil washing away, or instead faced them with layered reed-grass, brushwood and pine needles.

No description of a Japanese pond's shore before 1868 was found, nor, anywhere, a pond bank before modern times that was mown and burned. The limits: the poem is a love song whose plants are images, and its water is a marsh held by a bank, not a farm reservoir; the reading that the plants stand inside the bank is the commentary's, and the page gives the commentary no date; the Song bank is one manual's advice; and the Edo turf is on one domain's river embankments, not a pond's.

What this means for the maps. The reeded fringe stays: water plants in the shallows inside a bank are attested before modern times, in China. The reed-free bank stays too, because the same commentary keeps the plants off the bank itself; but its reason is the older one, a dry, firm bank, turfed or trodden, and not a bank mown and burned, which is attested only today. A pond bank with a few mulberry or cudrania trees on it, as the Song manual plants it, is a second attested form, and the maps do not draw it yet.
