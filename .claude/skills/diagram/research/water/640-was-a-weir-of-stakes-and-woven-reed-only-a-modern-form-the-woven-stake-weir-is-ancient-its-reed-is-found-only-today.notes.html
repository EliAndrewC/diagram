• kotobank-shigarami - 「Stakes driven in a row in a river, with brushwood, bamboo and the like twined onto them from both sides, to hold back the flow of water.」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; ) (the Seisenban Nihon Kokugo Daijiten's entry)
  • kotobank-shigarami - 「[Earliest examples] "The Asuka River - if one laid a shigarami across it and dammed it, would even the flowing water go gently?" (Man'yoshu (late 8th century) 2.197) "at the meeting of the two rivers they cut great trees and laid them as an abatis, and heaped up shigarami in great number" (Heike monogatari (early 13th century) 7)」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; ) (the poem's fence is imagined, not one that stood; the Heike one is a work of war)
  • suido-ishizue-manyoshu - 「"In the Asuka River the fine water-weed grows in every shallow, but because of the shigarami it cannot sway together" (book 7). What was laid across the river was a shigarami (fence), stakes driven in with wood or bamboo laid across to dam the water, the same structure as the one that appeared at the Kodera site (Ehime prefecture).」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; ) (the build is the column's reading; the poem does not describe it)
  • kotobank-seki - 「The oldest example is stakes and sheet piles set up in a channel at the Itazuke site in Fukuoka prefecture (of the Yusu pottery phase), said to be either Final Jomon or the start of the Yayoi period.」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; ); 「A representative example is the weir of the Kodera site in Ehime prefecture (Kofun period). Slanting members are driven in from downstream toward upstream, a crosspiece is laid, and upright members are driven in against it on the downstream side. This is repeated in three tiers (4.2 m wide).」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; ) (Sahara Makoto's section of the Heibonsha World Encyclopedia entry)
  • kotobank-seki - 「Weirs of the Edo period were almost all for obtaining farm water, and according to the Kenrei Shuchi they can be broadly divided into grass weirs and washing weirs.」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; ) (Kitamura Toshio's section; the section does not say what a grass weir was built of)
  • mizu-shiba-izeki - 「The weir was built here in the early Edo period. It began with the new-field development by the Satsuma domain that started about 380 years ago. It is handed down that at first it was either a stone weir or plants woven onto a foundation of wooden stakes.」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; ); 「Built every year in March; in September, when the rice harvest is over, only half is taken down, and the next year the brushwood is laid anew」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; ); 「In 1902 (Meiji 35) the foundation became stone, but it was often washed away by typhoons, so in 1950 (Showa 25) a concrete foundation was made.」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; ) (the page is of 2018, so about 380 years before is about 1638)
