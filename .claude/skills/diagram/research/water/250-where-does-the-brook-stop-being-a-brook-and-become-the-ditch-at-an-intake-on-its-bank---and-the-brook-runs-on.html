Where does the brook stop being a brook and become the ditch? At an intake on its bank - and the brook runs on

Sources: japanriver-koborebanashi-21 (the works in order; the weir since the Yayoi period), jawiki-toshuko (the parts of the head works), fao-irrigation-manual-m7 (the physics: offtake, weir, the lower flow returned to the river), maff-toshuko-history (half-river closures; the surplus returned), jawiki-seki (the overflow weir), fao-water-sources-u5835e (water flowing over the weir), zhwiki-dujiangyan and zhwiki-lingqu (the river divided in stated proportions), tabayashi-1987 (small streams dammed; the canal divided below the intake), icid-diversion-weirs and maff-nogyoyosui-suiden (whole diversion as a possibility)

The question. The GM asked whether there is any logic to where a stream becomes a ditch, and whether the stream should not stay a stream right up to the point where it branches in two.

What the record found: the transition is a set of works, in a fixed order, and the stream goes on below them. Both traditions name the same sequence. In Japan the head works were originally the whole series - the weir, the intake mouth, the settling basin, the division works - and the modern term still lists the intake weir, the intake mouth and the settling basin as its parts. A general irrigation manual gives the same three: an offtake at the side of the river, a regulating structure across the river or part of it, and a sediment flushing arrangement. The weir on a small mountain stream for taking water into paddy is old - its prototype is placed in the Yayoi period, more than two thousand years ago - and in the mountains "small streams are dammed and the water is conducted through small canals usually for short distances and on gentle slopes". The canal, once it has left the stream, is what divides: water taken by a diversion dam "enters a main canal and is delivered into secondary and lesser canals one after another by gravity", the secondary canals tapping "diversion works on the main canals". The fork is on the dug canal, below the intake, and no source read puts one on the stream itself.

And the stream is not swallowed. In old Japan the closures were mostly half-river closures; a weir may be of the kind that lets water over its crest at all times, and Japanese has a name for it; the Ishi-ibi works of 1615 took water at the sluice and returned the surplus to the main river between the island and the sluice; a properly designed intake "should divert only the relatively clean upper part of the water flow into the canal and dispose of the lower part down the river", and when the river runs high "water will flow over the weir". The two great Chinese diversions divide their rivers in stated proportions rather than taking them: Dujiangyan sends six parts to the irrigation channel and four to the flood channel in spring and the reverse in flood, and Lingqu returns seven parts to the Xiang and passes three to the canal. So the GM's reading is refuted in its letter and confirmed in its spirit: the stream does not become the ditch at the fork - the fork is the dug canal's own division point - but the stream does stay a stream below the intake, and the ditch begins where it leaves the bank.

Whole capture: possible by definition, shown nowhere. A diversion weir is defined as "a low dam or wall across a stream for the purpose of diverting part or all the water from a stream into a canal", and in an Edo drought "all the water flowing out of the mountain catchment" was taken into agricultural use, with each district's return flow used in full by the district below. No source read shows a single brook extinguished by a village's ditch. The maps therefore draw the attested form - the brook continues - and the whole-capture reading is recorded here as declined rather than as a second form: the maps roll between two forms only where the record shows a thing done two ways, and this one is shown done one way.

What the map draws. The field is cut AWAY from the brook - the supply canal on the brook's flank stops short, so the fan leaves the stream its margin rather than the stream bending round the fan. The brook comes down off the high ground, passes the head of the fan on one side, and runs on down that flank of the field to leave the map or to receive the field's drain (below). At the fan's head, on the fan's bank, is the intake. Whether a weir crosses the brook there is the next question; where on the bank the intake stands, and how the head race runs from it to the fork, is the one after.
