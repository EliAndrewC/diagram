Where does the brook stop being a brook and become the ditch? At an intake on its bank - and the brook runs on

Sources: japanriver-koborebanashi-21 (the works in order; the weir since the Yayoi period), jawiki-toshuko (the parts of the head works), fao-irrigation-manual-m7 (the physics: offtake, weir, the lower flow returned to the river, the angle), maff-toshuko-history (the premodern forms and materials; the surplus returned; the bend), jawiki-seki (the overflow weir), fao-water-sources-u5835e (water flowing over the weir), zhwiki-dujiangyan and zhwiki-lingqu (the river divided in stated proportions), tabayashi-1987 (small streams dammed; the canal divided below the intake; Toro), icid-diversion-weirs and maff-nogyoyosui-suiden (whole diversion as a possibility), jsidre-miwa-2023 (natural intake; the oblique weir from the intake mouth), shimane-toshuko-lecture (one-bank intake; where the current touches the bank; the intake court), wangzhen-nongshu-18 (the village brook's timber palisade), zhwiki-zhengguoqu (crib weir or bare bend intake, both readings of one ancient head), maff-toshuko-sand-basin (the settling basin at the canal's start)

The question. The GM asked whether there is any logic to where a stream becomes a ditch, and whether the stream should not stay a stream right up to the point where it branches in two.

What the record found: the transition is a set of works, in a fixed order, and the stream goes on below them. Both traditions name the same sequence. In Japan the head works were originally the whole series - the weir, the intake mouth, the settling basin, the division works - and the modern term still lists the intake weir, the intake mouth and the settling basin as its parts. A general irrigation manual gives the same three: an offtake at the side of the river, a regulating structure across the river or part of it, and a sediment flushing arrangement. The weir on a small mountain stream for taking water into paddy is old - its prototype is placed in the Yayoi period, more than two thousand years ago - and in the mountains "small streams are dammed and the water is conducted through small canals usually for short distances and on gentle slopes". The canal, once it has left the stream, is what divides: water taken by a diversion dam "enters a main canal and is delivered into secondary and lesser canals one after another by gravity", the secondary canals tapping "diversion works on the main canals". The fork is on the dug canal, below the intake, and no source read puts one on the stream itself.

And the stream is not swallowed. In old Japan the closures were mostly half-river closures; a weir may be of the kind that lets water over its crest at all times, and Japanese has a name for it; the Ishi-ibi works of 1615 took water at the sluice and returned the surplus to the main river between the island and the sluice; a properly designed intake "should divert only the relatively clean upper part of the water flow into the canal and dispose of the lower part down the river", and when the river runs high "water will flow over the weir". The two great Chinese diversions divide their rivers in stated proportions rather than taking them: Dujiangyan sends six parts to the irrigation channel and four to the flood channel in spring and the reverse in flood, and Lingqu returns seven parts to the Xiang and passes three to the canal. So the GM's reading is refuted in its letter and confirmed in its spirit: the stream does not become the ditch at the fork - the fork is the dug canal's own division point - but the stream does stay a stream below the intake, and the ditch begins where it leaves the bank.

Whole capture: possible by definition, shown nowhere. A diversion weir is defined as "a low dam or wall across a stream for the purpose of diverting part or all the water from a stream into a canal", and in an Edo drought "all the water flowing out of the mountain catchment" was taken into agricultural use, with each district's return flow used in full by the district below. No source read shows a single brook extinguished by a village's ditch. The maps therefore draw the attested form - the brook continues - and the whole-capture reading is recorded here as declined rather than as a second form: the maps roll between two forms only where the record shows a thing done two ways, and this one is shown done one way.

Two intake forms, both attested - so a knob. The first is the bare bank intake: "in old times, neither technique nor materials were worth much, so in many cases no intake weir was built at all - water was taken naturally, a place where natural intake was possible was sought for the intake mouth, and a conveyance canal was cut; where raising the water level ... was necessary, there were places that set up a temporary weir useful only for the intake season". Natural intake sets the intake mouth on the bank the current runs along, and a weir is added when the river falls too low to feed it. The same holds in the general manual: where the base flow keeps the level high enough, "a simple offtake structure to control the water diversion is sufficient". The second is the built weir. Before modern materials it was ox-frames, gabions and brushwood, singly or combined and covered with earth and gravel; the Tatai weir of 1183 was timber frames packed with stone, an oblique weir closing the whole river, whose upper frame at the deep channel was removed after each season and "installed anew every year in the irrigation season"; up to the end of the Sengoku period intake works were "mostly small-scale, based on timber frames packed with stone". Many old weirs were oblique, "extended long in the diagonally upstream direction from the intake mouth", damming the shallow riffle and standing clear of the flood's fastest water. China has the village case in Wang Zhen's treatise of 1313: "if the banks of the brook are somewhat deep and the fields lie high, so that the water cannot reach them, then upstream on the brook one makes a palisade to check the water, causing it to issue out at the side and run down to irrigate, so reaching the place of the fields" - and the head of the Zhengguo canal is read by scholars as either stone-filled crib baskets or a low weir, or as no dam at all and a diversion off the river bend. The record gives no proportion between the two forms, so each hamlet rolls one or the other with an even chance, and that proportion is a guess.

Which bank, and at what angle. The intake is on one bank, the side of the land it serves - the rule on torrent and steep rivers, and the principle on gentle ones - at a point where the current's deep line touches that bank, beside the sand sluice. The offtake should stand in a straight reach of the river; failing that, on the outside of a bend, where silt does not settle; it may leave the river at an angle when the water is clean, and at a right angle when it is silt-laden - the same page noting that the outside of a bend erodes and may need its bank protected. A Japanese torrent-river intake sited just downstream of the middle of a bend's concave bank took in little sediment. A mountain brook feeding a hamlet fan is drawn with the clean-water offtake: the head race leaves the bank at an acute angle pointing downstream, the angle the record already gives for an offtake leaving its parent (below, where that angle is itself a guess: no source read gives a figure for it). A 1902 treatise placed the head works "where the river issues from the mountains", which is where a hamlet's brook meets the head of its fan.

How far from the intake to the fork. No source read gives a distance. In modern practice what stands in that first stretch is a settling basin, "immediately following the intake mouth, at the starting point of the canal", reached through an intake court that narrows the flow smoothly from the mouth into the canal. - whether a village dug one is not recorded, and the maps draw none. Tabayashi says only that the small canals run "for short distances"; the third-century Toro fields, 7.5 hectares, were watered by a canal "more than 370 meters long". So the length of the head race is not a number the record holds. On the maps it follows from the geometry: the head race runs from the intake on the brook's bank to the division point at the fan's head, and its length is whatever that distance is, the intake's lead upslope of the fork rolled per map within the "short distances" the record allows - a guess as to the number, derived as to the shape.

What the map draws. The field is cut AWAY from the brook - the supply canal on the brook's flank stops short, so the fan leaves the stream its margin rather than the stream bending round the fan. The brook comes down off the high ground, passes the head of the fan on one side, and runs on down that flank of the field to leave the map or to receive the field's drain (the next question). At the fan's head, on the fan's bank, is the intake, and the head race leaves it at an acute angle pointing downstream and runs to the division point. On a weir hamlet a bar of stone-packed timber crib crosses the brook there, running diagonally upstream from the intake mouth as the old oblique weirs did - the old form as the modern engineering histories describe it, since no period drawing of a village weir was read. It is drawn in stone gray with the crib's baulks ticked across it and a lip along its upstream face, so that it reads as a thing that holds water back rather than as one of the timber planks that carry a lane over a ditch. Note: we have drawn that weir as a full closure of the brook, in order to make it visible on the map at this scale, although half-river closures were the common old form - at a brook seven feet wide a half-bar is a pixel or two - and its thickness is drawn at five feet, a guess, because no source read gives the thickness of a village weir. On a bare-intake hamlet nothing is built across the brook: the head race simply leaves the bank.
