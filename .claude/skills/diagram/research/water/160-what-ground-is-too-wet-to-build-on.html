What ground is too wet to build on?

Sources: the GM's rulings of 2026-07; the map review of 2026-08-12; this project's measurements of the drawn hamlets; the wetland finding at Marsh - wet rice is reclaimed FROM wetland; rwsshp-hand-dug-well and wateraid-hand-dug-wells (where a hand-dug well is not sited, and what its head is detailed to keep out), maff-drain-shape, kohai-shicchi-jawiki

The bog, and the water itself. A marsh on these maps is unbuildable ground, and the first thing that means is that nothing SACRED or funerary stands on it. The GM, 2026-07: "no one would ever put a graveyard - or a shrine - in a marsh". You do not raise a hall or dig graves in a bog. So a drawn marsh registers its own footprint as ground nothing may be built on - collision-placed features, houses, wells and byres alike, keep off it - and a hand-sited shrine or churchyard goes on dry ground, the spur or the high ground. A pond's reed FRINGE is exempt: it is a water margin wherever the pond lies, not a valley bog. The case that produced the rule: a split village's shrine and its graveyard first landed on the broad wet toe below the fields, and were moved to the dry ground beside the western block.

And no wellhead is sunk in the bog. You do not dig a draw-well in standing surface water: a well wants a water table beneath dry ground. Construction guidance for the same artifact - a hand-dug, hand-lined, bucket-drawn village well - says it in the form a builder needs, that such a well is not sited in swampy or flood-prone ground, and details its whole head to keep surface water out of the shaft and to stop water ponding at the mouth.71 That the rim and the windlass also need somewhere dry to stand is this record's own reading of the same fact; the manuals give a sanitary reason rather than that one. A well is asked about the wet band at the moment it is placed.

Two things the measurement has to get right. First, the test is against ground below the crop's LOWEST point, not against the drawn marsh polygon. The wet band deliberately begins about 90 ft ABOVE the crop's low edge so the reeds tuck under the field, so its uphill lip overlaps farmed and built ground that carries no reeds at all - and testing the polygon would refuse every wellhead standing on that lip. Second, the rule is scoped to hamlet and village, because towns and cities draw no toe marsh at all.

Dwellings keep off the wet low toe below the drain, for the same reason. The GM, 2026-07: "would there be farmhouses below the drainage ditch if south of it is marshland?" A field drains to its lowest edge, and the ground downslope of that collector - reed marsh, or low reclaimed paddy, or the drainage pond itself - is the wettest in the valley.152 It is not always marsh; but whatever it is, it is wet, so no farmhouse sits there.

"Below the drain" is not the same as "low ground", and one story that sounded right was refuted by measurement. The terrain is modeled as a tilted plane, so height depends only on position along the fall: the wet toe is a band across the slope and its upper boundary is a CONTOUR, not the ditch. And the drawn collector is not a contour - it descends along its length to reach the pond it empties into, a descent that is itself a legibility exaggeration of a very gentle real fall (see The wet toe is as wide as the FAN). The refuted story is that a rectangular field implies a drain running across the slope: measured on the drawn maps, a collector's height spread is 269 ft on Akagahara, 340 ft on Kikuta and 352 ft on Hoshigaoka. It is not a cross-slope line. So the rule flags a dwelling only within the drain's OWN cross-slope span, projected onto the declared fall.

And it is scoped to DISPERSED settlements, which was measured rather than assumed. Each strewn farm is sited individually and must individually stand on dry ground; a NUCLEATED cluster is placed as one unit and is governed by its own rule. A tight cluster beside a DIAGONAL drain reads as "downslope of the drain line" without standing in any wet toe at all, which is what a rule not so scoped would flag at Ueda, whose west-margin cluster sits south-west of a north-west to south-east drain. A farm off to the SIDE, past the drain's ends, is a legitimate flank homestead and is not flagged; only the central toe below the drain is.

The rule the map follows: Hamlet tier. No sacred or funerary ground stands on marsh: no shrine, temple hall, cemetery or mausoleum, and not by any part of its footprint - a clipped corner is as wrong as a center. A pond's reed fringe does not count as marsh for this. A shrine or churchyard that has nowhere dry inside the settlement goes onto the spur or the high ground outside it.

The rule the map follows: Hamlet tier. No dwelling stands on the wet toe below a field's drain. The ground judged is what lies downslope of the collector along the declared fall and within the collector's own cross-slope span; a farmstead out past the drain's ends, on a dry flank, is not on the toe and stands there legitimately. The rule applies where farmsteads are strewn individually across the ground, not to a nucleated cluster placed as one block, and it needs both a declared fall and a drawn drain to mean anything.

The rule the map follows: Town and city tier, where no generator draws the settlement yet. A channel is a no-build corridor: nothing is built within about 33 ft of its centerline, which is the width calibrated to keep a farmhouse's whole FOOTPRINT off the water rather than merely its center - a hamlet farmhouse's half-diagonal is about 26 ft - and at a coarser tier the corridor is whatever keeps the largest neighboring footprint clear. The test is on the footprint: a corner clipping a channel is a failure, which is how one village once shipped farmhouses standing on its field-to-field channels.
