Did a village have a water mill - and where was a town's grain milled?

Sources: mizu-no-bunka-28-suisha (what village mills were for, who owned them, how many), suisha-jawiki (the power wheel's date in Japan), tiangong-gongdao (the household foot pestle and the water pestle), tiangong-gongmai (ox, donkey, hand and water mills), kotobank-tsukigomeya (the town's rice polished by its retailers)

The question. Rice has to be hulled and polished and wheat ground before either is eaten. Did a village have a water mill to do it, how many, where on the stream and whose; and where was a town's grain milled?

In Japan, a mill of the mid-Edo period and after. The full use of power water wheels is said to date from the Edo period, for polishing rice and milling grain as the eating of white rice spread. They were used mainly for pounding rice and pressing rapeseed oil, and what drove their growth from the mid-Edo period was the brewing trade and the crowding of people into castle towns; a large wheel drove many mortars. So the mills clustered where a city ate: rice wholesalers lined the entrances of the five highways into Edo, and the villages around them built mills to polish rice. On the shogun's own land even one mill needed the intendant's permission; licensed mills multiplied in 1772-1780, and a water-supply record of 1788 counts 33 on the whole Musashino plateau west of Edo. By 1897 there were 60,000 in the country.

Whose. Most mill owners were wealthy farmers and village officials - which meant the powerful using the village's common water for themselves, and villagers objected hard - though Japanese mills were often used in common by the village. One working mill in Mitaka stands about 200 m downstream of the one Osawa village's headman built, called the "big mill"; in 1903 the newer mill had 10 pounding mortars and one millstone.

In China, by the household and by the river. A household of eight or more buried a stone mortar in the ground and pounded with a foot-trodden beam; the water pestle was built by the people of hill country who lived by rivers, two or three mortars where the stream was small and the ground cramped, ten side by side where the flow was large; in one prefecture of Jiangnan it was set on an earth-filled boat moored in the stream; some wheels ground flour, pounded rice and lifted water to the paddies at once; and in river country with water pestles some people lived and died without seeing a husking mill. Wheat was ground on millstones turned by a strong ox, a lesser mill by a donkey, the smallest by hand, and the water mill, built like the water pestle, was three times as handy as the ox.

In a town. Edo's rice retailers bought brown rice, polished it and sold it white, working some 5,500 mortars among them in 1744. That a town's rice was polished in its shops while its water mills stood in the villages on the streams outside it, as round Edo, and that a Chinese town without a stream ground its flour with the ox and donkey mills above, is this page's reading of the passages together.

What was not found. How large a village mill-house was, and where on a stream it was sited against the village's own intake, are not in anything read; nor is the share of villages that had one.

The decision it drives. A village mill is attested in two distinct forms - none, each household pounding its own rice, which leaves nothing on the map; or one water mill on the stream, owned by the headman or a wealthy farmer - so it is a knob, rolled per settlement, and only where a stream with fall passes. A town draws no mill of its own: its rice is polished in the shops, and a water mill may stand on the stream in the villages outside it.

The rule the map follows: a village or hamlet whose stream has fall rolls from its seed whether it has a water mill (the proportion a guess); if it does, one mill stands on the stream bank below the settlement's intake, its wheel in a short race off the stream, the mill-house about 15 by 20 ft, near the headman's house where the stream allows - the seat, the race and the size all guesses. A town draws no mill of its own: its milling is in its shops and yards, and on the streams of the villages outside it.
