Was a weir of stakes and woven reed only a modern form? The woven stake weir is ancient; its reed is found only today

Sources: kotobank-shigarami (the stake fence woven with brushwood or bamboo across a river, and its earliest written examples), suido-ishizue-manyoshu (a Man'yoshu poem's fence read against an excavated weir), kotobank-seki (excavated stake weirs; the Edo classing of weirs), mizu-shiba-izeki (an irrigation weir of early Edo, said to have been woven onto stakes)

The question. One of the four forms the maps roll for a weir on a hamlet's brook is a fence of driven stakes with reed (kaya) woven between them, drawn in What was a village weir built of, and how thick was it? The only source that question quotes for it describes such a weir as still in use today in Saga, and gives it no date. Under the GM's ruling of 2026-09-28 that nothing attested only in modern times is drawn, is the form attested before modern times?

What the record found. The fence is; the reed is not.

  • The woven stake fence across the water. The shigarami is a row of stakes driven into a river with brushwood or bamboo twined onto them from both sides, to hold back the flow, and the word's earliest written examples are a Man'yoshu poem of the 8th century, which imagines one laid across the Asuka River to dam it, and the Heike monogatari of the early 13th century. A history of Japanese irrigation reads the fence of another Man'yoshu poem as stakes with wood or bamboo laid across, damming the water, the same build as the one found at the Kodera site.
  • Stake weirs in the ground. The oldest weir excavated in Japan is stakes and sheet piles set in a channel at the Itazuke site, from the end of the Jomon period or the start of the Yayoi, and the Kodera weir of the Kofun period is stakes and crosspieces in three tiers.
  • The Edo grass weir. Edo-period weirs were mostly for irrigation, and by the Kenrei Shuchi they divide broadly into grass weirs and washing weirs, without saying what either was built of. The Kawaharazono weir at Kanoya, in Kagoshima, was built in the early Edo period, about 1638, and is said to have been at first either a stone weir or plants woven onto a foundation of wooden stakes; its brushwood is laid anew each March to this day.

What the premodern record weaves into the stakes is brushwood and bamboo. A weave of reed in particular was found only in the present-day Saga weir. The limits: the Man'yoshu fence is a poem's image, and the Heike one a work of war; the Kodera comparison is the history's own; and the Kanoya weir's first form is a local tradition, with today's weir standing on a concrete base.

What this means for the maps. The fence form stays, as a fence of stakes woven with brushwood or bamboo, the weave the premodern record names, not with reed. A fence of brushwood draws the same as a fence of reed, so the drawn weir does not change, and its thickness stays the convention the weir question gives.
