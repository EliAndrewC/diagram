Moats: where their water comes from and where it goes (hori)

A wet moat (hori) held water that came from wherever it could be had: water welling up from the ground, rain, water let out of the castle, or water drawn in on purpose from a nearby river, lake or even the sea. On sloping ground a moat was divided by earthen weirs into pools at different levels, so that its water would not run away downhill. Xi'an's moat holds water all year round, and is 20 m (~66 ft) wide and 10 m (~33 ft) deep.

A moat fed by a live stream needs a way out as well, as a pond needs a spillway, or its water rises until it overtops its banks. Beijing's moats took their water in from springs to the northwest and let it out to the east, into the Tonghui River. A moat was more than a defense: it could take a city's flood water, hold water in store, and water the fields at a lord's gate.   • Moat water came from the ground, the rain, the castle, or a nearby river, lake or sea.
    A Japanese column on castle moats lists the ways a wet moat filled: water welling up of itself from underground, water no longer needed in the castle and let out, rainwater collecting, and, to strengthen the castle's defenses, water drawn in on purpose from a nearby river, lake or the sea. A Japanese castle site notes that when a moat was dug, water sometimes welled up in it of itself. A moat dug below the water table fills of itself and stays full as a pond does, so keeping a castle's inner moat full was the easy half of its water; that is this project's reconstruction.
  • Two castles on the Seto Inland Sea take seawater into their moats.
    Takamatsu's moat draws seawater from the Inland Sea, and sea bream swim in it; at Imabari, seawater flows into the moat and out again.
  • A moat on sloping ground was held in pools at different levels (mitochigai).
    A wet moat was sometimes divided by earthen banks into parts with different water levels, instead of one continuous surface. On sloping ground this kept the water from running off, and stored it.
  • Boats were often used on moats fed from a river.
    The Japanese encyclopedia article on moats says so in passing; it takes river-fed moats for granted rather than describing them.
  • That a castle preferred groundwater because a besieger could not cut it off is this project's GUESS.
    A channel from a river can be cut by a besieger, and water that wells up in the moat itself cannot, yet no page we read says a castle chose its water for that reason.

Water that comes into a moat has to go somewhere, and a ring of water behaves differently from a stream.

  • A ring moat has no upstream end.
    Water that enters a ring at one point and leaves at another has two ways round, one each way, and it takes both: the ring splits the incoming flow into two arcs that meet again at the way out.
  • Could a moat fed by a stream do without an outlet?
    Not in a wet climate. At a steady level, the water leaving a moat equals what comes in, plus the rain, less what evaporates and seeps away; this is textbook hydrology, stated from principle. With no outlet, all of a stream's flow would have to evaporate or seep. Ponds do lose water both ways - evaporation may exceed 2 m (~7 ft) a year - but a live stream, in this project's reading, brings far more than a moat's surface and bed can lose, so the level climbs until the banks overtop. That is why a pond whose runoff can exceed what it holds is given a spillway.
  • Could a moat be raised high enough to hold back its feeder?
    No. Above a gate a stream's water stands higher for a stretch, then runs again at the normal depth its own bed's slope sets; in one worked example it does so about 2,200 m (~7,218 ft) above the gate. So raising a moat backs its feeder up for a distance above the mouth, but cannot lift the whole stream to take its flow.
  • Where can a pond with no outlet hold its level?
    Where little water comes in and much evaporates. Basins with no outlet lose their water by evaporation and seepage, and are found most often in deserts; a moat of that kind would be one filled by springs or rain in a dry climate. In wet rice country, where the water table is high, evaporation modest and the paddies' trampled mud seals the ground against seepage, a moat could not absorb a live stream, and had to shed its surplus by a channel. That is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • How could a moat look still and yet have an outlet?
    By an overflow weir or a sluice, holding the water at a set level and letting only the surplus pass. Water commonly flows over a weir's crest to a lower level, and weirs are used to control a river's flow at high water; that one holds a pond at a set level is this project's reading.

Beijing and Edo show a city's moats taking water in at one end and letting it out at the other.

  • Beijing's moats took their water in from springs to the northwest and let it out to the east.
    The water of Beijing's moat system (hucheng he) came from springs to the northwest, the Jade Spring hill and Baifu spring among them, by way of the Chang River; at the Dongbianmen water gate it joined the inner city's south and east moats and flowed out into the Tonghui River.
  • Beijing's inner city had seven water gates: one to let water in, six to let it out.
    Of the inner city's seven water gates, one, at Deshengmen, was an intake and six were outfalls; they were arched gates, not weirs.
  • Part of Beijing's moat water ran through the city and the palace moat on its way.
    Past the Gaoliang Bridge outside Xizhimen the water divided. One branch ran east and divided again, and its southern arm filled the lakes inside the city (the Jishuitan and the Three Seas), entered the palace moat, and flowed on through a water gate into the inner city's south moat.
  • Edo dug part of its outer moat by moving a river.
    The Sotobori-gawa, Edo Castle's outer enclosure in the Keicho period, was dug by moving the course of the Hirakawa, which had emptied into Hibiya Inlet.
  • Edo's outer moat joins the Kanda River near Iidabashi.
    Near the east exit of Iidabashi Station the outer moat meets the Kanda River coming from the north, and east of that point it too is called the Kanda River.

Water that stops moving goes foul, and a moat and its channels had to be kept.

  • Niigata's moats went foul when their water stopped moving, and were filled in.
    In the twentieth century, the Shinano River's level fell and the ground sank as natural gas was drawn from beneath it; the moats' water stagnated and stank, the townspeople came to dislike them, and before the 1964 national sports festival the moats were all filled in.
  • Did a still castle moat grow green?
    Very likely - duckweed, algae and scum are what still water grows - but it is this project's GUESS: no page we read describes them on a moat.
  • Were moats dredged, flushed and stocked with carp?
    Rivers were dredged: kawazarae is the scooping out of the earth, sand and filth that collect on a river bed. That moats were kept the same way, that water let through them kept them from going stale, and that carp were kept in them, is this project's GUESS; no page we read says any of it.
  • How much of a stream's sand and gravel a branch channel takes depends on how much of its water it takes.
    A conference paper on keeping sediment out of intakes reports that the share of bed load each channel takes is governed by the share of the flow it takes.
  • At what angle does a channel best leave a straight river?
    About 120 degrees, in a study the same paper reports, though the text does not say from which side the angle is measured.
  • Did an offtake that left a moat at a right angle silt up its own mouth?
    Perhaps; it is this project's GUESS, since a moat's water barely moves. No page we read says so: the drainage standards read warn that the water at a right-angle bend superelevates and overtops, not that the bend silts. Which way channels join and part is at Which way water flows, and how channels bend and join.

The cases of moats draining a city, storing water and watering fields come from a capital, a canal-laced castle town and a lord's country house.

  • A capital let its flood-season water out into its moat.
    At Zhongdu, the Jin dynasty's capital, the water that gathered inside the city in the flood season ran through a water gate into the moat south of the city.
  • Yanagawa's castle-town moat held stored water, let in from a river through a sluice.
    The moat ringing Yanagawa's old castle town is filled from the Futatsugawa through a sluice; to drain it, the sluice is closed and the stored water let out to the Ariake Sea. The town's canals, of which the moat is one ring, were dug to drain the land, and also store fresh water and hold the rain when a high tide stops it draining.
  • Yanagawa's canal mud was spread on the fields.
    The mud that settled in the canals was dried, broken small and spread on paddies and fields as fertilizer.
  • The moat round a medieval country lord's house watered the fields at its gate.
    The moat enclosing a resident lord's house served for defense and also irrigated the paddy and dry field at its gate.
  • At Ueda, a channel drawn at the castle town watered three villages' farms.
    The Masuami channel, drawn from Amagafuchi at the Ueda castle town when the Sengoku family held the domain, is the farm water of Suwabe, Akiwa and Kamishiojiri, and today also carries the town's rainwater. The page does not say the channel was drawn from the castle moat itself.
  • Did a city's moat water the farmland outside its wall?
    Probably, in places; it is this project's reading. A capital's moat that took its flood water, Yanagawa's moat holding stored water and a country lord's moat watering the fields at its gate show that a moat could drain a city and water fields, but not how a provincial seat's moat was worked, and no page we read says a city's moat watered the farmland outside its wall.
  • How big a channel did it take to keep a moat full?
    Something like the moat itself, in this project's reading, a GUESS. A trickle cannot keep a moat full against seepage and evaporation, so the channel that supplies one carries something like what the moat holds; no page we read sets a feeding channel's size against its moat's.
