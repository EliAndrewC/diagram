Where the drawn net STOPS - the tier below the last ditch we can draw

Sources: nougyoudoboku-matsutan, tabayashi-1987, shanghai-tudi-zhengli (the five grades named in order, and what the lowest of them is sized to), aichi-yosui-jawiki, kangai-jawiki, kaogongji-wikisource and zhouli-suiren-wikisource (the classical ladder of field channels), akiba-1948-kangai (when plots and channels were first properly arranged), hattori-site-yayoiken (water openings in an early paddy's bunds)

The question (GM 2026-08-17). Should a delivery ditch keep thinning past its drawn minimum - taper away to a point - rather than ending at a visible width?

What the research found: no, and the reason is that there is a whole further TIER, not a vanishing. Both reference traditions terminate the fixed net at a channel with a real working cross-section, and then hand over to something that is not a channel at all.

  • The tier ladder is explicit and finite, and old. The Rites of Zhou already name five field channels, each twice as wide as the one below, from the quan a pair of spades cuts to the hui that "leads directly into the rivers", and lay the countryside out with one of them between the allotments of single farmers and a larger one for every ten, hundred and thousand, each with a path along it. Modern China names five grades - 干渠 / 支渠 / 斗渠 / 农渠 / 毛渠 (main / branch / lateral / farm / field): the first three are the open-channel canal system, the last two are the in-field distribution, and the maoqu at the bottom is laid out to the size of the individual basin and may be dug by the farmers themselves. Japan's design vocabulary is the same shape with three: 幹線用水路 -> 支線用水路 -> 末端用水路 (matsutan yosuiro, the terminal channel), and one canal scheme's own description names its trunk and branch canals the same way (幹線水路 / 支線水路). In every one of these ladders the last rung is a channel with a width of its own, not a taper's vanishing point.
  • The finest tier is far below our visibility floor. The finest rung of the ladder above, the one-chi quan, is about 1 ft across or less, a hairline at hamlet scale and invisible at a city's. The tier we actually stop at, ~3 ft, is the lateral of the ladder of channel widths, whose figures are sourced there, and drawing one grade finer would put the stroke below the minimum-visibility floor - 1.5 ft at hamlet scale - and collapse the hierarchy the water-width ladder exists to keep legible.
  • And the last tier can be no channel at all. Japanese paddy can be watered 田越し灌漑 (tagoshi kangai) - plot-to-plot: water enters the head paddy of a string and cascades down through the aze from one plot to the next. Where it is, the interior plots have no ditch of their own to draw. Tabayashi records, for the Tedori fan's ditches as rebuilt in the early 1900s and seen in 1976, a related relationship from the tenure side: the ditches that bring water to the plot inlets "are often considered parts of the paddy fields they serve". A 1948 essay on the history of Japanese irrigation dates the change: only with the land consolidation law's amendment of 1910 was "a proper arrangement of paddy plots and irrigation channels" made, and before it most irrigation had been flow-through irrigation. The opening the water passes through is older still: the excavated early-Yayoi paddies of the Hattori site in Shiga already had water openings set in their bunds. The maps already draw this: a column of plots cascades several to ~10 rows past where its ditch stops - so the drawn ditch ending mid-field IS the handoff to the cascade, not a line that was left unfinished.

The decision it drove. Keep the floor: the finest DRAWN channel ends at the lateral tier (~3 ft) with a blunt end, because that is a real terminal channel handing its flow to a mizuguchi and a cascade, and both of those are below what this sheet can render. The taper law above is what makes that ending read correctly - the stroke arrives at the floor by dwindling. Both halves of that ending stand on a record from before modern times: a finite ladder whose last rung is a channel of its own width, and water that leaves it through an opening in the bund and passes on from plot to plot. What is modern is a channel reaching every plot, and the maps do not draw one (How our maps lay out irrigation canals (yosuiro)).
