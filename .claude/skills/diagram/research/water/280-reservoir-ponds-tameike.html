Reservoir ponds (tameike)

A reservoir pond, in Japanese a tameike, holds water behind an earthen embankment for the fields below it; most are kept for farm water. Its water is drawn off through a pipe laid down the inside of the embankment into a conduit beneath it. A Chinese farming manual of 1149 gives up two or three parts in every ten of a high field to the pond that waters it. Where a pond stands among the canals it feeds is at Irrigation canals and how they are laid out (yosuiro), and the ponds dug into low ground in the middle of the fields at Ponds, rocks and graves in the middle of the fields.

A pond's shore and its embankment are two different grounds. The shallow water at its edge carries a belt of tall water plants - reed, cattail and wild rice. The embankment above it is kept clear of growth to keep it strong: mown and burned today, turfed or trodden firm in the older record.

  • A pond's shallow shore carries a belt of tall water plants.
    The agriculture ministry's material on ponds draws the natural shore, from the land out to open water, as waterside forest, then wet-ground plants, then the emergent plants - reed, cattail and wild rice - then floating-leaf and submerged plants. A study of the ponds of Kagawa divides a pond's plants the same way, by the depth of the water and the slope, with reed and cattail among the large emergent plants.
  • On the plains, reed, wild rice and cattail ring the shallow dish ponds.
    A pond museum in Hyogo says that a plains pond is a shallow dish pond ringed by embankments, rich in nutrients, with colonies of reed, wild rice and lesser cattail around it and water chestnut and prickly waterlily over its surface.
  • Does keeping a pond clear away the reeds at its shore?
    A working pond is tended: its water is often let out after the rice harvest. But the Kagawa study found more kinds of emergent and floating-leaf plants in the ponds whose silt was dredged and whose weed was cut, not fewer. It names the giving up of a pond's use and upkeep as a cause of the water plants' decline, and finds that giving up - of the water's use and of the mowing of the embankment - furthest along in small ponds. So a tended pond keeps its reeded shore.
  • A pond walled in concrete in a town holds far less life.
    The National Institute for Environmental Studies counted six kinds of dragonfly at a town pond walled in concrete, against 29 to 30 over the year at a pond ringed by satoyama and full of water plants.
  • A pond's embankment is kept bare of reeds, for its strength.
    No crops are grown on it, and it is mown and burned to hold back grass and trees. In the sun that gives it, dry-meadow flowers grow well - great burnet, balloon flower, gentian and golden lace, and many of the seven autumn grasses. It is dry, firm ground, not the wet shore below it.
  • Today a pond's water face is armored against the waves.
    The inner slope of the embankment is faced with riprap, stone pitching or concrete blocks to keep the waves from wearing it away.
  • Was a pond's reed cut as a crop?
    No page we read says so. Reed was cut every winter on Lake Biwa and in river reed beds and made into blinds (yoshizu), roofs and screens, and a bed left uncut often went to ruin; how a village's reed marsh was cut each winter is at Were the reed beds cut? But every page on reservoir ponds names reed only as a plant growing there, and none carries that harvest to a pond's margin. A pond's embankment and the inside of the pond were mown regularly, but no page we read says what became of the cut.
  • What did a pond give the village when it was drained?
    Its mud and its fish, by a modern conservation booklet's account: the soil dredged from a pond's bottom, rich in nutrients, went onto the vegetable fields as fertilizer, and the pond's fish were eaten. The booklet says "in the past" and gives no date or place.

Every source above describes the ponds of today. The older record is Chinese, apart from one Japanese domain's river banks, and it shows the same two grounds.

  • A Chinese classic's commentary puts cattail and lotus inside a marsh's bank, not on it.
    The Book of Songs, whose poems date from the 11th to the 7th centuries BC, has a poem that opens "by the embankment of that marsh there are cattail and lotus". The commentary printed with it glosses the embankment as the bank at the marsh's edge that holds back the water, and says the poem names the bank's edge because the two plants stand inside the embankment: they do not grow on the embankment itself. The poem's plants are images (the commentary calls the line an evocation), its water is a marsh held by a bank rather than a farm reservoir, and the page gives the commentary no date.
  • A Song-dynasty farming manual plants a pond's bank with a few trees and lets cattle tread it firm.
    Chen Fu's manual has the bank raised high and planted sparsely with mulberry and cudrania, to tether cattle: the cattle get cool shade, and the bank, trodden by them, grows firm. It is one manual's advice.
  • In the Edo period, one domain turfed the slopes of its river embankments.
    The Matsushiro domain turfed them to stop the soil washing away, or instead faced them with layered thatch grass, brushwood and pine needles. These are river embankments, not a pond's.
  • Were pond banks mown or burned before 1868, when Japan began to modernize?
    No page we read says so, anywhere, and no description of a Japanese pond's shore from before 1868 was found. The mowing and burning are attested only today; the bare bank is older.
  • How large was a pond beside the field it watered?
    Chen Fu's manual, completed in 1149, has the farmer of a high field dig a pond where the high water gathers, giving up about two or three mu of every ten mu of field to store it, its bank high and its middle deep and wide enough to hold the rains of the turn of spring and summer. The measure is an area, not a volume: the pond is a quarter to over two-fifths the size of the field it waters.
  • Did any source give how much water a pond stored for each acre it watered?
    No page we read, of any period, gives a storage for each hectare watered, and none gives a ratio of pond to field in Japan before modern times.
  • A pond's water is drawn off through one intake into a conduit beneath its embankment.
    The Kagawa prefecture page on a pond's structure describes the intake as an inclined or vertical pipe drawing off the stored water and a bottom conduit leading it away, with a sediment gate at the very bottom for managing the pond and drawing off its bottom water. No page we read says whether a pond ever had a second intake.
  • Does a pond's water leave by one channel that forks?
    This record's reading is that it does: one intake feeds one main channel, which divides at junctions downstream as Japan's large canal systems branch, their main canals tapering as branch canals draw from them. No page we read says how many channels leave a pond.
  • Did a diked polder's wet edge carry different plants from an undiked valley's wet foot?
    No page we read compares them.
