Canal junction angles - an offtake leaves pointing downstream

Sources: ignou-silt-control (the angle at which a canal is taken off a river, and the angle at which a distributary leaves a parent canal), kotobank-seki (the Edo-period weir that slants with the flow, and its intake)

Canal practice: an offtake leaves its parent at an ACUTE angle pointing downstream. A canal taken off at 90 degrees to the main flow is the most objectionable orientation there is, and a diversion angle of 30 to 45 degrees with respect to the river's flow is what is normally recommended, because it gives the flow lines a suitable curvature through the regulator. That recommendation is for a canal leaving a RIVER; the same manual prescribes 60 to 80 degrees for a distributary leaving a parent CANAL, so a drawn junction has to say which of the two it is. A lower bound below 30 degrees is on no page read for this record.

Is the rule older than modern engineering? Its direction is; its figure is not. Edo-period weirs were classed "by the main direction of the river's flow and the weir's angle to it", and the one built slanting with the flow put its intake "on the side toward which the water flows", which this record reads as the same downstream lean: the water reaches the intake travelling along the weir with the current, not across it. Slanting weirs were the common kind before the Meiji period. No source read from before modern times gives the angle, in degrees or as a rule, at which a canal left its river or a ditch its parent; the 30 to 45 degrees above is modern course-book engineering, from an Indian open-university unit. So an offtake that leans downstream is an Edo form and stays; the angle it is drawn at is a guess, not a figure from before modern times, and under the GM's ruling of 2026-09-28 that nothing attested only in modern times is drawn, the modern 30 to 45 degrees cannot be its warrant.
