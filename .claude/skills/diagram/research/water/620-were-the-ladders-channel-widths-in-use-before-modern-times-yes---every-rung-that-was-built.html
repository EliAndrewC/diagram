Were the ladder's channel widths in use before modern times? Yes - every rung that was built

Sources: kaogongji-wikisource (the classical ladder of field channels and their widths), kosaka-2014-tamagawa-bunsui (the intakes of Edo branch channels), hattori-site-yayoiken (an excavated early paddy's canal and drain)

The question. The water-width ladder sizes its two finest rungs, the field ditch and the distribution lateral, from modern Chinese design documents. Under the GM's ruling of 2026-09-28 that nothing attested only in modern times is drawn, do those widths have a record from before modern times?

What the record found. They do, and so does every rung a person built.

  • The field ditch, about 0.3 m. The Rites of Zhou name five field channels, each twice as wide as the one below, and the finest is one chi wide, "the cut of one ou", a pair of spades working together; the next, at the head of the field, is two. In Edo, a branch of the Tamagawa aqueduct that served one village was taken off through an intake seven-tenths of a shaku square, about 21 cm.
  • The distribution lateral, about 1 m. An excavated paddy of the earliest rice farming in Japan, at the Hattori site, had a drain 0.5-1 m wide and a central canal 1.4-2 m. An Edo branch that served fourteen villages was taken off through an intake three shaku, about 91 cm, square.
  • The main canal, the city canals and the moats carry their dated figures in the ladder's own table.
  • The brook and the town river are natural watercourses, not things anyone built. No record from before modern times was found that gives either a width, and their figures stay the ladder's estimates.

The limits are plain: the classical ladder is an ideal of the state, not a survey of fields; the Edo figures are the openings of intakes, not the channels below them; and the early paddy is one site, far older than the setting. They agree with each other and with the modern documents on the size of each rung, which is all the ladder asks of them.

What it means on the map. The ladder stands as drawn. The modern design figures stay in its table as corroboration, and no width on it is drawn only because a modern standard gives it. The comb's strokes, measured against the same figures, are at the comb net drawn at true size.
