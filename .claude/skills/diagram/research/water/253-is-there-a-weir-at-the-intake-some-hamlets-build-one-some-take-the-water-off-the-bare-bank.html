Is there a weir at the intake? Some hamlets build one, some take the water off the bare bank

Sources: maff-toshuko-history (the premodern forms and materials), jsidre-miwa-2023 (natural intake; the oblique weir from the intake mouth), fao-irrigation-manual-m7 (a simple offtake where the level serves), shimane-toshuko-lecture (small intakes of stone-packed timber frames), wangzhen-nongshu-18 (the village brook's timber palisade), zhwiki-zhengguoqu (crib weir or bare bend intake, both readings of one ancient head)

The question. The brook runs on past the intake (above). Is anything built across it where the head race leaves?

Two intake forms, both attested - so a knob. The first is the bare bank intake: "in old times, neither technique nor materials were worth much, so in many cases no intake weir was built at all - water was taken naturally, a place where natural intake was possible was sought for the intake mouth, and a conveyance canal was cut; where raising the water level ... was necessary, there were places that set up a temporary weir useful only for the intake season". Natural intake sets the intake mouth on the bank the current runs along, and a weir is added when the river falls too low to feed it. The same holds in the general manual: where the base flow keeps the level high enough, "a simple offtake structure to control the water diversion is sufficient". The second is the built weir. Before modern materials it was ox-frames, gabions and brushwood, singly or combined and covered with earth and gravel; the Tatai weir of 1183 was timber frames packed with stone, an oblique weir closing the whole river, whose upper frame at the deep channel was removed after each season and "installed anew every year in the irrigation season"; up to the end of the Sengoku period intake works were "mostly small-scale, based on timber frames packed with stone". Many old weirs were oblique, "extended long in the diagonally upstream direction from the intake mouth", damming the shallow riffle and standing clear of the flood's fastest water. China has the village case in Wang Zhen's treatise of 1313: "if the banks of the brook are somewhat deep and the fields lie high, so that the water cannot reach them, then upstream on the brook one makes a palisade to check the water, causing it to issue out at the side and run down to irrigate, so reaching the place of the fields" - and the head of the Zhengguo canal is read by scholars as either stone-filled crib baskets or a low weir, or as no dam at all and a diversion off the river bend. What decides between the two forms, what the opening in the bank looks like, and why the maps still roll the two evenly are below.

What the map draws. On a weir hamlet a weir crosses the brook at the intake, running diagonally upstream from the intake mouth as the old oblique weirs did - the old form as the modern engineering histories describe it, since no period drawing of a village weir was read. What it is built of, and how thick each form is drawn, is below; the stone-packed timber crib is drawn in stone gray with the crib's baulks ticked across it and a lip along its upstream face, so that it reads as a thing that holds water back rather than as one of the timber planks that carry a lane over a ditch. Note: we have drawn that weir as a full closure of the brook, in order to make it visible on the map at this scale, although half-river closures were the common old form (above) - at a brook seven feet wide a half-bar is a pixel or two. On a bare-intake hamlet nothing is built across the brook: the head race simply leaves the bank.
