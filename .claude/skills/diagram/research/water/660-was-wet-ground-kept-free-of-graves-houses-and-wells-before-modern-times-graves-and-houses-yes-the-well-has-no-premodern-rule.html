Was wet ground kept free of graves, houses and wells before modern times? Graves and houses yes; the well has no premodern rule

Sources: lushi-chunqiu-juan10 (a burial placed on high ground, away from the damp of groundwater), lushi-chunqiu-enwiki (that text's date), nagano-shishi-kodai-mura (Heian villages on the natural levees, the wet ground behind them farmed), taixi-shuifa-quanlan (where a late Ming book sites a well, and what it says of marsh country), fukuoka-museum-ido (Japan's earliest wells, at lowland sites)

The question. The maps treat a marsh as ground nothing is built on: no grave, shrine or house stands on it, and no wellhead is sunk in it (What ground is too wet to build on?). The one dated source for the graveyard is a Home Ministry instruction of 1884, and the well rests on two present-day manuals for village wells. Under the GM's ruling of 2026-09-28 that nothing attested only in modern times is drawn, are these attested before modern times?

What the record found.

  • Graves on high ground. The Lushi chunqiu, compiled about 239 BC, says a shallow grave is dug up by foxes and a deep one reaches groundwater, and so every burial must be on high ground, to escape the foxes and the damp of the groundwater.
  • Houses on the dry ground, the wet ground farmed. In the Nagano basin in the mid-Heian period, the late 9th and 10th centuries, large villages stood on the natural levees of the Chikuma River and similar slightly raised ground, and are thought to have farmed the wet ground behind them as paddy; the city history calls that siting a tradition seen from the Yayoi period on.
  • Wells: no rule found. The one premodern Chinese book on well-digging read, Xiong Sanba's of 1612, a Westerner's, ranks the sites as the mountain foot first, then sunny ground where gardens and houses stand, then open country, and says that in low marsh country a well finds water anywhere; it never warns against wet ground. Japan's earliest wells, of the end of the Early Yayoi, were found at lowland sites.
  • Shrines: nothing read. No premodern page read, nor any page dating the custom, says where a shrine was sited against wet ground; the two studies of shrine siting found could not be read here. The rule rests on the GM's ruling of 2026-07: "no one would ever put a graveyard - or a shrine - in a marsh".

The limits: the Lushi chunqiu argues for modest funerals and speaks of high ground in general, not of a marsh or a village graveyard, and its next chapter lets a burial lie on low wet ground so long as the grave blends into it, so high ground is a preference there, not a ban on wet ground; the Nagano villages are one basin's archaeology, and the same city history has small villages of that time spreading over almost the whole city and new land being opened up on low wet ground and other land unsuited to paddy; and the 1612 book was written by a Westerner for Chinese readers.

What this means for the maps. Graves and houses keep off the marsh: both are attested before modern times. A shrine keeps off it by the GM's ruling. The well's own rule, that no well is sunk in the bog, is found only in present-day manuals, which the maps do not draw on; the maps keep the well off the marsh only because a well serves the houses beside it and the houses stand on dry ground, which is this project's reasoning rather than a finding.
