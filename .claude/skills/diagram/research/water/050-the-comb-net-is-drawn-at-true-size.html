The comb net is drawn at TRUE SIZE

Sources: lacey-regime, fao-paddy-duty, conghua-2026-design (the lateral's built width), weir-enwiki, mill-race-enwiki, fao-irrigation-canals-ch5

What prompted it. Having sourced the taper law, the same sources let the ABSOLUTE widths be priced: Inashiro's 20.9-acre (~8.5 ha) fan carries roughly 0.025 m3/s at the puddling peak,47 for which Lacey gives a wetted perimeter near 0.76 m6 and Manning a ~0.6 m top width on a hand-dug 1:1 earthen section - call it 2.5 to 5 ft depending on how much freeboard and bank you count as "the channel".176 The GM's ruling (2026-08-17), given the measurement: "we can narrow the widths to be more realistic... update the net to be actual size (at least for hamlets)... it sounds like we want to keep things absolute scale."

How each figure was set. The attested tier ladder is primary and the hydraulic calculation only corroborates it, deliberately: the tiers are measurements of real channels, while a Manning figure rests on four assumed parameters (roughness, grade, section aspect, freeboard) and moves by a factor of two if you nudge them. So each stroke is placed on the attested ladder by the COMMAND AREA it serves - a field ditch watering one paddy ~0.3 m, a distribution lateral ~1 m,48 a district main (yosui) ~5 m140 - and a hamlet fan of 8.5 ha lands its head-race between the lateral and small-main tiers. The result, in true feet:

stroke true width tier it sits on
head-race (whole fan, above the fork) 6.0 small main
canal A (high margin) 4.5 -> 1.5 main dwindling to lateral
canal B (far margin) 4.0 -> 1.5 branch dwindling to lateral
delivery ditch 2.5 -> 1.2 lateral dwindling to field ditch
drain / akusui 1.2 -> 5.5 field ditch gathering to a branch drain

Where the head-race sits on that ladder, and why it is 6.0 ft rather than 5.0. A fan of this size lands its trunk between the lateral and the small-main tiers, which is a band rather than a point, and low in that band the top THREE tiers sit within a foot of each other, so the one junction where the hierarchy most wants to read - the division point where the trunk forks into its two arms - was where it read least. The trunk carries both arms, and under the width-goes-as- the-square-root-of-discharge law this record already adopts, a trunk carrying a 4.5 ft arm and a 4.0 ft arm is the root of the sum of their squares: 6.0 ft. That is TIER SELECTION within the attested band, using the law as a sanity check on where the trunk sits - not conservation at junctions, which is separately ruled against (drawn width is RANK, not discharge: never chain drawn widths down from the source). It also has a real referent: a sluice-fed head-race genuinely is slower than the water feeding it, ponding above the weir (wider is this page's reading; a mill's own race below the pond is the narrow, swift member).171

A collector is sized for drawdown plus storm runoff rather than for the irrigation duty, so it may carry appreciably more water than the supply ever delivered - a reading of the hydrology, not a sourced figure. The maps do not draw it that way: the drain's tail is 5.5 ft against a 6.0 ft head race, so the collector is the wider stroke only against the ditches it collects from, not against the race at the head. Raising the tail above the race was tried and MEASURED on the reference hamlet: the wider band takes the margin the settlement stands on, and the cluster came to rest 102 ft from the field it works, against a 60 ft bar. The figure is left where it is and the relationship is stated as drawn.

The decision, and the one place SCALE enters. Widths are stored in FEET and converted at draw time so the same real channel is the same real size on every sheet - which is what "to-scale" has always meant for every other feature on these maps. The single scale-dependent figure is the floor a channel's stroke may not go below, a foot and a half: true widths are honest at hamlet and village resolution and vanish above it (at a provincial city's scale the terminal tier is no longer a line at all), so a stroke draws at its true width or the floor, whichever is larger. The coarser the sheet, the more of the ladder collapses onto that floor. This is the honest form of the minimum-visibility convention the water-width ladder already sanctions - a floor on the finest tier.

Measured on Inashiro, which draws at a foot to the pixel. Head-race 6.0 ft; canal A 4.5; delivery ditches 2.5 at the head and 1.5 at the tail; drain outfall 5.5. The paddy fabric dominates the sheet and the water reads as a fine engineered net over it, rather than as blue ropes laid across the crop. The hierarchy survives the narrowing (6.0 > 4.5 > 4.0 > 2.5 > 1.5 ft, a 4:1 span) because the floor sits below the finest tier rather than on top of it.

The departure we are taking knowingly. The floor is a real, if small, inflation of the finest tier at hamlet scale: a 1.2 ft delivery tail draws at 1.5 ft rather than 1.2. The GM sanctioned a floor of about 2 ft at the narrowest point; 1.5 was chosen instead precisely so the finest tier still SHOWS a taper rather than arriving pre-flattened at the floor. Re-inflating the FEET to make a coarse-scale map read better would lie about every scale to fix one.
