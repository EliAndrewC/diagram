Where does a field's drain let its water go?

Sources: maff-drain-shape (a drainage channel lets its water go into a river or a natural watercourse); shonairyo-akusuiro-jawiki (a farm drain that carries the spent water of a district its canals watered in the Edo period to a river); nanjing-chengqiang-zhwiki (water carried through a city wall by a conduit and a water gate); the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25; this project's measurements of its drawn maps

Off the map, into a brook, or into a drainage pond. A drainage channel gathers the runoff of the field drains and lets it go into a river or a natural watercourse, and it is laid mainly in the low-lying ground. An older farm drain did the same: one on the Kanto plain was built as the drain of a district that canals watered in the Edo period, its source the fields' spent water and its mouth a river. A drain that ends in a pond of its own is a guess: no page read describes one. Which of these a drain reaches is settled by what lies below it; WHICH END of the collector it leaves by is the question, because that is what says whether the water is running the right way. Each field carries its own fall and each drain is judged against its own field.

The decision (the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25): every discharge channel NAMES the field it drains. A collector then matches its own discharge channels exactly, and nothing is inferred from position.

Read the SINK end, then pool all the evidence and take the LOWEST. A discharge may leave from a point partway along a collector - one map's west drain runs off the map edge from the middle of its run - so the informative end of a discharge channel is its sink: whichever of its two ends lies farther from the drain, falling back to the last point when both lie on it. And a collector legitimately has SEVERAL sinks: that same west drain also tees a relief culvert back to the stream at its far end, and another runs off the bottom of the sheet at BOTH ends. So every end carrying evidence of a discharge - a named channel, a brook alongside, the frame edge - is gathered, and the LOWEST of them is taken as the outfall. The rule then has teeth only on the case that matters: when EVERY piece of evidence puts the discharge at the drain's HIGH end, the water is running backwards.

An outfall at the sheet's edge must be at the DOWNHILL edge. Nearness to an edge is evidence of a discharge only when that edge is downhill. One city comb fan has its HEAD about 84 ft from the top of the frame - inside the 96 ft tolerance, and on the UPSLOPE side - so nearness alone would call that high end an outfall and report the water as running backwards. Nearness to a STREAM is evidence on its own, since a brook is a real sink wherever it lies.

And a TRIMMED in-wall drain is not a contour collector. A drainage ditch inside a city wall is cut off short of the patrol strip and dropped through the wall into a culvert - as a walled city carried water through its wall in a conduit and let a river out by a water gate - so what survives of it on the page is only the last leg to that outfall: one such drain is two points and about 400 ft long where every untrimmed drain on the same map runs 480 to 690 ft. Judging that stub's bearing against "a collector runs along the contour" is a category error, so a drain that was trimmed is exempt from that rule.
