What does the intake mouth look like, and what decides whether a weir is built?

Sources: suido-ishizue-iseki (a bare entrance where the level serves, a weir where it falls), people-zhishui-2025 (the two forms of head works; the mouth at a bend; the gate on the mouth), pwsannong-quxi (the two forms; one region all damless; a wooden gate rebuilt in stone), pwsannong-gudai-shuili (a timber intake gate), thepaper-guangai (gate boards slotted into grooves), hrr-agagawa-dento (timbers laid across a weir and lifted in flood)

The question. Where a hamlet's head race leaves its brook, what did the opening look like - and why does one hamlet build a weir there and another not? The two forms of intake, and why the maps roll between them, are above; this question is about the opening itself and the choice.

The mouth. Where the water stood high enough, the intake was nothing more than an opening: "if a river's flow is ample and its level always high, water can easily be taken just by providing an entrance for it". The Chinese classification says the same of a damless intake, which "uses a bend in the river, opening a mouth at the concave side, and leads water into the canal". On the great canals a gate "sits on the intake mouth of main and branch canals, to open and close the canal and regulate the flow"; early gates were timber, later rebuilt in stone, and at the Mulan weir at Putian the water held was set with gate boards in cut grooves, "adding or removing gate boards" to hold more or less water. At the Hamura intake, timbers laid across the weir let the water in by way of them, and were removed in flood so that the gate would not be destroyed. All of these are great works; what the opening of a village's head race was lined or shut with is not recorded.

What this means for the maps' mouth. The attested village form is the bare one - an opening in the bank - so the head race is drawn opening OUT of the brook's bank, the ditch beginning at the bank's edge, and not as a channel laid on top of the stream. No gate or boards are drawn: none is recorded at a village intake, and at a two-foot opening either would be smaller than the map can show.

What decides between a weir and none. The water does, not chance. The bare entrance serves where the level is high, "but where or when the level falls and water cannot be drawn, something must be placed in the stream to hold it back and raise the level". The Chinese division is the same two forms: "intake head works are of two types, with a dam and without", the one "an overflow dam or diversion dam in the channel", and whole regions could take one form throughout - the Ningxia district on the Yellow River "in ancient times drew its water entirely without dams". No source read counts how many small intakes took each form.

So the knob stands, and its evenness is still a guess. Which form a hamlet built followed from its brook's level through the irrigation season - a brook that kept its level took a bare mouth, one that fell took a weir. The maps give a brook no level or season, so they cannot follow the condition; each hamlet rolls one form or the other with an even chance, and that even chance is a guess.
