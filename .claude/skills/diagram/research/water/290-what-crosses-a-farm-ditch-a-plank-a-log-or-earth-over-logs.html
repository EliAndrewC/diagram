What crosses a farm ditch - a plank, a log, or earth over logs?

Sources: kotobank-marukibashi (the single log laid across), kotobank-ipponbashi (the one-log bridge over a brook or mountain stream), zhwiki-dumuqiao (one trunk or one board, for walkers and livestock), dobashi-jawiki (earth over logs as the common bridge, the plank deck the rare one), xinhua-jiahou-muqiao (a household's logs over a farm canal, in modern times)

The question. The maps lay a small crossing over a farm ditch wherever the water is too wide to step over, and draw it as a planked deck. What that crossing was made of is a question about how a village was built, so it owes the record an answer. What a plank bridge is for, and where one may be laid, are on the ways page; the width at which one is laid is above.

Three forms of crossing over small water are attested. The first is a single log: a Japanese dictionary defines the log bridge as "a bridge that is just a single log laid across", and the one-log bridge as one laid "across a brook or mountain stream" - the size of water a hamlet's brook is. The Chinese single-log bridge is the same object, made "of a single tree trunk or board" and used "to let walkers or livestock cross narrow rivers and gorges", so one board laid across is counted with one log rather than as a different thing. The second is the earthen bridge, logs laid side by side with earth trodden over them, and it was the common one: "until the Edo period the overwhelming majority of bridges over Japan's rivers were earthen bridges", while "plank-decked bridges were limited to an important few". The same article says of a waterway spanned by many small bridges in a Hiroshige print that "small bridges of this kind were mostly built this way". The third is the planked deck the maps draw. The one statement of proportion read makes it far rarer than the earthen bridge, and says nothing of the single log, though that statement is about bridges over rivers, and the article that makes it cites no sources of its own.

Who laid one over a farm ditch. The only account read of a household bridging a farm channel is modern: a memoir of a Chinese rice village, criss-crossed by ditches, in which the grandfather "found four log sections and laid them across the canal as a simple timber bridge", a thing the writer calls "a common practice" there; the logs were slippery in the rain. That canal was 2-3 m wide, several times a delivery ditch, and the memoir is a modern one, so it shows the habit of a farm household laying its own crossing, not what a pre-modern village laid over a two-foot ditch.

What the record does not say. Nothing read says what crossed a paddy ditch in a pre-modern Japanese or Chinese village, at what width a ditch was bridged rather than stepped across, or how often a crossing stood along one. So the width line stays the GM's ruling and the spacing stays a guess, as above.

What this means for the maps. The crossing over small water was made in three ways, and the record cannot say which was laid over a paddy ditch, so the form is a knob rather than a choice: each settlement lays its ditch crossings in one of the three forms - a single log or board, logs under trodden earth, or a planked deck - rolled from the map's seed. No source gives the three forms' shares for a farm ditch, so the roll is even and that evenness is a guess; the one proportion read, for river bridges, would make the planked deck far less likely than logs under earth.

The rule the map follows: a settlement's ditch crossings all take one form, rolled per map with an even chance among a single log laid across, a short deck of logs under earth, and a planked deck. Where and at what width a crossing is laid does not change with the form.
