How was a ditch's water split between villages, and what shows at the split?

Sources: bunsui-jawiki (division passing to villages, and the rotation rules), ishizue-jugo-yosui (why a split is touchy, and widths fixed by rule), kotobank-bunsui (the graduated stake), nagdoren-usonokuchi (a split rebuilt each spring with sandbags), kuji-entobunsui-jawiki (a proportional flume), cylinder-divider-jawiki (the round divider's date), tcmap-huoquan (a Chinese split by counted openings), tabayashi-1987 (the smallest ditches kept by small groups)

The question. Where a ditch forks at a division point, two villages, or two groups of fields, share one supply. How was the water split between them, and is there anything at the fork that a map should draw? What a village weir was built of, and what an intake mouth looks like, are their own questions on this page; this one is the split between users below the intake.

Mostly by rule rather than by structure. In the medieval period the management of water passed from the estate and local lords to leading farmers and village organizations, and the quarrels between areas over how the water was divided grew fierce. The answer was rotation, bansui: the width of each channel and the hours its weir or gate could stand open were fixed place by place, and in dry seasons the flow was measured strictly and each intake adjusted. Even so, the village upstream, which the split favored, and the village downstream quarreled often.

Why a split is so easily cheated. Water runs toward ground lower by a few millimeters; a slight difference in the cross-section where two channels part changes each one's share, and a single stone thrown into the fork changes it sharply. So the rules were written down to the inch. On the Juugou canal off the Kuzuryu River, dug in 1110 and shared by ten villages, the regulations of 1537 fixed a channel at 6.1 shaku wide and 3.21 shaku deep and put the whole under a supervising official; by the Edo period the canal served 118 villages.

What stood at the split: four attested forms.

  • A mouth cut to an agreed width, rebuilt by its users. On one Nagano canal two quarreling villages set their split each spring by unsealing two wisteria vines of different lengths, cutting measuring sticks to them, and building water mouths of sandbags to those widths at a pool on the channel - a custom that lasted until a circular divider replaced it in 1953.
  • A graduated stake, the bunboku: where one channel was divided between two areas or villages, a marked post was set at the division point and the split was made by its height.
  • A proportional flume. At Kuji, on the Nikaryo canal, a flume built in the mid-Edo period divided the canal four ways in proportion to each district's cultivated land - and the quarrels went on.
  • Counted openings, in China. At Huo Spring in Shanxi, iron pillars under a pavilion part the outflow into ten openings, three to the southern county and seven to the northern, and a stele records the split with a diagram on its back. That is a split between two counties, far above a village ditch.

The round concrete divider that splits a canal exactly is modern: the first was built in 1914. It never belongs on these maps.

Inside one village. Below the split - on the Tedori fan, as a 1987 survey found them - the smallest ditches were kept by the small groups of farmers whose paddies they fed, and counted as part of those paddies, and sharing there was a matter of turns, which leave nothing to draw.

The decision it drives. A fork inside one settlement's own fields is drawn as the fork and nothing else: its sharing was by rule and by turn. A split between two settlements is where a work might show, and the record gives two forms a map can draw: an open fork whose mouths were cut, staked or sandbagged to agreed widths (none of that is big enough to draw at a hamlet's scale), and a division flume across the fork (drawn as timber, a guess: no source read gives the Kuji flume's material). That is a knob, rolled per settlement where a map shows a neighbor's ditch leaving its own. How big the flume was is not in anything read; the size below is a guess.

The rule the map follows: no division work is drawn at a fork inside one settlement's fields. Where a ditch forks between two settlements, the map rolls the form from its seed: an open fork (the likelier, a guess at the proportion) or a division flume (timber, a guess), a box across the fork about as wide as the ditch and about 6 ft long along it (a guess). The round divider is never drawn.
