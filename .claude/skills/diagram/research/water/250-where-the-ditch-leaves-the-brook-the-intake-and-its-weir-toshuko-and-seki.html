Where the ditch leaves the brook: the intake and its weir (toshuko and seki)

A hamlet's irrigation ditch began at an intake on the bank of its brook. Japan's word for the works there, toshuko, first named the whole series that took water from a river - the weir, the intake mouth, the settling basin and the division works. The brook was tapped, not swallowed: in old times a weir mostly closed only half the river, and it was the dug canal, below the intake, that divided the water among the fields.

Where a brook ran high enough, the intake was nothing more than an opening in its bank. Where its level fell, something had to be placed in the stream to hold it back and raise it, and that was the weir (seki). The first weirs were built of whatever wood and stone lay near, too fragile to stand anywhere but on small streams, and weirs on small mountain streams go back more than two thousand years. They were fences of stakes woven with brushwood or bamboo, or frames of timber packed with stone.

  • The head works came in a fixed order: weir, intake mouth, settling basin, division works.
    The word toshuko first named that whole series - with the intake mouth a culvert where it crossed an embankment - and only later came to mean the weir alone. The modern term still lists an intake weir, an intake mouth and a settling basin among its parts, and a general irrigation manual gives the same three: an offtake at the side of the river, a regulating structure across all or part of it, and a way to flush out sediment.
  • Weirs on small mountain streams are more than two thousand years old.
    The prototype of the weir that takes a small mountain stream into paddy is placed in the Yayoi period, so weirs have been built for over two thousand years. In the mountains, small streams are dammed and their water led off through small canals, usually for short distances and on gentle slopes.
  • Where did the water divide?
    On the dug canal, below the intake. Water taken by a diversion dam enters a main canal and is passed on into secondary and lesser canals, which tap it at diversion works on the main canal. Every fork we read of is on a dug canal below its intake, and none on the stream itself.
  • The brook runs on below the intake.
    In old Japan the closures across a river were mostly half-river closures. A weir may be built to let water over its crest at all times, and Japanese has a name for that kind, the washing weir (araizeki). The Ishi-ibi works of 1615 at Saga took their water at a sluice and returned the surplus to the main river. A well-made intake takes only the cleaner upper part of the flow and sends the lower part on down the river, and when the river runs high, water flows over the weir.
  • China's two great diversions divide their rivers in stated shares.
    Dujiangyan sends six parts of the Min River into its irrigation channel and four into the flood channel in spring, and the reverse in flood. Lingqu returns seven parts to the Xiang River and passes three into its canal.
  • Did a village's ditch ever take a whole brook?
    A diversion weir is defined as a low dam or wall across a stream to divert "part or all" of its water into a canal, and in an Edo-period drought all the water flowing out of a mountain catchment was taken for farming, each district's return flow used in full by the district below. But no source we read shows a single brook taken whole by a village's ditch.

Where on its bank the intake stood was chosen for the water it could take there.

  • The intake stands on one bank, the side of the land it serves.
    A Japanese lecture on head works makes the one-bank intake the rule on torrents and steep rivers, and the principle on gentle ones.
  • It is set where the current's deepest line runs against the bank.
    A natural intake needs the thalweg against the bank, deep and steady water, and a river always above the level the canal needs; where a weir holds the level, a point where the thalweg touches the bank is enough. The mouth usually sits beside the sand sluice.
  • An offtake is best in a straight reach, or else on the outside of a bend.
    A modern irrigation manual prefers a straight reach, and failing that the outside of a bend, where silt does not settle, though that bank erodes and may need protecting. It lets the offtake leave at an angle where the water is clean, and asks for a right angle or a scour sluice where it carries silt. A Japanese torrent-river intake of 1974, sited just below the middle of a bend's concave bank, took in little sediment.
  • A Japanese treatise of the early twentieth century put the head works where the river leaves the mountains.
    A land-improvement treatise of 1902 advised: "It is best to place the head works generally where the river issues from the mountains."
  • How far did the head race run before it forked?
    No source we read gives a distance. Tabayashi says only that the small mountain canals run "for short distances"; the third-century paddies at Toro, 7.5 hectares (~19 acres), were watered by a canal "more than 370 meters long" (~1,214 ft).
  • Did a village dig a settling basin at the head of its race?
    In modern practice a settling basin follows immediately after the intake mouth, at the start of the canal, reached through an intake court that narrows the flow smoothly from the mouth into the canal. Whether a village dug one is not recorded.

Whether a weir was built at the intake at all was decided by the brook's water.

  • Where the water stood high, the intake was only an opening in the bank.
    "If a river's flow is ample and its level always high, water can easily be taken just by providing an entrance for it." In old times intake weirs were hardly built at all: the intake mouth was sought out where water could be taken naturally, and a canal cut from it. A natural intake puts its mouth on the bank the deep current runs along, and a modern manual likewise finds a simple offtake enough where a river keeps its level all year.
  • Where the level fell, a weir was built to raise it.
    Where or when the level drops so that water cannot be drawn, something must be placed in the stream to hold it back. When a natural intake's river falls, a weir keeps the intake's level up, and some places set up a temporary weir for the intake season alone.
  • China divides intakes into the same two kinds, with a dam and without.
    "Intake head works are of two types, with a dam and without": the first builds an overflow or diversion dam in the channel, the second opens a mouth on the concave side of a bend in the river. A whole region could take one form throughout: the Ningxia district on the Yellow River drew all its water without dams.
  • How was the head of the ancient Zhengguo canal built?
    Scholars read it three ways: as a large earth-and-stone dam, as stone-filled crib baskets or a low weir, or as no dam at all and a diversion off the river's bend.
  • A fourteenth-century Chinese farm treatise describes a palisade across a village brook.
    Wang Zhen, writing in 1313, says that where a brook's banks are deep and the fields lie high, "upstream on the brook one makes a palisade to check the water, causing it to issue out at the side and run down to irrigate".
  • How many small intakes had a weir?
    No source we read counts them.
  • What did a village's intake mouth look like?
    What the mouth of a village's head race was lined or shut with is not recorded. On the great canals a gate sat on the intake mouth to open and close the canal and regulate the flow. Early gates were timber, later rebuilt in stone. At the Mulan weir at Putian, begun in 1064, the water held back was regulated "by adding or removing gate boards" set in cut grooves. At the Hamura intake on the Tama River, timbers laid across the weir let the water in, and were taken away in flood so that the gate would not be destroyed.

A weir on small water was built of what lay to hand.

  • Many old weirs ran obliquely upstream from the intake mouth.
    An oblique weir, "extended long in the diagonally upstream direction from the intake mouth", dams the shallow riffle rather than the deep pool, and stands off the fastest water in a flood, so it is hard to destroy.
  • The first weirs could stand only on small streams.
    Held back with nearby wood and stone, they were fragile, so they were built only on mountain streams with small flow or on small tributaries.
  • Before modern materials, weirs were built of timber frames, gabions and brushwood.
    They were used singly, or combined and covered with earth and gravel. The Tatai weir on the Takahashi River, built in 1183, was of timber frames packed with stone, an oblique weir about 230 m (~755 ft) long closing the whole river; the upper frame at its deep channel was taken out after each irrigation season and set in anew each year. Up to the end of the Sengoku period, in the late sixteenth century, intake works were mostly small, of timber frames packed with stone.
  • A fence of stakes woven with brushwood or bamboo is an ancient weir.
    The shigarami is a row of stakes driven into a river with brushwood or bamboo twined onto them from both sides, to hold back the flow. The word's earliest written examples are a poem of the eighth-century Man'yoshu, which imagines one laid across the Asuka River to dam it, and the Heike monogatari of the early thirteenth century, where one is heaped up as a work of war. A history of Japanese irrigation reads the fence of another Man'yoshu poem as stakes with wood or bamboo laid across, the same build as a weir excavated at the Kodera site - a reading that is the history's own.
  • The oldest weirs dug up in Japan are rows of stakes.
    The oldest is stakes and sheet piles set in a channel at the Itazuke site, from the end of the Jomon period or the start of the Yayoi, and the Kodera weir of the Kofun period is stakes and crosspieces in three tiers, 4.2 m (~14 ft) wide. At Kodera the stakes were driven at a slant, logs laid along them and more stakes driven to hold the logs, the joints tied with wisteria vine and the gaps packed with clay and gravel.
  • An irrigation weir of the early Edo period is said to have been woven onto stakes.
    The Kawaharazono weir at Kanoya, in Kagoshima, built about 1638, is said by local tradition to have been at first either a stone weir or plants woven onto a foundation of wooden stakes. Its brushwood is still laid anew every March, though the weir has stood on a concrete foundation since 1950.
  • Was the weave ever reed before modern times?
    A weir of driven stakes with reed (kaya) woven between them - the grass weir - is still used today at Chiyoda, in Saga, where an agreement with the users downstream rules out a permanent weir. An official handbook divides the Edo-period weirs, almost all built for farm water, into grass weirs and washing weirs, without saying what either was made of. What the premodern record weaves into the stakes is brushwood and bamboo; a weave of reed we found only in the present-day Saga weir.
  • The weirs of large rivers in the Edo period were timber frames weighted with stone.
    Diversion weirs of the Edo period were made of timber frames, of stone, and of kaya-haguchi, a bank-protection work named for its reed, a frame being timber joined with mortise and tenon into a square, set in the river and fixed by stones thrown in from above. These passages are about the weirs and groynes of large rivers, but the build is the same crib of timber and stone that the old village intakes were made of.
  • A gabion is a long basket of stones about 40 to 60 cm (~16-24 in) across.
    It is "a cylindrical basket woven of bamboo, wire or the like, packed with cobbles or rubble", mostly about 40-60 cm (~16-24 in) in diameter and 4-10 m (~13-33 ft) long. The word is in the Japanese-Portuguese dictionary of 1603-04.
  • Were gabions laid across small streams?
    The sources give them for bank protection, for groynes and as weights on river frames, and the only gabions we found damming water were on large rivers.
  • How thick was a village weir?
    No source we read gives the thickness or cross-section of a village weir; the only dimensions found are of river works, frames many meters long. The forms fix a range. A woven stake fence is as thick as its one row of stakes, and a single course of gabions one basket, about 1.3 to 2 ft. A crib is as thick as its frame, which at a village's scale is not recorded.
