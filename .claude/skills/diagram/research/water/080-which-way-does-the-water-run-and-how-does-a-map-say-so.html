Which way does the water run, and how does a map say so?

Sources: the GM's rulings of 2026-07-24 and 2026-07-25; this project's measurement of the drawn pool; canal-enwiki (a canal being level, and the gated chamber that joins it to water at another level), contour-canal-enwiki, contour-trenching-enwiki

Not from the picture. These maps carry no topographic markings at all, deliberately (the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25): they already hold a great deal of visual detail, and contour lines would add clutter faster than they add understanding. So the terrain exists nowhere on the sheet. It exists only in two figures declared for every map - the LAND'S FALL, the direction the ground slopes, and the DRAINAGE BEARING, the direction the water leaves - plus a direction carried by each watercourse. That is exactly why those declarations are mandatory rather than optional: they are the only place the terrain exists at all, and a reader cannot catch by eye what the data does not say. The omission is a map drawing convention, disclosed here because a reader may reasonably wonder where the hills are.

What the declarations are FOR: verisimilitude, not uniformity (the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25, and the standard every later map is held to). The goal is that a map reads like a real place of the kind that actually existed - not that its water all points one way. A map is never flattened into a single flow direction, because real landscapes are not, for two separate reasons. Local topography rises and falls at small scale, so neighboring fields legitimately drain different ways: Tango's fans span 210 degrees between them, and the farms round its moat differ by their own ground - some drain INTO the moat, others have paddies FED BY it. And most of these works are artificial: a ditch dug ALONG a slope rather than down it is precisely what is built to intercept downhill water and keep it out of somewhere, which is why the drainage standard quoted in The wet toe is as wide as the FAN puts a field-toe interceptor drain nearly along the contours. A contour-hugging channel is engineering with a purpose, not an error.

The one hard rule, and the anti-pattern it exists to kill. Water never gains elevation. The GM's named objection to the published maps of the game this setting is built on is a river drawn running parallel to a mountain range along its base - it looks superb and it is impossible. Water at the foot of a slope runs ALONG the valley that slope drains into, descending as it goes; a course that hugs a contour without descending is a canal, and a canal needs an engineered gradient and a reason to exist. So a watercourse running across the fall rather than down it is perfectly allowed - but the reason has to be sayable, and it is recorded: a collector gathering runoff along the low margin, an interception ditch protecting something, a navigation cut held at level. A course with no descent and no purpose is the mistake.

Two figures, not one, because slope and flow genuinely differ. The fall is the LAND'S; the drainage bearing is where the WATER leaves, and a valley floor runs across the fall of the valley sides it lies between, so the two diverge by design. Hoshizora's ground slopes south-southwest at 115 degrees while its valley stream runs 145; Hikari no Sato has a drain 88 degrees off its map's fall. Deriving the flow from the slope would force every stream straight down the fall line, which is wrong for exactly the valley-floor and contour-drain cases the pool already contains.

How far the two may diverge: up to, but never reaching, a right angle. A dug contour canal is laid nearly along the contours, deviating only enough to keep a working gradient - so a divergence approaching 90 degrees is the defining property of a dug channel rather than a defect. What is impossible is 90 degrees or more, which would give the water a net UPHILL component. The bound is therefore a strict "under 90" and not a tighter tolerance. A tighter bound was priced and declined: an earlier version of this rule proposed plus or minus 60 degrees, which would have condemned legitimate contour works. The widest real divergences in the drawn pool are 60 degrees (a map's bearing against its fall) and 69 (one course against its map's bearing), so nothing drawn comes near the bound.

The LIMIT of both angle rules, measured and ACCEPTED. Each of them compares a local fact - one map's fall, one course's heading - against a SINGLE map-level bearing, and confluence geometry can legitimately break that. A bank settlement whose land falls perpendicular into a trunk river that runs along it has a fall and a drainage bearing 90 degrees apart or more with nothing wrong anywhere, and Nagahara is exactly that shape. The rules are kept anyway, because the defect they actually catch is a REVERSED direction - a 180-degree error, which no legitimate geometry produces - and because no drawn map comes near the bound. What that costs: a future bank-sited map can trip one of them while being entirely correct. The alternative declined: loosening or dropping the bound, which would give up the reversed-direction catch that is the whole of their value. If a bank map ever does trip one, the bearing model is revisited; the map is not "fixed".

A known gap, ACCEPTED rather than closed: the two provincial cities declare no land fall. They declare a drainage bearing like every other map, and no fall, and that is deliberate - so nothing judges their drainage slope. A sweep of every bearing at 10-degree steps found NO satisfying value for Tango and EXACTLY ONE for Nagahara - 350 degrees, the land falling east into the river - and that one is curve-fitting rather than terrain: two of Nagahara's own three comb fields declare falls of 100 and 95 degrees, southward, so a map-level east bearing contradicts the map's own geometry. What was accepted: the two largest maps stay unjudged on slope rather than carry a fictitious single bearing. What it costs: nothing checks their drainage slope. The alternative declined: adopting the 350 to turn them green.

The one watercourse with no downstream direction to declare. A cargo canal is cut at the level of the water it joins, which is what lets a barge be poled BOTH ways along it: its gradient is nil, a water gate holds it at river level, and a dead-end dock basin has no through-flow at all. Canals need to be level, and where one meets a river or the sea at a different level the device that connects them is a gated chamber, the pound lock - which is in period for this setting, the double-gated form dating from tenth-century China. Giving such a cut a downstream bearing would be a fiction.

The rule the map follows: Town and city tier, where no generator draws the settlement yet. Every map declares its land fall and its drainage bearing, and every watercourse on it declares which way its water runs; a moat declares an inlet and an outlet instead (below). A navigable canal declares that it is held at a LEVEL, and the drainage-bearing rule then skips it - Nagahara's canal runs 187 degrees against that map's 90-degree bearing and is correct, because it is a navigation cut and not a drain. No map carries contour lines or any other topographic marking.
