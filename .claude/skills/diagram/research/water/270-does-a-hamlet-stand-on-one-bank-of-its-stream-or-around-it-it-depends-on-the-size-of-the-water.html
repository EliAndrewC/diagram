Does a hamlet stand on one bank of its stream, or around it? It depends on the size of the water

Sources: imazato-1999 (one hamlet on a river terrace, and what its inhabitants call the far bank), harie-syozu and jawiki-harie (a settlement on one bank of its river with its own stream through the middle), mizu-no-bunka-60 (the channel that runs alongside the house), jawiki-senjo-shuraku (the both-sides that is a street, not a watercourse), jawiki-shijin-soo (the water on one side of a site), hu-shangantang (water on one side of a village), hu-shaanxi-villages (streams that flow around and past a village), ndl-yajima-shuraku (a farming linear village's houses on one side of its street or both), hirasawa-murazakai (villages named by their side of a river)

The question. The maps keep a hamlet's houses on one bank of the brook that passes it. That is a rule about how a place was built, so it owes the record an answer.

Against a river, one bank is what the record shows. Harie in Shiga "lies on the left bank of the alluvial fan at the lower reaches of the Ado River, the largest river in the Kosei region".120 A settlement-geography study of a hamlet on a river terrace records the hill behind the settlement as the Urayama, the "back mountain", and the hill across the river as the Mukoyama, the "facing mountain" - the settlement's houses and its own hill on one side, and the far side named as the far side.121 And on the Tenryu the boundary between villages lay, by custom, wherever the river's main current ran - a moving boundary, which an Edo-period suit over the river's islands was fought across, the parties lying on opposite banks.122

Against the settlement's OWN channel, the record shows the opposite. The same page that puts Harie on one bank of the Ado says the Harie Okawa "flows through roughly the center of the district".123 Its waterways are "the waterways within the settlement", feeding a named stream down its middle,124 and an account of the kabata water-huts describes "the channel that flows alongside the house".125 Harie's spring water, with the neighboring Shimofuri district, has been selected as a national Important Cultural Landscape,131 which suggests a form that is valued rather than an oddity. Its limit: this half of the answer rests on ONE place. The district's own site, the encyclopedia article on Harie and a magazine visit are three accounts of Harie rather than three settlements, and none of them dates the layout of the channels.

So the two are not rival answers. They are answers about watercourses of two different sizes: a settlement stands beside a river and around its own channel. A brook seven feet wide, tapped by the hamlet it passes, is the second kind.

What the siting doctrine adds, and its limits. The four-gods formulation wants "flowing water on the left" of a site, with hills behind and a pond in front;126 a Chinese village-landscape study describes Shanggantang as "mountains on three sides, water on one side";127 and a survey of Shaanxi villages describes one village with a stream on each side, east and west, that "flow around and past the village".128 None of the three passages contemplates a site divided by its water, and no source read states one either way (searched 2026-09-12: the Japanese and Chinese village-siting literature above). Its limit: this is siting doctrine for houses and capitals, and village-landscape scholarship, not a rule anyone states for a farming hamlet's brook. One trap is worth naming: a LINEAR settlement is one whose buildings, in most cases, are "lined up on both sides of a single street",129 and the standard definition of the farming linear village allows houses "on one side of it or on both"130 - but the thing with two sides there is a road, and neither says a settlement straddles a stream.

What the maps do, and why it is a guess. A hamlet is seated on one bank: a field margin the brook divides is struck out, and a homestead may not be seated across the water from the houses already standing. That is NOT the attested form for a brook at this size - the record shows the opposite at this scale - so it is labeled a guess. It stands for a reason in how the maps are drawn rather than a historical one, and the reason is worth a reader knowing: nothing in these maps can draw a path across a stream. The maps treat a watercourse only as ground to keep clear of, so no lane is ever routed over one and a bridge is only ever drawn where a path already crosses water. A hamlet seated astride its brook would therefore be a hamlet with houses cut off from their own wells and neighbors. The day a lane can cross a brook, which bank a hamlet builds on can differ from one map to the next instead of being fixed.
