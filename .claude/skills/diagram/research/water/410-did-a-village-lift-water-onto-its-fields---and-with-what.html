Did a village lift water onto its fields - and with what?

Sources: kotobank-fumiguruma (the treadle wheel, the swing bucket, and how little Japan lifted), fumiguruma-jawiki (the treadle wheel's dates and sizes), settsu-fumiguruma (one surviving wheel measured), ryukotsusha-jawiki (the chain pump in Japan), longgu-shuiche-zhwiki (the chain pump in China), tiangong-shuili (the noria and the pumps' daily rates)

The question. The fields on these maps are watered by gravity: a ditch leaves a stream above the fields and runs down to them. Where the water lay below the paddy, a farmer had to lift it. Did a village do that, with what, and would a map show it?

In Japan, little. Japanese paddies were generally watered easily by channels or simple weirs, so they depended little on lifting; the chain pump used in the seventeenth century was hard to make and did not spread, and the treadle wheel that followed spread only in the reclaimed lowlands and new-field districts downstream, where new channels were hard to dig. The chain pump - the "dragon-bone wheel", from China - was trodden by two people facing each other, used mainly around the Kinki region early in the Edo period, and driven out by the treadle wheel.

The treadle wheel. Made in Osaka in the Kanbun era (1661-72), it had spread through the provinces by 1751-1780; the one-person wheel came in three sizes, 4.5, 5 and 5.5 shaku, beside a larger two-person wheel and a small hand-turned one. It was used almost only for paddies, and it was not fixed in place: it was carried to wherever it was needed. It lifted water about a yard at most from ditch to paddy, two or three were set in series for a higher paddy, and one person worked it where the chain pump had needed two. A surviving wheel in Settsu is 164 cm across with 17 blades, lifts a few tens of centimeters, and could be taken apart and carried to the field by one person; for draining floodwater two-person wheels and a three-tier rig trodden by six were used. Where a farm had no wheel, two people swung a bucket on two pairs of ropes to scoop ditch water up into the paddy.

In China, much more. The chain pump dates from about the Eastern Han, improved and spread further in the Song dynasty, and suits the plains, lifting about 1 to 2 meters from a canal onto the fields by human or animal power. A Ming encyclopedia of technology gives the scale: by a river a noria, with a weir turning the current beneath it, runs day and night and waters a hundred mu; at a still pond the chain pump, two zhang long or half that, is turned by an ox or trodden by several people; one person's day waters 5 mu and an ox's twice that; a small hand pump a few chi long waters 2 mu a day; and the well-sweep and windlass do less still.

How many a village had is not in anything read.

The decision it drives. The comb field these maps draw is gravity-fed from above, so it needs no lifting, and the Japanese devices that did the lifting were portable tools carried out to a paddy's edge and home again, not fixtures - a map does not draw a tool. Two forms are fixtures and could be drawn, both only where a settlement's fields lie ABOVE the water they draw from: a chain-pump station at the lip of a polder or a low terrace, and, on a river bank, a noria in the stream with its trough to the fields. A polder is the likeliest such ground on these maps, and in Japan even there the lifting was done with the portable wheel.

The two traditions differ, so where fields do stand above their water the form is a knob rolled per settlement: the Japanese form, the carried wheel, which leaves nothing to draw, or the Chinese form, a fixed pump or a noria.

The rule the map follows: no lifting device is drawn at a gravity-fed field. Where a settlement's fields stand above the water they draw from, the map rolls the form from its seed: nothing drawn (the carried wheel), or one fixed lifting work per feeding ditch at the ditch's lip - a chain pump, about 10 to 21 ft long (1 to 2 Ming zhang; the conversion takes the zhang at about 3.2 m, a figure not cited here) up the bank, or, beside a river with current, a noria. The proportion between the forms, and one work per ditch, are guesses.
