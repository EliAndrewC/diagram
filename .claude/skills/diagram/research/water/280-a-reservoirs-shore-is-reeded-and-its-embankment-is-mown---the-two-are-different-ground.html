A reservoir's shore is reeded, and its EMBANKMENT is mown - the two are different ground

The question: is a reservoir's shore reeded, or is its bank dry ground? The intuitive answer is dry ground, and it was tested first: a working reservoir is maintained - the silt dredged, the water often let out after the rice harvest - so perhaps a managed tameike's margin is kept clear, and reeds there would mean neglect.

The record contradicts that outright, and the contradiction is the finding. Mineta 2007 in the Journal of the JSIDRE, reporting a Kagawa Prefecture study of tameike management against aquatic flora: "a significant positive correlation was recognized between the number of emergent and floating-leaved plant species appearing and silt dredging and the cutting of weed within the pond" (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「抽水植物や浮葉植物の出現種数に対して泥上げや池内の藻刈りとの間に有意な正の相関が認められた」) - a POSITIVE correlation - and while the water plants of tameike are in decline overall, with abandoned management named as a cause, the smaller the pond, the further its water use and its embankment mowing have been given up or cut back. Active management goes with more emergent species at the margin, not fewer - it is not what clears the fringe. So the reeds stay, and they stand on wet ground.

The embankment is not the shore. Why the reeds stop at the foot of the bank, and what the bank itself carries, is its own question, Why is a reservoir's embankment bare of reeds?

What the shallow shore carries, positively. MAFF's own technical material draws the zonation from land to open water as riparian forest -> wet-meadow plants -> emergent plants -> floating-leaf -> submerged, and names the emergent belt as ヨシ・ガマ・マコモ (reed, cattail, wild rice); Mineta divides the pond's plants by water depth and slope: 「The aquatic plants growing in a tameike can be divided, according to water depth and slope, into large emergent plants such as yoshi (common reed) and gama (cattail); small emergent plants such as tachimo (Ussuri water milfoil) and harii (spikerush), which become submerged plants depending on the growing season and the setting; submerged plants such as itomo (Potamogeton pondweed) and subuta (Blyxa); floating-leaf plants such as hishi (water chestnut) and asaza (fringed water lily); and free-floating plants such as ukikusa (duckweed) and sanshoumo (water fern) (Hamashima 2001).」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「ため池に生育する水生植物は水深や傾斜によって,ヨシやガマなどの大型抽水植物,生育時期や環境によっては沈水植物となるタチモやハリイなどの小型抽水植物,イトモやスブタなどの沈水植物,ヒシやアサザなどの浮葉植物,そしてウキクサやサンショウモなどの浮遊植物に分けることができる(浜島2001)。」). A tameike museum in Hyogo says the same of lowland ponds specifically: 「Tameike in the plains are dish ponds (sara-ike) ringed about by embankments; the water is shallow and eutrophication of the water quality has advanced. Around such ponds, large emergent plants such as yoshi (common reed), makomo (Manchurian wild rice) and himegama (lesser cattail) form colonies, and the water surface is covered by floating-leaf plants such as hishi (water chestnut), onibishi (large water chestnut) and onibasu (prickly waterlily).」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「平野部にあるため池は、まわりを堤防に囲まれた皿池で、水深は浅く水質は富栄養化が進んでいます。このような池の周囲にはヨシ、マコモ、ヒメガマなどの大型抽水植物が群落を形成し、水面はヒシ、オニビシ、オニバスなどの浮葉植物がおおっています。」). The low end is the concrete-armored urban pond - measured, for dragonflies, as six species against 29 to 30 at a satoyama pond full of water plants.

The rule this produces:

  1. A pond fringe is REEDED and reads WET. The wet-tint haze belongs there as much as it does in a waterside bed.
  2. This is not a map drawing convention. It is what the record describes.

Whether that reed was itself cut as a crop is its own question, Was the reed at a reservoir's margin cut as a crop?
