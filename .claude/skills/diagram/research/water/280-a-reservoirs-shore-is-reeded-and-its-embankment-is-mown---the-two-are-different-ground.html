A reservoir's shore is reeded, and its EMBANKMENT is mown - the two are different ground

The question: is a reservoir's shore reeded, or is its bank dry ground? The intuitive answer is the counter-hypothesis, and it had to be tested first: a working reservoir is maintained - the bank repaired, the silt dredged, the water drawn down each season - so perhaps a managed tameike's margin is kept clear, and reeds there would mean neglect.

The record contradicts that outright, and the contradiction is the finding. Mineta 2007 in the Journal of the JSIDRE, reporting a Kagawa Prefecture study of tameike management against aquatic flora: "a significant positive correlation was recognized between the number of emergent and floating-leaved plant species appearing and dredging or the mowing of algae within the pond" (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「抽水植物や浮葉植物の出現種数に対して泥上げや池内の藻刈りとの間に有意な正の相関が認められた」) - a statistically significant POSITIVE correlation between the number of emergent- and floating-leaf plant species and the practice of dredging silt and cutting algae in the pond - and it is the small ponds where water use has stopped and mowing has declined that are LOSING plant diversity. Active management is what sustains the reed fringe, not what clears it. So the reeds stay, and they stand on wet ground.

But the record also draws a line we were already drawing, which is the pleasing part. The EMBANKMENT itself is a different piece of ground and is kept clear: 「the embankment is managed to keep its strength - cultivation on it is forbidden, and mowing and burning-off are carried out to hold back the growth of grass and trees」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「堤は耕作を禁じたり、草刈りや野焼き を行ったりして草木の生育を阻む等、強度を維持するための管理が行われる」) - cultivation is forbidden on it and it is mown and burned to stop woody growth, to keep the bank strong - and the plants named as growing there are dry-grassland herbs (ワレモコウ, キキョウ, リンドウ, オミナエシ - great burnet, balloon flower, gentian, golden lace - several of the seven autumn grasses), not wetland ones. The dam's water-contact slope is armored outright against wave erosion - "riprap, stone facing, concrete-block facing" (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「捨石、石張り、コンクリートブロック張」). This is an INDEPENDENT confirmation of the rule the GM's own complaint produced on 2026-08-28 ("the hazy blue that denotes the marsh is clearly overlaid on top of the greenery of the earthen mounds"): reeds keep off a raised, maintained, planted bank and root in the shallow water outside it. We drew that rule from the GM's eye; the record says the same thing, and gives the reason - the bank is mown to keep it strong.

What the shallow shore carries, positively. MAFF's own technical material draws the zonation from land to open water as riparian forest -> wet-meadow plants -> emergent plants -> floating-leaf -> submerged, and names the emergent belt as ヨシ・ガマ・マコモ (reed, cattail, wild rice); Mineta puts 「The aquatic plants growing in a tameike can be divided, according to water depth and slope, into large emergent plants such as yoshi (common reed) and gama (cattail); small emergent plants such as tachimo (whorled water milfoil) and harii (spikerush), which become submerged plants depending on the growing season and the setting; submerged plants such as itomo (Potamogeton pondweed) and subuta (Blyxa); floating-leaf plants such as hishi (water chestnut) and asaza (fringed water lily); and free-floating plants such as ukikusa (duckweed) and sanshoumo (water fern) (Hamashima 2001).」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「ため池に生育する水生植物は水深や傾斜によって,ヨシやガマなどの大型抽水植物,生育時期や環境によっては沈水植物となるタチモやハリイなどの小型抽水植物,イトモやスブタなどの沈水植物,ヒシやアサザなどの浮葉植物,そしてウキクサやサンショウモなどの浮遊植物に分けることができる(浜島2001)。」) in the shallow, gently-sloped part of the pond by depth and slope. A tameike museum in Hyogo says the same of lowland ponds specifically: 「Tameike in the plains are dish ponds (sara-ike) ringed about by embankments; the water is shallow and eutrophication of the water quality has advanced. Around such ponds, large emergent plants such as yoshi (common reed), makomo (Manchurian wild rice) and himegama (lesser cattail) form colonies, and the water surface is covered by floating-leaf plants such as hishi (water chestnut), onibishi (large water chestnut) and onibasu (prickly waterlily).」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「平野部にあるため池は、まわりを堤防に囲まれた皿池で、水深は浅く水質は富栄養化が進んでいます。このような池の周囲にはヨシ、マコモ、ヒメガマなどの大型抽水植物が群落を形成し、水面はヒシ、オニビシ、オニバスなどの浮葉植物がおおっています。」). The low-diversity end of that spectrum is the concrete-armored urban pond, not the farmed one.

The rules this produces:

  1. A pond fringe is REEDED and reads WET. The wet-tint haze belongs there as much as it does in a waterside bed.
  2. Reeds and haze keep OFF the embankment and off a pond's raised bank - because it is mown and burned to keep it strong, which is also why nothing is cultivated on it.
  3. Neither of the above is a map drawing convention. Both are what the record describes.

Deliberately NOT concluded. That the reed at a tameike margin was itself cut as a thatch or screen crop. The reed-harvest economy (yoshizu screens, thatch, an uncut bed going to ruin (荒れる)) is well attested - for Lake Biwa and river reed beds, by an organization named for Lake Biwa. No source, primary or scholarly, was found putting that economy on tameike margins, and extending it there is an inference the sources do not make.

Searched and not found. Nothing read compares the vegetation of a DIKED polder's wet foot to an UNDIKED valley toe as such; the search was run and found nothing that does. The adjacent river-engineering vocabulary exists (堤外地, the unprotected land outside a flood levee, whose default vegetation in that literature is reed bed, against 堤内地, the protected land), but none of it draws that comparison.
