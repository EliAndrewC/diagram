What did a river-plain village build against floods, and where?

Sources: ishizue-waju (villages on natural levees; wells beside the intake inside the ring dike), waju-jawiki (the ring dike's history, the house on its stone base, the refuge storehouse and the communal mound), maff-waju-mizuya (the raised plot and the higher mound), mizuka-jawiki (the mound's height and what it held), goka-mizuka (the horse stone and the boat), cssn-jiangnan-weitian (the Chinese polder)

The question. A village on a river plain floods. What did it build against that - a ring levee around the village, a refuge on a mound, a raised house - and where on the map would those stand?

First, the high ground. On the low plain of the Kiso rivers, farmers from early on put their villages and fields on the natural levees and other slight rises.

Then the ring dike, late and local. The first levees there guarded only the upstream side and stood open downstream, the shirinashi levee; from the seventeenth century strong levees were thrown right round existing villages. Flood control by continuous river levees became general in Japan only from the Meiji era; before it, embankments were built where they were needed to guard particular villages and fields. Ring dikes of the same kind stand along the Shinano, the Arakawa, the Tone and the Yodo, and in the delta, where storm surges struck, the houses crowded onto the highest levee inside the ring - "if the levee breaks, flee to the levee". A waju generally had its intake at the upstream head of the ring and its drain at the tail, but an intake through the dike so often caused a breach that drawing river water was hard, and bored wells into the groundwater came into use beside it.

At the house. Villagers raised their house plots on fill; since that was not enough, they piled a still higher mound inside the plot and built the mizuya on it, anything from a hut to a spare dwelling, and every one of them a store. The waju house stood facing south on a stone base, with the mizuya on a stone base a step higher to its northwest; only fairly wealthy houses had one, and for those that did not there were raised earth mounds, the "life-saving platforms", built together by the community or by a landlord at their own cost - and a shrine on raised ground could serve the same turn. Along the Arakawa and the Tone the same refuge is the mizuka: a storehouse on fill some tens of centimeters to 3 m higher than the main house, rice, miso and soy sauce below and bedding and chests above, and it goes by other names on the Kiso, the Yodo and the Shinano. Beside one, a farm might set a stone to tether the horse, since the plow animals had to be saved too, and hang a small boat under the eaves.

In China, the polder. From the Five Dynasties and the Song dynasty, the Taihu plain was laid out as polders ringed by dikes and cut by channels, "a transverse channel every five or seven li, a longitudinal channel every seven or ten li", and in the Song several polders came to be joined within one dike. That is a field system on the scale of a district.

What was not found. What share of a flood-plain village's farmsteads had a mizuya, beyond "fairly wealthy houses", is not in anything read.

The decision it drives. A village on a flood plain sits on its natural levee; that is the attested first answer, and it is not a knob. Whether a ring dike encloses the village and its fields is one: attested as a regional form of the lowest, most flood-prone plains, and late, so it is rolled per settlement only on such ground. At the farmstead the refuge storehouse on its mound belongs to the better-off households only, and the communal mound or a raised shrine stands in for it for everyone else.

The rule the map follows: a flood-plain village seats its houses on the natural levee, the highest ground by the river. On the lowest plains the map rolls from its seed whether a ring dike is drawn round the village and its fields; a ringed village draws its water from wells as well as through an intake at the upstream head of the dike. The richest farmsteads (a guess at the share: the headman's and one or two more) carry a mizuya: a storehouse on a stone-faced mound 1 to 10 ft higher than the house, to its northwest; a village without enough of them has one communal refuge mound, or its shrine on raised ground.
