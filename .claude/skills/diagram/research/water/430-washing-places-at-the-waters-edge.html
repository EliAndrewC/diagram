Washing places at the water's edge

A well gave a household its drinking water, but the mud off vegetables brought in from the fields, and the household's washing, went to running water. Villages and towns built washing places for it at the edge of a channel, a stream or a spring-fed tank: a footing or steps down to the water, shared by the neighbors on a channel or set at a single house. In the canal towns of China nearly every riverside house had a small landing of its own at its door.

A town drew water from more than its wells. Castle towns had channels led from a river through their streets, with washing places along them, and a town could also pipe its water by bamboo to shared and household wells. No source we read counts a village's washing places or measures one.

  • At Gujo Hachiman, a washing place on a channel is an araiba, and one on the river a kawado.
    A paper on the town's water landscape calls the footings and other works built for washing clothes and vegetables by those two names, by the water they stand on.
  • A board slotted into a channel ponded the water for the day's washing.
    At Gujo Hachiman a board set in a groove in a channel or small stream holds the water back for a while; it is used most for daily washing, and also to split the flow.
  • A spring-fed tank was stepped, the cleanest water at the top.
    Water from a spring, or gathering in a valley from the hills, was piped into a tank of two or three tiers: the top for drinking, the middle for rinsing and washing food, the bottom for dirty things and the mud off vegetables.
  • In Hirosaki, the drinking tanks and the washing tanks were kept strictly apart.
    A government survey of local water customs records spring-fed wells and washing places across the city, still in use for some time after the Second World War.
  • In Fuji, in Saga, ponds reached into the kitchens, and the settlement's channels had washing places with rules for their use.
    The same survey records it.
  • At Harie, on Lake Biwa, the soil was washed off the vegetables at the kabata.
    The district's own page lists it among the uses of the household water basins.

In the canal country of the Lower Yangzi, in China, the water ran past every door, and the washing place came with the house.

  • In the Jiangnan water towns nearly every riverside house had a small landing at its door.
    There the housewife washed the clothes and the vegetables and rinsed the rice, and small boats could moor.
  • Washing, shopping and cooking were all done at the riverside.
    A magazine account of the region's houses says so of its canal towns.

A town's water besides its wells came by channel or by pipe.

  • After a fire in 1652, early in the Edo period, the lord of Gujo cut two canals through his castle town.
    One was dug against fire and one for irrigation, with small channels branching from them; the shared kawado on them are still used as washing places, and each channel has its cleaning rota.
  • The river-fed water systems rulers built into castle towns nearly all doubled as irrigation.
    They ran household and fire-fighting water, and fed the moats and garden ponds as well.
  • A town could also be piped, as Omihachiman was.
    A parent well at the source fed shared wells and each house's own well by bamboo pipe, run by the townspeople themselves - water that surfaces in the town only as wells.
  • How many washing places did a village have, and how wide was one?
    No page we read says.
