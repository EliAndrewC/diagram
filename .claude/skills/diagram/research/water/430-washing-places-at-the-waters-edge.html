Washing places at the water's edge

In Japan, villages and towns built washing places for clothes and vegetables at the edge of a channel, a stream or a spring-fed tank: a footing at the water, shared by the neighbors on a channel or set at a single house. In China's canal towns the washing place was the house's own landing.

A town drew water from more than its wells. Castle towns had channels led from a river through their streets, and at Gujo Hachiman washing places along them, and a town could also pipe its water by bamboo to shared and household wells.

  • At Gujo Hachiman, a washing place on a channel is an araiba, and one on the river a kawado.
    A paper on the town's water landscape calls the footings and other works built for washing clothes and vegetables by those two names, by the water they stand on.
  • At Gujo Hachiman, a board slotted into a channel ponds the water for the day's washing.
    A board set in a groove in a channel or small stream holds the water back for a while. It is used most for daily washing, and also to split the flow.
  • At Gujo Hachiman, a spring-fed tank is stepped, the cleanest water at the top.
    The same town's water is piped from a spring, or from the hills where it gathers in a valley, into a tank of two or three tiers: the top for drinking, the middle for rinsing and washing food, the bottom for dirty things and the mud off vegetables.
  • In Hirosaki, the drinking tanks and the washing tanks were kept strictly apart.
    A government survey of local water customs records spring-fed wells and washing places across the city, still in use for some time after the Second World War.
  • In Fuji, in Saga prefecture, the washing places on the kitchen ponds and the settlement's channels had rules for their use.
    A government survey of local water customs records the rules, at ponds that reached into the kitchens and on the channels through the settlement.
  • At Harie, on Lake Biwa, the soil was washed off the vegetables at each house's kabata.
    The district's own page says most of its houses have their own spring water, and that the kabata was needed for all the water work, washing the vegetables among it.
  • No page we read counts a village's washing places or gives the width of one.
    The pages read describe the washing places and who used them, and stop there.

In the canal country of the Lower Yangzi, in China, the water ran past every door.

  • In the Jiangnan water towns nearly every riverside house had a small landing at its door.
    There the housewife washed the clothes and the vegetables and rinsed the rice, and small boats could moor.
  • In the Jiangnan canal towns, washing, buying food and cooking could all be done at the riverside.
    A magazine account of the region's houses says so.

Back in Japan, a town's water besides its wells came by channel or by pipe.

  • After a fire in 1652, early in the Edo period, the lord of Gujo Hachiman cut two canals through his castle town.
    One was dug against fire and one for irrigation, with small channels branching from them.
  • Gujo Hachiman's town channels still have shared washing places, each channel with its cleaning rota.
    A government survey of local water customs records that the shared kawado on them are still used for washing, and that a cleaning rota is set for each channel.
  • The river-fed water systems rulers built into castle towns nearly all doubled as irrigation.
    They ran household and fire-fighting water, and fed the moats and garden ponds as well.
  • A town could also be piped, as Omihachiman was.
    A parent well at the source fed shared wells and each house's own well by bamboo pipe, run by the townspeople themselves - water that surfaces in the town only as wells.
