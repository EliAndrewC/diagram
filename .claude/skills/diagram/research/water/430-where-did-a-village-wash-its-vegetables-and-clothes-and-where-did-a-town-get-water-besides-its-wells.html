Where did a village wash its vegetables and clothes, and where did a town get water besides its wells?

Sources: gujo-jsce-2017 (the washing places named and described), mlit-mizubunka-sanko (washing places in towns and a village; a castle town's channels; piped town water), harie-syozu (a village's spring-fed washing places), nbgjz-jiangnan-minju and dili360-jiangnan-minju (the Chinese house landing), mizu-no-bunka-12-jokamachi (castle-town water systems)

The question. A well gives drinking water, but vegetables brought in muddy from the fields and a household's washing went to running water. Where did a village do that, how many places did it have, and did a town draw water from anything besides its wells?

The washing place. In Gujo Hachiman the footings and other works built for washing clothes and vegetables are called araiba where they stand on a channel and kawado where they stand on the river; a board slotted into a groove in a channel or small stream ponds the water for the day's washing, and is also used to split the flow; and the spring-fed tanks are stepped, the top for drinking, the middle for rinsing and washing food, the bottom for dirty things and the mud off vegetables. The same pattern turns up across the country in a government survey of local water customs: Hirosaki's spring-fed washing places kept the drinking and washing tanks strictly apart, and in Fuji, a village in Saga, ponds reached into the kitchens and the village's own channels had washing spots with rules for their use. In Harie, on Lake Biwa, the soil was washed off the vegetables from the fields at the kabata.

In China, a landing at every door. In the Jiangnan water towns nearly every riverside house had a small landing at its door, where clothes, vegetables and rice were washed and boats moored; in that canal country washing, shopping and cooking were all done at the riverside.

A town's water besides its wells. After a fire in 1652 the lord of Gujo remodeled his castle town and cut two canals through it, one for fighting fire and one for irrigation, with small channels branching from them; the shared kawado on them are still washing places, and each channel has its cleaning rota. The river-fed water systems that rulers built into castle towns nearly all doubled as irrigation, and fed the moats and garden ponds as well as the households. A town could also be piped: in Omihachiman a parent well at the source fed shared wells and each house's take-well by bamboo pipe, run by the townspeople themselves - water that surfaces only as wells.

What was not found. How many washing places a village had, and how wide the steps of one were, are not in anything read.

The decision it drives. Two forms of washing place are attested - a shared one on the stream or ditch bank, and one at each house on a channel running past it - so it is a knob, rolled per settlement where water passes the houses. A town's water is a second knob: wells alone; channels led from a river through the streets, with washing places along them, in a castle town; or piped water, which shows only as wells.

The rule the map follows: where a stream or ditch passes a settlement's houses, the map rolls from its seed a shared washing place - a footing of steps down the bank, about 4 ft wide (a guess), one for every few households on that bank (a guess) - or, where a channel runs past the houses, one step at each house on it. A town rolls wells alone, or street channels drawn from its river with washing places along them; piped water draws as wells.
