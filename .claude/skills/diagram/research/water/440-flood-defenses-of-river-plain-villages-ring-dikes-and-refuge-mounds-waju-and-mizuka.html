Flood defenses of river-plain villages: ring dikes and refuge mounds (waju and mizuka)

On the low plains where Japan's great rivers come together, floods came often, and villages met them at three scales. They settled on the natural levees, the slight rises a river builds along its own banks. On the lowest plains, from the seventeenth century, they threw a ring dike right round a village and its fields - the waju. And at the house they raised the plot on fill and built a refuge storehouse on a still higher mound, the mizuya or mizuka, stocked with food and bedding.

The refuge stood up to about 3 m (~10 ft) above the house, and only the better-off households had one; the rest had a mound raised in common.

  • Villages on the flood plain stood on the natural levees.
    On the low, poorly drained plain where the three Kiso rivers meet, often flooded, farmers made their villages and fields from early on on the natural levees and other slightly raised ground.
  • The first dikes guarded only the upstream side, and ring dikes came in the seventeenth century.
    The early levees on the Kiso plain stood against the direct blow of the flood on the upstream side and were left open downstream. From the seventeenth century strong levees were thrown right round existing villages over a wide area.
  • Continuous river levees became general in Japan only in the Meiji era, after 1868.
    Up to and through the Edo period the main way of fighting floods was to build levees where they were needed to guard particular villages and fields.
  • Ring dikes stand along other great rivers besides the Kiso.
    Levees enclosing villages and fields also survive in the basins of the Shinano, the Arakawa, the Tone and the Yodo.
  • In the delta, where storm surges struck, the houses crowded onto the highest levee inside the ring.
    Natural levees are little built up in the delta, and its settlements gathered on the highest levee within the ring dike; the saying handed down there is "if the levee breaks, flee to the levee".
  • A ringed village's intake stood at the upstream head of its dike, but wells did much of the drawing.
    A waju generally had its intake at the head of the ring and its drain at the tail. An intake through the dike so often caused a breach that drawing river water was hard, and bored wells into the groundwater came into use instead.
  • Farmers raised their house plots on fill, and a storehouse on a mound higher still.
    Raising the plot was not enough to keep the water out, so inside the grounds they piled a still higher mound and built the mizuya on it. Every one was once used as a store, and they ranged from huts to spare dwellings.
  • The refuge stood northwest of a south-facing house, a step higher on its own stone base.
    The house of a waju faced south on a stone base against the rising water, with the mizuya to its northwest on a stone base one step higher.
  • How much higher than the house was the refuge?
    Anything from some tens of centimeters to 3 m (~10 ft). Along the Arakawa and the Tone the refuge is the mizuka, a storehouse or other building on fill piled that much higher than the main house. It goes by other names on the Kiso, the Yodo and the Shinano.
  • The refuge held food below and household goods above.
    Rice bales, miso barrels and soy sauce went on the ground floor; bedding, low tables and the chests and wicker trunks of clothing went upstairs.
  • The farm animals were saved too.
    Until farm machinery spread nearly every house kept a horse or an ox for the fields, and some set a stone to tether it beside the mizuka. Some hung a small boat under the eaves for getting away and carrying things.
  • How many farmsteads had a refuge storehouse?
    Only fairly wealthy houses had one, and no page we read counts the share of a village's farmsteads that had one.
  • Households without a refuge had a mound raised in common, or a shrine on raised ground.
    For those who could not have a mizuya there were raised earth mounds called life-saving platforms, made jointly by the people of the district or sometimes by a landlord at their own cost, and a shrine on ground piled above its surroundings could serve the same turn.

On the Taihu plain of south China the low ground was enclosed on a far larger scale.

  • From the Five Dynasties and the Song dynasty, the Taihu plain was laid out as polders ringed by dikes and cut by channels.
    The pattern was "a transverse channel every five or seven li, a longitudinal channel every seven or ten li", and in the Song several polders came to be joined within one dike.
