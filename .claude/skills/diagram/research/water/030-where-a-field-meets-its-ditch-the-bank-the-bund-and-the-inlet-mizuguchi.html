Where a field meets its ditch: the bank, the bund and the inlet (mizuguchi)

A rice paddy is a basin of level, flooded ground, held in on every side by a low bank of earth, the bund, heaped from the paddy's own mud so the water does not leak away. Where a ditch runs beside a paddy, the bund on that side is the ditch's bank. Water comes in through a small opening in the bund, the inlet, and leaves through another, the outlet; paddies were built that way in Japan from their beginnings. Below the last ditch the water often needed no channel at all: it passed from paddy to paddy through the bunds.

The bund between two paddies was about a foot wide: an Edo land survey reckoned one at 1 shaku, about 30 cm (~1 ft). The bund along a canal was wider, about 3 to 5 ft at the one early site where it was measured. The bunds between paddies themselves are at Bunds between the paddies (aze), the village's intake from the brook at Where the ditch leaves the brook: the intake and its weir (toshuko and seki), and the scrub kept back from a channel's bank at The cut bank.

  • Below the last ditch, water passed from paddy to paddy.
    Japanese paddy can be watered plot to plot: water enters the head paddy of a string and runs down through the bunds from one plot to the next, so the plots inside the string have no ditch of their own. A modern paddy draws from a supply canal and lets its water out into a drain, while the old way is still kept at Tashibu-no-sho Osaki, in Oita.
  • When did every paddy get a ditch of its own?
    Around 1900, as Japan began consolidating its paddy land. A 1948 essay on the history of Japanese irrigation says that only with the amendment of the land consolidation law in 1910 were paddy plots and irrigation channels properly arranged, and that before it most irrigation had flowed through from field to field - though it gives this as a consequence and cites no evidence for it. In the Konosu model, begun in 1902, each plot of about 54 m (~177 ft) by 18 m (~59 ft) touched its irrigation and drainage channel and a road.
  • On one Japanese fan, the smallest ditches were counted as part of the paddies they watered.
    Tabayashi records, for the ditches of the Tedori fan as rebuilt in the early 1900s and seen in 1976, that the smallest ditches bringing water to the plots' inlets are run by small groups of farmers and are often considered parts of the paddies they serve.
  • Openings in the bunds are as old as Japan's paddies.
    The excavated paddies of the Hattori site in Shiga, from the start of the Yayoi period, already had water openings set in their bunds and channels. A general history of Japan's water works gives paddies with intakes and outlets fitted with weirs from the Late Jomon on. The inlet has a name, the mizuguchi, and a rite held at a paddy's inlet is in a poem of about 1134.
  • How wide was a paddy's inlet?
    Under 50 cm (~20 in), by modern design practice - the only source of a size. No page from before 1868 we could read gives one. Modern practice asks for one inlet per plot, two or more only where they would stand 50 m (~164 ft) or more apart, at most 50 cm (~20 in) wide, its sill 0-10 cm (~0-4 in) above the field so the inflow does not scour the bed, and water passing it at about 0.4 m (~1 ft) a second.
  • The outlet, the shirimito, is an overflow set below the field.
    Modern practice sets its sill 5-10 cm (~2-4 in) below the field surface and makes it an overflow, so it holds the depth of water on the paddy and stores water there in heavy rain. No page we read gives its width.

The ditches that bring the water down to the plots were graded by size, from the canal to the last ditch before the paddies, in China's oldest texts and in the modern standards of both China and Japan.

  • The Rites of Zhou name five field channels, each twice as wide as the one below.
    The smallest, the quan, is the cut a pair of spades makes, 1 chi wide and deep - about a foot or less; the largest leads directly into the rivers. The same text lays out the countryside with the smallest of them, the sui, between single farmers' allotments, and a larger one for every ten, hundred and thousand, each with a path along it.
  • Modern standards end the ladder at a channel with a width of its own.
    Modern China names five grades - main, branch, lateral, farm and field. The first three are the canal system and the last two the distribution inside the fields, and the smallest is laid out to the size of the paddy and may be dug by the farmers themselves. Japan's design vocabulary has three - trunk, branch and terminal canal, and one canal's own description names its trunk and branch canals the same way. In none of these ladders does the last rung dwindle to nothing. The widths of the rungs are at How wide canals and ditches are: the ladder of channel widths.

Where a ditch did run beside a paddy, the paddy's bund was its bank, and the oldest paddies dug up in Japan show it.

  • A paddy's bund beside an open ditch has a name of its own.
    Japanese distinguishes the bund between a paddy and the open channel beside it as a ditch bund (kohan). The channel is often public land, and the boundary is sometimes set at the paddy-side foot of that bund or at the water line.
  • Early paddies in Japan were built with a large bund along the canal.
    At the Hattori site, paddies of the first years of the Yayoi period, the central canal is enclosed by large bunds, which ran along it and seem also to have served as paths for the farm work. At the Sena site in Shizuoka, from the Late Yayoi into the Kofun period, the channel banks and the large bunds were both reinforced with rows of stakes and sheet boards.
  • The classical Chinese layout put a path along every channel.
    The Rites of Zhou put a path on the bank of every grade of field channel, from the smallest up, and a Tang-dynasty sub-commentary says the path on the smallest takes no cart, but oxen, horses and people on foot. It is an ideal scheme, not a survey.
  • An Edo aqueduct kept a protected strip along each side.
    A notice board of 1739 forbade cutting the trees, the undergrowth or the grass within 3 ken (about 18 ft) on each side of the Tamagawa aqueduct. It was a city's aqueduct, not a farm canal, and a strip kept from cutting rather than a bare bank top.
  • How wide was the bank along a village's own supply canal?
    No record from before modern times we could find gives it. The nearest figure is the Hattori site's large bund, 80-150 cm (~3-5 ft) wide with a flat top, and it has plain limits: one site of tiny plots, far older than the Edo period, whose use as a path is the excavators' "seem".
  • How wide was a bund between paddies?
    About a foot. An Edo land survey reckoned a bund at 1 shaku, about 30 cm (~1 ft), and took it out of the taxed area; the dictionary's example, a shogunate order of 1726, reckons that deduction with the adjoining land at 1 shaku 5 sun (a sun is a tenth of a shaku), about 45 cm (~18 in). The Hattori site's small bunds were 20-60 cm (~8 in to 2 ft) wide and not for walking on. A modern standard bund is 40 cm (~16 in) across its crest and 30 cm (~1 ft) high, at 1:1 side slopes.
  • The bund along a canal was wider than the bunds between plots.
    At the Hattori site the large bunds along the canal were 80-150 cm (~3-5 ft) wide, against the small bunds' 20-60 cm (~8 in to 2 ft). No width was found for a bund along a canal in the Edo period.
  • A bund cannot stand in its ditch.
    The bund is what holds a paddy's water in and the drain is what carries it away, so a bund across the drain would dam it. Where the ditch beside a paddy is the field's drain, then, the paddy's lowest bund stands on the drain's bank and runs with it - this project's reading of the ditch-side bund, which the source does not state.
  • Which paddies had a drain beside them?
    On a field watered from paddy to paddy, the drain would run along the field's lower edge rather than past every plot, which only the consolidated paddy of about 1900 was laid out to do, so only the row of paddies on that edge had one beside it. This is this project's reading of the two sources, which neither states outright.
