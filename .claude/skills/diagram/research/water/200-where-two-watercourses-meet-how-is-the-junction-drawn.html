Where two watercourses meet, how is the junction drawn?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-08-28

As ONE body of water, with no seam. Water on these maps is drawn semi-transparent, so two strokes laid down independently double up where they cross and leave a darker patch exactly at the junction - and a pond's dark shore rim drew a hard border straight across the mouth of every channel feeding it. The GM, 2026-08-28: "when a stream meets a irrigated channel or where an irrigated channel or a ditch meets a pond ... it clearly looks like one is rendered on top of the other ... water just flows", and the pond's rim "should not be present at the place of their intersection".

The decision. Every watercourse on a map is laid down in ONE pass, at the latest position any of them needs. Bottom to top: every pond RIM first, then every BED in a single shared transparency group with the pond's own fill last among them, then every SHEEN. So overlapping beds read as one continuous body of water - a crossing, or a feeder running into a moat, reads as a real confluence rather than as two lines laid over each other; a pond's rim lies under every bed that reaches it, so the shore opens exactly at each mouth; and because the pond's fill is painted last among the beds, it covers the small overshoot a feeder's rounded end makes inside the rim.

The label. This is a map drawing convention and nothing more. It changes how the ink is composited and never where any water lies, and it applies to every map at every tier. The village lanes took the same route, drawing through one shared ground block so that a junction reads as a single tread instead of one lane's shoulder crossing the other's.
