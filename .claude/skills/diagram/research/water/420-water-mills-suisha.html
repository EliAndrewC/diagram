Water mills (suisha)

A water mill used a stream to turn a wheel that drove pestles pounding rice in mortars, or millstones grinding grain. In Japan they are said to have come into full use in the Edo period, as the eating of polished white rice spread, and multiplied from the middle of it to feed the brewers and the crowded castle towns. The plateau west of Edo had 33 in 1788, and Japan had 60,000 by 1897.

A mill was most often a wealthy farmer's or a village official's, set on the village's common water. In China, a large household pounded its own rice with a treadle pestle, and the water pestle belonged to the hill country by the rivers.

  • Japan's water mills are said to have come into full use in the Edo period.
    As the habit of eating white rice spread, power water wheels were used to polish rice and mill grain.
  • The mills were used mainly for pounding rice and pressing rapeseed oil.
    A large wheel drove many pounding mortars at once.
  • Mills multiplied from the middle of the Edo period, with the brewing trade and the castle towns.
    Behind their growth lay the growth of the brewing trade and the crowding of townsfolk into castle towns.
  • Around Edo, rice-polishing mills were built in the villages near the rice wholesalers at the city's entrances.
    Rice wholesalers lined the entrances of the five highways into Edo, and in the villages around them, such as Shibuya and Meguro, mills were built to polish rice.
  • In the Musashino region west of Edo, the shogun's own land, even one mill needed a license.
    The region was the shogunate's direct domain, and building a single mill needed the permission of the intendant's office.
  • Licensed mills there multiplied in 1772-1780, in the middle of the Edo period.
    The same history dates the increase in properly licensed mills to those years.
  • In 1788, late in the Edo period, the Musashino plateau west of Edo had 33 water mills.
    A record of the water supply counts them.
  • By 1897, in the Meiji era, Japan had 60,000 water mills.
    The national statistics of that year count them.
  • Most mills belonged to wealthy farmers and village officials, and villagers objected.
    The owners were using for themselves the water that was the village's common resource, and the villagers objected hard.
  • Japanese mills were often used in common by the whole village.
    The same history, after naming the owners, says Japan's water mills were often used in common by the village.
  • One stream could carry two mills a short way apart.
    On the Nogawa, a tributary of the Tama River, a mill built in 1808, late in the Edo period, stood about 200 m (~656 ft) downstream of one built by the headman of Osawa village, called the "big mill", and so it was named the "new mill".
  • In 1903, in the Meiji era, one village mill on the Nogawa worked ten pounding mortars and a millstone.
    The Nogawa's "new mill", built in 1808, had 10 pounding mortars and one grinding millstone that year.

In China the record runs from the household's own mortar to rows of water pestles on a river.

  • A large Chinese household pounded its own rice with a treadle pestle.
    The Tiangong kaiwu, a Chinese craft manual, says a household of eight or more buried a stone mortar in the ground and pounded with a beam trodden at one end.
  • The water pestle was built by hill people who lived by rivers.
    The Tiangong kaiwu says it was made by the people of mountain country who lived on river banks.
  • A water pestle worked two or three mortars on a small stream and ten on a large one.
    Where the flow was small and the ground cramped it had two or three mortars; where the flow was large and the ground wide, ten side by side.
  • In one prefecture of Jiangnan, the water pestle stood on a boat moored in the stream.
    The boat was tied with stakes, earth was heaped in it and the mortars sunk into the earth, with a small stone weir built in midstream.
  • Some water wheels ground flour, pounded rice and lifted water to the paddies at once.
    The first stage turned a millstone, the second worked the pestles, and the third lifted water to irrigate the paddies.
  • The Tiangong kaiwu says some villagers in water-pestle country never saw a husking mill.
    Where water pestles worked, it says, some grew old and died without seeing one.
  • In China, wheat was ground by ox, donkey or hand.
    A large mill was turned by a strong ox, a lesser one by a donkey, and the smallest was pushed by hand.
  • The Tiangong kaiwu reckons a water mill three times as handy as an ox mill.
    The water mill was built like the water pestle, and was "three times as convenient as oxen".
