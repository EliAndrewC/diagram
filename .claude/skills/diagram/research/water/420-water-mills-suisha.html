Water mills (suisha)

A water mill used a stream to turn a wheel that drove pestles pounding rice in mortars, or millstones grinding grain. In Japan they came into full use in the Edo period, as the eating of polished white rice spread, and multiplied from the middle of it to feed the brewers and the crowded castle towns. The plateau west of Edo had 33 in 1788, and Japan had 60,000 by 1897.

A mill was most often a wealthy farmer's or a village official's, set on the village's common water. In China, a household pounded its own rice with a foot-trodden beam, and the water-driven pestle belonged to the hill country by the rivers.

  • Japan's water mills came into full use in the Edo period.
    As the habit of eating white rice spread, power water wheels were used to polish rice and mill grain.
  • The mills were used mainly for pounding rice and pressing rapeseed oil.
    Their growth from the mid-Edo period was driven by the brewing trade and by the crowding of townsfolk into castle towns, and a large wheel drove many mortars.
  • Mills clustered in the villages around where a city ate.
    Rice wholesalers lined the entrances of the five highways into Edo, and in the villages around them, such as Shibuya and Meguro, mills were built to polish rice.
  • On the shogun's own land, a mill needed a license.
    The Musashino region west of Edo was the shogunate's direct domain, and even one mill needed the permission of the intendant's office. Licensed mills multiplied in 1772-1780.
  • In 1788, late in the Edo period, the Musashino plateau west of Edo had 33 water mills.
    A record of the water supply counts them.
  • By 1897, in the Meiji era, Japan had 60,000.
    The national statistics of that year count them.
  • Most mills belonged to wealthy farmers and village officials, and villagers objected.
    The owners were using for themselves the water that was the village's common resource, and the villagers objected hard; yet Japanese mills were often used in common by the village.
  • One stream could carry two mills a short way apart.
    On the Nogawa, a tributary of the Tama River, a mill built in 1808 stood about 200 m (~656 ft) downstream of one built by the headman of Osawa village, called the "big mill", and so it was named the "new mill". In 1903 the new mill had 10 pounding mortars and one millstone.

In China the record runs from the household's own mortar to rows of water-driven pestles on a river.

  • A Chinese household pounded its own rice.
    A household of eight or more buried a stone mortar in the ground and pounded with a beam trodden at one end.
  • The water-driven pestle belonged to hill country by the rivers.
    It was built by mountain dwellers who lived by rivers: two or three mortars where the stream was small and the ground cramped, ten side by side where the flow was large. In one prefecture of Jiangnan it stood on an earth-filled boat moored in the stream, and some wheels ground flour, pounded rice and lifted water to the paddies at once. In river country with water pestles some villagers lived and died without seeing a husking mill.
  • Wheat was ground by ox, donkey, hand or water.
    A large mill was turned by a strong ox, a lesser one by a donkey and the smallest by hand; the water mill, built like the water pestle, was three times as handy as the ox.
