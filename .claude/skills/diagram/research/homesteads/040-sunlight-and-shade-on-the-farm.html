Sunlight and shade on the farm

A farm needed sun on its fields, on its kitchen beds and on the yard where its grain dried. In the northern hemisphere the midday sun stands to the south, so anything tall throws its shadow to its north, east and west, and the ground to its south keeps the sun. An old Chinese farm manual puts the consequence plainly: no grain grows in a tree's shade.

So a farm kept its working ground on the sunny side of its house, and its trees behind. In Tohoku the homestead groves stand on the north and west of the house, and on the Tonami plain the east front of the house held little grove, only flowering and fruit trees. Which sides a grove took is at Groves of trees around farmhouses (yashikirin), and how the yard dried its grain in the sun at Threshing and drying yards at farmhouses (niwa).

  • A tree's shade reaches about as far as the tree is tall.
    The sixth-century Chinese farm manual Qimin Yaoshu says the elm's shade reaches, on the east, the west and the north, as far as the tree itself, by its height and breadth, and tells the planter to set elms on the north edge of the garden ground - the side from which no noon shadow falls on it.
  • How tall were windbreak groves?
    Mostly 10 to 16 m (~33-52 ft), and taller where left alone. A Sendai planting list counts cedar, zelkova, black pine and hinoki as tall trees of 15 m (~49 ft) and more, with a sub-canopy of about 10 m (~33 ft) and shrubs of about 3 m (~10 ft); two surveys of Tohoku groves measured them at about 9-16 m (~30-52 ft); a 2022 paper describes one 10 m (~33 ft) high on the windward side; Tonami's trees stood 11-28 m (~36-92 ft), about 15 m (~49 ft) on average; and Okinawa's fukugi groves average 7-10 m (~23-33 ft).
  • Only the Izumo pines were kept to a fixed height.
    The pines round the houses of the Izumo plain were clipped flat at a fixed height, though no page we read gives the height or how often. Elsewhere the groves were pruned of their limbs - at Sawadaue, in Osaki, the 23 households whose groves remain prune them and clear the fallen trees in common - and no page we read speaks of cutting their tops.
  • How tall is a working windbreak, then?
    About 10 m (~33 ft) for a belt kept in use, and 15-25 m (~49-82 ft) for an untended mature stand - this page's reading of the heights above, a GUESS.
  • Were the groves as tall before 1868?
    No page from before 1868 gives a grove's height. The Edo record of the Tonami grove speaks of large cedars sold from one, but not how tall they were, and every height found is from a survey of the 1980s or later.
  • How many hours of direct sun did a kitchen bed need?
    It depends on the crop. Vegetables fall into three classes by the direct sun they need: sun crops want direct sun all day - about six hours or more - and will not grow in shade; half-shade crops do with three to four hours, or with light dappled through leaves all day; shade crops grow on one or two hours. This is modern advice for modern cultivars: no Edo farm manual we could read says where a household set its vegetable bed for the sun.
  • Daikon, the kitchen bed's autumn crop, is a sun crop.
    Daikon, eggplant, cucumber and the beans are sun crops; turnip, komatsuna, taro, negi and ginger are half-shade crops; mitsuba, shiso and myoga are shade crops, and a farm cooperative's lists put the same crops in the same classes, though they name neither turnip nor shiso. Autumn daikon is sown in early autumn and pulled from late autumn into winter, when the sun stands lowest and throws the longest shadows.
  • On the Tonami plain, the tall trees stood south and west, and fruit trees at the sunny east front.
    A survey of the plain's groves found many tall trees - cedar, oak and zelkova - from the south round to the west of the house, and cedar, hackberry and alder, often mixed with bamboo, from the west round to the north. The east, the front of the house, had little grove, and flowering and fruit trees were often planted there. The grove held off the strong south wind of early spring and the cold seasonal wind of winter.
  • Accounts differ on where a farm's bamboo stood.
    The Tonami survey puts it in the grove from the west round to the north; the Japanese Wikipedia article on homestead groves puts Tonami's storehouse, barn, fruit trees and bamboo on the south side. The Qimin Yaoshu sets a garden's bamboo in its northeast corner, because bamboo spreads to the southwest and will fill the garden of itself in some years.
