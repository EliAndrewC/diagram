Does a hamlet have to be NUCLEATED at all?

Answer: no. Three forms are supportable, so the form becomes a seeded knob, and the access rule above is true of exactly one of them.

The entry above settled that a house in a nucleated cluster is reached by a way. It did not ask whether a hamlet must be nucleated. The GM challenged the assumption from the other end on 2026-08-23, asking whether lanes should be laid before houses at all when in life they are trodden by the households already living there. That question turns out to be the same question: a settlement whose lanes are pre-laid can only be one whose houses front lanes.

DISPERSED (散村 sankyoson) - decisive, and our terrain is its terrain

The Tonami Plain in Toyama is the canonical Japanese case: over 7,000 farmhouses scattered across roughly 220 km² on an alluvial fan built by the Shogawa and Oyabe rivers, a pattern more than 500 years old.53

The mechanism is irrigation, and it is the mechanism our own maps model. Farmers built their houses in the middle of their own cultivated fields in order to manage water for those fields directly.54 An alluvial fan drains well - which is a PROBLEM for wet paddy, not a benefit - so water control is per-holding and unremitting, and living on your holding is how it is done.152 The farmhouses scattered naturally (that no plan lay behind it is this page's reading).117 The Kaga domain kept title to reclaimed land but let the reclaimer farm it himself, which is held to be a major reason the houses stand scattered.107

Each house sits in its own grove. The regional term for the homestead woodland is kainyo; it shelters the house from winter seasonal winds and snowstorms, and from summer sun.55 Note this alongside the yashikirin entry above - both terms are real, kainyo is the Tonami one for exactly this feature, and the two describe the same thing at different regional scales.

Why this matters here: the field pattern these maps draw is an alluvial fan's, and a nucleated settlement shelters behind ONE grove rather than per-house belts - so the dispersed form's most visible signature is the road not taken.

Consequence for the access rule: a dispersed hamlet has no interconnected lane network to be reached by. The rule in the entry above is a rule about nucleated settlements.

A scattered farm is measured against the rice that is DRAWN, not against the field the map claims. Every farm in a scattered settlement fronts its own fields, so each house has to stand within reach of one - and a field has two extents that can disagree. The water net claims a smoothed envelope; the planted plots stop where the drainage stops them, whatever the map asked for. The gap between the two is a tail of empty ground, invisible on the sheet and fully real to any distance measured from the envelope, so a farmstead hugging that tail reads as field-adjacent while standing well past the last rice. The GM saw the result on a hand-drawn hamlet in 2026-07 - "a single line of farmhouses that stretches much further south than the rice paddies themselves do", "there are still 3 farmhouses south of the paddies" - and the cause was a claimed field about 180 ft longer than the comb could fill. The answer was to size the claim to what is actually planted, not to nudge the farms. Two hand-drawn villages carry tails of about 420 ft and keep them on purpose: their houses are seated as one cluster and never ride the envelope, so a tail there changes nothing, and reshaping two finished fields to satisfy a rule that protects nothing on them would be churn.

The rule the map follows: a scattered settlement needs roughly TWICE the margin room per farm that a clustered one does - each homestead carries its own grove and keeps its own spacing, and the whole line is hugged to the field edge in single file - so a settlement declared at a clustered village's household count will not fit that many scattered farms in the same margin. Draw a scattered settlement on a roomier field, or declare fewer households; do not compress the homesteads to make them fit. This is a rule about fitting the drawing, not a finding about how far apart scattered farms stood.

LINEAR (路村 / row village) - supportable, but the weakest of the three

Settlements strung along a linear feature - a road, a riverbank, a valley floor - are a standard morphological category. The German Reihendorf is a well-documented type: one or two rows of farmsteads either side of a village street, each holding's farmland adjacent to its dwelling, which saves travel and transport effort.116 That functional argument transfers directly to a rice hamlet strung along a track between its paddies.

Recorded limitation: the English-language record for the specific term 路村 (roson, row village) was thin, and the strongest documentation of the row form is European. The functional logic is not culturally specific, and elongated road- and river-following forms ARE attested in the Chinese village-morphology literature (settlements in mountainous southwest Zhejiang "tend to expand in the direction of rivers and roads").115 But this is weaker ground than the dispersed case, so the linear form is drawn less often than the dispersed one.

Consequence: linear is the one form in which the road genuinely comes first, and therefore the one form whose houses legitimately front a pre-existing way - the connector - the road that reaches the settlement from outside, which was there before the houses.

The connector and the field spur predate the settlement; the lanes inside it are the households' own.
