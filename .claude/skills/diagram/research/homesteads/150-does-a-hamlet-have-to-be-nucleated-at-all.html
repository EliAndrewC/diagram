Does a hamlet have to be NUCLEATED at all?

Answer: no. Three forms are supportable, so the form becomes a knob set at random for each map, and the access rule above is true of exactly one of them.

The entry above settled that a house in a nucleated cluster is reached by a way. It did not ask whether a hamlet must be nucleated. The same doubt comes from the other end: should lanes be laid before houses at all, when in life they are trodden by the households already living there? That turns out to be the same question: a settlement whose lanes are pre-laid can only be one whose houses front lanes.

DISPERSED (散村 sankyoson) - decisive, and our terrain is its terrain

The Tonami Plain in Toyama is the canonical Japanese case: over 7,000 farmhouses scattered across roughly 220 km² on an alluvial fan built by the Shogawa and Oyabe rivers, a pattern more than 500 years old.

The mechanism is irrigation, and it is the mechanism our own maps model. Farmers built their houses in the middle of their own cultivated fields in order to manage water for those fields directly. An alluvial fan drains well - which is a PROBLEM for wet paddy, not a benefit - so water control is per-holding and unremitting, and living on your holding is how it is done. The farmhouses scattered naturally (that no plan lay behind it is this page's reading). The domain lord kept title to reclaimed land but let the reclaimer farm it himself, which is held to be a major reason the houses stand scattered.

Each house sits in its own grove. The regional term for the homestead woodland is kainyo; it shelters the house from winter seasonal winds and snowstorms, and from summer sun. Note this alongside the yashikirin entry above - both terms are real, kainyo is the Tonami one for exactly this feature, and the two describe the same thing at different regional scales.

Why this matters here: the field pattern these maps draw is an alluvial fan's, and a nucleated settlement shelters behind ONE grove rather than per-house belts - so the dispersed form's most visible signature is the road not taken. Where the farms carry their own groves, in the dispersed and the linear forms, no village shelter belt is drawn as well (Does a shelter belt wrap the settlement?).

Consequence for the access rule: a dispersed hamlet has no interconnected lane network to be reached by. The rule in the entry above is a rule about nucleated settlements.

A scattered farm is measured against the rice that is DRAWN, not against the field the map claims. Every farm in a scattered settlement fronts its own fields, so each house has to stand within reach of one - and a field has two extents that can disagree. The drawn irrigation network claims a smoothed envelope - an outline drawn round its plots; the planted plots stop where the drainage stops them, whatever the map asked for. The gap between the two is a tail of empty ground, invisible on the sheet and fully real to any distance measured from the envelope, so a farmstead hugging that tail reads as field-adjacent while standing well past the last rice. So the claim is sized to what is actually planted, and the farms are not nudged. Two hand-drawn villages carry tails of about 420 ft and keep them on purpose: their houses are seated as one cluster and never ride the envelope, so a tail there changes nothing, and reshaping two finished fields to satisfy a rule that protects nothing on them would be change for its own sake.

The rule the map follows: a scattered settlement needs roughly TWICE the margin room per farm that a clustered one does - each homestead carries its own grove and keeps its own spacing, and the whole line is hugged to the field edge in single file - so a settlement declared at a clustered village's household count will not fit that many scattered farms in the same margin - the ground beside the field that its houses are seated on. Draw a scattered settlement on a roomier field, or declare fewer households; do not compress the homesteads to make them fit. This is a rule about fitting the drawing, not a finding about how far apart scattered farms stood.

LINEAR (路村 / row village) - supportable, but the weakest of the three

One settlement strung along a linear feature is the German Reihendorf: one or two rows of farmsteads either side of a village street, each holding's farmland adjacent to its dwelling, which saves travel and transport effort. That functional argument transfers directly to a rice hamlet strung along a track between its paddies.

The Japanese row: the row village is attested in Japan too - along roads on new fields, and along levees, dikes and spring lines, its farms on one side of the line or both - at How was a row village laid out?; elongated road- and river-following forms are attested in the Chinese village-morphology literature as well (settlements in mountainous southwest Zhejiang "tend to expand in the direction of rivers and roads"). But the Japanese rows whose layout is measured are planned colonies on new fields, so for a hamlet that grew by itself this is weaker ground than the dispersed case, and the linear form is drawn less often than the dispersed one.

Consequence: linear is the one form in which a line comes before the houses - on planned new fields the road was laid first, and on a levee, a dike or a spring line the ground set the line (Along what a row ran, and what came first) - and therefore the one form whose houses legitimately front a pre-existing way: the connector, the road that reaches the settlement from outside, which on the map is laid before the houses.

On the map the connector and the field spur are laid before the settlement, and the lanes inside it are drawn from the placed houses. A linear hamlet's farms front a street laid along the road, the row as long as its households need at the frontage a planned row gave each farm (How wide was a farm in a row?); for a row that grew by itself, how long it ran is a GUESS.
