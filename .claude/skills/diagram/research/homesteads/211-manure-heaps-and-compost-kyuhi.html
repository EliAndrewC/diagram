Manure heaps and compost (kyuhi)

A farm's manure heap was where the dung of its cattle and horses, trodden into straw and fallen leaves, rotted down into stable manure (kyuhi) before it went onto the fields. The household's night soil was rotted the same way, in a jar buried in the ground or a plastered pit, because fresh night soil harms the crop. The night-soil store and where it stood are at Farm privies and their night soil (benjo).

The practice is old. A Chinese farm manual of 1149 calls for a manure house beside every farm dwelling, and a Japanese one of the 1690s, early in the Edo period, tells the farmer to fit one out and heap the beasts' dung like a small mountain. No page we read says where in the yard the heap stood, or how many farms had one before Japan began to modernize in 1868.

  • Stable manure was the byre's litter, trodden by the beasts until it rotted.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on stable manure describes it as livestock dung and urine mixed with straw, fallen leaves and the like, and trodden by cattle and horses until it rots.
  • Night soil was rotted in a buried jar or a plastered pit.
    The vessel had to hold its liquid without leaking. The pit looks like a well; the jar was usually unglazed earthenware, about 1 to 1.5 m (~3-5 ft) across the mouth, buried in the ground.
  • How long did night soil take to rot?
    One to two weeks in summer and three to four in winter, by the Japanese Wikipedia article on night soil, which also cites the Sendai city history for a year of fermenting.
  • Two Japanese farm manuals of the late 1600s, early in the Edo period, tell the farmer to rot dung before it goes on the fields.
    The Hyakusho Denki says fresh filth on a dry field brings disease and insects to the crop, and that it should be rotted well in the storage jar; the Nogyo Zensho says fresh dung works poorly and ripened dung works well. Both are read as the Japanese Wikipedia article on night soil quotes them, not in their own texts. The Nogyo Zensho was published in 1697.

The manuals of China and Japan both called for a building to keep the manure in.

  • A Chinese farm manual of 1149, under the Song dynasty, calls for a manure house beside every farm dwelling.
    Chen Fu's manual, finished in 1149 under the Song dynasty, says the manure house is to be low-eaved, to keep off wind and rain, because manure left out under the sky loses its richness. In it a deep pit is dug and lined with brick, and swept-up earth, ash, chaff, broken straw and fallen leaves are heaped, burned and soaked with liquid manure.
  • The Nogyo Zensho tells the farmer to fit out a manure house and to heap the beasts' dung like a small mountain.
    In it night soil, rotting things and the kitchen's and the bath's dirty water are stored until they rot down; the dung of cattle and horses is piled layer on layer, heaped like a small mountain where there are many beasts. It is read here in a modern Japanese rendering by Japan's Environment Ministry, which dates the book 1696, and which does not say where the pile stood.
  • A village record of 1754, in the Edo period, has night soil, bran and ash spread with leaves, straw and turf on the dry fields.
    Ogawa village's record, in what is now Kodaira city, lists them; the city's history adds, without a date, that fallen leaves were made into compost.
  • Where in the yard did the heap stand?
    No page we read says.
  • How many farms kept a heap or a manure house before 1868?
    No count says. Both manuals are advice, calling for a manure house on every farm, and neither says how many farms had one. Neither of the village counts from before 1868 in the record lists a manure shed (see The farmstead and what stood on it (yashiki)).
  • A modern count, of 1972, found about one compost shed to every four of the oldest farmhouses.
    Sugiura's survey of July 1972, of all 87 households in three Miyagi hamlets, counts 0.24 compost sheds a household on the farms whose main house was built in or before 1944, and 0.14 across all the farms. It counts the shed, not the open heap.
