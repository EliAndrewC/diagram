Ancestral halls in south-China villages (citang)

A lineage's ancestral hall was the symbol of its village, and in the villages of the Pearl River delta the building that led the village's layout: the halls stood at the very front, facing the village pond across open ground, with the houses behind them. Commoners were widely able to set up halls only from the sixteenth century, and they survive mostly in the south.

The common hall was three bays wide and three halls deep, the halls one behind another on one axis, or two halls deep for a small or less wealthy lineage. The hall also served the lineage for its meetings and feasts, at The headman's house and the rich farmer's homestead (shoya, gono).

  • The halls stood at the front of a Cantonese water-country village.
    The villages of the Pearl River delta were laid out in a plan of parallel lanes facing a river channel or a pond, and the ancestral halls and the other public spaces stand mainly at the front of the village, with the houses behind them.
  • A hall faced the pond, its door to the water.
    At Songtang, as in most Cantonese villages, the halls stand at the very front of the village next to the pond, facing it with their doors to the water, and a visitor walking in passes from the water to the drying floor, the heping, and then into the lanes. The water before the halls, and the pond it often was, is at Half-moon ponds before southern Chinese villages (banyuechi).
  • Most halls had water and a plaza before them.
    A study of the halls of Panyu finds water and a plaza at the very front of most halls, for fengshui and for drainage, fire and fish.
  • A Hakka enclosed house has the same order: pond, drying floor, gate.
    In most Hakka enclosed houses a half-moon pond fronts the house, the drying floor lies between pond and house, and the main gate stands straight ahead on the house's axis. So in both forms the pond faces the hall, or the house and its gate, across open ground.
  • Not every village had a hall.
    One historian of these villages sorts them, by period, into three layouts: a village centered on one hall, a village led by a row of halls across its front, and a single-row village of flatter social structure with no hall at all.
  • Commoners could build halls widely only from the Jiajing reign of the Ming (1522-1566).
    Before it, commoners were not widely able to set up ancestral halls in their homes, and the halls survive mostly in the south.
  • Guangzhou keeps about 1,300 old halls, most of its later ones small and middling.
    Those built in the Cantonese region's second great period of hall building, after Guangzhou became the one port open to foreign trade, were mostly small and middling halls three bays wide and three halls deep.
  • Three bays wide and three halls deep was the common form.
    In Guangzhou prefecture most small lineage and branch halls were three bays wide, and a single axis of three halls is a form with many examples, spread very widely across the prefecture, used by a lineage's founding-ancestor halls, branch halls and sub-branch halls alike. Halls five bays wide are rare, about twenty known.
  • A small or less wealthy lineage's hall was mostly two halls deep.
    Three halls deep was the first and commonest choice of most halls of higher standing and larger size, but the branch halls, family schools and memorial halls to the living of lineages with few members and no great wealth were mostly two halls deep.
  • How big was a hall on the ground?
    Only one depth is measured: the rear halls of several halls at Langtou are 8.5 to 9 m (~28-30 ft) deep. No page we read gives the whole footprint of a common village hall, its width and length on the ground.
