Along what a row ran, and what came first

Japanese geography names two rows: the road village, houses strung along a road, and the linear village, houses strung along a natural levee or the spring line at a hill's foot; an encyclopedia adds springs and waterways to what a row follows. The record shows four kinds (all accurate):

  • A road laid first, on new fields. An Edo-period new-field village laid out its roads, fields and house plots on a plan, so its form was regular; at Santome, opened on the Musashino plateau in 1694-1696, roads 6 ken wide (about 36 ft) were laid lengthwise and crosswise and the lots set along them. On the plateau's south the rows follow the canals dug along the highways; on its north, watered by wells, they run in a comparatively free direction.
  • A dike, on reclaimed ground. On land reclaimed from the sea the farmhouses stood in line on the dike, and round a drained lake many rows ran along the old shore.
  • A natural levee. On alluvial lowland the long-established settlements stood first on the levee, a slight rise above the backswamp behind it; a photograph on the same page shows one running in a curve beside a river's old channel.
  • A fan's foot. On the Shuso plain of Iyo, settlements often sit at an alluvial fan's head or, where the water that sank into the fan rises again as springs, at its foot; and at the foot of the Sekiya river's fan many of them line up in an arc. That is a row of villages; how the houses lay within each is not said.

So on new fields the road came first, and on a dike, a levee or a fan's foot the ground did and the houses followed its line. A Chinese parallel: the villages of mountainous southwest Zhejiang form mainly clusters and bands, and tend to grow along rivers and roads.

One side of the line, or both

Both are attested. A road village is defined as a farming settlement whose houses stand along a fairly narrow street on one side of it or on both; the linear village too has its houses on one side or both. Santome's lots lay on both sides of its road, and so did those of the Nobidome villages of 1661 along the Kawagoe road; but Shimotome, one of Santome's three villages, is a one-sided road village, with farmland across the road from its house lots. No page read says which was commoner.

What it means for a map

  1. What the row follows is set by the ground where the map knows it: on flood-prone ground - low ground reclaimed behind dikes, or houses on a dike - the row runs along the dike (accurate). Elsewhere the map rolls between the two lines the record attests: a street laid first, drawn straight, and the dry edge, drawn curving with the field's margin, which stands on the map for a levee, a dike or a fan's foot (this project's reading). Even odds between the two are a GUESS.
  2. One side or both is a knob rolled per settlement: the farms on one side of the street with the field across it, or on both sides (accurate; even odds between them are a GUESS).
