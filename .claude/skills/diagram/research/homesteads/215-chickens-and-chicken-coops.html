Chickens and chicken coops

Chickens were the commonest livestock on a Chinese farm. Farmers in most regions kept a pig and some chickens in the yard, beside a draft animal or two, and a survey of the 1920s found chickens on about four farms in five. A sixth-century Chinese farm manual tells the farmer to shut them at night in a coop on the ground with a perch inside, since birds left to roost in the trees sicken in the wind and cold, and the one coop dug up by archaeologists, from the Ming dynasty, is square.

  • The chicken was one of China's six domestic animals.
    The liuchu, the six domestic animals, were the horse, the ox, the sheep, the chicken, the dog and the pig.
  • Most farmers kept a pig and some chickens in the yard.
    Animals through Chinese History, a Cambridge history, finds that farmers in most regions of China kept a pig and some chickens in their yard, along with a draft animal or two. Its chapter leans on a survey of farming made in the late 1920s and early 1930s, and this record reads it back into the countryside of the last empire.
  • How many farms kept chickens?
    About four in five, in the 1920s. Buck's survey of 2,866 farms in seventeen localities of seven provinces, made in 1921-1925, found chickens on 82 percent of all farms, the most common livestock of all, and found them more often in east-central China than in the north.

That count was made after the Qing dynasty fell in 1912. Two travelers' accounts from before it show chickens already an ordinary part of the village.

  • In North China in 1899, before the empire fell, chickens were part of the village yard.
    A missionary describing the villages of the North China plain wrote that a family's small yard was in as great confusion as its house, and that its dogs, cats, chickens and babies spilled out into the street, "which is but an extension of the court". After a village theater performance, he wrote, there was not likely to be a single chicken left in a village, so the villagers disposed of their fowls beforehand rather than have them stolen by "expert chicken-thieves" - chickens common enough that thieves could count on finding them.
  • Near Suzhou in 1909, three years before the empire fell, nearly every family kept a few chickens.
    F. H. King, touring the lower Yangzi, was told by a doctor at a hospital near Suzhou that nearly every family in the cities and the country villages raised a few, but only a few, chickens. King himself wrote that there were no statistics on China's poultry.
  • Was the 1920s share already true under the Qing?
    Probably, though no count from before 1912 was found. "Nearly every family" near Suzhou and the chickens of the North China yard sit with the survey's 82 percent, so this record takes its number from the survey and, from the older accounts, that it was already so.
  • A sixth-century farm manual says to keep chickens in a coop, not in the trees.
    The Qimin Yaoshu's chapter on raising chickens says the roost is best built as a cage on the ground with a perch inside: the birds crow less clearly, but are safe, fatten easily and are spared the fox, while birds left to the trees are harmed by one spell of wind and cold, the big ones wasting and the small ones perhaps dying. The accounts from before 1912 do not say whether the yard's chickens slept in one.
  • The one excavated coop, of the late Ming dynasty, is square.
    A salvage excavation at Zhengzhou found a coop of the early seventeenth century: a square structure with six niche-like openings in its west wall and eggshell fragments inside them. The page reporting it gives no size.
