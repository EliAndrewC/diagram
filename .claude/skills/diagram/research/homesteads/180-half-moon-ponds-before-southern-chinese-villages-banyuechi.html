Half-moon ponds before southern Chinese villages (banyuechi)

Before most Hakka enclosed houses lay a pond dug in a half circle, the half-moon pond, and one lay before some ancestral halls and at the heart of some villages. In fengshui a well-sited house has high ground behind it and water in front, and where no river or stream lies in front, a household can simply dig the pond by hand to complete the arrangement.

The pond was also useful. Its water was stored against drought, stocked with fish, let out onto the fields and kept against fire, and between it and the house lay the drying floor where the household dried its grain and gathered for festivals, weddings and funerals.

  • The pond had several names.
    Before a Hakka enclosed house it is called the fengshui pond, and a Taiwan folk dictionary gives it as the eyebrow-moon pond, also called the half-moon pond, the fengshui pond or the fish pond.
  • Fengshui wants a house backed by a hill and facing water.
    The water in front need not be a natural river or stream: where none lies there, a household can dig a half-circle pond by hand to complete the arrangement.
  • Among the Hakka the pond and the mound behind the house are the yin and yang of one whole.
    The pond lies lower than the mound of earth behind the house; the pond is yin and the mound yang, and the common idea that water brings a household wealth seems to count for less than that, the Taiwan Hakka Museum says.
  • Seen from above, the pond and the house's rear wing close into one circle.
    A Hakka enclosed house has its half-moon pond in front and its half-moon wing behind, the two halves surrounding the main hall; fengshui reads them as heaven, which is round, and the hall-and-wing house between them as earth, which is square.
  • Why is the pond half a circle?
    No source settles it: a survey of the explanations says there are not yet the historical sources to confirm the shape's origin. The Taiwan Hakka Museum offers two hedged reasons: most likely the pond copies the one before a school of the imperial examinations, and, it infers, commoners kept to a half circle so as not to exceed what the regulations allowed their betters. A popular fengshui page gives it a meaning of its own: a moon only half full, because a full moon can only wane, which the householder takes as a spur to keep striving.
  • Its water was led in from the fields, slowly.
    Most farmhouses stood among their fields, and a channel led stream water from the fields round the main building into the pond. The channel's fall was measured so the water ran slowly, because fast water was held to carry wealth away as fast as it came.
  • The pond's water stored, fed fish, watered fields and put out fires.
    The Chinese Wikipedia article on the Hakka enclosed house lists storing water against drought, raising fish and irrigating, tempering the air a little and holding water against fire; a Taiwan folk dictionary adds washing. A study of the Cantonese ancestral halls of Panyu finds water before most halls, with the practical value of drainage, fire prevention and raising fish.
  • Before one ancestral hall in Pingtung, the neighbors drew their household water from the pond's water system.
    Before the Guangyu ancestral hall in Wugou village, the neighbors' household water, laundry, washing and irrigation were all tied to the water system that fed its half-moon pond.
  • The pond grew with its house.
    As the enclosing wing was extended the pond could be dug wider by hand, and the Hakka hold that it must be wider than the span between the two dog-holes beside the side gates, so that the fengshui does not flow out.
  • The drying floor lay between the pond and the house.
    The heping, the grain-drying floor, lies between pond and house, and it is the household's space for the new year and the festivals, for weddings and funerals. Where a village's ancestral hall stands against its pond is at Ancestral halls in south-China villages (citang).
  • A fengshui grove stood behind the house or the village.
    Behind a Hakka enclosed house stands a fengshui grove of hardy trees and bamboo, holding the soil and breaking the wind, and many south-China villages were sited against a dense wood that screened their rear, which the villagers guarded as the village's luck.
  • Were the grove behind and the pond in front planned as one?
    That they were is this page's reading of the pages above, a GUESS: the article on the enclosed house closes its circle with the house's own rear wing, not with the grove.
  • In Hongcun, a Huizhou village, the half-moon pond lies at the village's middle.
    The pond is formed by the village's water channel, the houses are laid out around it, and the lineage's ancestral hall stands at the middle of its north bank.
  • Was the pond a place of worship?
    Probably not, though this is a GUESS. The worship the article on the enclosed house names happens inside the house, in the dragon hall at the middle of the rear wing, and every page we read gives the pond only water, fish, fengshui and the yin of the pair; none says outright that it has no god.
