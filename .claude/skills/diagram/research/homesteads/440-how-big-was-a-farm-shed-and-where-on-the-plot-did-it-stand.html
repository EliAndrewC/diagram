How big was a farm shed, and where on the plot did it stand?

Sources: naya-jawiki (what a naya is), boso-no-mura-kazusa (what it held), bunka-minami-naya and nerima-mitome-naya (two measured sheds of the Edo period's end), bunka-fujii-naya, bunka-fujino-naya and bunka-mori-naya (three later measured barns)

What it was. A naya is a storage shed, mostly built apart from the house; on a farm it holds the crop and the implements. The reconstructed late-Edo headman-class farm in Chiba keeps its farm tools in one. How many a farm had is at the farmstead inventory.

Size before 1868: a shed of about 18 to 27 feet. A work shed of 1830-1867 in the farm work area of a farmstead at Hannan, in Osaka prefecture, is 3 by 2 ken, gabled and tiled, covering 28 sq m, and is divided into two rooms, one of them earth-floored; if its ken is 6 shaku, about 1.82 m (this project's arithmetic), that frame is about 18 by 12 feet and 20 sq m, half again as long as it is deep. The register's 28 sq m is more than that frame, and the page does not say what makes up the difference, so the shed may have been somewhat larger than 18 feet. A farm's naya from Nerima, in Tokyo, dated by its roof frame and its uneven posts to the end of the Edo period or the Meiji years, is 8.17 by 4.54 meters (about 27 by 15 feet), hip-roofed and first thatched with straw. These two are the only measured sheds found from before or at the Restoration; how big an ordinary Edo farm's naya was beyond them no page read says.

The later barns are bigger: 23 to 38 feet long. Three barns (naya) registered as Tangible Cultural Properties give sizes. The plainest, of the 1910s or 1920s near Osaka, is 6.9 by 3.9 meters (about 23 by 13 feet), earth-floored inside and simply built. A Meiji barn of another household near Osaka is 11.7 by 4.9 meters (about 38 by 16 feet). A 1907 barn of a Kagawa farm covers 99 square meters. All three are gabled and tiled, about 1.8 to 2.4 times as long as deep where the length is given, and all three are fireproofed like a kura. They belong to households of the late nineteenth or early twentieth century, one of them an old family that prospered by milling rice and barley. They were built after 1868, so the maps do not size a shed from them.

Where it stood: on no fixed side. The Edo work shed stands in the farm work area on the west side of its plot; of the later barns, one stands to the southeast of its main house, one between the detached room and the rice kura, one at the east end of its plot facing west.

What this means for the map. A farm shed is drawn freestanding, about 18 to 27 feet long and about 1.5 to 1.8 times as long as it is deep - the two sheds of the Edo period's end, not the larger barns built after it; that they stand for an ordinary farm is a GUESS - on a side of the yard rolled per farm. Its roof is gabled on the Osaka shed and hip-roofed on the Tokyo one: two attested roofs, which makes the roof a knob rolled per farm rather than always gabled.
