How big was a farm shed, and where on the plot did it stand?

Sources: naya-jawiki (what a naya is), boso-no-mura-kazusa (what it held), bunka-fujii-naya, bunka-fujino-naya and bunka-mori-naya (three measured barns)

What it was. A naya is a storage shed, mostly built apart from the house; on a farm it holds the crop and the implements. The reconstructed late-Edo headman-class farm in Chiba keeps its farm tools in one. How many a farm had is at the farmstead inventory.

Size: a long, narrow gabled building, 23 to 38 feet long. Three farm barns registered as Tangible Cultural Properties give sizes. The plainest, of the 1910s or 1920s near Osaka, is 6.9 by 3.9 meters (about 23 by 13 feet), earth-floored inside and simply built. A Meiji barn of another household near Osaka is 11.7 by 4.9 meters (about 38 by 16 feet). A 1907 barn of a Kagawa farm covers 99 square meters. All three are gabled and tiled, about 1.8 to 2.4 times as long as deep where the length is given; all three are fireproofed like a kura and belong to households of the late nineteenth or early twentieth century, one of them an old family that prospered by milling rice and barley. The size of an ordinary Edo farm's naya no page read gives.

Where it stood: on no fixed side. One stands to the southeast of its main house, one between the detached room and the rice kura, one at the east end of its plot facing west.

What this means for the map. A farm shed is drawn as a long, narrow gabled building, freestanding, about 20 to 30 feet long and half as deep - the low end of the measured barns, since those are of well-off households, and that is a GUESS for an ordinary farm - on a side of the yard rolled per farm.
