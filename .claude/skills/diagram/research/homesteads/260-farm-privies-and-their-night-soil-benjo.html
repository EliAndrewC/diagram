Farm privies and their night soil (benjo)

On a farm the privy was usually a building of its own, apart from the main house: a urinal and a privy under one roof was the usual arrangement. It stood outside because what it gathered was manure. A survey of three villages published in 1959 found more than nine households in ten with an outdoor privy, and judged that it stood outside the house because the spot was handy for manuring the fields. The night soil was kept in a tank beside the privy, or in a pit out by the fields.

For the same reason the privy stood in the sun, where its contents rotted quickly: a farm manual of about 1680 puts it southeast of the house, and in one Shiga village's farmsteads, mapped as they stood after 1868, nearly three privies in four stood south or southeast of the house. How the sun fell on the rest of the farm is at Sunlight and shade on the farm.

  • A farm's privy was usually a building of its own.
    The Nipponica encyclopedia says farm households usually put one urinal and one privy together in a single building independent of the main house. A page on the history of the farmhouse floor plan calls the outhouse and the privy under the eaves the general form.
  • Where did a farm's privy stand?
    The same floor-plan pages think it stood under the eaves in front of the stable, to the left of the entrance, or else as a separate outhouse in the yard. None of the pages we read puts it anywhere else around the house, such as by the back door or at the gate.
  • In northern Miyagi, the privy usually stood in the front yard.
    A prewar report on rural housing in Tohoku, as a geographer passes it on, says the bath and the privy were usually put in the front yard of the main house there.
  • In Miyagi, was a separate privy out front a fashion of the years after 1868?
    The same prewar report says so, as the geographer passes it on: making the bath and the privy buildings of their own was a fashion of early Meiji. The report is also cited for the move of the privy to the front of the house in one Miyagi town being a kind of fashion.
  • At several farms of one Miyagi village, the privy was a tub sunk in the barn floor.
    The 1959 survey found there a tub buried in a barn outside the main house, with boards simply laid across it - the simplest form of pit privy.
  • How usual was an outdoor privy?
    Usual almost everywhere, though not in every village. The 1959 survey, of one village each in Saitama, Tokyo and Miyagi, found households with an indoor privy alone at 6.0% in Saitama and 9.4% in Tokyo and none in Miyagi, and concluded that more than 90% of households had an outdoor privy. Reconstructions of three Tohoku villages in the 1930s and 1940s put outdoor privies at 85% of farms in Tono and 79% in Shiokawa, but at none in Inawashiro.
  • In the villages surveyed in 1959, small houses tended to have only an outdoor privy, and large ones an indoor one as well.
    In the villages of the 1959 survey, small houses kept no privy inside and had an outdoor one, while considerably large houses had an indoor privy; most households with two or more privies had one of each.
  • In three Miyagi hamlets surveyed in 1972, the older farms had nearly one privy building each.
    On the farms whose main house was built in 1944 or earlier, the oldest in the survey, there were 0.87 privy outbuildings a household, and 12 of those 38 farms (31.6%) had a privy inside the house.
  • Did most farms already keep their privy outside the house before Japan began to modernize in 1868?
    Two villages' records say so. In the 1824 house-by-house plan register of Hasuda village, most houses had a well and a privy outside the main house. At Kakimochi, counted in 1885, a main house and a privy were the basic form of a farm, and all 16 households had a privy; the village's historian reads the count back to the farms of the last years of the shogunate.
  • In six villages' registers, at first only village officials had a privy joined to the house.
    In the house-plan registers of six villages, an "upper privy" joined to the raised-floor rooms of the house is found from the 1720s to 1771 only in the one or two houses of the headman or other officials; by 1824 about one house in ten had one, and by 1842 about two in ten, ordinary farmers' among them.
  • How large was a privy?
    In the village of Kakimochi, counted house by house in 1885, from 5 by 5 feet to about 27 by 15 feet. The count gives each of the sixteen privies' frontage and depth. Ten are 3 by 2 ken (about 18 by 12 feet) or smaller, five are 4 by 2 ken (about 24 by 12 feet), and the largest is 4.5 by 2.5 ken. The table does not say what else the larger privy buildings held.
  • A farm manual of about 1680, early in the Edo period, puts the privy southeast of the house, in the sun.
    The Hyakusho Denki, written about 1680, sets a farm's privy southeast of the main house, so that its contents rot quickly into good manure, as a modern reader of the manual reports it.
  • In one Shiga village, 72.7% of farm privies stood south or southeast of the house.
    Two researchers, Wang and Ochiai, mapped the farmsteads of Arakawa village, swept by windstorms off the Hira mountains toward Lake Biwa, as they stood in the Meiji era. The toilets, a source of fertilizer for the paddy fields, stood southeast and south of the house in 72.7% of cases, because the warmth there helped the night soil ferment quickly.
  • Did farms set their privy downwind of the house?
    No source we found says so, though windswept Arakawa is where such a rule would show if farms followed one. There it is the storehouses and retirement houses that stand to the southwest and west of the house, set to form wind fences round the open ground in front of the entrance, where rice was threshed and vegetables dried, while the privies are placed by the sun; the paper never speaks of leeward, downwind, odor or hygiene.
  • Night soil was kept beside the privy, or in a pit out by the fields (nodame).
    The 1959 survey found night soil kept two ways: in a storage tank beside the privy, or in a field pit away from the house. Its authors think the tank was moved from beside the privy to near each household's fields, or to the roadside, to make manuring easier.
  • How many households kept a field pit?
    From almost none to most, village by village: 2 of 83 households in the Saitama village, 19 of 53 in Tokyo's and 15 of 18 in Miyagi's.

In China the privy and the farm's pigs could share one structure.

  • Under the Han dynasty, Chinese latrines were customarily built over the pigsty.
    The Art Institute of Chicago's catalog says of its pottery model of a pigsty and latrine, a grave good of about 100 BCE to 100 CE, that latrines were customarily built above a pigsty and connected by pipes to a cesspool.
