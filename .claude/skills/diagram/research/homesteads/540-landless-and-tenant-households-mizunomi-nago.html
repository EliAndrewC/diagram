Landless and tenant households (mizunomi, nago)

In a village before 1868, in the Edo period, the farmers who held assessed land stood above the mizunomi, who held none. The mizunomi were not counted as full villagers and took no part in village government, and they lived as tenants, by day or migrant labor, or by a craft. Some poorer households, the nago, rented not only their fields but their house and its plot as well.

Many such households had no house plot of their own. They lived in a hut or a gate-hut inside a richer farmer's enclosure, under that household's protection and control; in one Tosa village only a third of the registered cultivators held a house plot. A new household that did get a house plot of its own was sent to the edge of the fields.

  • The landless were not full villagers.
    An encyclopedia entry on the mizunomi sets them against the landholding farmers: they owned no fields, were not counted as full farmers, were not allowed a part in village government, and lived as tenants, or by migrant service and day labor, or by a craft, the poor of the farming village.
  • A nago rented the house plot and the house as well as the fields.
    Where the mizunomi was a tenant who rented fields, the nago is distinguished by renting the house and its plot too.
  • Dependents lived in huts inside a master's enclosure.
    The dependent farmers called kadoya took the name from the gate-hut on their master's plot where they lived. Many of the house plots registered in the land surveys held, inside the plot's enclosure and beside the main house, huts, gate-huts and retirement houses, where weak dependent farmers lived while the master's family lived in the main house.
  • In Sukumo, in Tosa, only 120 of 360 registered cultivators held a house plot.
    The land surveys of Sukumo register many cultivators with no house plot. Most of them, by the town history's account, were kin, retainers or nago of a powerful farmer, living in a separate building or a hut on the head household's plot; in the village of Yamana only 120 of the 360 registered cultivators held a house plot of their own.
  • The Nanbu domain gave a nago no house plot as a rule.
    By its land survey of 1737 the Nanbu domain, in the north, gave a nago no house plot as a rule, though a house allowance alone might be granted.
  • A new branch household was sent to a vacant plot or the edge of the fields.
    Nanbu's order of 1715 forbade a branch household to take its house plot out of the cultivated land, and allowed it on a vacant plot, at the edge of the fields, or along the roads at the foot of the hills. In Shiwa district the least a branch household was given was three buildings - a main house, a stable and a privy - as one hearth, that is, one household.
