Farm storehouses (kura)

A kura was a farm's fireproof storehouse: a small block, commonly about 15 by 18 ft and two stories high, walled in thick earth under a tile roof. It was built against fire and theft, and it came to be a sign of wealth. A farm built one when money had accumulated, so most farms had none: before 1868, in the Edo period, one village of sixteen households had two, both in the households of its leading families.

  • Who had a storehouse?
    The well-off, on this project's reading. Storehouses were built in great numbers against fire and theft and later became a symbol of affluence. Their walls were earth laid coat after coat on a bamboo lattice, costly work, so it was the well-off farmers and landlords who had one; that last is this project's reading of the construction the pages describe.
  • How many farms had a storehouse before 1868?
    In Kakimochi village, 2 of the 16 households had one, each 2 by 3 ken (about 12 by 18 ft). Both were among the households the town historian calls the powerful ones. The count is of 1885, and the historian reads it back to the last years of the shogunate, since houses were not often rebuilt. It is one village's count. The farmstead it describes is at The farmstead and what stood on it (yashiki).
  • Storehouses came late to one district west of Edo.
    An architect who surveyed the storehouses of a new-field village there reports it said that its farmhouses probably came to own them only from the last years of the shogunate.
  • How common were storehouses in the twentieth century?
    A survey of farmhouses in Miyagi in 1972 counts 0.24 storehouses per household among those whose main house was built in or before 1944, and 0.13 over all the households surveyed. That is a modern figure.
  • Was a shed commoner than a storehouse?
    Yes, though a shed was not on every plot either. The same 1972 survey counts 0.58 sheds (naya) per household in the older farmhouses, against 0.24 storehouses. Many households worked and dried their crop inside the farmhouse's own earth-floored room, the doma, and the poorest had little to store; that is this project's reading. The shed is at Farm sheds and barns (naya).
  • Did the largest houses have the storehouses?
    Mostly. The Kakimochi historian ties the outbuildings to the size of the house: the more of them a household had, the larger its main house, and the powerful farmers who served as village officials owned such houses. The village's table bears that out for the storehouse, though not to the letter. Its two storehouses stood with main houses of 40 and 32 tsubo (about 1,400 and 1,100 sq ft), the second and third largest of the sixteen; the largest, 45 tsubo, had none; and seven of the sixteen main houses were 18 tsubo.
  • How big was a farm storehouse?
    About 15 by 18 ft, in two stories. An architect who surveyed the storehouses of a new-field village west of Edo found the commonest, standard size there to be 2.5 by 3 ken, about 15 by 18 ft, in two stories. Kakimochi's two were 2 by 3 ken, about 12 by 18 ft.
  • A grain storehouse of 1890 from Sado island is of the same order.
    It has two stories and covers 34 sq m (~366 sq ft), and the national register calls it an ancillary building a farmhouse could not do without.
  • A storehouse's walls were thick earth.
    Earth was laid on a lattice of bamboo and palm rope, coat after coat, to 20 to 30 cm (~8-12 in) thick, and finished in lime plaster.
  • Its tile roof stood over an earthen one.
    The reconstructed headman-class farm in Chiba has a two-story storehouse, entered at its gable end and walled in earth roof and all, with a tiled roof set over the earthen one. Both storehouses read, this one and Sado's, are tiled.
  • Where on the plot did a storehouse stand?
    Free of the house, in front of it or behind it, and on any side, in the district the architect surveyed. A site was set aside for it, but circumstances changed before it was built, so storehouses ended up in front of the house or behind; where a seasonal wind blew, few were built against the house on its downwind side; and they stood east, west, north or south. How rare an attached storehouse was the source does not say beyond "few".
