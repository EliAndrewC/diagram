How big was an ordinary farmhouse, and how much bigger was a well-off one?

Sources: kotobank-minka (the eighteenth-century count of house sizes), nihonminkaen-kanto and nihonminkaen-kanagawa (measured surviving farmhouses), boso-no-mura-kazusa (a headman-class house), kotobank-nihon-no-minka (an upper farmhouse 30 meters long)

Most farmhouses were small - far smaller than the old houses that survive. When the shogun went on pilgrimage to Nikko, the villages near Utsunomiya had to draw up a floor plan of every house, to lodge his retainers. Those eighteenth-century plans show that the commonest house was about 20 tsubo, some 66 square meters (about 710 square feet); many were under 10 tsubo (about 355 square feet), some only 4 or 5, and large houses were few. A 20-tsubo house with the survivors' proportions would be roughly 35 by 20 feet (a GUESS; see below).

The houses that survive are the large ones. A house that lasted three centuries was most likely a big, well-built one (this page's reading), and three of the five farmhouses of about 1700 quoted here from the Nihon Minka-en open-air museum were headmen's. They measure, length by depth: two ordinary farmhouses of 14.5 by 8.9 meters (about 48 by 29 feet) and 13.6 by 8.2 meters (45 by 27 feet), and three headmen's houses of 15.6 by 8.9, 16.4 by 9.1 and 14.5 by 7.3 meters (51 by 29, 54 by 30 and 48 by 24 feet). Those are about 106 to 149 square meters - 32 to 45 tsubo, one and a half to over two times the village's commonest house (this page's arithmetic). A reconstructed headman-class farm of the late Edo period in Chiba has a house of 10 by 6 ken, about 60 by 36 feet and 193 square meters, and a hereditary headman's house in Aomori is 30 meters (about 98 feet) long, with every work space under its roof.

How much longer than deep. The measured single-roof houses above run from about 1.6 to 2.0 times as long as they are deep (the Chiba house 1.7); this page's arithmetic on the figures quoted. How long a 20-tsubo house was against its depth no page read says. The reason a farmhouse is longer than it is deep is taken up in Why is a farmhouse longer than it is deep?

What this means for the map. The farmhouse the maps draw, 46 by 28 feet, is the size of the surviving houses - a headman's or a well-off farmer's - and not the commonest house of a village, which was about half its floor. So an ordinary household's house should be drawn near 35 by 20 feet, a poorer one smaller (a 10-tsubo house is about 25 by 14 feet), and the 46 by 28 feet and upward kept for the headman and the few better-off. The drawn length and depth of a small house carry over the survivors' 1.6-to-2.0 proportion to houses no survivor measures, and that is a GUESS.
