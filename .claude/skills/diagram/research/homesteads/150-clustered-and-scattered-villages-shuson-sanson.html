Clustered and scattered villages (shūson, sanson)

Most farming villages in Japan were clustered: the farmhouses gathered in one limited area and the fields lay around them. A few regions had scattered villages instead, each farmhouse standing apart among its own fields - on the Tonami plain of Toyama, over 7,000 farmhouses spread across about 220 km² (~85 sq mi). Between the two stood the row village, its houses strung in a line along a natural levee, a spring line at a hill's foot, or a road.

The form followed the ground and the water. A clustered village took the higher, drier ground beside its paddy, ground unfit for rice, while on the Tonami plain, an alluvial fan, the farmers built their houses in the middle of their own rice fields so that each could manage the water for their own fields. No page we read says how common each form was.

  • In Japan the clustered village is the general form.
    The farmhouses gather in a limited area, and the arable land is laid out around them. Among clustered forms the commonest is the block village, the kaison, rather than a street village strung along a road; that ranking is a general one, not made for Japan alone.
  • How tightly a clustered village gathered differed by region.
    Around the old capital and the Inland Sea, dense villages of farmhouses behind walls, standing side by side, are common; in the Kanto and the Tohoku, loosely clustered villages are common, each house on a wide lot with its own grove, the houses set well apart.
  • How common was each form?
    No page we read gives a national count or share of the clustered, block, street or scattered forms, for any period. So the clustered village can be called the usual form and the scattered village a regional one, with no number on either.
  • Scattered villages are named on four plains.
    The encyclopedia gives the Tonami plain, the lower Oi river, the Sanuki plain and the Izumo plain as examples of the irregular scattered village.
  • The Tonami plain is the best-known scattered settlement.
    It is an alluvial fan built mainly by the Sho and Oyabe rivers, and its landscape of farmhouses, each surrounded by its own woodland, has been some 500 years in the making.
  • Why did the Tonami farms scatter?
    To manage water: the farmers built their houses in the middle of their own fields to look after the water for those fields, and the houses scattered apart naturally - that no plan lay behind it is this page's reading of the word. An alluvial fan's river sinks underground, so its water table is low and its ground hard to use for paddy; that the answer was for each household to control its own water by living on its holding is this page's reading.
  • The lord's terms for reclaimed land are held to be a major reason the Tonami houses scattered.
    The reclaimed fields belonged to the domain lord, but the peasant who had reclaimed them was allowed to farm them themselves.
  • Each Tonami farm stands in its own grove, the kainyo.
    The grove sheltered the house and its household from the cold seasonal wind and snowstorms of winter and from the summer sun. Kainyo is the local name for the homestead grove found across Japan, at Groves of trees around farmhouses (yashikirin).
  • A Tonami farm's own water was often a channel led into its grounds, not a well.
    On the fan the water table lay deep and wells were hard to dig, so in many areas a small channel was led off the irrigation water into the house's grounds and used for cooking, washing and drinking. How the other areas drew their water no page we read says. A village's shared water is at Washing places at the water's edge and Communal wells (ido).
  • Were the earliest valley farms scattered too?
    Probably. On two estates of the late Heian period, in Satsuma and Bingo, the paddies lay almost without exception in small valleys, and a narrow valley is thought to have held one house or a few, each beside its own small valley paddy, dotted as small villages or single farmsteads up each branching valley.

The row village is a third form, recorded in Europe and in Japan alike.

  • In a row village each farm's land lies beside its house.
    The German Reihendorf is one or two rows of farmsteads, either side of the village street, and the farmland of each dwelling lies next to it, which saves time and the effort of carrying. The Japanese row village, along roads on new fields and along levees, dikes and spring lines, is at How was a row village laid out?.
  • Chinese villages in the hills also stretched along rivers and roads.
    The traditional villages of mountainous southwest Zhejiang are mostly clusters and bands, held in by the ground, and tend to expand in the direction of rivers and roads.
  • The Japanese rows whose layout is measured were planned.
    They are colonies laid out on new fields (at How was a row village laid out?), so for a row that grew by itself the record stands on weaker ground than for the scattered village.

Two clustered villages could look nothing alike, because each took its shape from its own site.

  • A clustered village stood on the high, dry ground beside its paddy.
    Old settlements were sited first on the natural levee, a slight rise that drains well within the wet lowland, with the paddy on the back lowland behind it.
  • A clustered village's shape followed its density, its ground and its roads.
    A nucleated village takes various forms by its housing density, its ground and its traffic. Settlement geography names three: houses gathered irregularly in a lump; houses in a line along a natural levee or a spring line at a hill's foot; and houses in a line along a road. The lump village grows at the foot of a hill or plateau, on a river terrace or at a highway crossing, where water can be got.
  • In China a periodic rural market could grow into a market quarter.
    From the Song dynasty on, some periodic rural markets grew into the 草市 (caoshi, "grass market"), a market quarter outside a city's walls, mostly at a waterway, a ford or a post station.
  • Water was let down from pond to pond.
    Water released from a valley pond was stored in the ponds on the flat and from there let onto the fields, and several ponds strung together in a terrace-like series have names of their own, the stacked pond or the parent-and-child pond.
  • A surveyed field was laid out in squares of 1 cho, the jori grid.
    The grid lines crossed at right angles at intervals of 1 cho, about 109 m (~358 ft).
  • Not a few surveyed grids were tilted off the compass.
    The jori lines usually ran north-south and east-west, but not a few were tilted with the region or the ground.
  • A planned new-field colony gave each farm a long strip of equal area.
    At Santome the farmhouses stood along both sides of a road about 10.9 m (~36 ft) wide, each with its field and woodlot running behind it in a long strip; in Kamitome each house had a frontage of about 72.7 m (~239 ft), a depth of about 681.8 m (~2,237 ft) and an area of about 49,500 m² (~12 acres).

How tightly a clustered village packed its houses is said only in comparison.

  • A clustered village's houses stood closer together than a scattered village's, but by no fixed measure.
    A nucleated village is by definition one whose houses stand packed more closely than a dispersed village's, but there is no definition of how dense it must be to count as one.
  • How much of its ground did a clustered village build on?
    No page we read gives a clustered village's built share of its ground, its houses to the hectare or the size of its house plots, nor any rule or measure, from before 1868, of how far apart its houses stood.
  • A modern survey of 1956 counted houses within 200 m (~656 ft) of each other as one settlement.
    A survey of the Kanto lowland, as its own counting convention, counted houses within 200 m (~656 ft) of each other as one settlement and farther apart as two, because a household farming about 1 cho, about 1 hectare (~2.5 acres) - the average the survey reports for its two districts - needed about that much room. It is a modern geographer's rule, not one the villagers kept.
