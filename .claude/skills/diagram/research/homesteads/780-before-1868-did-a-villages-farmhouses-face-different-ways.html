Before 1868, did a village's farmhouses face different ways?

Sources: yamamoto-2014-koshijo-shuraku (the curved streets of an eighteenth-century Okinawan village)

The map turns each farmhouse a little from its neighbors, and the spread it draws comes from a count of the houses of Okinawan villages published in 1985, which puts the smaller turns down to the houses following curving roads. This question asks what the record holds from before 1868.

The curving street is older than the houses counted. Planned villages of rows of house plots are said to have spread across southern Okinawa and the islands beyond it under the Ryukyu kingdom from the eighteenth century. One of them, Maekawa, was allowed to move to its present site in 1736 and stands on a gentle slope facing south. On cadastral maps thought to date from the time of the land survey project, which show the village before its house plots multiplied and were subdivided, its streets curved widely and its blocks were bow-shaped, most of them one plot deep north to south and three to seven across; the authors suppose the streets were laid along the contours of the slope, and the plots kept narrow north to south so that no plot rose much from front to back. So a village laid out before modern times already had curving streets. That its houses turned with those streets is a guess: the paper says nothing of which way the houses faced.

Not found. No count of which way the houses of a village faced was found from before 1868, in Japan or in China, and no excavated farmstead's bearing was read; the only count is the one published in 1985.

What this means for the map. The smaller turns, a house following the curve of its lane on a south-facing slope, rest on curving streets attested before modern times, the houses turning with them a guess, and stay. How far the houses spread and the one house in ten turned a quarter away rest only on that count, a modern record: the map should stop drawing the quarter-turned tenth, and turn each house by its lane's curve rather than by a spread taken from that count.
