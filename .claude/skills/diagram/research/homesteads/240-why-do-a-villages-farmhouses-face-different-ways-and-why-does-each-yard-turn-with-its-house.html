Why do a village's farmhouses face different ways - each turned a little by the curve of its lane - and why does each yard turn with its house?

Sources: sakamoto-tsubaki-1985-omoya-muki (how far the houses of one village spread in bearing); the GM's ruling of 2026-09-26

A farmhouse in a clustered village faces south, toward its work (threshing) yard (see the threshing-yard entry and where the yard and garden went), but no two stand at exactly the same bearing. The survey below puts part of the spread down to the roads curving, and that houses were otherwise set out by eye rather than surveyed to the compass is a GUESS; either way a row of houses all square to the map would read as drawn rather than built.

How far they strayed. A survey of the houses of 27 Okinawan villages on slopes, which noted which of the sixteen points of the compass each main house faced, found them spread over a village's commonest bearing and the two points either side of it, the neighboring bearings arising where the roads curve; every other house faced right, left or back from that spread. Of all the houses, 87% faced within the spread, 11% were turned to the right, and only 1% each faced left or back - much as in the same authors' count of villages on flat ground. In one village the same survey found at the foot of hills, on a slope falling to the northeast, 15 of 21 houses faced southeast and 3 south-southeast, turned a quarter away from the fall of the ground toward the south. Three points of the compass span some 67 degrees, so a house could stand as much as about 30 degrees from its neighbors' common bearing and still count as facing with them, and about one house in eight faced a different way altogether. The survey counts by compass point, so how the houses spread inside one point is not measured; how many stand close to the common bearing and how many out toward the edge of the spread is a GUESS. It is also one island region's houses, counted for a paper of 1985. No count of house bearings from before 1868 was found; what is attested before modern times is the cause the survey gives for the smaller turns, streets curving along the slope, in an Okinawan village laid out in 1736 (see Before 1868, did a village's farmhouses face different ways?).

The work yard and the kitchen-garden beds turn with their house. The yard is the ground in front of the house's south wall (the entries named above); a yard set square to the map beside a house turned four degrees would look like a mistake, and the GM ruled that the two always line up, "because that's just how they would be naturally laid out". The homestead turns as one piece about the middle of the house, and the ground cleared for each part is the ground it is drawn on.

The rule the map follows: each farmhouse is turned from its village's common bearing, which is south or near it, by the curve of the lane it stands on, so neighbors along a bending lane face a little apart (how far, a GUESS bounded by the survey's three compass points); no farmhouse is turned a quarter away, since the right-turned tenth rests only on the modern count published in 1985. Its work yard and garden beds are turned by the same amount, so their edges run with the house's walls, and the yard's edge toward the house is kept straight and parallel to it, even though its other edges wander a little as a hand-laid plot's do. (Attested: the spread over three points of the compass, in a modern count; before 1868, streets curving along the slope. Guess: how the turns are spread inside that range. The GM's rulings: the yard and beds sharing it, and the yard's edge toward the house running parallel to it.)
