Threshing and drying yards at farmhouses (niwa)

A Japanese farmhouse kept open ground beside it, the niwa or work yard, where the harvest was threshed and the grain dried. Threshing was usually done in the yard, on straw mats with the threshing combs set out on them, the rice drawn through the combs a handful at a time. The threshed grain was then dried on mats spread to fill the yard, and husked later, indoors, usually as night work.

So a household measured its yard by the straw mats it held - ordinarily forty to sixty, a few past a hundred. An ordinary yard was some 700 to 1,100 square feet.

  • Grain was dried on straw mats across Japan.
    The mats are recorded in the Kinki region, the grain spread on them in the sun; in Shikoku; and in northern Kyushu, where the grain went out on the mats every day so it would not rot. That the mats covered the whole yard is Kitamoto's record alone.
  • How long did the grain lie on the mats?
    A day to a week. At Kitamoto, rice that had hung on a rack to dry dried on the mats for about a day, and rice threshed without much drying for about three fine days. In Shikoku, rice first dried lying on the ground spent three to about six more days on mats.
  • How big was an ordinary yard?
    A Kitamoto (Saitama) city history gives forty to sixty mats ordinarily, past a hundred for a few, at two mats to the tsubo - so 20-30 tsubo (~710-1,070 sq ft) ordinarily and past 50 tsubo (~1,780 sq ft) at the top, this page's arithmetic on the history's figures; a large farm kept a hundred or a hundred and fifty mats ready. An Okayama museum counts about fifty mats a farm, kept for sun-drying the unhulled rice, and a mat woven at Nishidani measured about 90 x 180 cm (~3 x 6 ft), so about 870 sq ft. A yard at Kodaira, a large Musashino holding's, is given as 70 tsubo (~2,490 sq ft), from a page we could not read. Both mat counts are local memory with no date, and may be of the Meiji years or later.
  • A farm's whole lot was much larger than its yard.
    Farms needed a wide front yard, so their homestead lots ran 100-300 tsubo (~3,560-10,670 sq ft) - a figure easy to mistake for the yard's own. It comes from a city history whose page did not load.
  • Did a yard's size depend on what the household grew?
    In part. The Kitamoto history ties its yard to the household's barley acreage. Rice was hung on racks in the field to dry before it was threshed, so a rice household might need less floor - yet the Okayama count above, of mats kept for rice, falls in the middle of Kitamoto's band.
  • Could a yard's size be worked out from the harvest?
    Not from anything old. A yard could be sized from a paddy's yield - the standard for a middle-grade paddy was 1.3 koku a tan - the share of the grain left after hulling, and the depth the grain was spread to dry. The only depth found is the International Rice Research Institute's 2.5 cm (~1 in), modern advice for farmers of today; how thick an Edo household spread its grain, and how big a rice district's yard was, no page we read gives.
  • How did yard sizes vary from farm to farm?
    Lopsidedly - most farms near the middle and a few far above it. No survey counts yards by size, so the shape comes from what the old registers do count, and each is lopsided the same way: the Kamikanai tax ledger of 1771, in the Edo period, gives the size of every main house, the village headman's standing apart at 70.5 tsubo, about three times most of the others; Yamada's study of Kikoba village puts homestead lots at 20 to 100 bu, its table of lots and house footprints together peaking at 30 and 50; and Kitamoto's forty to sixty mats, with a few yards past a hundred, is the same shape.
  • By the Genroku era, every peasant household held a house plot.
    Early registers carry a class of households with no homestead (無屋敷登録人, muyashiki torokunin), but by the Genroku era every peasant household, the fieldless included, had come to hold one - a statement we could not find on any page we could read.
  • Did a larger household have a larger yard?
    Larger, but not in proportion. At Kamikanai in 1771 a main house grew by about 10 tsubo for each 5 koku more that a household held - the house, not the yard - while elsewhere the same paper finds no fixed correspondence between holding and house size. At Kikoba the homestead lot averaged 67.3 tsubo among the households with the most land and 33.3 tsubo among those with the least: twice the lot, not four times.
  • In a planned colony, every settler got the same homestead.
    When the new fields of Santome were laid out, all but finished by the spring of 1696, in the Genroku era, the plots varied, but the homesteads were an entirely equal allotment.
  • No page we read says how the mats were laid out.
    Edge to edge, in rows or with walkways between - the sources are silent, and silent too on whether anything else stood in the yard while the grain dried.

Rice farmers in south China did the same work on different ground: a floor of beaten earth before the house, and in some villages one floor for the whole village.

  • In south China, rice was threshed on a floor, often with an ox and a stone roller.
    Song Yingxing's Tiangong Kaiwu says half the rice was threshed by beating the sheaves by hand, and half by spreading them on the threshing floor and having an ox drag a stone roller over them. The roller saved three times the labor, so southern households with large plantings mostly threshed on the floor with an ox. In a wet harvest, sheaves that could not be brought to the floor were beaten in a wooden tub out in the field. Whose the floor was, the text does not say.
  • Some south-China villages shared one drying floor.
    The Hakka built it into their great walled house, the weilongwu, between its half-moon pond and the house, and one such house could hold a dozen, dozens or even a hundred households. In the Cantonese village the main lane, at the front of the village, was sometimes widened into a drying ground, a village's common wealth going into its lanes and open spaces.
  • Did other south-China villages give each household its own floor?
    Some did. A writer from a rice valley below Mount Heng, in Hunan, remembers every household there with its own drying floor in front of the house.
  • A drying floor had to be prepared before each harvest.
    Bare earth would not do. A wealthy household laid lime and sand over it, but hardly one household in a hundred had such a floor; the rest hoed the ground level, tamped it and washed it with cow dung and water a few days before each harvest.
  • How big was a shared floor?
    The Hakka drying floor was generally made as wide as the main house is tall, measured with a builder's ruler of lucky and unlucky lengths - a proportion, not a measurement. No page we read gives a shared floor's size, or says how much of it each household used.
