Threshing and drying yards at farmhouses (niwa)

A Japanese farmhouse kept open ground beside it, the niwa or work yard, where the harvest was threshed and the grain dried. Threshing was usually done in the yard, on straw mats with the threshing combs set out on them, the rice drawn through the combs a handful at a time. The threshed grain was then dried on mats spread to fill the yard, and husked later, indoors, usually as night work.

So a household measured its yard by the straw mats it held - ordinarily forty to sixty, a few past a hundred. An ordinary yard was some 700 to 1,100 square feet.

  • How big was an ordinary yard?
    A Kitamoto (Saitama) city history gives forty to sixty mats ordinarily, past a hundred for a few, at two mats to the tsubo - so 20-30 tsubo (66-99 sq m (~710-1,070 sq ft)) ordinarily and past 50 tsubo (165 sq m (~1,780 sq ft)) at the top, this page's arithmetic on the history's figures. An Okayama museum counts about fifty mats a farm, and a mat measured about 90 x 180 cm (~3 x 6 ft), so about 80 sq m (~860 sq ft). A yard at Kodaira is given as 70 tsubo (231 sq m (~2,490 sq ft)), a large holding's, from a page we could not read. No survey tabulates yards as areas.
  • A farm's whole lot was much larger than its yard.
    A figure of 100-300 tsubo (~3,560-10,670 sq ft) that is easy to mistake for the yard is the whole homestead lot: farms needed a wide front yard, so their lots ran 100-300 tsubo, by a city history whose page did not load.
  • A yard's size followed the household's crops.
    The Kitamoto history ties its yard to the household's barley acreage. Rice was hung on racks in the field to dry before it was threshed, so a rice household might need less floor. For rice there is the Okayama museum's count of about fifty mats a farm, kept for sun-drying the unhulled rice - about 25 tsubo (~890 sq ft), the middle of Kitamoto's band. Both counts are local memory with no date, and may be of the Meiji years or later.
  • Could a yard's size be worked out from the harvest?
    Not from anything old. A yard could be sized from a paddy's yield - the standard for a middle paddy was 1.3 koku a tan - the share of the grain left after hulling, and the depth the grain was spread to dry. The only depth found is the International Rice Research Institute's 2.5 cm (~1 in), modern advice for tropical farmers of today; how thick an Edo household spread its grain, and how big a rice district's yard was, no page we read gives.
  • How did yard sizes vary from farm to farm?
    Lopsidedly - most farms near the middle and a few far above it. No survey counts yards by size, so the shape comes from what the old registers do count, and each is lopsided the same way: the Kamikanai tax ledger of 1771 gives the size of every main house, the Kikoba land-survey register of the Genroku era puts homestead lots at 20 to 100 bu, peaking at 30 and 50, and Kitamoto's forty to sixty mats, with a few yards past a hundred, is the same shape.
  • Did every household have a yard?
    Early registers carry a class of households with no homestead (無屋敷登録人, muyashiki torokunin), but by the Genroku era every peasant household, the fieldless included, had come to hold a house plot - a statement we could not find on any page we could read.
  • Did a larger household have a larger yard?
    Larger, but not in proportion. At Kamikanai in 1771 a main house grew by about 10 tsubo for each 5 koku more that a household held - the house, not the yard - while at Iwahara no fixed correspondence is found. At Kikoba the homestead lot averaged 67.3 tsubo among the largest holders and 33.3 tsubo among the smallest: twice the lot, not four times.
  • In a planned colony, every settler got the same homestead.
    When the new fields of Santome were laid out, finished by the spring of 1696, the plots varied, but the homesteads were an entirely equal allotment.
  • Grain was dried on straw mats across Japan.
    At Kitamoto, rice that had hung on a rack dried on the mats for about a day, and rice threshed without much drying for about three fine days. The mats are recorded in the Kinki region, the grain spread on them in the sun; in Shikoku, three to about six more days on mats for rice first dried on the ground; and in northern Kyushu, on the mats every day so the grain would not rot. That the mats covered the whole yard is Kitamoto's record alone. A large farm kept a hundred or a hundred and fifty mats ready.
  • How were the mats laid out?
    No page we read says - edge to edge, in rows, or with walkways between - nor whether anything else stood in the yard while the grain dried.
  • In south China, rice was threshed on a floor, often with an ox and a stone roller.
    A Ming account of 1637 says half the rice was threshed by beating the sheaves by hand, and half by spreading them on the threshing floor and having an ox drag a stone roller over them. The roller saved three times the labor, so southern households with large plantings mostly threshed on the floor with an ox. In a wet harvest, sheaves that could not be brought to the floor were beaten in a wooden tub out in the field.
  • Some south-China villages shared one drying floor.
    The Hakka built it into the great house itself, between its half-moon pond and the house, and one such house could hold a dozen, dozens or even a hundred households. In the Cantonese village the main lane, at the front of the village, was sometimes widened into a drying ground, a village's common wealth going into its lanes and open spaces.
  • Elsewhere in south China, each household had its own floor.
    A writer from a rice valley below Mount Heng, in Hunan, remembers every household with its own drying floor in front of the house. Bare earth would not do: a wealthy household laid lime and sand over it, but hardly one household in a hundred had such a floor, and the rest hoed the ground level, tamped it and washed it with cow dung and water a few days before each harvest.
  • How big was a shared floor?
    The Hakka drying floor was generally made as wide as the main house is tall, measured with a builder's ruler of lucky and unlucky lengths - a proportion, not a measurement. No page we read gives a shared floor's size, or says how much of it each household used.
