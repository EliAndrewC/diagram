How big is a draft-animal byre, and what stands in it?

Sources: bunka-ueno-umaya and bunka-okamoto-umaya (two measured Edo stables); fao-farm-structures-cattle (shade and stall dimensions per animal) and tnau-cattle-housing (covered and open space per cow), both modern cattle standards; buck-1930-farm-economy (east central China's oxen and water buffaloes) and nakanishi-1994-kogyu-koba (eastern Japan's plow horses); this project's drawing decision

Oxen and water buffaloes are both found on the farms of east central China's rice country, the buffaloes the larger share, and in Japan the plowing beast was an ox in the west but a horse in the east, with both in Kyushu and Shikoku; which beast, where, is at Did a farm plow with an ox or a horse? The shed drawn for one is a single-bay open-fronted lean-to of about 16 by 11 ft, some 15 sq m. That is the room one or two draft animals need and no more.

What an Edo stable measured. Two freestanding stables of the Edo period survive on the national register with their sizes. One at Maizuru, in Kyoto prefecture, built between 1751 and 1829, is a single-story gabled building of 2.5 by 2 ken, about 15 by 12 feet at the common ken of 1.82 m (the record's arithmetic); the register gives its building area as 30 sq m, which that frame alone does not reach, and the page does not say what makes up the difference. One in Kumamoto prefecture, of about 1840, covers 34 sq m and stands in the southeast corner of its plot, along the road. Both belong to substantial households - the Maizuru stable stands beside the house's gatehouse row - and the size of an ordinary smallholder's byre no page read gives. The drawn 16 by 11 ft is the Maizuru stable's frame, and the building areas say a well-off household's stable could be twice that under its roof. Two modern cattle standards agree on the order of size - a design manual for the tropics reckons a shade structure at 2.5 to 3 sq m per animal and a single stall at 2.2 m by 1.2 m for a cow of 500 to 600 kg, and an Indian extension table allows a cow 3.5 sq m under cover with 7 sq m of open ground beside it and 60 to 75 cm of manger space - but they are today's advice for a modern beast, a cross-check and not what the size rests on. The size is doing a second job as well. Anything larger starts to read as a European multi-stall barn, and there are none on these maps, because the draft animal of an East Asian wet-rice farm lives under the farmhouse roof (What else stood on or near a farmstead?). A byre on the sheet is a shelter for a beast or two, kept visibly well under the farmhouse's own 120 sq m.

The rule the map follows: a byre is drawn as a small plank-roofed shed of about 16 by 11 ft with a dark stall mouth, distinct from the solid gray storehouse and from the dwellings, and it is kept small on purpose.
