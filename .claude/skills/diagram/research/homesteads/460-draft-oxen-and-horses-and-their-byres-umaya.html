Draft oxen and horses and their byres (umaya)

A farm that plowed with a beast kept an ox, a water buffalo or a horse, and which one depended on the region. In Japan horses plowed in the east and cattle in the west, with both in Kyushu and Shikoku; in the rice country of China it was the ox and the water buffalo, and the horse, the donkey and the mule belonged to the north. Not every household kept one: in one western province from the early 1700s only about half did, and fewer as time went on.

The beast lived with the household that kept it. Across most of Japan its stall took a corner of the farmhouse's earth-floored work space, the inner stable; elsewhere it stood in a stable of its own. In northern Japan's L-shaped farmhouses the stable wing took the warm air of the kitchen hearth, and in one mountain district of Hyogo the stall was inside the house partly because there was too little flat land to spare for a separate shed. Two free-standing stables of the Edo period that survive with their sizes cover 30 and 34 sq m (~323 and ~366 sq ft).

The national counts of Japan's plow beasts come from the 1870s and 1880s; that the village of a generation earlier, before 1868, plowed with the same beasts is this project's reading.

  • The earliest national count of Japan's plow beasts this record has found is an army inventory of 1886.
    The army counted, district by district across the whole country, what could be requisitioned from the population when it moved, and in 1886 it divided the cattle into cart, pack and plow cattle and the horses into riding, carriage, pack and plow horses.
  • Plow cattle were spread widely over western Japan, and almost absent from the east.
    They were found west of Shiga and Mie. In the east they were clearly found only on the island of Sado, at the southern tips of the Izu and Boso peninsulas and in the north of the Kitakami hills.
  • Plow horses were many in the east, and in Kyushu and southern Shikoku too.
    They were the reverse of the cattle, numerous from Niigata, Nagano and Yamanashi eastward, but many too in the southwest. The count's historian divides the country into three: an eastern horse region, a western cattle region centered on Kinki and Chugoku, and a mixed region of Kyushu and Shikoku.
  • A national survey of 1877, within a decade of the Edo period's end, also found cattle in the west and horses in the east.
    Cattle were concentrated in the west, and horses far more numerous in the east and in southern Kyushu.
  • How many plow beasts were there to each acre of Japan's farmland?
    The 1886 army count averages about a quarter of a beast to every cho (about 2.45 acres) of cropped land, but the count's historian warns that the national average means almost nothing, so widely did the provinces differ.
  • In the setting, a horse can turn up on a farm in cattle country.
    A daimyo with more horses than the domain needs often gives the surplus to the village headmen to hand out to the farms as draft animals.

Rice farmers in China plowed with other beasts, and a survey of Chinese farms made in the 1920s sets out which.

  • In China's rice country the plow beasts were the ox and the water buffalo, the buffalo the larger share.
    Both were found on the farms of the rice-growing east-central provinces, and the buffalo, which wants a warm climate and water to wallow in, was not suited to the north.
  • In China, horses, donkeys and mules were northern animals.
    They were far commoner in the north, and the mules were kept out of the south because they could not work the wet rice fields.
  • Half the farms of north China had an ox, and fewer than a third in the rice country.
    The survey counted farms with oxen in each region.

A household with a beast kept it at home, and a household without one borrowed or hired one.

  • From the early 1700s only about half the farm households of Bizen, in western Japan, kept an ox or a horse.
    In Bizen, from the early 1700s, the share of farm households keeping cattle or horses was about half, the same as in neighboring Mimasaka, and it fell further as time went on.
  • In Bizen in the mid-1600s, most beasts belonged to the farmers who kept them.
    In Bizen in the mid-1600s more than 80% of the cattle and horses had been bought by the farmers who kept them, 14% were beasts placed in their care, and 4% had come through the domain.
  • Large owners lent their cattle out to tenants.
    One headman's household of 1818-43 owned about a thousand cattle, and had them placed with borrowers in its own neighborhood and as far away as other provinces.
  • Sanuki, short of draft cattle at plowing time, hired them from neighboring Awa.
    Sanuki took cattle from the mountain villages of neighboring Awa, whose grassland let them keep more than their paddies needed, for one koku of rice in return, and at times as many as several hundred came.

Where the beast stood followed from who kept it.

  • A farmhouse's stable was either inside the house or a building of its own.
    An encyclopedia article by an architectural historian divides it into the inner stable, inside the main house, and the outer stable, standing on its own, and says the inner stable, taking a corner of the house's earth-floored work space, was widespread across the country, though few in regions such as southern Kanto. It gives no date or count.
  • In some regions the beast was mostly stabled inside its keeper's house.
    In the Okayama region before modern times the cattle shed was mostly of the inner-stable kind, which this project reads as a stall under the house's own roof. In the mountain district of Mikata, in Hyogo, many households kept their cattle in a stall inside the house - about 4,000 such houses at the peak in the 1950s - partly, the local cattle museum says, because there was too little flat land to spare for a separate shed.
  • In northern Japan's L-shaped farmhouse (magariya), the stable is a wing of the house.
    The house is named for its main body and stable made one in an L. Around the Tono basin the stable joins the lower end of the house and projects off its south side, and the article records both mirror-image forms. The warm air from the kitchen hearth flows into the stable and warms the horses, which this project reads as a sign of what the horses were worth.
  • The L-shaped farmhouse was common among the better-off farm households.
    The article finds it common among the upper farm households, such as a village headman's.
  • In Shaanxi, in north China, a wealthy courtyard house often gave its east wing to grain, a kitchen or a horse stable.
    The east wing takes the summer afternoon sun and the winter northwesterly, and so is unfit to live in; in Shaanxi it is often given over to a store, a kitchen or a stable.
  • Did several households share a byre on common ground, or keep one at the village edge?
    No page we read describes either: every arrangement found ties the beast to one keeper - its owner, or the tenant or region it was lent to. A shared shed among the houses and a shed beyond the last house are both GUESSES.

A few farm stables of the Edo period are registered tangible cultural properties, measured and dated.

  • How big was the Edo-period stable at Maizuru, in Kyoto prefecture?
    Its frame is 2.5 by 2 ken. Built between 1751 and 1829, it is a single-story gabled building under a tile roof. If its ken is 6 shaku, about 1.82 m (~6 ft), the frame is about 15 by 12 ft, some 17 sq m (~183 sq ft), this project's arithmetic; the register gives its building area as 30 sq m (~323 sq ft), which that frame alone does not reach, and does not say what makes up the difference.
  • A stable in Kumamoto prefecture, built about 1840 in the Edo period, covers 34 sq m (~366 sq ft).
    It is a timber building of one story under a tile roof.
  • The two measured Edo-period stables stand toward the road and the gate.
    The Kumamoto stable stands at the southeast corner of its plot, along the road; the Maizuru one stands southwest of the main house and southeast of its gate rowhouse.
  • How big was an ordinary smallholder's byre?
    No page we read gives it: both measured stables stand on farmsteads listed as tangible cultural properties.
  • How much room do modern cattle standards give a beast?
    About 2.5 to 3.5 sq m (~27-38 sq ft) under cover. A design manual for the tropics reckons a shade structure at 2.5 to 3 sq m (~27-32 sq ft) an animal, and a single stall at 2.2 m (~7 ft) by 1.2 m (~4 ft) for a cow of 500 to 600 kg (~1,100-1,320 lb); an Indian extension table allows a cow 3.5 sq m (~38 sq ft) under cover, 7 sq m (~75 sq ft) of open ground beside it and 60 to 75 cm (~24-30 in) of manger. They are today's advice for a modern beast, a cross-check and not a measure of a premodern byre.

How near a farm kept its beast to its drinking water the record answers only in part.

  • How far was a household's beast from its well?
    About the width of a farmhouse, some 20 to 40 ft, a GUESS. The stable-wing farmhouse kept its horse under its own roof, and this project reads the well, where a house had one, as standing in the rear corner of the earth-floored work space or in a rear room, inside the same building; the pages we read give the well no position and the house no dimension.
  • Rural China set the privy over the pigsty.
    The one old Chinese custom we found on where livestock stood concerns the pig, not the plow beast. The pig toilet, an outhouse over a pigsty, was once common in rural China, where one character means both "pigsty" and "privy"; Han-dynasty funerary models show the custom is ancient, and by 1988 farmers were fermenting the two kinds of dung together for the fish ponds. That cattle sheds and chicken houses joined the group is a GUESS, on no page we read. It is a rule about where the dung went, not one holding livestock away from drinking water.
  • Was there an old rule keeping livestock away from a well?
    None that we found. No premodern rule turned up in the geomantic or village-layout material; modern popular feng-shui pages name a livestock pen among the places not to dig a well, but none cites an older text.
  • Many towns built public troughs for their horses to drink at.
    Troughs were common in many towns and cities, for horses to drink while tethered; such a basin was built to give humans or animals fresh drinking water, so watering the beasts there was what the works were for. That a trough had a well and a pump beside it is this project's expectation, a GUESS on no page we read.
  • Modern guidance keeps a protected well away from animal yards.
    A well should stand uphill of animal yards and privies, at 20 m (~66 ft) on heavy soils and twice that on light ones, and a shallow well is readily fouled from the ground. That is a finding about the consequences, not evidence that old builders sited a well to avoid them.
