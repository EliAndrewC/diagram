Was a farmhouse one straight block, an L, or built around a court?

Sources: kotobank-minka (the forms and their regions), magariya-jawiki (the L as a mark of rank), nihonminkaen-kanto (two-roof houses in the Kanto), sanheyuan-zhwiki and minnan-architecture-zhwiki (the Chinese forms)

In Japan: a straight house, an L, or two roofs side by side. A farmhouse under one roof is the straight house; one with a wing joined on is called a tsunoya in the west and, in the north, a magariya (Iwate and around) or a chumon-zukuri (Yamagata, Akita and around); and a house whose two roofs stand eaves to eaves and are used inside as one is the bunto house. Each belongs to a region. The magariya is the farmhouse of northern Miyagi to southern Iwate, an L with the stable wing thrust forward from the front of the earth-floored work room under one continuous roof; the chumon-zukuri is the farmhouse of Yamagata, Akita and Fukushima with its wing projecting from the main house; and the bunto house stands from southern Kumamoto to Kagoshima. The bunto house was not only southern: in the eighteenth century many stood near Utsunomiya, far from the coast, and two of the houses at a Kawasaki museum are two-roofed, a headman's from Ibaraki and a net-owner's from Chiba. In the Nanbu country of Iwate the L was also a statement of standing: it was commonest among the upper farmers, such as the village officials, so choosing it over the straight house is thought to have said something about the household's rank.

In China: a single range, an L, or three sides of a court. The three-sided sanheyuan - a main range with a wing on each side and a wall across the front - is a basic Han Chinese house, the courtyard house with its front range replaced by a wall, and the smaller forms beside it are the single straight range and the L of a main range and one wing. It is a form found all over China, not in one province.

What the record does not say. How many households in a village lived in each form no page read counts.

What this means for the map. These are distinct forms, each attested and each regional, so the plan is a choice rolled per settlement, not a single answer: the straight house as the plain form everywhere; the L - magariya or chumon-zukuri - in the horse-raising north, and there most of all for the headman and the better-off; the two-roof bunto house in the far south of Kyushu and on the Pacific side; and the three-sided court, or its single-range and L forms, in a Chinese-leaning village. How often each is rolled is a GUESS.
