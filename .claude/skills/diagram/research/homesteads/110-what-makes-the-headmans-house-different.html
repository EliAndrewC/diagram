What makes the headman's house different?

Sources: the GM's rulings of 2026-07-21; the GM's campaign notes, l7r-budgets; oamishirasato-kaoku (why a headman's house was large, what stood on its plot, and how big an ordinary farmer's house was), kawai-jutaku-bunka (one measured headman's house), shakkanho-jawiki (the tsubo in square meters), jikata-monjo-kotobank (where the village's records were kept), nta-nengu (the tax delivered to the headman first), gogura-kotobank (where the tax rice waited, and where a village store was built)

Its size, and almost nothing else. The GM ruled on 2026-07-21 that the headman's house is a FARMSTEAD and not an exception: the grandest house in the village gets its work yard and its dooryard garden like every other farm, and both are capped at an ordinary farm's sizes, because a big house does not mean a field-sized kitchen bed. On a map of dispersed farmsteads the headman's homestead brings its grove too, where there is room for one. What makes the house read as the headman's is that it is plainly the largest - about 92 by 56 ft against a plain farmhouse's 46 by 28 - and not a different plan or a compound wall. A headman's plot could have a gate-house: the one the record has read in full, the Ohara family's of 1849 (below), has a nagayamon with a storeroom attached. The map draws none - a deliberate deviation, following the ruling that the headman's house is a farmstead.

Why it was the largest. A headman's house was large because the office needed it to be: the house that served as headman's lodged the lord's officials when they toured the villages, so the family kept a large residence of necessity. How MUCH larger is less settled. In one village's table of 1885, nearly half the households had a main house of about 18 tsubo, the usual size then, and the leading households 40 and 45 - about 60 sq m against 130 to 150 (a tsubo is about 3.31 sq m), two to two and a half times. One measured house of a family that served as shoya, built in 1805 in Gifu, is about 12.5 by 6.5 ken, with 291 sq m of building area over two stories. The GM's campaign notes give a village "a slightly larger headman's household". The drawn 92 by 56 ft, about 480 sq m, is four times a plain farmhouse's area and larger than any house read, so it is a GUESS on the large side.

What the office does add is a storehouse. The office of the village headman (shoya) kept things. The village's records - its land registers among them - were made village by village and survive in the houses of the families that served as headmen. The tax came to the headman first; the village packed the tax rice into bales and held it for a time in the gogura, and on the set day it went on to the lord's storehouse. The gogura was the village's own storehouse, but not every village had one: several villages might share one, or a headman's storehouse was used in its place, and until the shogunate ordered storehouses built at the villages' expense in 1789, after grain was found released improperly, the village officials' private storehouses were often used. The one headman's plot read in full, the Ohara family's of 1849, has an earthen storehouse (kura) among its barn, stable, sheds, miso store, firewood shed and privy. So the headman always has a kura, where an ordinary farm carries one only sometimes (the question below). The GM ruled that on 2026-07-21; left to the ordinary roll, a headman could come up without one, which is chance reading as doctrine. That the registers were kept IN the storehouse, rather than elsewhere in the house, is this page's reading.

Where the tax rice waited: two forms. The record attests two places for a village's tax rice: the headman's own kura, and a village gogura standing apart. A handbook of 1794 says where the second stood: within the village's dwelling area, mostly on dry-field ground, or on the plot of a vacant house.

The rule the map follows (village tier): a village rolls where its tax rice waits, from its own seed - in the headman's kura, or in a village gogura, one storehouse standing on its own among the houses, on dry ground or on an empty house plot and never in the paddy (the handbook says "mostly"; "never" is the map's choice). The headman keeps their own kura either way.

A map drawing convention, and the reason for it: the headman's house is drawn as a plain farmhouse enlarged rather than with the large-house glyph, because that glyph carries a storeroom wing that stands outside the ground the homestead reserves and lands on a neighbor's work yard.
