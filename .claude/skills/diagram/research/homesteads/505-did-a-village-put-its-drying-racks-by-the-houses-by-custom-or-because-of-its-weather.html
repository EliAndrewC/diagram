Did a village put its drying racks by the houses by custom, or because of its weather?

Sources: nishimura-makino-1959 (where racks stood by the house, and what decided the drying method: snow, rain at harvest, wet paddies, and tradition), ehime-mushiroboshi (the tall permanent racks of the mountain villages)

Where racks stood in general, and how long they were, is Where were the rice-drying racks put up?; this question is the rack by the house, and what decided whether a village had one.

Racks gathered by the house: one region's answer to changeable weather. A geographers' survey of the racks across Japan found them built together near the house on the San'in side, where the weather changes easily, so that the threshing could be done within the homestead. In the mountain villages of Ehime the tall racks of six or seven tiers were permanent, and some were roofed. Which side of the house such a rack stood on, no page read says.

The weather decided, over whole regions. The same survey reads the drying method as shaped by the natural and social setting of the country that took it up, not by any one village's choice. The tall rack of many tiers stands where the snow is deep, and the authors call it a climate landscape, an adaptation to the climate. Tall racks on the Sea of Japan side and low racks on the Pacific side divide along the mountain spine of the country, though the two often mix locally, as on the low wetlands around Lake Hamana. A map the authors drew from farmers' answers to a national agricultural conference of 1881 divides the country into broad regions, not villages: drying on the ground in the far west of Honshu, southern Shikoku and Kyushu, racks in the northeast, the Hokuriku, the Tokai, the Kinki and the Chugoku, and both in the Kanto. Within one mixed district the farmers switched method by the ground and the weather: racks only for wet paddies and when rain was expected at harvest, and in fine weather the rice dried on the bunds or the grass.

Custom slowed it at the margins. Where the harvest had many clear days the rack was taken up less readily, and in Kyushu the authors name both the climate and traditional habit as holding it back; the rack reached Kyushu only from the Meiji era on. In Fukuoka one man's advice held it back: a leading farmer who directed the prefecture's farm teaching judged the rack unsuited to its ground, and his view was a great force against its spread. Custom and teaching show in the record working over a whole prefecture or region, as a delay in a practice's arrival - not as neighboring villages in one climate choosing differently.

What this means for the map. The rack by the house is not chosen at random village by village. It follows the harvest weather of the country the settlement stands in, which the settlement's own description states, so that neighboring settlements in one climate draw the same: where the harvest weather is changeable, every farmstead gathers its rack by the house; where it is settled, the racks stand out on the paddies after the harvest, the seasonal map's business, and none is drawn at the house. That settled weather keeps the racks on the fields is our reading: the survey names the gathered racks only for the changeable coast, and racks were mostly built on the fields (Where were the rice-drying racks put up?). A settlement that states nothing takes settled weather, since the gathered racks are one region's form - this project's decision, as the regional wind is. Where at the house the rack stands is a GUESS, the sources being silent: along one side of the yard, in the half nearest the house where that leaves it room, and never in the yard's southern half, so that it shades the ground north of it and never the drying floor, which needs the sun from the south (The threshing yard's sun). Its length runs from the yard's edge by the house to its middle, or along the whole side where the near half has no room, a GUESS; it is drawn thicker than its poles so that it reads, a map drawing CONVENTION.
