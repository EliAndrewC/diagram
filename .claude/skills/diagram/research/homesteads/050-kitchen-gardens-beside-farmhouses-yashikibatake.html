Kitchen gardens beside farmhouses (yashikibatake)

A Japanese farm household grew food for its own table on a plot of its own, which had its own name, the yashikibatake, the "homestead field". Its staple grains, beans, tubers and bulk vegetables grew out in the household's dry fields, the hatake, so the bed beside the house was most likely a small one, a matter of a few tsubo rather than of tan. No page we read gives its size or lists its crops, so its size and its crops here are guesses. Where the bed stood for the sun is at Sunlight and shade on the farm.

  • What did a kitchen bed grow?
    Daily greens - daikon, onions, beans and herbs - on this project's reading, a GUESS. The dry field is defined as the tilled ground where grains, vegetables, beans, tubers and fruit trees were grown, and the bed's name says only that its crops were for the household's own use. That the bulk crops grew in the fields and not in the bed is a reading too: no page we read lists the bed's crops or says the bulk crops were kept out of it.
  • How big was a kitchen bed?
    From a few tsubo up to about 1.4 se, roughly 10 to 140 sq m (~108-1,507 sq ft), a GUESS. A tsubo is about 3.31 sq m (~36 sq ft), and a se is 30 tsubo, about 99 sq m (~1,067 sq ft). The article that names the bed gives no area for it, and no page we read gives one.
  • Was a Chinese farmhouse's dooryard plot the same kind of bed?
    In the rice-growing south of China, on this project's reading, it was the same small bed for the household's daily vegetables, with the bulk of the growing out in the household's fields. This is a GUESS: no page we read measures a Chinese farmhouse's dooryard plot.
