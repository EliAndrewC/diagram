The farmstead's fixtures - privy, woodpile, manure heap, bath, coop, household shrine, persimmon

Sources: kotobank-benjo, sinyoken-madori, artic-pigsty-latrine, sugiura-1973-fuzoku (the rates), jawiki-koedame, shimogoe-jawiki, kyuhi-jawiki (what stable manure was made of), nogyo-zensho-jawiki, kotobank-warazuka

The GM chose which of the farmstead's fixtures the map draws: "privies are something that we want ... firewood stacks are actually large enough to render ... Same thing with manure heaps, same thing with baths, same thing with persimmons ... a household shrine being something which is very rare, but which is notable when it does appear ... as for chicken coops ... go with whatever was the case in imperial China." Every drawn size is TRUE feet at the map's scale; the sizes are GUESSES unless a source is named, because the record describes these things and almost never measures them.

This question holds the privy and the manure heap, which the record ties together, and what the map draws of every fixture. Each of the others is its own question: the woodpile at Where was the firewood stacked?, the bath at Did a farmhouse have a bath shed?, the coop at Did a farmstead keep chickens?, the shrine at Which farmsteads had a household shrine? and the persimmon at Why does a persimmon stand beside so many farmhouses?

The privy (便所, benjo). The Nipponica encyclopedia (on kotobank): 「In farm households it was usual to provide one urinal and one privy as a single building independent of the main house.」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「農家では、小便所一つと大便所一つを、母屋から 独立した一つの建物として設けるのが普通であった」) - on a farm the urinal and the privy were ONE building independent of the main house, and that was the norm. Where it stood (sinyoken floor-plan (間取り) pages): 「it is thought the privy was under the eaves in front of the stable to the left of the entrance, or else a separate outhouse in the yard」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「便所は入口の左横の厩の前の軒下か、あるいは庭に別棟の外便所があったと考えられる」) - under the eaves by the stable beside the entrance, or a separate outhouse in the yard. No page read names a privy by the back door or at the gate (searched 2026-09-14: the three sinyoken pages on the floor plan and the privy's history). The map draws every privy as a separate outhouse in the yard and ROLLS per house where in the yard it stands: behind the house by the back door .60, by the gate .25, by the stable or shed .15, each falling back to the others. Only the stable seat is attested; the back-door and gate seats, and the weights that make the back door the commonest, are a GUESS. On most houses a seat on the house's sunny side is tried first - why is at The outhouse faces the sun. Size: no source gives one - 6 x 6 ft, the one-ken module, a GUESS. Share: the source's "普通" (ordinarily) reads as near-universal; Sugiura's 0.87 outbuildings + 0.32 in-house (overlapping) agree; drawn on 85-95% of homesteads. In early imperial China, the Art Institute of Chicago catalog says of its pottery model of a pigsty and latrine: "latrines—or toilets—were customarily built above a pigsty and connected by pipes to a cesspool" - in China the privy and the muck were one cluster; that a Japanese farmstead kept them together too is this page's reading (a GUESS), and it is why the manure heap below is seated BEYOND THE PRIVY. How usual an outdoor privy was, two more places it stood - the front yard and inside the barn - and where its night soil was kept are at Where did the privy stand, and where was its night soil kept?

The manure heap - the practice attested, the place a GUESS. ja.wikipedia 肥溜め / 下肥: night soil was fermented in 「a jar buried in the ground, or a pit finished with plaster」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「地中に埋めた壺や、漆喰をほどこした穴」), vessels from which the liquid cannot leak, the jar being one of 「about 1 to 1.5 meters in mouth diameter」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「口径1-1.5メートル程度」), 「1-2 weeks in summer, 3-4 weeks in winter」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「夏の場合1～2週間、冬の場合3～4週間」), though the same page also cites a year of fermenting from the Sendai city history; the night-soil pit's two attested places, beside the privy or out by the fields, are at the privy's question. Where the heap of stable manure stood in the yard no readable page says, and it is drawn near the stable or under the eaves - a GUESS. The stable's own litter - straw and fallen leaves mixed with the dung and trodden by the cattle and horses until it rotted - became stable manure (kyuhi, 厩肥). With the Chinese pigsty-privy finding above, the heap is drawn as one 8 x 6 ft mound beyond the privy (size GUESS); drawn on 40-70% of homesteads (Sugiura: 0.24 compost SHEDS per household - the open heap is commoner than its shed; GUESS).

The straw rick - NOT DRAWN. Seasonal: the straw rick (藁塚, warazuka) was built of the threshed straw on the reaped field or its banks: how long it stood the dictionary does not say, and the stand to spring is a GUESS; the GM deferred it with every seasonal thing.

What the map does: on the sample hamlet the map is tested on (15 houses), this puts a privy on 14 of the 15 houses, a woodpile on 14, a coop on 12, a manure heap on 9, a bath shed on 1, a persimmon on 10, and one household shrine.

Searched and not found: the text of the two farming manuals themselves. What can be read of the manuals is what the ja.wikipedia page on night soil quotes: Hyakusho Denki (1681) on letting night soil rot in the storage jar before it goes on the fields, and Nogyo Zensho (農業全書) (1697) on using dung only once it has ripened.
