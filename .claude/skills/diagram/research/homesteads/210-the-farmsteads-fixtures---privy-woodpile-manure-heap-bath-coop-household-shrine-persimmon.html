The farmstead's fixtures - privy, woodpile, manure heap, bath, coop, household shrine, persimmon

Sources: kotobank-benjo, artic-pigsty-latrine, boso-no-mura-kigoya, jawiki-koedame, mizumaki-goemonburo, cambridge-animals-china, qimin-yaoshu-yangji, pitt-zhengzhou-coop, zhwiki-liuchu, tokushima-yashikigami, jawiki-yashikigami, kameyama-yashikigami, toyoko-kaki, uekipedia-kaki, 326woods-stack (the stack's height), sugiura-1973-fuzoku (the rates), sinyoken-madori, shimogoe-jawiki, kotobank-warazuka

The GM chose from the inventory above: "privies are something that we want ... firewood stacks are actually large enough to render ... Same thing with manure heaps, same thing with baths, same thing with persimmons ... a household shrine being something which is very rare, but which is notable when it does appear ... as for chicken coops ... go with whatever was the case in imperial China." Every drawn size is TRUE feet at the map's scale; the sizes are GUESSES unless a source is named, because the record describes these things and almost never measures them.

The privy (便所, kawaya). Nipponica (kotobank 便所): 「In farm households it was usual to provide one urinal and one privy as a single building independent of the main house.」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「農家では、小便所一つと大便所一つを、母屋から 独立した一つの建物として設けるのが普通であった」) - on a farm the urinal and the privy were ONE building independent of the main house, and that was the norm. Where it stood (sinyoken floor-plan (間取り) pages): 「it is thought the privy was under the eaves in front of the stable to the left of the entrance, or else a separate outhouse in the yard」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「便所は入口の左横の厩の前の軒下か、あるいは庭に別棟の外便所があったと考えられる」) - under the eaves by the stable beside the entrance, or a separate outhouse in the yard; and that a back-entrance privy, a side privy and a door privy were named forms is this page's reading, on no page read (searched 2026-09-14: the three sinyoken pages on the floor plan and the privy's history). Two attested seats - under the eaves by the stable beside the entrance, and a separate outhouse in the yard - and two more this page reads into the record, the back door and the gate; the seat is ROLLED per house (back door .60 / gate .25 / by the stable or shed .15), each falling back to the others. Size: no source gives one - 6 x 6 ft, the one-ken module, a GUESS. Share: the source's "普通" (ordinarily) reads as near-universal; Sugiura's 0.87 outbuildings + 0.32 in-house (overlapping) agree; drawn on 85-95% of homesteads. In Han China (AIC catalog): "latrines - or toilets - were customarily built above a pigsty and connected by pipes to a cesspool" - the privy and the muck were one cluster, which is why the manure heap below is seated BEYOND THE PRIVY.

The woodpile - the shed attested, the stack's wall a GUESS. The Boso-no-Mura reconstructed farmstead lists a 木小屋: "a building for storing the charcoal, firewood and the like that are used as fuel" (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「燃料として使う、炭やたきぎなどを収めておく建物です」) (a building holding the charcoal and firewood used as fuel); where the pile stood relative to the house the page does not say. Sugiura counts a firewood SHED on 0.76 of pre-1944 households; the open stack under the eaves is the simpler form and the one drawn (that it is cheaper and older is this page's reading): 10 x 3.5 ft in plan, split logs stacked head-high - the 1.5 m height is modern stacking practice (326-woods, a modern page; the object has not changed). Wall: the back wall or the kura's outer wall, out of the rain - a GUESS; drawn on 75-95% of homesteads.

The manure heap - the practice attested, the place a GUESS. ja.wikipedia 肥溜め / 下肥: night soil was fermented in 「For rotting down to ripeness, a container from which the liquid cannot leak is used - such as a jar buried in the ground, or a pit finished with plaster (the koedame).」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「地中に埋めた壺や、漆喰をほどこした穴」) (buried jars, plastered pits) of 「about 1 to 1.5 meters in mouth diameter」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「口径1-1.5メートル程度」), 「1-2 weeks in summer, 3-4 weeks in winter」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「夏の場合1～2週間、冬の場合3～4週間」); where on the farm the pit stood the pages do not say - a search summary put it 「near the stable (horse shed) or under the eaves」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「厩（馬小屋）の近くや軒下」) (near the stable, under the eaves; a GUESS - no readable page supports it). Stable litter and grass composted into stable manure (kyuhi, 厩肥). With the Han pigsty-privy finding above, the heap is drawn as one 8 x 6 ft mound beyond the privy (size GUESS); drawn on 40-70% of homesteads (Sugiura: a compost SHED on 0.24 - the open heap is commoner than its shed; GUESS).

The bath shed - its use attested, its placement not found. Mizumaki museum: the goemon-buro "widely used in farm villages centered on self-sufficiency" (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「自給自足を中心とした農村で多く使われた」); that it spread into western-Japan homes in the late Edo is a GUESS (japaaan). Sugiura: a bath SHED on 0.29 of pre-1944 households and a bath INSIDE the house on 0.53 - two forms, so only the shed share is drawn (on 20-45% of homesteads) and the rest bathe indoors, undrawn. Where the shed stood no source says: seated at the back wall or a flank (GUESS), 6 x 6 ft (GUESS).

The chicken coop - imperial China's. Animals through Chinese History (Cambridge): “it seems that farmers in most regions of China managed to keep a pig and some chickens in their yard, along with a draught animal or two” (late imperial; the chapter leans on 1930s survey data, its own caveat). The Qimin Yaoshu 養雞第五十九 ("Raising chickens", its 59th chapter; wikisource): 「For the chickens' roost it is fitting to make a cage set on the ground, with perches fixed inside ... if they are left to the trees, at one encounter with wind and cold the large ones waste and grow thin and the small ones may die」 (translated from the Classical Chinese by this project; original: 「雞棲，宜據地為籠，籠內著棧 ... 若任之樹林，一遇風寒，大者損瘦，小者或死」) - build the roost as a ground-level enclosure with a perch inside; left to roost in trees the birds sicken - so a COOP, not a tree. A late-Ming coop at Zhengzhou (Pitt HAA): "square-shaped structure had six niche-like openings in the west wall, with some eggshell fragments" - square; no size given. The chicken is one of the 六畜 (liuchu, the six domestic animals) (zh.wikipedia). No source gives a household PROPORTION; the 50-80% of homesteads drawn with one is a GUESS bounded by "most regions", above Sugiura's Japan (0.16-0.28, where pre-Meiji chickens were timekeepers). 5 x 5 ft, square after Zhengzhou, size a GUESS; seated on the flank by the yard (GUESS - "in their yard").

The household shrine (屋敷神) - the GM chose between two attested forms. Tokushima prefectural library: 「in some cases every house has one, in others only certain old families do」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「各家にある場合と特定の旧家にだけある場合がある」) - in some places every house has one, in others only certain old families; ja.wikipedia 屋敷神 states the same two patterns. Two forms would ordinarily be a knob; the GM ruled for this map: "very rare, but which is notable when it does appear" - the old-families pattern; Sugiura's table puts the shrine outbuilding at 0.01 per household over all houses and at none among the pre-1944 ones; drawn on 3-8% of homesteads, and the count is CAPPED at the share so positional luck cannot make it common. The every-house pattern is the DECLINED alternative. Corner: NE (the kimon, 鬼門) - 「in a corner of the house plot, especially at the kimon (the northeast corner), a small shrine of stone or wood, or with a straw roof」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「屋敷の一隅、特に鬼門（北東隅）に、石や木、わら屋根の祠」) (ja.wikipedia, minka-en, satologue); NW 17 of 37 and NE 11 of 37 in a Kameyama survey (a GUESS); SW 「It is usual for it to be enshrined at the southwest corner of the homestead; there are some that take a tree or a stone as a mark to show where the god is, and there are also some that enshrine a small hokora.」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「屋敷の西南隅に祀られているのが普通」) in Tokushima - three corners attested, rolled NW .45 / NE .35 / SW .20. Size: 「small shrines of stone or of wood」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「石造か木造の小祠」); one measured example 「a stone shrine about 40 cm each in width, depth and height」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「幅・奥行き・高さ各40cm位の石の祠」) (Tokushima) - about 1.3 ft. DRAWN at 6 x 6 ft, the small-shed module (a 3 x 3 ft glyph was tried first and the GM "could not tell what it even is") - a MAP DRAWING CONVENTION above the one measured stone, the GM's ruling: "as a glyph rendering convention, we could make it the same size as one of those small sheds ... but also red and visually distinctive in some other way" - vermilion, a ridge line and a torii standing before the door.

The persimmon. "so a persimmon tree is planted in every dooryard" (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「だからどこの庭先にも柿の木が植えてある」) (toyoko-housing) - hence a persimmon in every dooryard; and "the Edo-period agronomist Miyazaki Yasusada encouraged planting persimmon trees around the house lot" (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「江戸時代の農学者 宮崎安貞は、家屋敷の周りに柿の木を植える事を奨励した」) - the Edo agronomist Miyazaki Yasusada urged planting them round the homestead; "in summer it makes shade for the house" (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「夏は家に木陰を作り」) - it shades the house in summer, so it stands BESIDE the house (which side, a GUESS: the flank, then a front or back corner). Height 3-20 m (uekipedia); crown width - no source gives one - drawn 18 ft across (GUESS); drawn on 80-95% of homesteads. Its fruit dots are a RENDERING convention naming the tree, not a season.

The straw rick - NOT DRAWN. Seasonal (waraguro built after the harvest, kept standing - to spring in this page's reading; the page gives autumn): the GM deferred it with every seasonal thing.

What the map does: on the reference hamlet this puts a privy on 12 of the 15 houses, a woodpile on 7, a coop on 7, a manure heap on 5, a bath shed on 3, a persimmon on 8, and one household shrine.
