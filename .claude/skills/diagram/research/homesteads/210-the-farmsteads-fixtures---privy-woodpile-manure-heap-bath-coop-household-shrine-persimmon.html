The farmstead's fixtures - privy, woodpile, manure heap, bath, coop, household shrine, persimmon

Sources: kotobank-benjo, sinyoken-madori, artic-pigsty-latrine, sugiura-1973-fuzoku (the rates), jawiki-koedame, shimogoe-jawiki, kyuhi-jawiki (what stable manure was made of), nogyo-zensho-jawiki, kotobank-warazuka

The GM chose from the inventory above: "privies are something that we want ... firewood stacks are actually large enough to render ... Same thing with manure heaps, same thing with baths, same thing with persimmons ... a household shrine being something which is very rare, but which is notable when it does appear ... as for chicken coops ... go with whatever was the case in imperial China." Every drawn size is TRUE feet at the map's scale; the sizes are GUESSES unless a source is named, because the record describes these things and almost never measures them.

The privy (便所, kawaya). The Nipponica encyclopedia (on kotobank): 「In farm households it was usual to provide one urinal and one privy as a single building independent of the main house.」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「農家では、小便所一つと大便所一つを、母屋から 独立した一つの建物として設けるのが普通であった」) - on a farm the urinal and the privy were ONE building independent of the main house, and that was the norm. Where it stood (sinyoken floor-plan (間取り) pages): 「it is thought the privy was under the eaves in front of the stable to the left of the entrance, or else a separate outhouse in the yard」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「便所は入口の左横の厩の前の軒下か、あるいは庭に別棟の外便所があったと考えられる」) - under the eaves by the stable beside the entrance, or a separate outhouse in the yard. The map always draws the privy as a separate outhouse, and ROLLS where it stands per house, each seat falling back to the others: by the stable or shed (.15), the one attested place; or by the back door (.60) or at the gate (.25), two seats no page read names (a GUESS; searched 2026-09-14: the three sinyoken pages on the floor plan and the privy's history). Size: no source gives one - 6 x 6 ft, the one-ken module, a GUESS. Share: the source's "普通" (ordinarily) reads as near-universal; Sugiura's 0.87 privy outbuildings a household and 0.32 of farms with a privy inside the house, both for farms whose main house was built by 1944 (a 1972 survey) (overlapping) agree; drawn on 85-95% of homesteads. That the privy stood outside the house on most farms before 1868 is at Before 1868, was a farm's privy a building of its own? In China under the Han dynasty, the Art Institute of Chicago catalog says of its pottery model of a pigsty and latrine: "latrines—or toilets—were customarily built above a pigsty and connected by pipes to a cesspool" - the privy and the muck were one cluster, which is why the manure heap below is seated BEYOND THE PRIVY.

The manure heap - the practice attested, the place a GUESS. ja.wikipedia 肥溜め (koedame) / 下肥 (night soil): night soil was fermented in 「a jar buried in the ground, or a pit finished with plaster」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「地中に埋めた壺や、漆喰をほどこした穴」), vessels from which the liquid cannot leak, the jar kind one of 「about 1 to 1.5 meters in mouth diameter」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「口径1-1.5メートル程度」), 「1-2 weeks in summer, 3-4 weeks in winter」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「夏の場合1～2週間、冬の場合3～4週間」), though the same page also cites a year of fermenting from the Sendai city history; where on the farm the pit stood the pages do not say, and no readable page places it. The stable's own litter - straw and fallen leaves mixed with the dung and trodden by the cattle and horses until it rotted - became stable manure (kyuhi, 厩肥). With the Han dynasty pigsty-privy finding above, the heap - or the pit, where a farmstead rolls one - is drawn beyond the privy, the heap as one 8 x 6 ft mound (place and size a GUESS). It is drawn on 40-70% of homesteads, a GUESS with no count behind it. That the manure house and the heap are premodern, and why no count sets their share, is Was there a manure heap on a farm before 1868?

The straw rick - NOT DRAWN. Seasonal: the straw rick (藁塚, warazuka) was built of the threshed straw on the reaped field or its banks: how long it stood the dictionary does not say, and the stand to spring is a GUESS; the GM deferred it with every seasonal thing.

What the map does: on the sample hamlet the map is tested on (15 houses), this puts a privy on 12 of the 15 houses, a woodpile on 7, a coop on 7, a manure heap on 5, a bath shed on 3, a persimmon on 8, and one household shrine.

Searched and not found: the text of the two farming manuals themselves. What can be read of the manuals is a modern rendering of the Nogyo Zensho's manure house (at Was there a manure heap on a farm before 1868?), and what the ja.wikipedia page on night soil quotes: Hyakusho Denki (1681) on letting night soil rot in the storage jar before it goes on the fields, and Nogyo Zensho (農業全書) (1697) on using dung once it has ripened, fresh dung working poorly.
