Before 1868, did a farm have a bath shed?

Sources: tsuda-1991-nikko-shasan-minka (the baths in village house-plan registers, 1770-1842), koshigaya-shishi-noumin-jukyo (the baths of Hasuda, 1824), sakurasha-hyakusho-denki (what the Hyakusho Denki says of the bath), kotobank-hyakusho-denki (the manual's date)

How many farms the map gives a bath shed is set at Did a farmhouse have a bath shed?, from counts of the twentieth century. This question asks what the record holds from before 1868; the privy is at Before 1868, was a farm's privy a building of its own?

The bath - part of the house, on two or three farms in ten. Villages along the shogun's road to Nikko drew up registers setting out the plan of every house, so that his retinue could be lodged; 19 registers of farm villages from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries survive, and an architectural historian has read them room by room. In the registers of 1770 and 1771 a bath of about half a tsubo (a tsubo is about 6 by 6 feet) appears in only a very small part of the houses of three villages: under the eaves beside the main door of four houses in one, beside the main door of six in another, and under the eaves beyond the stable of one house in the third. In the register of Shimohasuda of 1824, 21 of 76 houses had a bath of 1 to 2 tsubo, 17 of them out beyond the stable wing that projects from the earth floor; at Ukiya in 1842, 15 of 62 houses did, 12 of them beyond the stable. By then, the historian sums up, a bath stood in about two or three houses in ten. He suggests, as a guess, that the bath was put by the stable partly because its drain water also served as manure. The Koshigaya city history reads the 1824 register of Hasuda village and counts 21 houses with a bath beyond the stable wing - the historian's total of baths, though he puts only 17 of them there. Earlier, the farm manual Hyakusho Denki, written about 1680, treats a bath in the house as a luxury for a farm, as a modern reader of it reports, and has the family wash at a fixed place over a pit laid with bamboo slats, so that the water collects to thin the manure.

A bath shed standing on its own - not found before 1868. Every bath the historian reports is at the main house: beside its main door, under its eaves, beyond its stable wing, or, in a few houses, joined to its floored rooms or to a back room projecting behind it; whether the baths beyond the stable were joined to it he does not say, and his paper covers the main house alone. No readable page was found that places a bath standing as a building of its own on a farm before 1868; the pages that describe one describe the twentieth century.

What this means for the map. The GM ruled on 2026-09-28 that the maps drop anything attested only in modern times. The bath is premodern, but not as a shed: before 1868 it was a small room at the main house, on two or three farms in ten by the 1820s and on fewer before. So the maps do not draw a bath shed standing apart, and the higher shares of the twentieth-century counts are not used.

The rule the map follows: no bath shed standing on its own. A bath, where drawn, is a room of 6 by 6 to 6 by 12 feet joined to the main house, beside its main door, at the far end of its stable wing, or joined to its floored rooms (three attested places, a knob; the third is found in only a few houses, most of them headmen's), on 20-30% of homesteads (the counts of 1824 and 1842). The weights between the three places are a guess.
