Rice-drying racks (hasa, hasagi)

Before it was threshed, cut rice was dried in the sheaf, and across much of Japan the sheaves were hung on a rack, the hasa: poles, bamboo or rope laid across posts. Most racks were put up on the fields after the harvest, a little above head height, and the rice hung there slowly, usually twenty to thirty days. The rack's virtue was that it dried the sheaves even where the paddy stayed wet, so it was widespread in the rainy regions.

The weather decided which form a country took. The tall rack of many tiers belongs to the deep-snow coast of the Sea of Japan, and on the San'in coast, where the weather changes easily, the racks were gathered by the house so that the threshing could be done at home. How the threshed grain was dried again on mats is at Threshing and drying yards at farmhouses (niwa), and the straw ricks left on the reaped paddy at The paddy through the rice year.

  • Sheaves were dried in four ways.
    Laid on the paddy floor, seen a little everywhere. Stacked in heaps, from the Inland Sea coast to northern Kyushu, the Chubu region and parts of the northeast. Hung on single stakes set in a row along the paddy's bunds, 1 to 3 m (~3-10 ft) tall by region, most of all in the northeast. And hung on the rack proper, widespread in the rainy regions of the whole country.
  • Most racks stood on the paddies, a little above head height.
    Most were built where the ears were easy to hang, a little above head height; a few were large enough that the sheaves were hung from a ladder.
  • Some racks were built by the house.
    A tall rack of several tiers was built on the paddy, or sometimes the sheaves were carried home and the rack built around the dwelling.
  • How long was a rack?
    A single-tier rack could run as long as 100 m (~328 ft), straight, zigzag or bent into three sides. Some regions built two or three tiers, and some built tall walls of ten to fifteen. How many racks, or how long a run of rack, one household put up, no page we read says.
  • In Niigata, the posts were living trees, the hasagi.
    Alders were planted along the bunds at even spacing, their lower branches cleared, and their trunks used as the posts of a tiered rack, and such drying trees were an ordinary sight of Niigata's farmland.
  • Some long rows of drying trees beside farm roads are modern.
    One was made in 1943-1945, when about 250 hectares (~618 acres) of a district's paddies were consolidated and 26 farm households transplanted drying trees from various places onto the farm road.
  • In the mountain villages of Ehime, tall racks stood all year.
    Racks of six or seven tiers there were permanent, and some were roofed.
  • No page we read says which side of the house a rack stood on.
    The records of racks gathered by the house do not place them on any side of it, or of its yard.

A survey of drying racks across Japan by two geographers, Nishimura and Makino, published in 1959, reads which form a country used as set by its weather and ground over whole regions.

  • The tall rack follows the snow.
    The tiered rack's range nearly matches the deep-snow country of San'in and Hokuriku, on the Sea of Japan side, and the authors call it a climate landscape, an adaptation to the climate.
  • Tall racks on the Sea of Japan side, low racks on the Pacific side.
    The two divide roughly along the mountain spine of the country, though they often mix locally, as on the low wetlands around Lake Hamana.
  • In 1881, the farmers' answers divided the country into broad regions, not villages.
    From veteran farmers' answers to a national agricultural conference of 1881, the authors drew a map: drying on the ground in the far west of Honshu, southern Shikoku and Kyushu; racks in the northeast, the Hokuriku, the Tokai, the Kinki and the Chugoku; and both in the Kanto.
  • Within one mixed district, farmers switched method with the ground and the weather.
    In a district of Chiba, racks were used only for wet paddies and when rain was expected at harvest; in fine weather the rice dried on the bunds or the grass.
  • Was the rack a village's custom or its weather's answer?
    The weather's, over whole regions. The survey reads a practice as changed, where it is taken up, by the natural and social setting of that land. Custom and teaching show in the record working over a whole prefecture or region, as a delay in the rack's arrival, not as neighboring villages in one climate choosing differently - this record's reading of the cases below.
  • Where the harvest had many clear days, the rack was taken up less readily.
    The survey finds its acceptance did not go smoothly in regions blessed with fine days at harvest.
  • In Kyushu, climate and habit held the rack back, and it arrived only in the Meiji era, after 1868.
    The authors name both the climate and traditional habit as resisting it, and its spread there came wholly in the Meiji era and after, helped by better transport.
  • In Fukuoka, one leading farmer's advice held the rack back.
    A veteran farmer who in effect directed the prefecture's farm teaching took a view of the rack that was a great force against its spread.
