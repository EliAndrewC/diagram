Does a farmstead stand whole on one bank of the brook?

On the maps, yes - and that is a guess. A hamlet may stand astride its own small brook, the houses on both banks joined by a plank crossing (see "Does a hamlet stand on one bank of its stream, or around it?" on the water page), but each farmstead keeps its house, its work yard, its garden beds, its storehouse and its fixtures - the privy, the manure heap, the woodpile, the persimmon - on one bank. On the maps a household crosses the water to reach its fields, but never to reach its own vegetable beds or its privy.

What the record holds. Nothing read places a household's own garden, privy, yard or storehouse across a channel from its house, and nothing read says that never happened. What does turn up is a channel running ALONG the houses - at Harie, near the northwest shore of Lake Biwa, the channel flows alongside the house and its kabata. That a household's own buildings stood together on one side of such a channel is this project's reading of that picture, not something the page says, so the rule is labeled a guess. It is kept because a holding split by running water reads, on a map, as two holdings.

The rule the map follows: the straight line from a farmhouse to each part of its farmstead - the work yard, each garden bed, the storehouse, every fixture and the persimmon - crosses no reach of the brook.

Sources: mizu-no-bunka-60 (the channel that runs alongside the house)
