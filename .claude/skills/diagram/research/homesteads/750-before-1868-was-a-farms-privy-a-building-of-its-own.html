The privy - outside the house, on most farms. In the 1824 record of Hasuda village most houses had a well and a privy outside the main house. At Kakimochi, counted in 1885 and read by its historian back to the last years of the shogunate, a main house and a privy were the basic form of a farm, and all 16 households had a privy; the table gives each privy's frontage and depth, and the sixteen run from 5 by 5 feet and 1 ken by 5 feet (about 6 by 5 feet) up to 4.5 by 2.5 ken (about 27 by 15 feet); ten are 3 by 2 ken (about 18 by 12 feet) or smaller, five are 4 by 2 ken (about 24 by 12 feet), and the table does not say what else a privy building of the larger sizes held. A privy joined to the house belonged at first to the village officials: in the house-plan registers of villages along the shogun's road to Nikko, from the 1720s to 1771 an "upper privy" joined to the floored rooms is found only in the one or two houses of the headman or other officials, and by 1824 and 1842 in about one house in ten and two in ten, ordinary farmers' among them. The farm manual Hyakusho Denki, written about 1680, puts a farm's privy southeast of the main house, in the sun, so that its contents rot quickly into good manure, as a modern reader of it reports.

What this means for the map. The GM ruled on 2026-09-28 that the maps drop anything attested only in modern times. The privy outside the house is premodern, and the share drawn, on 85-95% of homesteads, sits between "most houses" at Hasuda and all 16 at Kakimochi, so it stays. The privy's size has premodern figures in the sixteen sizes of the Kakimochi table.

The rule the map follows: a privy outside the house on 85-95% of homesteads, its size rolled per homestead from the sixteen sizes of the Kakimochi table, each as likely as the next: from 5 by 5 feet to 27 by 15 feet, most of them 18 by 12 feet or smaller. This is a calibration against one village's table, not a figure the source gives as usual for a region.
