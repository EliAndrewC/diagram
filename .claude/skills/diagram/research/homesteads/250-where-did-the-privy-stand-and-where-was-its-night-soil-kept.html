Where did the privy stand, and where was its night soil kept?

Sources: suzuki-1959-noson-benjo (how usual the outdoor privy was, the privy in the barn, the field pit), sugiura-1977-tohoku (the front yard, the rates village by village), sugiura-1973-fuzoku (the move to the front)

The privy's first finding - one small building set apart from the house, under the eaves by the stable or out in the yard - is at The farmstead's fixtures, and which way it faced is at The outhouse faces the sun. This question adds how usual an outdoor privy was, two more places it stood, and where its night soil went.

How usual it was. A 1959 survey of farm households in one village each of Saitama, Tokyo and Miyagi found households with an indoor privy alone at 6.0% in Saitama and 9.4% in Tokyo, and none in Miyagi, and concluded that more than 90% of households had an outdoor privy; the authors infer it stood outside the house because that spot was handy for manuring the fields. Small houses tended to have only the outdoor privy, and considerably large ones an indoor privy as well. Further north the share was not the same everywhere: reconstructions of three Tohoku villages before the war put outdoor privies at 85% of farms in Tono and 79% in Shiokawa, but at none in Inawashiro. The map's privy on 85-95% of homesteads sits on the common case.

Two more places it stood. A prewar housing report, as Sugiura passes it on, says that in northern Miyagi the bath and the privy were usually put in the front yard of the main house, and that making them buildings of their own was a fashion of early Meiji; the same report is cited for the move of the privy to the front of the house in a Miyagi town being a kind of fashion. And at several farms of the surveyed Miyagi village the privy was no building of its own: a tub sunk in the floor of the barn outside the main house, with boards laid across it. With the two seats found first, the record holds four distinct places - under the eaves by the stable, a separate outhouse in the yard, the front yard, and inside the barn - so the seat is a knob among them; how often each is drawn is a GUESS.

The night soil - beside the privy, or out by the fields. The survey found night soil kept two ways: in a storage tank beside the privy, or in a field pit (nodame) away from the house, and the authors think the tank was moved from beside the privy to near each household's fields, or to the roadside, to make manuring easier. How many households kept a field pit varied widely: 2 of 83 in the Saitama village, 19 of 53 in Tokyo's, 15 of 18 in Miyagi's. These are two distinct forms, so where the night-soil pit stands is a knob - beside the privy, or at a field edge or roadside - and the share of each is a degree that varies from almost none to most households.

Its size - not found. No readable page gives a privy's size; the 6 x 6 ft drawn, one ken on a side, is a GUESS.

The rule the map follows: an outdoor privy on 85-95% of homesteads, seated per house among the four attested places - under the eaves by the stable, a separate outhouse in the yard, the front yard, inside the barn - with the sun side preferred as the outhouse question says; and the night-soil pit, where a homestead has one, beside its privy or out at the edge of one of its fields or beside a road, the share at a field edge rolled per hamlet between almost none and most households. The weights among the seats are a guess; the size is a guess.
