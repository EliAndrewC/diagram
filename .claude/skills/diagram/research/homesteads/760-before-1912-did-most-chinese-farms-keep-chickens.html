Before 1912, did most Chinese farms keep chickens?

Sources: smith-1899-village-life (chickens in the village yard, North China), king-1911-farmers-forty-centuries (nearly every family kept a few, near Suzhou, 1909), buck-1930-farm-economy (the first count, 1921-1925)

How many farmsteads the map gives a coop, and the GM's ruling that the coop follows imperial China rather than Japan, are at Did a farmstead keep chickens? The one count of farms with chickens is a survey of the 1920s, after the empire fell; this question asks what the record holds from before 1912.

Chickens were in the village yard before 1912. A missionary describing the villages of the North China plain wrote in 1899 that a village family's small yard was in as great confusion as its house, and that its dogs, cats, chickens and babies spilled out into the street, "which is but an extension of the court". He also wrote that after a village theater performance, "there is not likely to be a single chicken left in a village", so villagers disposed of their fowls beforehand rather than have them stolen by "expert chicken-thieves" - chickens common enough in a village that thieves could count on finding them. In 1909 an American soil scientist touring the lower Yangzi was told by a hospital doctor near Suzhou that "nearly every family in the cities, and in the country villages raise a few, but only a few, chickens", though King himself wrote that there were no statistics on China's poultry.

How many. No count of farms keeping chickens was found from before 1912: the first is Buck's survey of 1921-1925, which found chickens on 82 percent of the farms it studied. The two accounts from before 1912 say "nearly every family" in the lower Yangzi and show chickens as an ordinary part of the North China yard, so the 1920s share agrees with what was seen under the Qing; the survey is used for the number, and the older accounts for the fact that it was already so.

What this means for the map. The coop stays, on about four farmsteads in five: keeping chickens at the farmhouse is attested before 1912 in both North China and the lower Yangzi, and "nearly every family" in 1909 sits with the survey's 82 percent. The coop as a building is the sixth-century Qimin Yaoshu's ground-level roost (see Did a farmstead keep chickens?); the accounts from before 1912 do not say whether the yard's chickens slept in one.
