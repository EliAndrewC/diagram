The garden's sun, and how far the windbreak shades

Sources: yashikirin-jawiki; derived from the threshing-yard entry above; tonami-yashikirin-haichi (the sides of a Tonami house the grove and the fruit trees stand on, and the winds it held off) puts Tonami's bamboo from the west round to the north, and ja.wikipedia 屋敷林 puts it on the south, kikanchiiki-igune (how an igune is kept), hasa-jawiki, kurita-2019-igune, minami-2024-igune, daikon-jawiki

What prompted it (GM 2026-08-25): "there is not enough space for sunlight to hit the gardens and thrashing yards ... The Windbreak Forest ... is so close to the gardens ... that I do not believe that those gardens would get sufficient sunlight."

The garden takes the yard's corridor - one number, not two. A dooryard garden's binding season is the shoulder month too: daikon (and, in this page's reading, the other autumn greens) is in the ground under the same 28 deg 9am sun the threshing yard works under, so a minka's ~20 ft ridge shades a bed 39 ft off exactly as it shades a yard. The growing-season figure is shorter (a 60-70 deg noon sun in the 4th-7th months throws 7-12 ft), so the shoulder month binds, as it does for the yard. Rule: 39 ft of clear ground south of every bed, whichever is placed first, the bed or the house that would shade it. The bed may stand on any of four sides of its house (SE/SW/E/W), so a bed that the SE side would put in shadow moves to the other flank.

And the morning sun, which a neighbor's grove takes. The bed sits on a sheltered flank, and a neighbor's windward belt stands against that neighbor's west wall - which is to say just EAST of our bed, in the way of the rising sun. Morning light is what a kitchen bed lives on, and the fix is placement rather than felling a windbreak: the GM asked in 2026-07 to "move it a bit south". Two things keep the east open. The communal copse keeps a sun lane to its east as well as to its south, so no clump of it is ever seated directly east of a bed; and once every homestead grove is drawn, an east-shaded bed is nudged a little south into open ground, so the trees fall to its northeast instead. It is a best effort and not a slot - most farms keep the plain east bed, and a bed hemmed in to the south by paddy, lane or neighbor stays where it is - so the rule is broken only where a clear southward shift existed and was not taken.

The belt's height, from the record. Searched: igune (Sendai/Osaki), kainyo (Tonami), tsuijimatsu (Izumo), Musashino yashikirin, Okinawa fukugi, Chinese fengshui woods. Found: the Sendai igune planting list classes sugi/keyaki/kuromatsu/hinoki as "tall trees" at >= 15 m, sub-canopy ~10 m, shrubs ~3 m; two surveys of Osaki's igune measured the grove's highest point on the ground and put a grove of tall and low trees at about 9-16 m; a 2022 paper describes an igune 10 m high on the side of the prevailing wind, standing on the west and north of the site; Tonami's kainyo put tall sturdy trees, chiefly sugi, on the west and south, 11-28 m tall and about 15 m on average; Izumo's tsuijimatsu pines are clipped flat at intervals (the 8-12 m every 4-5 years carried for them is on no page read), the one attested topping practice; Okinawa fukugi belts average 7-10 m. Limb-pruning (edauchi) is part of keeping an igune: in the Sawadaue district of Osaki the 23 households whose igune remain formed a preservation society that does the pruning and clears the fallen trees in common. That kainyo and igune were never height-capped is this page's reading: no page read speaks of cutting their tops, and Izumo's level topping is the one trimming practice attested. So the band this page reads from the figures above is ~10 m for a working belt, 15-25 m for an untended mature stand.

Where the plots stood, from the record. On the Tonami plain the east side is the front of the house, and a survey of the plain's groves found little grove there - flowering trees and fruit trees, persimmon and peach among them, were often planted instead; from the south round to the west it found many tall trees - sugi, kashi and keyaki - and from the west round to the north sugi, enoki and alder often mixed with bamboo. The grove held off the strong south wind of early spring and the cold winter monsoon. A model homestead the Tonami research institute drew up in 1996, whose text is on no page read, is carried here for two more things: the front (E) yard as the work yard, kept open, with a persimmon at its center, and the N/W bamboo strip as shady and damp and given to the kitchen drain and service sheds. (ja.wikipedia 屋敷林 itself puts Tonami's bamboo on the SOUTH side with the storehouses - see the vegetation research.) Tohoku: the igune stand on the north and west, which leaves the south side open (this page's inference; that it served as a drying yard is this page's reading too, and the rice itself was rack-dried in the field). So the record's answer, in this page's reading, is that the HOUSE BODY was the spacer - plots on the sunlit lee side, the belt behind - and it holds no measured plot-to-treeline distance. For a nucleated village's communal belt the record is silent altogether. Hence the number is DERIVED, as the 39 ft was.

The shadow, computed for the season that matters. At 38N in the shoulder month the 3pm sun stands 28 deg high at azimuth ~232, so a belt throws 1.9 x its height to the NORTHEAST - a 10 m (33 ft) belt ~63 ft, of which ~50 ft reaches eastward; a 15 m belt ~95 ft / ~75 ft. In the 7th month the same hour gives ~35 ft / ~50 ft. The shoulder binds.

The decision: 50 ft of clear ground west and southwest of every yard and bed (the 3pm shadow starts from the southwest, so the keep-out runs from a plot's north edge to 50 ft below its south edge), enforced when the belt is planted (a clump that would shade a plot is refused and re-seated deeper, and the belt's near face is moved out by the sun lane where the wind puts the belt west). Windbreak-role groves only: the copse is the dooryard's own persimmon and bamboo, and the Tonami survey puts the persimmon on the open east front of the house.

75 ft was tried first and declined - a ruling, not a taste. 75 is the same geometry at 15 m, the class floor of an untended stand. At 75 the belt must stand so far off the westernmost houses that it falls outside the edge of the drawn map, which is set by the houses, yards, beds and roads, and the map's edge does not open for the belt (GM 2026-07-20: the communal windbreak clips at the view edge). Measured on Inashiro: at 75 the belt fell from 131 clumps to 38 and was no longer continuous. At 50 it stands whole (81 clumps, continuous) inside the map's edge. What is accepted: a belt kept at working height. Two rulings conflicted - a belt that stands off the plots (2026-08-25) and a map edge that does not open for the belt (2026-07-20) - and the GM reconciled them the next day: "treat the innermost edge of the windbreak forest as being something that is preserved". The belt's inner FACE sets the map's edge, standing off the page edge by the same margin every other feature gets; the belt's depth still clips. What was priced and declined: letting the windbreak set the map's edge (this reverses the GM's ruling of 2026-07-20); a sun lane scaled to 15 m without moving the map's edge (measured: the belt is dropped). Who chose: this was settled on the measurement, and the GM on 2026-08-26 chose to open the map's edge at the face rather than shrink or raise the belt - 10 m stays as the record's measured working height.

Departures taken knowingly: the sun lane is a square, not a solar wedge that swings through the afternoon (the yard's south corridor takes the same shortcut); the small stand-off the belt's face keeps from the plots is an average plot overhang; what guarantees it is the rule that refuses any clump that would shade a plot.
