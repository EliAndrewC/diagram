Where did a south-China village's ancestral hall stand, and how big was it?

Sources: sohu-guangfu-comb-layout (the Cantonese comb-plan village and where its halls stand), cssn-citang-panyu (the water and the plaza before a hall), weilongwu-zhwiki (the Hakka hall-and-wing house and its pond), douban-feng-jiang-citang (the forms and sizes of the halls of Guangzhou prefecture), gz-gov-citang (the halls of Guangzhou and their usual size), citang-zhwiki (when commoners could build one)

At the front of the village, facing the pond. The Cantonese water-country villages of the Pearl River delta were laid out in a comb plan facing a river channel or a pond; the ancestral halls and the other public spaces stand mainly at the front of the village, with the houses behind them. At Songtang, as in most Cantonese villages, the halls stand at the very front of the village next to the pond, facing it with their doors to the water, and a reader walking in passes from the water to the drying floor (the heping) and then into the lanes. A study of the Panyu halls finds water and a plaza at the very front of most halls, for geomancy and for drainage, fire and fish. In most Hakka weilongwu a half-moon pond fronts the house, the drying floor lies between pond and house, and the main gate stands straight ahead on the house's axis. So in both forms the pond faces the hall, or the house and its gate, across open ground.

Not every village had one. One historian of these villages sorts them, by period, into three layouts: a village centered on one hall, a comb plan led by a row of halls, and a single-row village of flatter social structure with no hall at all. Commoners were widely able to set up halls in their homes only from the Jiajing reign of the Ming (1522-1566), and the halls survive mostly in the south.

How big. Guangzhou alone keeps about 1,300 old halls, and those built in the Cantonese region's second great period of hall building, after Guangzhou became the one port open to foreign trade, were mostly small and middling halls three bays wide and three halls deep (three halls one behind another on one axis). In Guangzhou prefecture most small lineage and branch halls were three bays wide; a single axis of three halls is a form with many examples, spread very widely across the prefecture, used by a lineage's founding-ancestor halls, branch halls and sub-branch halls alike; halls five bays wide are rare, about twenty known; and the rear halls of several halls at Langtou are 8.5 to 9 meters deep (about 28 to 30 feet). The whole footprint of a common village hall, its width and length on the ground, no page read gives.

What this means for the map. Whether a south-China village has a hall is a KNOB with the three attested forms: one hall at its center, a row of halls across its front, or none. Where it has one and a pond, the hall stands in the front row facing the pond, its door to the water, with the drying floor between them left open. The hall is drawn three bays wide and three halls deep; its footprint in feet is a GUESS, built from the rear hall's measured depth.
