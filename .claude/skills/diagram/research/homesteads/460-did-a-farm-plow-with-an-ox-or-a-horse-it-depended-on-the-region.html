Did a farm plow with an ox or a horse? It depended on the region

Sources: nakanishi-1994-kogyu-koba (the 1886 count of plow cattle and plow horses, region by region), nikkei-2017-nishi-ushi (the 1877 count), buck-1930-farm-economy (oxen, water buffaloes and horses in China's north and in its rice country), l7r-stable-horse-culture (the setting's surplus horses)

How big the beast's byre is and what stands in it is at How big is a draft-animal byre? This question asks which beast it was.

In Japan: horses in the east, cattle in the west, both in the south. The nearest national count to the Edo village is the army's inventory of 1886, which listed plow cattle and plow horses province by province. Plow cattle were spread widely over western Japan, west of Shiga and Mie. Eastern Japan had almost none, apart from a few cattle-breeding districts such as the island of Sado and the southern tips of the Izu and Boso peninsulas. Plow horses were the reverse: many in the east, from Niigata, Nagano and Yamanashi eastward - but many too in Kyushu and southern Shikoku. The author divides the country into three: an eastern horse region, a western cattle region centered on Kinki and Chugoku, and a mixed region of Kyushu and Shikoku. A national survey of 1877 shows the same split: cattle concentrated in the west, horses far more numerous in the east and in southern Kyushu. These are Meiji counts; that the Edo village a generation earlier plowed with the same beasts is this page's reading. The 1886 figures average about a quarter of a beast per cho of cropped land, but the author warns that the national average means almost nothing, so widely did the provinces differ.

In China: the water buffalo in the rice country. A survey of farms in the 1920s found oxen and water buffaloes together in the rice-growing east-central provinces, the buffaloes the larger share, and no buffaloes in the north, which is too cold and too dry for them. Horses, donkeys and mules were northern animals, the mules kept out of the south because they could not work the wet rice fields. Half the northern farms had an ox; fewer than a third of the east-central farms did.

In the setting. The GM's notes add a source of farm horses history does not: a daimyo with more horses than the domain needs gives the surplus to the village headmen to hand out to the farms as draft animals. A horse can therefore turn up on a farm in cattle country.

What this means for the map. Ox against horse is two attested forms, so it is a knob, not a choice: which beast a settlement's farms keep is rolled per settlement from the map's seed. A Japanese-model village rolls toward horses where it stands for the east, toward cattle where it stands for the west, and between them for the south; a south-China paddy hamlet keeps a water buffalo or an ox, the survey's rice-country beasts. Which part of the setting stands for which region of Japan is this project's decision, not a finding; the share of each is calibrated liberty. The byre is drawn the same for either beast, so the roll changes what a modal says rather than what the map draws.
