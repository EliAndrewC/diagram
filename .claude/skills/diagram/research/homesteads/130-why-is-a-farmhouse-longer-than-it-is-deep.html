Why is a farmhouse longer than it is deep?

Sources: kotobank-minka; this project's drawing decision

No two farmsteads were copies of one another, so the houses on a map vary - but they vary along their LENGTH and hardly at all across their DEPTH.123 A minka grew by adding bays to its long axis: another bay under the same ridge, an aisle down one side. It got longer, not much wider, and it never became a square or a four-to-one shed (the direction of growth is this page's reading of the plan types).122 So a clustered map varies a house's length by roughly 0.85 to 1.35 of its base and its depth by only 0.90 to 1.10, and the proportions that come out stay inside the range a minka keeps, about 1.3 to 2.5 long to deep; past about 2.7 to 1 the house is drawn wrong rather than drawn differently. (This rests on general reading of minka plans; no source is cited for the band.)97

A scattered map varies the same thing from the other end. There the whole homestead takes a wealth standing - poorer, ordinary or better-off - which scales the house and its grove together, so a richer farm reads bigger across the board. That scaling reaches what is DRAWN and not what is reserved, so it never moves a neighbor and never changes how many households fit on the map.

The rule the map follows: farmhouses differ in size and in proportion within a modest range, and a farmhouse is never drawn squarer than about 1.3 to 1 or longer than about 2.7 to 1. The largest house on the map is the headman's (the question above).
