Does the village's name say where it stands?

Sources: the GM's general rule of 2026-07; chimei-jawiki (place names made from the terrain, and how often a name's origin is uncertain), yaji-tani-sawa (what tani, ya and sawa mean, and where)

A Rokugani place name usually says something about where the settlement sits relative to its fields, its water or its ground, and the GM made it a general rule in 2026-07 that the name is read BEFORE the settlement is seated: "the map should be able to be read back into the name". The worked example is Ueda (上田, "upper paddy"), whose paddies are the upslope ones - so its dwellings stand DOWNSLOPE of them, facing up-valley toward the fields. On that map's northeast-high ground that puts the cluster on the LOWER, southwest flank with the bulk of the paddy rising above it, which is the opposite of the default reading, a village on the high flank.

What the history says. Japan's names bear the rule out: making a place name from the features of its terrain is a common way of naming, most of all for small-scale terrain and for old names, as a valley is named X-tani or a hill X-ga-oka - though many names' origins are disputed or wholly unknown. Two terrain words, each making about four percent of Japan's indexed place names, also change with the region. Names in 谷 read tani or dani are mostly in western Japan, while the same character read ya is mostly eastern and most common where shallow valleys cut into uplands or low mountains; names in 沢 (sawa) are almost all eastern, and sawa means a mountain valley in the east but a marsh in the west.

Other cues worth reading the same way: shimo- (下, lower) says the reverse of Ueda; kawa- or -gawa (川, river) puts the settlement hard by the watercourse; and yama- (山, mountain), saka (坂, slope), hara (原, plain or moor), tani, -dani or -ya (谷, a valley; -ya most often a shallow one cut into higher ground), sawa (沢, a mountain valley in the east, a marsh in the west), hama (浜, shore) and -guchi (口, a mouth or entrance or pass) each name the ground the place stands on. Where the name says nothing about the ground, the default holds.

The rule the map follows: a settlement whose name names its ground is seated to match the name, and the default - a clustered village on the dry high flank, hugging its paddy - applies only where the name is neutral. A sawa name is read as a valley or as a marsh by the part of the setting the map stands in. No generator reads a name; this is a rule for whoever specifies the map.
