Where did the privy stand, and where was its night soil kept?

Sources: suzuki-1959-noson-benjo (how usual the outdoor privy was, the privy in the barn, the field pit), sugiura-1977-tohoku (the front yard, the rates village by village), sugiura-1973-fuzoku (the move to the front; the privy outbuildings of 1972), kotobank-benjo (the privy as one building apart from the house), sinyoken-madori (under the eaves by the stable, or an outhouse in the yard), artic-pigsty-latrine (the Han latrine over the pigsty)

The privy (benjo). The Nipponica encyclopedia (on kotobank): 「In farm households it was usual to provide one urinal and one privy as a single building independent of the main house.」 (translated; original: 「農家では、小便所一つと大便所一つを、母屋から 独立した一つの建物として設けるのが普通であった」) - on a farm the urinal and the privy were ONE building independent of the main house, and that was the norm. Where it stood (sinyoken floor-plan pages): 「it is thought the privy was under the eaves in front of the stable to the left of the entrance, or else a separate outhouse in the yard」 (translated; original: 「便所は入口の左横の厩の前の軒下か、あるいは庭に別棟の外便所があったと考えられる」) - under the eaves by the stable beside the entrance, or a separate outhouse in the yard. No page read names a privy by the back door or at the gate. The map draws every privy as a separate outhouse in the yard and ROLLS per house where in the yard it stands: behind the house by the back door .60, by the gate .25, by the stable or shed .15, each falling back to the others. Only the stable seat is attested, and there the source's privy is under the eaves rather than a separate outhouse; the back-door and gate seats, and the weights that make the back door the commonest, are a GUESS. On most houses a seat on the house's sunny side is tried first - why is at The outhouse faces the sun. Size: each privy takes one of the sixteen sizes one village's count gives, from 5 by 5 feet to 27 by 15 feet - why is below. Share: the source's "ordinarily" reads as near-universal; Sugiura's 0.87 privy outbuildings a household and 0.32 of farms with a privy inside the house, both for farms whose main house was built by 1944 (a 1972 survey) (overlapping) agree; drawn on 85-95% of homesteads. That the privy stood outside the house on most farms before 1868 is at Before 1868, was a farm's privy a building of its own? In China under the Han dynasty, the Art Institute of Chicago catalog says of its pottery model of a pigsty and latrine: "latrines - or toilets - were customarily built above a pigsty and connected by pipes to a cesspool" - in China the privy and the muck were one cluster; that a Japanese farmstead kept them together too is this page's reading (a GUESS), and it is why the manure heap (Where did the manure heap stand?) is seated BEYOND THE PRIVY. Which way the privy faced is at The outhouse faces the sun; how usual an outdoor privy was, two more places it stood, and where its night soil went follow.

How usual it was. A 1959 survey of farm households in one village each of Saitama, Tokyo and Miyagi found households with an indoor privy alone at 6.0% in Saitama and 9.4% in Tokyo, and none in Miyagi, and concluded that more than 90% of households had an outdoor privy; the authors infer it stood outside the house because that spot was handy for manuring the fields. Small houses tended to have only the outdoor privy, and considerably large ones an indoor privy; where a household had two or more privies, most had one outdoor and one indoor. Further north the share was not the same everywhere: reconstructions of three Tohoku villages before the war put outdoor privies at 85% of farms in Tono and 79% in Shiokawa, but at none in Inawashiro. The map's privy on 85-95% of homesteads sits on the common case. The same share holds before 1868: most houses at Hasuda in 1824 and every one of the 16 households of Kakimochi village, counted in 1885 and read by its historian back to the last years of the shogunate, had a privy outside the main house (see Before 1868, was a farm's privy a building of its own?).

Two more places it stood. A prewar housing report, as Sugiura passes it on, says that in northern Miyagi the bath and the privy were usually put in the front yard of the main house, and that making them buildings of their own was a fashion of early Meiji; the same report is cited for the move of the privy to the front of the house in a Miyagi town being a kind of fashion. And at several farms of the surveyed Miyagi village the privy was no building of its own: a tub sunk in the floor of the barn outside the main house, with boards laid across it. With the two seats given there, the record holds four distinct places - under the eaves by the stable, a separate outhouse in the yard, the front yard, and inside the barn - so the seat is a knob among them; how often each is drawn is a GUESS.

The night soil - beside the privy, or out by the fields. The survey found night soil kept two ways: in a storage tank beside the privy, or in a field pit (nodame) away from the house, and the authors think the tank was moved from beside the privy to near each household's fields, or to the roadside, to make manuring easier. How many households kept a field pit varied widely: 2 of 83 in the Saitama village, 19 of 53 in Tokyo's, 15 of 18 in Miyagi's. These are two distinct forms, so where the night-soil pit stands is a knob - beside the privy, or at a field edge or roadside - and the share of each is a degree that varies from almost none to most households.

Its size. The Kakimochi count gives each of its sixteen privies' size, from 5 by 5 feet to 27 by 15 feet, most of them 18 by 12 feet or smaller, and the map rolls each homestead's privy from those sixteen (see Before 1868, was a farm's privy a building of its own?).

The rule the map follows: an outdoor privy on 85-95% of homesteads, seated per house among the four attested places - under the eaves by the stable, a separate outhouse in the yard, the front yard, inside the barn - with the sun side preferred as the outhouse question says; and the night-soil pit, where a homestead has one, beside its privy or out at the edge of one of its fields or beside a road, the share at a field edge rolled per hamlet between almost none and most households. The weights among the seats are a guess; the privy's size is rolled from the Kakimochi table.
