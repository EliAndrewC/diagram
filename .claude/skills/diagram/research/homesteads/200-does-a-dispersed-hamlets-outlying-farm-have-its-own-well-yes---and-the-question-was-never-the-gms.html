Does a DISPERSED hamlet's outlying farm have its own well? It has its own water - on the Tonami plain, often a channel led into its grounds

Sources: visit-toyama-sankyoson, mdpi-sho-fan-groundwater, tonami-sankyoson-museum

The finding. On the Tonami Plain in Toyama - a Japanese dispersed settlement, ~7,000 farmhouses over ~220 km2 - farmers "used to build their houses in the middle of their cultivated rice fields so that they could easily manage the water for their own rice fields". The dispersal is not incidental to water; water is the REASON for it. Each house also carries its own kainyo homestead grove, so the farmstead is a self-contained unit by construction. The plain is an alluvial fan, and on it a farm's own water was often a channel rather than a well: the water table lay deep and a well was hard to dig, so in many areas a small channel was led off the irrigation water into the house's grounds, for cooking, washing and drinking.

The answer. A shared well with a reach radius is a NUCLEATED settlement's arrangement: it presupposes a center to be near. A dispersed farmstead has no center to share with - that is what dispersed MEANS - so it carries its own water. A 500 ft reach from a shared well is the wrong rule for this form.

The consequence: a nucleated hamlet shares wells within reach; a dispersed one gives each farmstead its own water. That is the same shape as the map's rule that every farmhouse reaches a lane, which is conditional on the settlement's form rather than waived.

On the map, a dispersed farm's own water is a knob, rolled for each dispersed settlement and recorded on the map: a channel led into the grounds, or a well. The two come up at even odds, a GUESS - no page read says how many areas used each. The channel is historically accurate: the Tonami farms led one into the house's grounds in many areas. That the other areas dug a well of their own is this record's reading, a GUESS.

The channel is led off the nearest irrigation ditch the map draws - a main or a branch, never the drainage ditch - or off the brook where the brook is nearer. Taking the nearest is a map drawing convention; the brook standing for the irrigation water the ditches are fed from is this project's reading, a GUESS. A later farm's channel may be led off one already drawn, a map drawing convention: it is still the irrigation water, carried on. The channel runs round the buildings, yards, gardens and the other farms' groves, through the farm's own grove into its grounds, and ends in the dooryard a step off the threshing yard; where in the dooryard it ended no page read says, so that is a GUESS. Its way back to the field is not drawn, a deliberate deviation: no page read says where it left the lot. It is drawn at the field channel's 2.5 ft bed, a map drawing convention - the narrowest water the map draws legibly; no page read gives the real width of a channel into a farm's grounds.

The well stands in the dooryard, inside the farm's own lot, nearest its work yard and off its way in. Where on the plot the well stood no page read says, so its place is a GUESS. Where the roll is a channel, a farm no channel can reach has its own well instead.
