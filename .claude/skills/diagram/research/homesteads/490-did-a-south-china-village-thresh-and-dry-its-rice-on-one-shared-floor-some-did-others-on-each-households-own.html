How the rice was threshed. A Ming account of 1637 says that half the rice was threshed by beating the sheaves by hand and half by spreading them on the threshing floor and having an ox drag a stone roller over them. The roller saved three times the labor, so southern households with large plantings mostly threshed on the floor with an ox. In a wet harvest, sheaves that could not be brought to the floor were beaten in a wooden tub out in the field. Whose the floor was, the text does not say.

A shared floor, in two regional forms. The Hakka built the drying ground into the great house itself: between its half-moon pond and the house lies the drying floor, and one such house could hold a dozen, dozens or even a hundred households, so its floor served them all. In the Cantonese village, the main lane usually lay at the front of the village and was sometimes widened into a drying ground, a village's common wealth going into its lanes and open spaces.

The household's own floor. A writer from a rice valley below Mount Heng, in Hunan, remembers every household having its own drying floor, the open ground in front of the house. Bare earth would not do; a wealthy household laid lime and sand over it, and a poor one could not.

How big. The Hakka house's drying floor was generally made as wide as the main house is tall, measured out with a builder's ruler of lucky and unlucky lengths - a proportion, not a measurement. No page read gives a shared floor's size in feet or says how much of it each household used.

What this means for the map. The drying floor of a south-China paddy hamlet is a knob with two attested forms, rolled per settlement: each household's own floor before its house, as a Japanese farm's yard is drawn; or one shared floor at the front of the village, beside its pond or before its great house, with little or no yard at each house.
