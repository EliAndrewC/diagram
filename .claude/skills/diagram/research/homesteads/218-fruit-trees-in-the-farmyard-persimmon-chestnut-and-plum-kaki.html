Fruit trees in the farmyard: persimmon, chestnut and plum (kaki)

A Japanese farmhouse commonly had a persimmon beside it. Accounts of the old farm villages give one to every dooryard, the yard in front of the house: it gave fruit and, in summer, shade to the house, and an Edo-period agronomist is said to have urged farmers to plant it round the homestead. A full-grown persimmon stands about 12 m (~39 ft) tall and spreads about 7 m (~23 ft).

The persimmon was not the farmyard's only fruit tree. Plum and persimmon were planted in the homestead grove to be eaten from, and fruit trees in part of the yard; chestnut was among the commonest trees of the northern farmsteads surveyed. No source counts how many fruit trees a farm had. The grove as a whole is at Groves of trees around farmhouses (yashikirin), and a farmstead's bamboo at Did every farmstead keep its own bamboo?

  • A persimmon is said to have stood in every farm's dooryard.
    A Japanese blog on the farmhouse persimmon says the people of the old days treasured the tree, and so a persimmon is planted in every dooryard. A newspaper history of the Fuyu persimmon says the farm villages of the past had an old giant persimmon in the dooryard of every house, a valued source of sweetness.
  • The persimmon was planted to shade the house in summer.
    The same blog says the tree casts shade over the house in summer, and that birds do not nest in it nor insects settle on it. So it stood beside the house rather than out in the fields.
  • An Edo-period agronomist is said to have urged planting persimmons round the homestead.
    The blog reports that Miyazaki Yasusada, an agronomist of the Edo period, is said to have encouraged planting persimmons around the house and homestead. It is a report at second hand: his own words were not read.
  • A persimmon stood in front of the house, most often, or behind it.
    Besides the dooryard tree of every house, the newspaper history names one such persimmon, in the middle of the Meiji period, in the bamboo grove behind a house in Gifu. On the Shonai plain, where the rice was prepared indoors on the house's wide earthen floor, the front yard was narrow and often planted with fruit trees, flower beds or vegetable beds. A persimmon on the side of the house, its flank, is attested nowhere.
  • How big did a persimmon grow?
    A Japanese tree reference gives a height of 3 to 20 m (~10-66 ft), and a modern horticultural database a full-grown tree of about 12 m (~39 ft) by 7 m (~23 ft), a crown of about 23 ft. Both are modern figures for the species, not for a farmhouse tree before 1868.
  • How big did a farmhouse persimmon grow before Japan began to modernize in 1868?
    No record from before 1868 gives the crown of an ordinary farmhouse persimmon. The one Edo-period tree measured is the extreme: the largest persimmon in Okayama, at Omachi in the town of Kagamino, said to be the largest in Japan, about 400 years old, stands 17 m (~56 ft) tall with a crown of 20.17 m (~66 ft) east to west and 14.65 m (~48 ft) north to south. It was growing through the Edo period, but it is measured as it stands today.

The tree's long life and shade were praised in China long before.

  • A ninth-century Chinese saying gives the persimmon seven virtues, long life and much shade first.
    The Youyang Zazu records the common saying that the persimmon has seven virtues: long life, much shade, no birds' nests, no insects, frosted leaves fine to look at, fine fruit, and large thick fallen leaves. It describes a long-lived tree that shades the ground around it; it gives no size.

Other fruit trees stood in the yard and the grove beside the persimmon.

  • Plum and persimmon were planted in the homestead grove to be eaten from.
    Besides protecting the house from disaster, a homestead grove carried trees that could be eaten from, such as plum and persimmon. An encyclopedia entry on homestead groves adds that fruit trees were planted in part of the yard.
  • On the Tonami plain, fig, grape and persimmon grew on the south side, by the storehouses and barns.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on homestead groves puts the Tonami farmstead's storehouses and barns on the south side, and there fig, grape and persimmon, with bamboo. A survey of the plain's groves puts the flowering and fruit trees at the open east front of the house instead (see Sunlight and shade on the farm).
  • Chestnut was among the commonest trees of two northern districts' farmsteads, and persimmon of Tonami's.
    A survey of homestead groves in four districts found sugi cedar and chestnut among the three commonest trees in the two northern districts, Isawa and Iide, and sugi, persimmon and an evergreen oak on the Tonami plain; the fourth district, Hikawa, had no fruit tree among its three. The sample is small, four to ten homesteads a district.
  • How many fruit trees did a farmstead have?
    No source we could read counts them. The nearest figure is the four-district survey's count of all woody kinds, about 9 to 18 at the average homestead, not of fruit trees. One to three fruit trees near the house is a GUESS, since the sources speak of fruit trees as a part of the yard rather than an orchard.
  • The loquat was kept out of the homestead by a folk belief.
    A folk belief is recorded that a loquat planted in a garden or a homestead brings sickness that never ends.

Tea and mulberry were trees of the field, not the yard.

  • Some farms planted tea in a single row along the banks between their fields.
    Bund tea (keihan-cha) was tea planted in one row along the banks between paddies and fields. It also marked the boundary with the neighbor, broke the wind and kept the soil from washing away, and what the household did not drink was sometimes sold to wholesalers. How many villages kept it is not recorded.
  • Tea and mulberry were grown in the fields as crops for sale.
    They were two of the "four trees" of the early-modern period, with lacquer and paper mulberry: crops grown for sale and worked up into goods. The shogunate restricted them by its rule on what a field might grow, but from the middle of the period they were grown as each region's specialties.
