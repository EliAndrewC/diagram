Which farmhouses have a storehouse?

Sources: naya-jawiki, doma-jawiki, sugiura-1973-fuzoku (the 1972 count), oamishirasato-choshi-kaoku (the premodern count), dozo-jawiki, kotobank-dozo, sekkei-sya-sayama-kura (when a farm built one); this project's measurement of the drawn maps

A minority, and that is the whole point of it. A fireproof kura was costly to build, so well-off farmers and landlords were the ones with one (this page's reading of the construction the pages describe), and it reads on the map as a wealth marker precisely because most farms lack one (the counts below). A farm built its kura when money had accumulated, and in one district west of Edo farmhouses probably came to own them only in the last years of the shogunate. A simpler naya outbuilding was commoner but still not on every plot: many households worked and dried their crop inside the farmhouse's own earth-floored doma, and the poorest households had little to store (this page's reading). Before 1868 the storehouse was rarer still. In Kakimochi village, whose building count of 1885 its town historian reads back to the last years of the shogunate, 2 of the 16 households had one, each 2 by 3 ken (about 12 by 18 feet), and both were among the households the historian calls the powerful ones (see What stood on a farmstead before 1868?). The Miyagi survey of 1972 counts 0.24 storehouses per household among farmhouses whose main house was built in or before 1944 (see the farmstead inventory); that is a modern figure.

The share: about one farm in eight, calibrated to the premodern count. The GM ruled on 2026-09-28 that the maps drop what is attested only in modern times, so the share is set from the Kakimochi count, 2 in 16, and not from the 1972 one; the alternative, about 30%, just above the 1972 count, was not kept because that count is modern. It is one village, so the figure is a calibration rather than a finding; it keeps a storehouse uncommon enough to mean something at a glance. The maps as drawn were calibrated to the 1972 count and carry more: measured over them, the share is 28.4%, 343 of 1,208 farmhouses.

The roll is positional, and what that costs was accepted on 2026-08-17. Whether a given farm has a storehouse is decided from where its house stands rather than from the order the houses were drawn in, so the same map drawn twice gives the same answer. The cost is that a re-packing - the map laying its houses out afresh, so that a house can end up somewhere else - which moves a house re-rolls its storehouse: one re-pack took a hamlet from 25% to 15% in a single roll, which read like storehouses disappearing. The arithmetic behind the roll is honest - measured at 0.2993 over 200,000 positions - so what changed was which houses drew it. The declined alternative is keying the roll to a household number instead, which would hold across a re-packing; it was not taken.

The rule the map follows: about one farmhouse in eight carries a storehouse, the larger houses first, drawn as an annex against the house wall rather than as a free-standing shed - on the WEST wall of a scattered farm, and on the shaded NORTH wall in a cluster, where the sunny walls belong to the garden and the north is the one side the garden never takes. That annex on a fixed wall is a drawing convention, and the record contradicts it: the kura read stood free of the house, in front of it or behind, on no fixed side, at about 15 by 18 feet (see How big was a farm storehouse). The headman always carries one (the question above), and the storehouse's ground is reserved with the rest of the homestead, so a neighbor never lands on it.
