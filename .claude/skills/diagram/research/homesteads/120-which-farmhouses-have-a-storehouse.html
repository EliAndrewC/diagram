Which farmhouses have a storehouse?

Sources: naya-jawiki, doma-jawiki, sugiura-1973-fuzoku (the 1972 count), oamishirasato-choshi-kaoku (the premodern count), dozo-jawiki, kotobank-dozo, sekkei-sya-sayama-kura (when a farm built one); this project's measurement of the drawn maps

A minority, and that is the whole point of it. A fireproof kura was costly to build, so well-off farmers and landlords were the ones with one (this page's reading of the construction the pages describe), and it reads on the map as a wealth marker precisely because most farms lack one (the counts below). A farm built its kura when money had accumulated, and in one district west of Edo farmhouses probably came to own them only in the last years of the shogunate. A simpler naya outbuilding was commoner but still not on every plot: many households worked and dried their crop inside the farmhouse's own earth-floored doma, and the poorest households had little to store (this page's reading). Before 1868 the storehouse was rarer still. In Kakimochi village, whose building count of 1885 its town historian reads back to the last years of the shogunate, 2 of the 16 households had one, each 2 by 3 ken (about 12 by 18 feet), and both were among the households the historian calls the powerful ones (see What stood on a farmstead before 1868?). The Miyagi survey of 1972 counts 0.24 storehouses per household among farmhouses whose main house was built in or before 1944 (see the farmstead inventory); that is a modern figure.

The share: about one farm in eight, calibrated to the premodern count. The GM ruled on 2026-09-28 that the maps drop what is attested only in modern times, so the share is set from the Kakimochi count, 2 in 16, and not from the 1972 one; the alternative, about 30%, just above the 1972 count, was not kept because that count is modern. It is one village, so the figure is a calibration rather than a finding; it keeps a storehouse uncommon enough to mean something at a glance. On the generated hamlets the share is drawn as a count, one in eight of the hamlet's farmhouses rounded to a whole house: one storehouse on a hamlet of ten or eleven farmhouses, two on twelve to nineteen, three on twenty - 11 of 82 over the five hamlets drawn. The hand-drawn maps keep the higher share they were drawn with, 324 of 1,126 farmhouses.

Which farms: the largest houses first. The Kakimochi historian ties the outbuildings to the size of the house: the more of them a household had, the larger its main house, and the powerful farmers who served as village officials owned such houses. The village's table bears that out for the storehouse, though not to the letter. Its two storehouses stood with main houses of 40 and 32 tsubo (about 1,400 and 1,100 square feet), the second and third largest of the sixteen; the largest, 45 tsubo, had none; and seven of the sixteen main houses were 18 tsubo. So on a generated hamlet the storehouses go to the largest farmhouses, measured by the main house's floor, and no farmhouse without one is larger than a farmhouse with one. Making a strict rule of what one village's count shows as a strong tendency is a deliberate deviation: the map never leaves a great house without a storehouse while a smaller one has it, though Kakimochi's largest house, at 45 tsubo, had none. The alternative, a draw weighted toward the larger houses, would let a great house go without now and then; it was not taken, because the larger houses first is the tendency the record shows, and a draw would sometimes put a hamlet's storehouse on one of its middling houses. Where two farmhouses are the same size the map has nothing else to tell their households apart by, so which of them has it is a random draw keyed to the map's seed, and the same map drawn twice gives the same answer. What decided it between two such households the records read do not say, so that is a guess.

The rule the map follows: about one farmhouse in eight carries a storehouse - on a generated hamlet a count, rounded to a whole house - and they are the hamlet's largest farmhouses, a tie between two of one size going to a draw. It is drawn as an annex against the house wall rather than as a free-standing shed, 18 to 27 feet long and 1.5 to 1.8 times as long as deep, the band of the two Edo farm sheds (see How big was a farm shed) - on the WEST wall of a scattered farm, and on the shaded NORTH wall in a cluster, where the sunny walls belong to the garden and the north is the one side the garden never takes. That annex on a fixed wall is a drawing convention, and the record contradicts it: the kura in the sources read stood free of the house, in front of it or behind, on no fixed side. Its size is a deliberate deviation: the commonest kura in a new-field village west of Edo was about 15 by 18 feet, and Kakimochi's two were 2 by 3 ken, about 12 by 18 feet (see How big was a farm storehouse), so the annex is drawn as long as a kura was or up to half again as long, and at the short end of its band 10 to 12 feet deep, shallower than the commonest kura and no deeper than Kakimochi's. A village headman's house always carries one (the question above); a hamlet has no headman, so on a generated hamlet the largest houses take them all. The storehouse's ground is reserved with the rest of the homestead, so a neighbor never lands on it.
