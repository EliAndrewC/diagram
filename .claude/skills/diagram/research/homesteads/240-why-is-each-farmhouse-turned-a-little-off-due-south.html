Why is each farmhouse turned a little off due south, and why does its yard turn with it?

Sources: none that can be read; the GM's ruling of 2026-09-26

A farmhouse in a clustered village faces south, toward its work (threshing) yard, but no two stand at exactly the same bearing: each is turned by up to five degrees either way. Real farmhouses were set out by eye on their own plot, against a lane, a field edge or the lie of the ground, not surveyed to the compass, so a row of houses all square to the map would read as drawn rather than built. How far a village's houses actually strayed from south is not something this project could find measured: no public page gives the spread of house bearings within one hamlet, so the five degrees is a guess, chosen to be visible without looking careless.

The work yard and the kitchen-garden beds turn with their house. The yard is the ground in front of the house's south wall and is laid out along that wall; a yard set square to the map beside a house turned four degrees would look like a mistake, and the GM ruled that the two always line up, "because that's just how they would be naturally laid out". The homestead turns as one piece about the middle of the house, and the ground cleared for each part is the ground it is drawn on.

The rule the map follows: each farmhouse is turned by up to five degrees from due south, set by where it stands, and its work yard and garden beds are turned by the same amount, so their edges run with the house's walls, and the yard's edge toward the house is kept straight and parallel to it, even though its other edges wander a little as a hand-laid plot's do. (Guess: the size of the turn. The GM's rulings: the yard and beds sharing it, and the yard's edge toward the house running parallel to it.)
