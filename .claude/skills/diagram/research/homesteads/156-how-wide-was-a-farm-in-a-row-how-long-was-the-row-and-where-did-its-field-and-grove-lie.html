How wide was a farm in a row, how long was the row, and where did its field and grove lie?

Sources: saitama-santome-history, kotobank-santome-shinden, kawashima-1986-santome and miyoshi-santome-shinden (Santome's frontage and depth, the order back from the road, its households, road grid and grove), ome-shinmachi-machinami (a narrow-fronted planned row), ishizue-musashino (the Musashino holdings and strips), shinden-shuraku-kotobank (the strips of the Kanto plain, and the compact holding on reclaimed ground)

What a row followed, and whether its farms stood on one side of it or both, is at How was a row village laid out?. This question asks how wide each farm was, where its field and grove lay, and how long a row ran.

How wide a farm fronted the line

Two planned rows give a frontage. At Santome each household's strip was 40 ken wide by 375 ken deep, about 240 ft by 2,240 ft; the Nobidome strips of 1661 ran about 400 ken deep. At Ome Shinmachi, opened on the Ome road in 1596-1615, a lot had only 9 ken of frontage (about 54 ft), 33 lots on each side, the main houses on the road and the fields in strips behind, and wide front yards, said to be because the village was meant to hold a market and become a post town - a farming row, but one laid out with a street in mind. The holdings differed too: Santome gave each household about 5 cho, where the Musashino new fields, taken as a whole, gave about 1 cho; a 1-cho strip's frontage was not found (a GUESS), nor how far apart the houses of a levee or dike row stood (a GUESS).

The house stood at the road end of its strip, so neighbors stood about one frontage apart, lot against lot with no gap, and in the 1980s paths still ran sideways from homestead to homestead.

Where the fields lay

On the planned rows, behind each house in its own strip: the land ran back from the road as house lot, then field, then woodland; the Kanto plain's planned rows set each house's holding behind it in strips of equal area; and the woodland at the strip's far end gave fodder, compost and fuel - the same form, the page notes, as the north German forest village. On reclaimed ground, where the row stood on a dike, the holding lay near the house and fairly compact, no strip named. The Musashino strips are dry field; no strip row of paddy was found, so a strip of rice behind each house is a GUESS.

How long a row ran, and how many farms it held

Santome counted 180 homesteads in its three villages at the survey of 1696 - 91, 40 and 49; the 1980s survey gives 172 at the opening (83, 40 and 49). The colony covered 33 cho east to west by 20 north to south (about 2.2 by 1.4 miles) on roads laid like a go board, so on this page's reading a big colony grew more roads, each with its own row, rather than one row doubling back or a second rank behind it. How long a row ran where it grew by itself was not found (a GUESS).

Where the homestead grove stood in a row

Bamboo, oak and zelkova were planted round a Santome homestead, the zelkova kept as timber for the house's own need. As a rule the homestead was covered by its grove, though by the 1980s the groves had much thinned; the main house then faced south, the work buildings round its front yard; and on the road side zelkova and white oak were planted, making an avenue. In Kamitome great zelkovas, 2 to 3 ft thick, stood in a line. Those are trees standing in the 1980s, not dated to the founding; which side the grove took on a levee or dike row was not found (a GUESS).

What it means for a map

  1. Frontage is a knob with two attested values: about 240 ft a farm, from Santome's 40 ken, and about 54 ft, from Ome Shinmachi's 9 ken (accurate for those two rows; where a paddy row, or one that grew by itself, falls between them is a GUESS). At 240 ft, twenty farms make a row about 2,400 ft long on both sides of the road, or 4,800 ft on one; at 54 ft, about 540 ft or 1,080 ft.
  2. The field lies in a strip behind each house on a road row (accurate for dry field; for paddy a GUESS), and near each house, fairly compact, on a dike row (accurate); a sheet shows only the near end of a 2,240-ft strip.
  3. A row too long for the sheet takes a second road with its own row, not a second rank behind the first (accurate for Santome's plan; how many farms before the second road, a GUESS).
  4. The grove stands on the road side of the house, forming an avenue along the row, and round the house (accurate for Santome as surveyed in the 1980s); on a levee or dike row, a GUESS.
