The plot was enclosed. A farmer's plot, in the historians' general account of the Edo village, had its main house and its barn inside the plot's enclosure, the house set back behind the middle and the open ground south of it used as the work and drying yard. A plot was usually surrounded by a hedge or an earthen wall, and some dug a ditch against intruders.

What enclosed it changed from east to west. Some farm plots on the plains carried a grove on the north and northwest, called igune in the northeast, kune in the Kanto and tsuijimatsu in Izumo. The grove was a boundary as well as a windbreak: kune means the line between two holdings, and the igune divides the inside of the plot from the outside. The same word names a hedge or a fence of woven bamboo, in use by the end of the Muromachi period. From the northeast to the Kanto the plots were fairly large with many outbuildings; going west the plots grew smaller, and a plot was more often closed in by its own outbuildings than by a grove.

An earth bank, and a stone wall, in particular places. The Izumo pines were planted to strengthen an earth bank built around the house against floods, and before the Meiji era they enclosed the whole house. In Okinawa, the traditional house was generally walled in piled coral and limestone against the typhoons.

What was not found. No page read counts how many farm plots were closed by each form, gives the height or width of a farm hedge or earthen wall, or describes a farm plot with nothing at its edge.

What this means for the map. The edge of a farm plot is a knob with four attested forms, rolled per settlement from the map's seed: a hedge (or a bamboo fence) along the plot line; a grove on the north and west, which also serves as the windbreak; the plot closed by its own house, barn and sheds set around the yard, the western form; and, where a map stands for a flood plain or a storm coast, an earth bank under trees or a stone wall. A hedge is drawn as a thin green line along the plot's edge, broken for the way in; an earthen or stone wall as a thin gray line. How thick these lines are is a map drawing convention, since no size was found. An open plot with nothing along its edge rests on no page read and is a GUESS, kept only as the rare case.
