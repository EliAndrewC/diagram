Bath sheds on the farm (furo)

Before 1868, in the Edo period, a farm's bath, where it had one, was a small room at the main house - beside its main door, under its eaves, or out beyond its stable wing - and few farms had one: a bath in the house was a luxury for a farm household, and in the villages whose house plans survive only two or three houses in ten had one by the 1820s. No page we read places a bath standing as a building of its own on a farm before 1868.

The bath shed - a hut of its own in the yard - is a thing of the Meiji period and after. A prewar report calls it a fashion of early Meiji, when housing improvement set the bath apart from the house as unsanitary. By the 1930s the share of farms with one ran from none to eight in ten, village by village.

  • Village house-plan registers record a farm's bath room by room.
    When the shogun went on pilgrimage to Nikko, his retinue lodged in the villages along the road, and those villages drew up registers of the plan of every house so that it could be lodged. Nineteen registers of farm villages from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries survive, and an architectural historian has read them room by room.
  • In the registers of 1770 and 1771, only a very few houses had a bath, of about half a tsubo.
    A tsubo is about 6 by 6 feet. In three villages a bath room appears under the eaves beside the main door of four houses in one, beside the main door of six in another, and under the eaves beyond the stable of one house in the third - in only a very small part of each village's houses.
  • How common was a bath by the 1820s?
    About two or three houses in ten, in the two villages whose registers show it. In the register of Shimohasuda of 1824, 21 of 76 houses had a bath of 1 to 2 tsubo; at Ukiya in 1842, 15 of 62 houses did. The historian sums them up as about 20-30% of all houses.
  • Most of the baths of 1824 and 1842 stood out beyond the stable wing.
    At Shimohasuda 17 of the 21 were beyond the stable that projects from the earth floor, and at Ukiya 12 of the 15. The Koshigaya city history, reading the 1824 register of Hasuda village, counts 21 houses with a bath beyond the stable wing - the historian's total of baths, though he puts only 17 of them there.
  • Why was the bath put by the stable?
    The historian suggests, as a guess, that one reason was that the bath's drain water also served as manure.
  • A few baths were joined to the house's floored rooms, or to a back room behind it.
    The historian finds a bath sometimes joined to the floored part of the main house, and one joined to a back room projecting behind it.
  • Did a bath ever stand as a building of its own before 1868?
    None is found. Every bath the historian reports is at the main house; whether the baths beyond the stable were joined to it he does not say, and his paper covers the main house alone. The pages we read that describe a bath standing apart describe the twentieth century.
  • A modern reader of a farm manual of about 1680 reports that it treats a bath in the house as a luxury.
    The manual, the Hyakusho Denki, was written between 1680 and 1682. As its reader reports it, the manual has the family wash at a fixed place over a pit laid with bamboo slats, so that the water collects to thin the manure.
  • A town museum says the goemon-buro was widely used in farm villages.
    It says so of farm villages that lived chiefly by their own produce, and does not say when.

The counts of the twentieth century tell of a different arrangement, in which many farms kept the bath in a hut of its own.

  • How many farms had a bath shed before the Second World War?
    It differed from village to village. Reconstructions of three Tohoku villages for 1935-1944, from town-office records and interviews, put bath sheds at 80% of farms in Tono, 33% in Inawashiro and none in Shiokawa - figures the author thinks probably too high, since the records may count one building twice.
  • What did a 1972 count of one Miyagi town find?
    About 0.29 bath sheds a household over all its houses, and a bath inside the main house in 20 of the 38 houses built by 1944. These are this project's reading of tables the paper prints as images, and which column holds the bath shed is unconfirmed.
  • In northern Miyagi, the bath and the privy were usually put in the front yard of the main house.
    A prewar housing report says so, as a geographer passes it on; it is the one place for a bath shed that the pages we read give.
  • A prewar report dates the bath as a building of its own to a fashion of early Meiji.
    The same report makes their standing apart from the house a fashion of the early Meiji period.
  • Meiji housing improvement set the bath apart from the house.
    An architectural historian describes what it aimed at: bath and toilet kept apart from the main house as unsanitary, sometimes joined to it by a corridor, sometimes standing alone outdoors - and, he adds, "farmhouses are like that". Whether "farmhouses are like that" takes in the corridor as well as the bath standing alone, the passage leaves open.
  • Some farm families kept the bath in a hut of its own; others had it inside.
    A memoir remembers a thatched farmhouse with neither bath nor privy inside, each in a hut of its own, while other farm families had both indoors.
  • Where a new farmhouse in one Miyagi town had its bath inside, it stood in the northeast corner.
    In the newly built houses of the town in the 1972 count, the bath inside the main house stood without exception in the northeast corner, where in the old houses the stable inside the house generally stood.
  • How large was a bath shed?
    About 6 by 6 ft, a GUESS: no page we read measures one.
