Was the homestead grove there before 1868, and what size and shape was it?

Sources: miura-2014-kainyo (the Edo documents on the Tonami grove), tonami-kainyoclub (the 1789 farm manual's account of the grove), yashikirin-jawiki (the Takada order of 1625, and the Izumo grove before and after the Meiji era and its flood bank), irie-2020-igune (the Sendai plain's two-sided grove), kashima-kainyo-1987 and tonami-yashikirin-haichi (the Tonami grove's sides and open front), isa-auf-fukugi-2011 (Okinawa's north-and-east groves)

The size of the grove the maps draw, and the survey it comes from, are at Homestead groves (yashikirin) - the real scale and prevalence. This question asks which of that is older than the modern surveys, and which sides of the house the grove took.

The grove itself is premodern. The Kaga domain, which ruled the Tonami plain, treated the homestead grove - written 垣根 in its documents, spelled kaine in village papers - as a stand of timber and bamboo under its control: a 1658 notice for the Tonami district put the cutting of the domain's reserved trees and bamboo around homesteads and on field ridges under permit; a 1750 document calls a grove kept thick a fire- and windbreak; and in 1782 a villager whose rice tax fell short sold four large sugi from within his own grove to his headman to make it up. A Tonami farm manual of 1789, Miyanaga Shoun's Shika nogyo dan, lists what the grove gave: shelter from wind and cold, a guard against thieves and a neighbor's fire, firewood from its branches and leaves, its core trees thinned and cut for use, and fallen leaves for the hearth and the fields.

Its size is not. No Edo page read counts a grove's trees: the one number is the four large sugi sold in 1782, which is not the grove's total, so the ~30-40 mature trees the maps draw is a 1987 count with no premodern figure to set against it.

Its shape was a regional custom. The regions in the record disagree:

  • The full ring, on the Izumo plain. There the pine groves enclosed the whole circuit of the house before the Meiji era, and took a hook shape covering only the north and west afterward, as flood damage lessened and building changed; the pines had been planted in the first place to strengthen an earth bank built round the house against floods.
  • The Takada order of 1625. In the Takada domain an order had farmers plant camellia and bamboo grass on the south side and sugi around the homestead. Read as written, it does not say the sugi closed a ring, and it plants the south side with something else.
  • Two sides, on the Sendai plain, since the early Edo period. Against the strong north-northwesterly winter wind, the grove there is said to form on the north or the west of the plot and often to lack the south or the east side; and on the homesteads built on the plain's new fields, trees were planted in many layers on the north and west sides at the urging of Date Masamune, the first lord of the Sendai domain - so from the early Edo period - and kept as a windbreak for more than several hundred years.
  • Open at the front, on the Tonami plain. Tall sugi, oak and zelkova stood from the south face round to the west, hackberry and alder with a little bamboo from the west round to the north, and flowering and fruit trees on the east; the east is the front of the house, and there the grove is thin. Neither page dates this planting.
  • North and east, in Okinawa, where most large trees stand north of the house and the next most to the east.

The decision. On the record read, the dense stand of large sugi with its bamboo is premodern, the count that sizes it is modern, and the grove's sides were a regional custom: the two windward sides in the most regions, the front alone left open on the Tonami plain, the full ring on the Izumo plain's flood ground. The GM ruled on 2026-09-29: because it does sound as if groves completely surrounding farmhouses was a thing, then that should indeed be a tunable knob. It seems like two sides is the minimum. Three sides would sometimes be the case, and all four sides would also sometimes be the case. Offered the split and the flood-ground rise below, the GM accepted both: Your suggestions sound great, so yes, please go with all of that. Both the percentage split and the flood ground adjustment. So the maps keep a dense stand, with its tree count labeled a GUESS set from the 1987 survey, and they roll its sides.

The rule the map follows: a settlement's farmstead groves take their sides from one roll per settlement, the same at every farm, because the shape is reported as a regional custom. Two sides half the time, three sides three times in ten, all four sides twice in ten. Two sides are the windward pair - the form reported in the most regions, and the only one with a general statement and an early-Edo date. Three leave open only the front, the lee side where the yard and the way in are, as on the Tonami plain. Four close the ring, as on the Izumo plain. Where the farms stand on flood-prone ground - fields of reclaimed low ground behind dikes, or houses on a dike - four sides rise to four times in ten and the other two keep their 5 : 3 ratio (two sides 37.5%, three sides 22.5%), because the Izumo ring's own stated cause was flood; a map may declare its ground either way. Every one of these shares is a GUESS: no page read counts how many farmsteads took each shape, on any ground or in any period. The windward arms are the deep stand; the other planted sides are a thinner band of lesser trees, the Tonami pattern of tall cedar on the windward faces and lesser trees elsewhere. Carrying that pattern round a full ring is a GUESS, and so is the thinner band's depth. Which windward pair a map takes is rolled per map, at the question linked above. The village's shelter belt is a different planting and not this roll: it stays on one or two windward sides (Does a shelter belt wrap the settlement?).
