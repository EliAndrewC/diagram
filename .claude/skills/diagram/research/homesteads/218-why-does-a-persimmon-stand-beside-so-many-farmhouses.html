Why does a persimmon stand beside so many farmhouses?

Sources: toyoko-kaki, uekipedia-kaki, jataff-fuyu-kaki (the old tree in the dooryard, and one behind the house), sato-1962-haichi (fruit trees in the front yard), pfaf-kaki (the crown's spread)

The persimmon is one of the farmstead's fixtures the GM asked to see drawn; the whole set, and how many of each the map draws, is at The farmstead's fixtures.

The persimmon. "so a persimmon tree is planted in every dooryard" (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「だからどこの庭先にも柿の木が植えてある」) (toyoko-housing) - hence a persimmon in every dooryard; and "the Edo-period agronomist Miyazaki Yasusada is said to have encouraged planting persimmon trees around the house lot" (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「江戸時代の農学者　宮崎安貞は、家屋敷の周りに柿の木を植える事を奨励したとの事」) - the Edo agronomist Miyazaki Yasusada is said to have urged planting them round the homestead; "in summer it makes shade for the house" (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「夏は家に木陰を作り」) - it shades the house in summer, so it stands BESIDE the house.

Which side. A newspaper history of the Fuyu persimmon says the farm villages of the past had an old giant persimmon in the dooryard of every house, and names one such tree in the mid-Meiji period standing in the bamboo grove behind a house in Gifu. On the Shonai plain, where the rice was prepared indoors on the house's wide earthen floor, the front yard was narrow and often planted with fruit trees, flower beds or vegetable beds rather than kept as a drying yard. So two sides are attested - the dooryard in front of the house, most often, and behind it. A seat on the house's flank, where the map's generator still puts the tree first, is attested nowhere: it is a GUESS, and the rule below replaces it.

How big. Height 3-20 m (uekipedia); a persimmon grows to at most about 12 m tall and 7 m across, a crown of up to about 23 ft, against the 18 ft the map's generator still draws. That is an upper figure from a modern horticultural reference, which fits the old giants the newspaper history remembers better than an ordinary tree. Drawn on 80-95% of homesteads. Its fruit dots are a RENDERING convention naming the tree, not a season.

The rule the map follows: the persimmon stands in the dooryard in front of the house, at the edge of the work yard where there is one (a GUESS), or behind the house - two attested seats, a knob, the front the likelier - with its crown about 23 ft across. The weights are a guess.

Searched and not found: a government farm-ministry page saying a persimmon stood at every farmhouse "without exception".
