Row villages (resson)

A row village is a farming settlement whose houses stand one beside the next in a long line, on one side of the line or on both. Japanese geography names two kinds by what the line was: the road village, strung along a road, and the linear village, strung along a natural levee or the spring line at a hill's foot; an encyclopedia adds springs and waterways to what a row follows. Where new fields were opened in the Edo period, roads, fields and house plots were laid out on a plan, and at Santome each farm's land ran back from its road: house lot, then field, then woodland.

The best-measured rows are planned colonies on the Musashino plateau. There a farm fronted its road for between about 50 and 240 ft, and at Santome its strip ran back for nearly half a mile. On low ground the line was the land's own: a dike on land reclaimed from the sea, or the slight rise of a river's natural levee, which drained well above the wet ground behind it.

  • On new fields the road was laid first, and the lots along it.
    An Edo-period new-field village laid out its roads, fields and house plots on a plan, so its form was regular. At Santome, a dry-field colony on the Musashino plateau opened in 1694-1696, roads 6 ken wide (about 36 ft) were laid lengthwise and crosswise and the lots set along them.
  • On the Musashino plateau the rows followed canals in the south and ran freely in the north.
    In the south of the plateau the rows run with the canals dug along the highways; in the north, where the water came from wells, they run in a comparatively free direction.
  • On land reclaimed from the sea or a lake, the farmhouses stood in line on the dike or the old shore.
    On land reclaimed from the sea the farmhouses lined the dike; round a lake drained for new fields, many rows ran along the old shore.
  • On a river plain, the old settlements stood on the natural levee.
    On alluvial lowland the long-established settlements were sited first on the levee, a slight rise that drains well above the wet backswamp behind it, and the levee carried their dry fields too. A photograph on the same page shows one running in a curve beside a river's old channel. How the levee and the wet ground behind it divided a village's crops is at Dry fields and their crops (hatake).
  • Did a row of houses follow a fan's foot?
    A row of villages did. On the Shuso plain of Iyo, settlements often sit at an alluvial fan's head or, where the water that sank into the fan rises again as springs, at its foot, and at the foot of the Sekiya river's fan many of them line up in an arc. How the houses lay within each village is not said.
  • No page we read says which line was commoner, a road laid first or the land's own.
    The pages name rows along a road and along a dike, a levee and a fan's foot, and none counts one kind against another.

Villages in the hills of south China followed the same two kinds of line.

  • In mountainous southwest Zhejiang, villages tended to grow along rivers and roads.
    The traditional villages there form mainly clusters and bands, and tend to grow along rivers and roads.

Whatever its line, a row could stand on one side of it or on both.

  • A row's farms stood on one side of its line or on both.
    A road village is defined as a farming settlement whose houses stand along a fairly narrow street, on one side of it or on both; the linear village too has its houses on one side or both.
  • Santome's lots lay on both sides of its road, and so did Nobidome's.
    Santome's house lots, fields and coppice were set out in strips on both sides of its road, and the lots of the Nobidome villages of 1661 lay the same way along the Kawagoe road.
  • Shimotome, one of Santome's three villages, stood on one side only.
    It is a one-sided road village, its farmland across the road from its house lots, where house lots for second and third sons have since appeared.
  • No page we read says whether one side or both was commoner.
    The definitions and the Santome survey name rows of both kinds and count neither.

On the planned rows each farm's land was one strip, as wide as the farm's frontage on its road.

  • How wide was a farm's frontage on a planned row?
    From about 54 ft to about 240 ft on the rows measured. At Santome each household's strip was 40 ken wide by 375 ken deep, about 240 ft by 2,240 ft, and the Nobidome strips of 1661 ran about 400 ken (about 2,400 ft) deep. At Ome Shinmachi, opened on the Ome road in 1596-1615, a lot had only 9 ken of frontage, about 54 ft, with 33 lots on each side of the road. A farm at Ogawa fronted its road for 13 to 15 ken, about 78 to 90 ft.
  • Ome Shinmachi's narrow lots had wide front yards, said to be meant for a market.
    Its main houses stood on the road and its fields ran behind them in strips, and the front yards were made wide, it is said, because the village was meant to hold a market and become a post town - a farming row, laid out with a street in mind.
  • How much land did a farm on a planned row hold?
    Santome gave each household about 5 cho (about 12 acres), where the Musashino new fields, taken as a whole, gave about 1 cho (about 2.5 acres). No page we read gives the frontage of a 1-cho strip.
  • Neighbors on a planned row stood one frontage apart, lot against lot.
    The house stood at the road end of its strip, and the strips lay side by side, so neighbors stood about one frontage apart. At Santome in the 1980s, paths still ran sideways from homestead to homestead.
  • No page we read says how far apart the houses of a levee or dike row stood.
    The spacing above follows from the planned rows' strips; no levee or dike row's lots were measured.
  • On a planned row, each farm's field lay behind its house, and its woodland beyond.
    Santome's land ran back from the road as house lot, then field, then woodland; the planned road villages of the Kanto plain set each house's holding behind it in strips of equal area. The woodland at the strip's far end gave fodder, compost and fuel - the same form, the page notes, as the north German forest village.
  • On reclaimed ground, a farm's land lay near the house and fairly compact.
    Where the row stood on a dike, the farmed land was near the house and comparatively compact, no strip named.
  • No row is recorded with its paddy in strips behind the houses.
    The strips measured on the Musashino plateau are dry field, and no page we read describes a strip row of paddy.

A village on a river plain held paddy and dry field both, in shares that varied from village to village; none of the three pages below says whether its village stood in a row.

  • How much of a lowland village's land was dry field?
    From about a fifth to more than half, in three villages of the Kanto plain. Nishikata, a Saitama lowland village, was assessed in 1825 on about 118 cho (about 290 acres) of paddy and 30 cho (about 74 acres) of dry field, dry field about a fifth of the whole, and its own records count some 44 cho (about 108 acres) of its paddy as waterlogged ground. Kumehara was assessed in 1698 on about 8 cho (about 20 acres) of paddy and 11 (about 27 acres) of dry field, dry field nearly three-fifths.
  • Shimomaruko, long called a typical paddy village, held as much dry field as paddy.
    The village lay on the Tama River's floodplain and has long been described as a typical paddy village, yet it held about as much dry field as paddy by the Genroku survey (1688-1704), and more after the Kyoho survey (1716-1736).
  • No page we read says whether a row farm's dry field lay in a strip behind its house.
    The three villages' pages give the dry field's share, but not whether it lay behind each house lot on a paddy-plain row, how deep, or which farms of a row held it.

Only Santome, a planned colony, had its farms counted.

  • How many farms did Santome hold?
    Between 172 and 180, by two counts. The town's history gives 180 homesteads in its three villages at the survey of 1696 - 91, 40 and 49; a survey paper gives 172 at the opening, 83, 40 and 49.
  • Did a big colony grow one long row, or many?
    Many, on this record's reading. Santome covered 33 cho east to west by 20 north to south (about 2.2 by 1.4 miles) on roads laid like the squares of a go board, so a big colony grew more roads, each with its own row, rather than one row doubling back or a second rank of farms behind the first.
  • No page we read says how long a row ran where it grew by itself.
    The rows measured above are all planned colonies.

On the dry plateau a row's farms shared their water.

  • Santome's farms shared deep wells.
    The Musashino plateau is poor in water and its water table deep, so Santome first dug 11 wells and shared them, adding more later; one at Nakatome reaches 25 m (~82 ft) down.
  • No page we read says whether the farms of a row on lower ground dug their own wells or shared them.
    The pages read tie shared wells to the water-poor Musashino plateau alone.

A row farm kept its grove like any other farm, and where its road ran on its north, the record shows two answers.

  • On the Toyama plain, a street village's farms kept windbreaks as the plain's scattered farms did.
    A geographer described it so in 1934.
  • Santome's homesteads were planted round with bamboo, oak and zelkova.
    The zelkova was raised as building timber and never cut but for the house's own need.
  • As a rule a Santome homestead was covered by its grove, though by the 1980s the groves had much thinned.
    So a survey of Santome's homesteads found them in the 1980s.
  • In the 1980s, zelkova and white oak lined Santome's road side as an avenue.
    On the road side zelkova and white oak were planted, making an avenue. In Kamitome great zelkova trees, 2 to 3 ft thick, stood in a line. These are the trees standing in the 1980s, not dated to the founding.
  • At Ogawa, a farm whose road ran on its north still kept its grove north of its house.
    A farm settled there in the Kanbun era (1661-1673) had its road on the north, and the grove north of its house was mostly zelkova, rather thin. Its land ran from that road southward as house lot, field, then woodland.
  • A row of grove farms across the road let a farm grow its bamboo on the south, its owners said.
    The Ogawa farm's bamboo grew all along its south side and was sold to basket makers: across the road to the north stood a row of farms with groves, so no bamboo was needed on that side as a windbreak. At Minami-cho, on the old Yanagikubo new fields, another farm south of its road had its bamboo on the south, which the survey ties to the row of grove farms across the road.
  • One farm kept a single line of white oak and zelkova along its road.
    The same grove survey found the two trees set alternately in a single line along the road north of its main house.
  • A Santome main house faced south, its work buildings round its front yard.
    So the same survey of Santome found them in the 1980s.
  • Some houses turned to face their road.
    At Yanagikubo a house faced east, its entrance path opening from the road on the east and lined with zelkova.
  • No page we read says where a farm with its road on the north had its gate.
    Nor does any say whether the farm reached its road through its grove or round it.
  • No page we read says which side a levee or dike row's farms kept their groves on.
    The groves the pages place all stand on planned rows.

In the Nara basin, the houses of a street village and of a clustered village faced different ways.

  • In Asuka, a street village's houses faced the street, and a clustered village's faced south.
    In a clustered village every main house had its roof ridge east-west, which the authors read as facing south, although its main street runs north-south; in two villages strung along a north-south street, about eight houses in ten were turned to the street.
