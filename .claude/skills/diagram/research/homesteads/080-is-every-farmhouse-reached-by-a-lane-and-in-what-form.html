Is every farmhouse reached by a lane, and in what FORM?

Answer: access is decisive (implement it); its FORM has two supportable shapes (make it a knob).

Decisive: a house in a nucleated cluster IS reached by a way. The Chinese material, as this page has it, holds that a village plan laid out as a gridiron of narrow lanes is the most efficient compact form, and that every house in the nucleated village is reached through that interconnected system of lanes and alleys. This is not a planner's ideal imposed after the fact; it is what compactness is FOR. The lanes are also socially live rather than purely circulatory - the narrow lateral ones are taken over as semi-private space by the adjoining house, which is why they are narrow, irregular, and sometimes barely more than the gap between two walls.

Two supportable forms for delivering that access. Both are attested, and neither dominates:

  1. Alleys off the spine. Narrow lateral lanes branch from the through-lane between house plots and run back to serve the rank behind. This is the Chinese gridiron-of-lanes form, and the one whose laterals get colonized as semi-private space by the houses they pass.
  2. A back lane. A way parallel to the main street, behind the plots, serving their rear. This is documented as a planned-village device - "back lanes on each side of the main street which, together with the main street itself, provides a rectangular framework" - and it typically "divided the village from the main agricultural area", i.e. it doubles as the field-ward edge. A traditional Manchu village in northeast China is NOT evidence for the back lane: there the ground behind the lots was opened by a state resettlement in about 1968, and the older row is described as courtyard joined to courtyard along the street. What that village does show is a village of this kind having more ways than its main street - two or three running east and west, supplemental lanes crossing them north and south, and blind alleys of 2 to 4 m reaching the lots - so a lane web is not a purely European shape, even though a lane serving the REAR of a row is not attested there.

Note what distinguishes them: a back lane implies PLANNING (someone laid the framework out at once, and the plots are regular), while alleys off a spine imply ACCRETION (each household cut its own way to the road, and the result is irregular). A Rokugani hamlet can plausibly be either, so this is exactly the axis the project wants varied between maps rather than settled once.

Therefore: every farmhouse is served by a way, and which of the two forms a settlement takes is rolled per map.

The GM asked the question the other way round (2026-08-27). Looking at Inashiro: "a bunch of random scattered lanes strewn about without much rhyme or reason ... a short section of lane, between three farmhouses. It does not really connect to anything on either end ... I would have expected something like a lane leading to the reference hamlet and then probably just not even anything between the farmhouses. Is that right? Is that wrong? What does our research show?" The record above already answers both halves. (1) A lane to the hamlet and NOTHING between the farmhouses is a real form - it is the DISPERSED hamlet of the next section (散居村 sankyoson, 散村 sanson in the general term - each steading reached from the field paths, no village street, in this page's reading of the form), and it is a knob value, not a correction. (2) For a NUCLEATED cluster, which is what Inashiro rolled, the record is decisive the other way: "every house in the nucleated village is accessible via the INTERCONNECTED system of narrow lanes and alleys" - the lanes are the point of compactness, and they are one network. So the research says the FORM on Inashiro is right: the lanes are one network, and a junction is where two treads MEET. A lane that runs along a garden fence to reach its junction belongs to that network too, because the plot fronts the lane; the 7 ft gap the map leaves between a lane and a garden fence is a drawing convenience, not a finding.
