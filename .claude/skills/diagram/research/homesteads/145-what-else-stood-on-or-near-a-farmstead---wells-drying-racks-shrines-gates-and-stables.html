What else stood on or near a farmstead - wells, drying racks, shrines, gates and stables

Sources: ido-jawiki, hasa-jawiki, jawiki-yashikigami, nakasendoway-dosojin, dosojin-jawiki (the stone at a village's entrance), jaanus-yashikimon, magariya-jawiki, boso-no-mura-kazusa

The counted inventory - how many outbuildings of each kind a household had, from the one survey that counted them - is What stood on a farmstead - the inventory, with numbers. This question is the rest of the record: what stood on or near a farmstead that the count does not settle, and which of it belongs to a commoner's plot at all.

The rest of the record: shared wells were common, 「because the cost of digging a well was high ... jointly owned wells were numerous」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「井戸の掘削費用が高額で ... 共同所有の井戸が多かった」) (ja.wikipedia 井戸) - the map's communal wells are the norm; the harvest drying rack (稲架, hasa) 「They are mainly, and in most cases, built in the paddies and dry fields after the harvest.」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「主に収穫後の田畑に作られることが多い」) - stands IN THE FIELDS after harvest, seasonal, some regions leaving the frame up (ja.wikipedia 稲架); the household shrine (屋敷神, yashikigami) is a small stone or wood hokora in a plot corner, N/W or N/E (ja.wikipedia 屋敷神) - attested, and the counted inventory (the question before this one) finds it RARE; dosojin and jizo stand on the road and at passes, jizo also "in the villages" (nakasendoway.com); and the stone at a village's ENTRANCE is attested: the dosojin was enshrined, as a stone monument or a stone image, chiefly at a village's entrances and exits, at passes, crossroads and the foot of bridges - the boundaries where disaster might come in with travelers (ja.wikipedia 道祖神) - so a map's village-entrance stone is a dosojin by the road where it comes into the village, not a stone on a farmstead; a gate (屋敷門) is a WARRIOR house's feature (JAANUS) - not a commoner farmstead's without a source; the magariya attached stable is a Nanbu/Tōhoku form from horse-breeding country, its stable often warmed from the kitchen stove (that the cold made it is this page's reading) - our separate byres are the temperate reading; a headman-class compound (Chiba museum's Boso-no-Mura reconstruction) had eight buildings: 主屋・土蔵・長屋門・馬小屋・納屋・木小屋・作業小屋・井戸 (main house, storehouse (kura), gatehouse (nagayamon), stable, barn (naya), firewood shed, work shed and well) - the top of the ladder, not the typical plot. Guesses, with no readable page behind them: persimmon planted in the gardens of every farming family from the late Edo (the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries, MAFF); firewood stacked behind a shed or along the yashiki's front wall (Chiba museum); straw ricks (waraguro) built around a pole to ~2 m and kept to spring; the goemon-buro bath as a standalone shed common among farmers; water mills RARE - a 1788 record counts 33 waterwheels on the whole Musashino plateau; kabata a spring-fed Shiga regional form; the communal village graveyard the Edo norm, the farmstead grave (屋敷墓) a Heian-Muromachi elite practice; chickens kept before Meiji mainly for time-telling and fighting, so a coop is uncertain.
