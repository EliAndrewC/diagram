The farmstead and what stood on it (yashiki)

A farming household's plot, its yashiki, held the main house and its barn inside the plot's enclosure; the house was set back behind the middle of the plot, and the open ground to its south was the work and drying yard. In one village of eastern Musashi, recorded house by house in 1824, most houses had a well and a privy outside the house, and many a firewood shed or a storage shed besides. Another village's count, read back to the last years of the shogunate, gives about one and a half outbuildings a household besides the privy and the retirement house, and a richer household more. A survey of 1972 counted more than four.

A hedge or an earthen wall usually ran round the plot. From the northeast to the Kanto the plots were fairly large, with many outbuildings, and some were bounded by a grove; going west they were smaller, and more often closed in by their own buildings.

  • The fullest count of a farmstead's outbuildings we found is a modern one, made in 1972 in three hamlets of Miyagi, in Tohoku.
    In July 1972 Sugiura Tadashi surveyed all 87 households of Asahi, Kita-Nagashida and Minami-Nagashida, on the terrace south of the Tagawa, a tributary of the Naruse river, and counted every roofed building that stood apart from the main house. He published it as "On the ancillary buildings of farmhouses in agricultural settlements: Miyazaki-machi, Miyagi", in the journal Tōhoku Chiri (Tohoku Geography) 25(3), 1973.
  • The 1972 figures are averages of buildings for each use, not shares of households.
    A building serving two uses was counted once under each, so the count is in effect one of uses. A household could also hold two of a kind - the non-farm households average 1.40 storage sheds - so each figure is an upper bound on the share of households that had one.
  • How many outbuildings did a household have in 1972?
    About four and a half: 4.38 a household over the three hamlets, 4.57 leaving out the 10 non-farm households. The author calls that surprisingly many for Tohoku, a region generally said to keep everything under one roof.
  • In the 1972 count's oldest houses, the privy was the commonest outbuilding, then the firewood shed and the straw shed.
    The paper gives the mean number of buildings a household by use. For the houses whose main house was built in or before 1944, the oldest in the survey, the privy comes first at 0.87 (the privy's own section is Farm privies and their night soil (benjo)). Then come the firewood shed at 0.76, the straw shed at 0.68, the barn at 0.58, the work shed at 0.55, the livestock shed at 0.32, the storehouse, the compost shed and the pickle shed at 0.24 each, the coop at 0.16 and the shed for the rice-drying racks at 0.13.
  • Over all its houses, the 1972 count found a storage shed, a bath shed, a coop and a well house on fewer than half the farms each.
    General storage at 0.48 a household, the bath shed at 0.29, the coop at 0.28 and the well house at 0.19. A bran shed and a bird shed are rarer, and a charcoal shed is 0.01, none among the houses built by 1944.
  • In the 1972 count's oldest houses, the livestock, the bath and the privy were often inside the main house.
    Of the 38 houses built by 1944, 60.5% kept their livestock inside the main house, 52.6% their bath and 31.6% their privy.
  • The 1972 count is of a snowy district, and of modern farms.
    Some 5 km (~16,000 ft) further inland, at Sabusawa, 121 days of snow cover and a greatest depth of 145 cm (~5 ft) are recorded, and the author sets his hamlets against the single-roofed houses usual in Tohoku. Its figures show the shape of a farmstead's outbuildings, and how many there could be, not how many there were before 1868.

Two village records from near Edo, now Tokyo, give the older picture: one drawn up in 1824, and a count of 1885 that its historian reads back to the last years before 1868.

  • In 1824, in the Edo period, most houses of Hasuda village had a well and a privy outside the main house.
    A house-by-house record of Hasuda, in the low wet ground of eastern Musashi, drawn up in 1824, covers its 76 households, the temples set apart. Every house had a wide earth floor for the farm's work, and most had a well and a privy outside the main house. The Koshigaya city history, which reads the record, takes the farmhouses of its own district to have been probably the same.
  • Many of Hasuda's houses in 1824 had a firewood shed or a storage shed of their own.
    They stood apart from the main house on the plot (the firewood's own section is Where was the firewood stacked, and how big was the pile?).
  • In 1824, 50 of Hasuda's 76 houses had a stable, most of them a wing of the house.
    Most were an L-shaped wing off the earth floor, the form called magariya. Whether the stables held horses is not known.
  • Twenty-one Hasuda houses had a bath at the far end of the stable wing.
    The 1824 record shows it beyond the stable.
  • Does Kakimochi village's count of 1885 show its farms before 1868?
    Roughly, its historian says. The Oamishirasato town history, in Chiba, prints every building of Kakimochi's 16 households from a count of 1885. Its historian says the table cannot simply be taken as the Edo houses, but that since houses are not rebuilt often it roughly shows the farmhouses of the bakumatsu.
  • In Kakimochi village, counted in 1885 and read back to the Edo period, a main house and a privy were the basic farmstead.
    A retirement house, a nagaya, a stable, a wood shed or a storehouse was added as needed, the town history says.
  • The more outbuildings a Kakimochi household had, the larger its main house.
    The powerful farmers who served as village officials owned such houses.

Kakimochi's table gives, of its 16 households:

  • a privy: all 16;
  • a stable: 8;
  • a retirement house, the old couple's own roof: 8 (see Households: how many live in a house, and under how many roofs (ie));
  • a wood shed: 6 households, 7 sheds, from 1.5 by 1.5 ken (about 9 by 9 feet) to 5 by 2 ken (about 30 by 12 feet), three of the seven 4 by 2 ken (about 24 by 12 feet);
  • a storage shed: 3, each about 4 by 2 ken;
  • a nagaya: 3;
  • a storehouse: 2, each 2 by 3 ken (about 12 by 18 feet).
  • How many outbuildings did a household have before 1868?
    About one and a half besides the privy and the retirement house. Three of Kakimochi's households had nothing but the main house and the privy, and the 16 held 23 outbuildings among them. It is one village's count, made in 1885 and read back to the Edo stock.
  • About one Kakimochi household in three had a storage shed or a nagaya.
    Six of the 16 had one.
  • A village headman's homestead, planned in 1849 in the Edo period, had eight buildings scattered over its plot.
    The same history prints the 1849 plan of the Ohara family, headmen of Seinagoya village: a gatehouse with a storage room, and a barn, a stable, a shed, a storehouse, a miso store, a firewood barn and a privy over the plot. It says an ordinary farmer's house was small, just enough for the day's living and work. How a headman's homestead differed from a plain one is at How did a rich farmer's homestead differ from a plain one?
  • A village headman's farmstead, rebuilt at a museum in Chiba, has eight structures.
    The Boso-no-Mura museum rebuilds a farmhouse of the village-headman class with a main house, an earth-walled storehouse, a gatehouse, a stable, a barn, a firewood shed, a work shed and a well. It stands at the top of the ladder, not for the ordinary plot.
  • Neither village history names a manure or compost shed.
    The Hasuda and Kakimochi accounts list no building for manure, compost or ash (the heap's own section is Where did the manure heap stand, and what went into it?).
  • A Japanese encyclopedia lists a cooking house, a bath, a privy, a storehouse, a work shed, a compost shed and a livestock shed inside a plot's enclosure.
    The Britannica entry on the yashiki names them besides the main house.

Some things a reader might look for on a farmstead stood elsewhere, and some that stood on it were rare.

  • Many wells were shared.
    A well could be one household's or several households'. Digging one was costly, and in many places the lie of the land made water hard to reach, so jointly owned wells were numerous. The wells a village shared are at Communal wells (ido).
  • The rice-drying racks stood mostly in the fields.
    The racks (hasa) were mostly put up in the paddies and dry fields after the harvest, and in some regions they were left standing nearby from year to year.
  • Where a household kept a shrine (yashikigami), it stood in a corner of the plot, often the northwest or northeast.
    Most were small shrines (hokora) of stone or wood. The 1972 count found them rare: 0.01 a household, and none among the houses built by 1944. The shrine's own section is Which farmsteads had a household shrine, and in which corner?
  • Stone guardian gods and jizo stood along the highways and near the mountain passes.
    The guardian deities stand most often on the lonelier stretches of a highway and near the mountain passes, and the jizo near the passes and in the villages too.
  • Boundary gods (dosojin) were set up in stone chiefly at a village's entrances and exits.
    Dosojin were set up, as a stone monument or a carved stone, chiefly at a village's entrances and exits, at passes, crossroads and the foot of bridges - the boundaries where defilement and disaster might come in with travelers. In Matsumoto, a city with more than 600 of them, most stand at a settlement's entrance, on the village boundary, and each of one district's three hamlets keeps its own, one of them beside the road at its entrance.
  • The gate called yashikimon belonged to a warrior's house, not a farmstead.
    A dictionary of Japanese architecture defines it as the front gate of a warrior's manor house. The gate this record finds on a farmstead is a headman's gatehouse (nagayamon), above.
  • In the old Nanbu domain, in what is now Iwate, the stable was a wing of the house (magariya).
    The Nanbu magariya stood over almost the whole of the old Nanbu domain, most often in its horse-breeding districts. Its stable projected to the south from the lower end of the main house, and was often laid out so that the warm air from the kitchen stove flowed into it and warmed the horses. That the form answered the cold is this project's reading.

What ran round the edge of the plot changed from one region to the next.

  • A plot was usually enclosed by a hedge or an earthen wall.
    Some households also dug a ditch against intruders.
  • On the plains, some plots had a grove on the north and northwest that also marked their edge.
    Some plots on the plains had a grove on the north and northwest, called igune in the northeast, kune in the Kanto and tsuijimatsu in Izumo. Kune means the line between two holdings, and the igune divides the inside of the plot from the outside. The groves themselves are at Groves of trees around farmhouses (yashikirin).
  • The word kune also names a hedge or a woven bamboo fence.
    It was in use in that sense by the late Muromachi or early modern period.
  • West of the Kanto, a plot was more often closed in by its own buildings than by a grove.
    From the northeast to the Kanto the plots were fairly large with many sheds; to the west they tended to be smaller, and closed in more often by their outbuildings than by a grove.
  • On the Izumo plain, a farmstead's pines were planted to strengthen an earth bank against floods.
    The Izumo pines were planted to strengthen an earth bank built round the house against floods, and before the Meiji era they enclosed the whole house.
  • In Okinawa, a house was walled in piled coral and limestone against typhoons.
    The traditional Okinawan house was generally walled in coral and limestone, against the typhoons.
  • No page we read says how common each kind of edge was, or how big a hedge was.
    No page we read counts how many plots were closed by each form, gives the height or width of a farm hedge or earthen wall, or describes a farm plot with nothing at its edge.
