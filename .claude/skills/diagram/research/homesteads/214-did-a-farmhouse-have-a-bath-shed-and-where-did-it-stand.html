Did a farmhouse have a bath shed, and where did it stand?

Sources: mizumaki-goemonburo, sugiura-1973-fuzoku (the rates, the indoor bath's corner), sugiura-1977-tohoku (the front yard, the rates village by village), mizu-no-bunka-11-furo (kept apart from the house), water21-furo (a hut of its own)

The bath shed is one of the farmstead's fixtures the GM asked to see drawn; the whole set, and how many of each the map draws, is at How our maps draw the farmstead and what stands on it (yashiki).

The bath shed - its use attested. Mizumaki museum: the goemon-buro "widely used in farm villages centered on self-sufficiency" (translated; original: 「自給自足を中心とした農村で多く使われた」); that it spread into western-Japan homes in the late Edo is a GUESS that no readable page supports. Sugiura: 0.29 bath SHEDS per household over all houses, and a bath INSIDE the house in 20 of 38 pre-1944 houses - two forms, a shed and a bath inside the house. How many farms had a bath shed differed from village to village: reconstructions of three Tohoku villages before the war put bath sheds at 80% of farms in Tono, 33% in Inawashiro and none in Shiokawa. Those counts are all of the twentieth century. Before 1868 a bath standing as a building of its own is not found; the bath was a room at the main house, in two or three houses in ten by the 1820s (see Before 1868, did a farm have a bath shed?). So the map draws no bath shed.

Where it stood. A prewar housing report, as Sugiura passes it on, says that in northern Miyagi the bath and the privy were usually put in the front yard of the main house. The same report makes their standing as buildings of their own a fashion of early Meiji; that is the one date the record has for a bath shed of its own, and before 1868 the bath was a room at the main house (see Before 1868, did a farm have a bath shed?). An architectural historian describes what Meiji-period housing improvement aimed at for bath and toilet: kept apart from the main house as unsanitary, sometimes joined to it by a corridor, sometimes standing alone outdoors - and, the interview adds, "farmhouses are like that". A memoir remembers a thatched farmhouse with neither bath nor privy inside, each in a hut of its own, while other farm families had both indoors. Where the bath was inside, in the newly built houses of Sugiura's Miyagi town it stood without exception in the northeast corner, where in the old houses the stable inside the house generally stood. So the shed has one attested seat, the front yard; a bath joined to the house by a corridor is a second form the same interview names, though whether its closing "farmhouses are like that" takes in the corridor as well as the shed standing alone the passage leaves open; a seat at the back wall or a flank is attested nowhere. Its size no readable page gives: 6 x 6 ft, a GUESS.

The rule the map follows: no bath shed standing on its own (a bath as a building of its own is not found before 1868 - see Before 1868, did a farm have a bath shed?). A bath, where drawn, is a room of 6 by 6 to 6 by 12 feet joined to the main house, beside its main door or at the far end of its stable wing, on 20-30% of homesteads - the sizes, the seats and the share are at Before 1868, did a farm have a bath shed?.
