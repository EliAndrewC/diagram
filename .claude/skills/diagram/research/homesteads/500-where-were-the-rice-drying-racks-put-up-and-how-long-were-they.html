Where were the rice-drying racks put up, and how long were they?

Sources: kotobank-hasa-nipponica (the drying methods, their regions, and where and how long a rack ran), hasa-jawiki (the rack's height and the living-tree posts), niigata-manganji-hasagi (a row of drying trees moved to a farm road in the 1940s), joetsu-yoneoka-hasagi (drying trees as an ordinary sight of Niigata)

A rack stands only for the weeks after the harvest, so a summer map draws none; this question is for the seasonal map, and for the modal.

Four ways to dry the sheaves, by region. Cut rice was dried in the sheaf before threshing in four ways. Laid on the paddy floor, a little of it everywhere. Stacked in heaps, from the Inland Sea coast to northern Kyushu, the Chubu and parts of the northeast. Hung on single stakes set in a line along the paddy's bunds, 1 to 3 meters tall (3 to 10 feet) by region, most of all in the northeast. And hung on the rack proper, the hasa, poles laid across posts, which dries the rice even where the paddy stays wet and was widespread wherever the autumn is rainy.

Where the rack stood: on the paddy, and sometimes by the house. Racks were mostly put up on the fields after the harvest, most a little above head height, a few tall enough to need a ladder. The tall racks of several tiers were built on the paddy, or the sheaves were carried home and the rack built around the house. In Niigata, alders were planted along the bunds at even spacing and their trunks used as the posts, and such drying trees were an ordinary sight of its paddy country. The long straight rows of drying trees beside farm roads that are photographed today are later: the best known was made in 1943-1945, when the paddies were consolidated and 26 farm households moved their drying trees to the new road.

How long. A single-tier rack could run as long as 100 meters (about 330 feet), straight, zigzag or bent into three sides; some regions built two or three tiers, and some built walls of ten to fifteen tiers. How many racks, or how many meters of rack, one household put up no page read says.

What this means for the map. The drying method is a knob with four attested forms, rolled per settlement: sheaves on the paddy floor, heaps, stakes along the bunds, or racks. A seasonal map from the harvest into early winter draws racks as thin lines on the reaped paddies and along their bunds, up to about 330 feet long; where the roll gives tall racks, some stand in the farmyards too. A row of drying trees along a lane is a modern arrangement and is not drawn. The length of rack per household is a GUESS until the record finds a figure.
