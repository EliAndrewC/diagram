What stood on a farmstead before 1868, and how many farms had each building?

Sources: koshigaya-shishi-noumin-jukyo (the house-by-house record of Hasuda village, 1824), oamishirasato-choshi-kaoku (the building count of Kakimochi village, and a headman's homestead plan of 1849)

The survey that gives the drawn maps most of their outbuilding figures was made in 1972 (see What stood on a farmstead - the inventory, with numbers). This question asks what the record holds from before 1868, and what it means for the counts the map draws.

Hasuda village, 1824. A house-by-house record of Hasuda village, in the low wet ground of eastern Musashi, drawn up in 1824, covers 76 households, the temples set apart. Every house had a wide earth floor for the farm's work, and most had a well and a privy outside the main house. Many had a firewood shed or a storage shed standing apart from the main house on the plot. Fifty houses had a stable, most of them an L-shaped wing off the earth floor, though whether they kept horses is not known, and 21 houses had a bath at the far end of it. The Koshigaya city history, which reads the record, takes the farmhouses of its own district to have been probably the same.

Kakimochi village, counted in 1885 and read back to the last years of the shogunate. The Oamishirasato town history, in Chiba, prints every building of the 16 households of Kakimochi village from a count of 1885. Its historian says the table cannot simply be taken as the Edo houses, but that since houses are not rebuilt often it roughly shows the farmhouses of the bakumatsu. The basic form was a main house and a privy; a retirement house, a nagaya, a stable, a wood shed or a storehouse was added as needed, and the households that had them had the larger main houses and were the powerful ones who served as village officials. The table itself gives, of the 16 households:

  • a privy: all 16;
  • a stable: 8; a retirement house: 8;
  • a wood shed: 6 households, 7 sheds, from 1.5 by 1.5 ken (about 9 by 9 feet) to 5 by 2 ken (about 30 by 12 feet), three of the seven 4 by 2 ken (about 24 by 12 feet);
  • a storage shed: 3, each about 4 by 2 ken; a nagaya: 3;
  • a storehouse: 2, each 2 by 3 ken (about 12 by 18 feet).

Three households had nothing but the main house and the privy. Besides the privy and the retirement houses, the 16 households held 23 outbuildings, about 1.4 a household. A headman's homestead plan of 1849 from a neighboring village shows what a rich farm had: a gatehouse with a storage room, a barn, a stable, a shed, a storehouse, a firewood barn and a privy scattered over the plot. The same history says an ordinary farmer's house was small, just enough for the day's living and work. Neither history names a manure or compost shed.

What this means for the map. The GM ruled on 2026-09-28 that the maps drop anything attested only in modern times. The 1972 count found 4.4 outbuildings a household; Kakimochi's found about 1.4 besides the privy and the retirement house. So the counts the map draws are calibrated to the premodern ones, not to 1972, and the 1972 figures stay in the record only as the shape of a farmstead's inventory. The alternative, keeping the 1972 survey as the calibration with a cut for the warmer lowland, was not taken because the survey is modern. These counts come from one village each, one of them a count of 1885 read back to the Edo stock. A storehouse is about one farm in eight (see Which farmhouses have a storehouse?). A firewood shed stands on about four farms in ten (see Where was the firewood stacked?). A storage shed or nagaya stands on about one farm in three: 6 of the 16 households had one, and the 1824 record says many did.

The rule the map follows: besides the privy, a farmstead carries on average about one and a half outbuildings. A few carry none, a headman's carries several, and the storehouse and the sheds go to the larger houses first. The shares are a storehouse on about one farm in eight, a firewood shed on about four in ten, and a storage shed on about one in three. A shed is 12 feet deep and 24 feet long, the common size in the count. This is a calibration against the counts above, not a figure any one source gives.
