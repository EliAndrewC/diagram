Farmhouses (minka)

A farmhouse (minka) was the dwelling of one farming household. Its basic form was a rectangle under a hipped or a gabled roof, and most were small: in villages near Utsunomiya in the eighteenth century the commonest house had a floor of about 20 tsubo, some 700 square feet. The old farmhouses that survive are larger, about 45 to 54 ft long, 24 to 30 ft deep and 1.6 to 2.0 times as long as they are deep.

The houses of a village did not all face exactly one way: a survey of Okinawan villages found most of each village's houses within three points of the compass of one common bearing, and about one house in eight turned from it. An old Chinese ideal of a site puts a dwelling with its back to the hill and its face to the water, and between a farmhouse and its rice stood the paddy's bank, the bund, which was also a path.

  • Most farmhouses were small.
    When the shogun went on pilgrimage to Nikko, the villages near Utsunomiya drew up a floor plan of every house, to lodge his retainers. Those eighteenth-century plans show the commonest house at about 20 tsubo, some 66 square meters (~710 sq ft); many were under 10 tsubo (~355 sq ft), some only 4 or 5, and large houses were few.
  • A village record made soon after the Edo period puts the usual house at about 18 tsubo.
    In a table of the sixteen households of Kakimochi village in 1885, main houses ran from 6 to 45 tsubo, and seven of the sixteen had one of 18 tsubo. The town's history calls about 18 tsubo the usual size then, and takes the table to show, roughly, the farmhouses of the end of the Edo period, since a house was not rebuilt often. One village's table is weak evidence on its own.
  • Were the farmhouses that survive typical?
    They are larger than the commonest house of the counts. A house that lasted three centuries was most likely a big, well-built one - this project's reading - and three of the five farmhouses of about 1700 measured at the Nihon Minka-en open-air museum were headmen's. Two ordinary farmhouses measure 14.5 m (~48 ft) long by 8.9 m (~29 ft) deep and 13.6 m (~45 ft) by 8.2 m (~27 ft); three headmen's houses 15.6 m (~51 ft) by 8.9 m (~29 ft), 16.4 m (~54 ft) by 9.1 m (~30 ft) and 14.5 m (~48 ft) by 7.3 m (~24 ft).
  • How much bigger was a well-off farmer's house?
    The surviving houses cover about 106 to 149 square meters (~1,141-1,604 sq ft), 32 to 45 tsubo: one and a half to over two times the commonest house of the Utsunomiya villages (this project's arithmetic). A reconstructed headman-class farm of the late Edo period in Chiba has a house of 10 by 6 ken, about 60 by 36 ft and 193 square meters (~2,075 sq ft), and a hereditary headman's house of 1769 in Aomori is 30 m (~98 ft) long, with every work space under its roof. What else set a headman's house apart is at What makes the headman's house different?

Large or small, a farmhouse was longer than it was deep.

  • How much longer than deep was a farmhouse?
    The measured houses under one roof run from about 1.6 to 2.0 times as long as they are deep - 1.63, 1.66, 1.75, 1.80 and 1.99 (this project's arithmetic on their figures) - and the Chiba headman-class house 1.7. All of them are large survivors: how long a 20-tsubo house was against its depth, no page we read says.
  • Why was a farmhouse longer than it was deep?
    This project reads it from the plan types: a farmhouse grew by adding bays along its long axis, under the same ridge, and so got longer rather than much deeper, and a square house or one four times as long as deep was rare. The source says only that a plan that looks complicated is almost always the four-room type with rooms added to its raised floor, or a stable set into its doma. No page we read describes the growth itself, or gives a band of proportions.
  • A house built as two blocks could be nearly square in its main block.
    The Ota house, a headman's farmhouse of the bunto type, has a main block 9.6 m (~31 ft) long by 8.3 m (~27 ft) deep, with its doma a second block of 10.0 m (~33 ft) by 8.3 m (~27 ft) beside it.

Not every farmhouse was one straight block. Japan had three plans, each with its region.

  • A Japanese farmhouse was a straight house, an L, or two roofs side by side.
    A farmhouse under one roof is the straight house (sugoya). One with a wing joined on is called a tsunoya in the west and, in the north, a magariya (Iwate and around) or a chumon-zukuri (Yamagata, Akita and around). A house whose two roofs stand eaves to eaves and are used inside as one is the bunto house.
  • Each plan belonged to a region.
    The magariya is the farmhouse of northern Miyagi to southern Iwate, an L with the stable wing thrust forward from the front of the doma under one continuous roof; the chumon-zukuri is the farmhouse of Yamagata, Akita and Fukushima, with its wing projecting from the main house; and the bunto house stands from southern Kumamoto to Kagoshima.
  • The two-roofed house was not only southern.
    In the eighteenth century many stood near Utsunomiya, far from the coast, and two of the houses at the Nihon Minka-en museum are two-roofed: a headman's from Ibaraki and a net-owner's from Chiba.
  • Did the L mark a household's standing?
    In the Nanbu country of Iwate, a horse-breeding region, it seems to have: the L was common among the upper farmers, such as the village officials, so choosing it over the straight house is thought to have said something about the household's rank.

Chinese farmhouses took comparable plans, built around a court.

  • A Chinese farmhouse was a single range, an L, or three sides of a court.
    The three-sided sanheyuan - a main range (one long building) with a wing on each side and a wall across the front - is a basic Han Chinese house, the courtyard house with its front range replaced by a wall. The other forms beside it are the yitiaolong ("one dragon") and the danshenshou ("one arm stretched"), the second glossed by the source as a two-sided court; that these are the single straight range and the L of a main range and one wing is this project's reading of the names.
  • The three-sided court was found all over China.
    It was not one province's form.
  • No page we read counts how many of a village's houses took each plan.
    Each source names where a plan stood, in Japan or in China, but none counts the share.

Over the plan stood the roof, in one of three shapes.

  • A farmhouse roof was gabled, hipped, or hip-and-gable (irimoya).
    The irimoya is gabled in its upper part and hipped in its lower, and the basic farmhouse of the Edo period was a rectangle under a hipped or a gabled roof.
  • Where each roof shape stood was regional.
    Ordinary houses were roofed irimoya mostly in the hill country from around Kyoto to Kai, Sagami and Musashi. Gabled thatched farmhouses were common around Enzan in Yamanashi (old Kai), and from southern Kawachi into Nara the gabled thatch was held down at each end by a strip of tile. What share of a village's houses took each shape, no page we read says.
  • One museum's Edo-period farmhouses show all three shapes, every one of them thatched.
    Its farmhouses of about 1700 are hipped, gabled and hip-and-gable, the ordinary houses and the headmen's alike.
  • Thatch was one roof covering among several.
    A folk house was roofed with thatch of grass or straw, cedar bark or boards held down with stones, or tile of two kinds.
  • Was thatch a mark of poverty?
    Not on the houses we read of: the headmen's surviving houses are thatched too, so this project reads thatch as the ordinary covering of rich and poor alike. Some roofs now of metal were thatch first.

South China's houses are known for tile.

  • In one well-known region of south China, a house was white walls under dark tile.
    That is the look of Huizhou architecture.
  • The showy upswept "swallowtail" ridge is recorded on temples, mansions and ancestral halls.
    The page we read, on southern Fujian and Taiwan, does not put it on a farmer's house.
  • No page we read says whether a poor Chinese farmer's house was thatched or tiled.
    The pages that would say could not be read.

A farmhouse faced south or near it, toward the yard where its household threshed and dried its grain (see Threshing and drying yards at farmhouses (niwa)) and where the sun fell (see Sunlight and shade on the farm). But no two houses stood at quite the same bearing.

  • How far did a village's houses stray from one bearing?
    A survey of the houses of 27 Okinawan villages on slopes noted which of the sixteen points of the compass each main house faced. Most were spread over the village's commonest bearing and the point either side of it, the neighboring bearings arising where the roads curve; every other house faced right, left or back from that spread. Of all the houses, 87% faced within the spread, 11% were turned to the right, and only 1% each faced left or back - much as in the same authors' count of villages on flat ground.
  • Three points of the compass span some 67 degrees.
    So a house could stand as much as about 30 degrees from its neighbors' common bearing and still count as facing with them, and about one house in eight faced a different way altogether (this project's arithmetic on the survey's figures).
  • On a slope, the houses turned toward the south rather than down the slope.
    In one village at the foot of hills, on a slope falling to the northeast, 15 of 21 houses faced southeast and 3 south-southeast, turned a quarter away from the fall of the ground toward the south.
  • No survey we read measures a house's bearing to the degree.
    The survey counts by compass point, so how the houses spread inside one point is not measured.
  • Did a village's houses face different ways before Japan began to modernize in 1868?
    The count is of one island region's houses, in a paper of 1985, and no count of house bearings from before 1868 was found, in Japan or in China; no excavated farmstead's bearing was read. What is attested from before modern times is the cause the survey gives for the smaller turns, streets curving along the slope.
  • A planned Okinawan village, laid out in 1736 under the Ryukyu kingdom, had curving streets by the time of its land survey.
    Planned villages of rows of house plots are said to have spread across southern Okinawa and the islands beyond it under the Ryukyu kingdom from the eighteenth century. One of them, Maekawa, was allowed to move to its present site in 1736 and stands on a gentle slope facing south. On cadastral maps thought to date from the time of the land survey project, which show the village before its house plots multiplied and were subdivided, its streets curved widely and its blocks were bow-shaped.
  • Why did its streets curve?
    Most of its blocks were one plot deep north to south and three to seven across, and the authors suppose the streets were laid along the contours of the slope, the plots kept narrow north to south so that no plot rose much from front to back.
  • Were those streets laid before 1868?
    The paper does not say which were: the villagers moved in by stages and their numbers grew, and by their own account the blocks were extended first uphill and later downhill, onto steeper ground.
  • Did its houses turn with its streets?
    That they did is a GUESS: the paper says nothing of which way the houses faced.

In front of or beside the house lay the household's rice, with the bund along the paddy's edge.

  • How close did a farmhouse stand to its paddy?
    Close up against it, as this project reads the old Chinese ideal of a site, with its back to the hill and facing the sun and the water, where the hills encircle and the water embraces. The source's water is a watercourse; taking the paddy as the water a farmhouse faces is this project's reading.
  • The bund between the paddies was a path as well as a bank for the water.
    Paddy levees were built and kept to hold water in the paddies and to let people pass and carry their tools, and farmers kept their grass down by mowing or with weedkiller. How wide a Japanese bund was is at Bunds between the paddies (aze).
  • Elsewhere, a bund between rice plots was built low and narrow.
    Some early Korean paddies on flat ground were divided by bunds about 10 cm (~4 in) high, and a modern rice-growing guide tells a farmer to build bunds no wider than 50 cm (~20 in) and no taller than 30 cm (~12 in) around the field.
  • A bund built to keep fish in a paddy was heavier.
    A manual of fish-farming in rice fields wants such a bund at least 25 cm (~10 in) high and recommends 60 cm (~2 ft), and describes a trial plot at Kipopo, in Zaire, whose bunds were 60 cm (~2 ft) high on a base of 180 cm (~6 ft).
  • No page we read gives how far a farmhouse's eaves reached past its wall.
    The pages we read say only that a farmhouse's eaves were deep.
