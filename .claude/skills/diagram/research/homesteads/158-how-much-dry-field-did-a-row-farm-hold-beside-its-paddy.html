How much dry field did a row farm hold beside its paddy?

Sources: koshigaya-nishikata-kyuki, miyashiro-kumehara-1698 and hayashi-2007-shimomaruko (the paddy and dry field of three lowland villages)

A row's frontages, and where its fields lay on the planned rows, are at How wide was a farm in a row?; what the map draws for a row is at What does the map draw for a row village?. This question asks how much of a row's land beside the rice was dry field.

How much of a lowland village's land was dry field

It varied widely. Nishikata, a Saitama lowland village, was assessed in 1825 on about 118 cho of paddy and 30 cho of dry field, dry field about a fifth of the whole, and its own records count some 44 cho of its paddy as waterlogged ground. Kumehara, a small village of the same lowland, was assessed in 1698 on about 8 cho of paddy and 11 of dry field, dry field nearly three-fifths. Shimomaruko, on the Tama River's floodplain and long described as a typical paddy village, held about as much dry field as paddy by the Genroku survey (1688-1704), and more after the Kyoho survey (1716-1736).

Where it lay

On a river plain the houses and the dry fields took the natural levee and the paddy the wet ground behind it (Where dry crops go). None of the three villages' pages says whether the dry field lay in a strip behind each house lot on a paddy-plain row, so that is a GUESS.

What it means for a map

The dry-field share is a knob with attested values from about a fifth of a village's land to more than half (accurate for those three villages). Where a row village's farms stand on both sides of a street laid first, the map draws each far-row farm a strip of dry field one lot wide and three lots deep behind it and the near farms none; on Kashikawa that comes to 28.7 acres of dry field against 27.5 of paddy, about half, inside the attested range. The strip behind the house and its depth stay a GUESS, and so does giving the far farms all of it.
