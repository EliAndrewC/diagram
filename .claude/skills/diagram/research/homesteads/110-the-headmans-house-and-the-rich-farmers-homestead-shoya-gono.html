The headman's house and the rich farmer's homestead (shoya, gono)

In Edo-period Japan a village headman (shoya) was a farmer by status, of a stratum above the other villagers, who answered for the village to its lord: the tax came to the headman first, the village's business was done in the headman's house, and the other village officials met there to share out the year's tax. In most villages an old family held the office by inheritance. The house was large because the office needed it: it lodged the lord's officials when they toured the villages. In one village's table its leading houses were more than twice the usual main house, and a reconstructed headman-class farm and a surviving headman's farm stand on plots of over half an acre, with a gatehouse and one or two storehouses.

The rich farmer-landlords (gono), who rose in the villages from the middle of the eighteenth century, often held village office, and the two of their homesteads we read are larger, walled or gated compounds. The village often met in the house of the headman or another village official.

  • A headman's house lodged the lord's officials, so it was kept large.
    A town history from Chiba says that in a house that served as headman, the residence itself became the lodging of the lord's officials when they toured the villages, so such a family necessarily kept a large one.
  • How much larger were a village's leading houses than an ordinary one?
    About two to two and a half times, in the one village's table we read. In its table of 1885, in the Meiji period, nearly half the households had a main house of about 18 tsubo, the usual size then, and the leading households 40 and 45 - about 60 square meters (~646 sq ft) against 130 to 150 (~1,399-1,615 sq ft), a tsubo being about 3.31 square meters (~36 sq ft). How big an ordinary farmhouse was, and how much bigger a well-off one, is at How big was an ordinary farmhouse, and how much bigger was a well-off one?
  • The largest houses in that table belonged to the village officials of the late Edo period.
    The town history argues that the table of 1885 roughly shows the farmhouses of the end of the Edo period, since a house was not rebuilt often, and that the houses with the most outbuildings and the largest main houses belonged to the powerful farmers who served as village officials then.
  • One measured headman's house of the Edo period, in Gifu, is about 12.5 by 6.5 ken (~75 by 39 ft).
    Built in 1805 and the residence of a family that served as the district's headman, it has 291 square meters (~3,132 sq ft) of building area over two stories.

The office also set the headman's homestead apart in what it was allowed to build on its plot.

  • The office brought the right to a gate and a formal entrance.
    A village headman was a farmer by status but of a stratum a step above the other villagers: they could build a gate on their plot and a formal entrance step (shikidai) on the main house, and wear silk and leather-soled sandals as others could not. The sources give the right, not how many headmen used it.
  • In farming villages the gatehouse with rooms (nagayamon) was allowed only to farmers who served as village officials.
    A farmer's gatehouse had no guardroom, and the rooms either side of its passage served as servants' rooms, a barn or a stable.
  • The one headman's plot we read in full has an earthen storehouse among its sheds.
    The Ohara family, headmen of Kiyonakoya village, had in 1849 a gatehouse (nagayamon) with a storeroom attached, and on the plot a barn, a stable, sheds, an earthen storehouse, a miso store, a firewood shed and a privy.
  • A reconstructed headman-class farm in Chiba has eight structures on 2,500 square meters (~26,910 sq ft).
    Modeled on houses of the late Edo period, it has the main house, a storehouse, a gatehouse, a stable, a barn, a woodshed, a work shed and a well, on a plot some 164 ft a side if square. The page names no hedge or wall round its plot.
  • The Yamada of Sennan, hereditary headmen and rich farmers, keep eight structures on about 840 tsubo.
    That is about 2,780 square meters (~29,924 sq ft). The family boasted a village assessment of 1,600 koku, and its structures, all of the middle to late Edo period, are the main house, an entrance wing, a kitchen wing, a rice storehouse, a second storehouse, a front gate, a gatehouse and an earthen wall.
  • How many storehouses did a headman's farm have?
    One or two, on the two farms we read: one at the reconstructed Chiba farm, two at the Yamada's.
  • A grand headman over a whole group of villages had a far larger plot, with three storehouses and a granary.
    The Kashiwakura, grand headmen over villages assessed at 6,700 koku, have in Yamagata a plot of some 2,300 tsubo (~1.9 acres) with an east-facing gatehouse, two storehouses to the south joined by a corridor, a north granary for 4,500 bales of rice and a house storehouse behind.
  • How much more ground did a headman's farm take than an ordinary farmer's house plot?
    Five to nine times as much, as a degree and not a measure. The Nanbu domain in the far north had until its survey of 1737 allowed a farmer working ten koku or more a house plot (yashikichi) of three to five se - about 300 to 500 square meters (~3,229-5,382 sq ft) - and from then on at most three se on an upland plot and five on a paddy plot. The Chiba and Yamada farms stand on five to nine times that; the figure is this page's arithmetic, across two regions and between a rule and two headmen's farms. No page we read measures an ordinary village headman's plot: every plot measured belongs to a grand house.

The house was also where the village's business was done.

  • The headman's house was the village office.
    The headman did the village's daily business at home, and the other village officials met at the headman's house to share out the year's tax and the village's costs and to draw up the registers and petitions. The entry speaks of the house, not of a separate office building.
  • The village's records survive in the houses of the families that served as headmen.
    The records - land registers among them - were made village by village.
  • The tax rice came to the headman first.
    The village packed it into bales and held it for a time in the village store (gogura), and on the set day it went on to the lord's storehouse.
  • The tax rice waited either in a village store or in the headman's own storehouse.
    Not every village had a store of its own: several villages might share one, or a headman's earthen storehouse (kura) was used in its place. Until the shogunate ordered stores built at the villages' expense in 1789, after grain was found released improperly, the village officials' private storehouses were often used.
  • An Edo-period handbook puts the village store among the houses, mostly on dry-field ground.
    A handbook of 1794 puts it within the village's dwelling area, mostly on dry-field ground, or on the plot of a vacant house.
  • Were the village's registers kept in the headman's storehouse?
    Perhaps, but that is this page's reading, a GUESS. The pages we read put the village's records in the headman's house and its tax rice in the village store or the headman's storehouse, and none names the building the registers were kept in.

Who held the office is the nearest the sources come to where the headman lived.

  • In the setting, a village district's headman lives in its main village.
    Never in one of its hamlets.
  • The headman was most often from an old family of the village.
    A headman was sometimes chosen by election or agreement of the landholding farmers, but in most cases an old family of the village held the office by inheritance.
  • The houses said to have founded a village held its offices and first rights at its places of worship and over its land and water.
    Whether or not they truly founded it, they held village offices such as headman generation after generation - and where they did not, still spoke as the head house of several branch houses - and held the first place at the village's places of worship and in the use of its shared land and water.
  • Where in the village did the headman's house stand?
    No page we read places it at the village's center, at its oldest spot, beside its shrine or on its road. That the founding house, which so often held the office, is likely to be among the first houses built, and so near the village's oldest ground, is this page's reading, a GUESS, not a statement of any source.

A headman's family was often also one of the village's richest, and the rich farmers whose homesteads survive were often headmen.

  • The rich farmers (gono) were village landlords who rose from the middle of the eighteenth century.
    They grew with the spread of small-scale production for market, some out of older landlord families and some out of trade and production; the second kind mostly won their standing as village landlords through village disputes.
  • Many rich farmers also held village office.
    The encyclopedia says many served as village officials, and the Yamada of Sennan were rich farmers who held the headman's office generation after generation.
  • The two rich farmers' homesteads we read are walled or gated compounds with two or more storehouses.
    The Yamada plot has its front gate, gatehouse, earthen wall and two storehouses. In Oka, in Yamagata, the Kashiwakura families' houses stand side by side around the head house, ringed by black board walls some 1,000 m (~3,281 ft) long in all; one of those kin houses, on about 1,600 tsubo (~1.3 acres) joining the head house's plot, has nine buildings centered on its main house, front and rear storehouses among them, with 310 tsubo (~11,030 sq ft) of buildings. A plain farm has one main house and a few sheds (What stood on a farmstead - the inventory, with numbers).
  • No page we read says how many rich farmers a village held.
    The pages describe single surviving families.
  • In the setting, most landlords are merchants rather than farmers.
    So the farming landlord who lives in the village is the exception there.

The village's meeting was one of the things the headman's house was used for.

  • The village often met in the house of the headman or another village official.
    The village meeting was often held in the house of the headman or another village official.
  • In the Kinki region, villages built meeting halls (kaisho), often inside the shrine precinct.
    In the region around Kyoto and Osaka such halls were set up from early times, and they often stood inside the precinct of the village's tutelary shrine.
  • Villages also met in temples, shrines and prayer halls, and later in meeting halls.
    In older times the officials' house, or a temple, a shrine or a hall for chanting the Buddha's name (nenbutsu), served; some villages met at the festival's host house after the festival; and meeting halls gradually became the usual place.
  • The head of every household attended, usually twice a year.
    The members were the heads of the village's households, and the regular meeting was usually held twice a year, with more when something urgent arose.
  • The Edo-period meeting was made up mainly of the landholding farmers, and the smaller farmers seem to have joined later.
    The meetings of the Edo period were called by the village officials and made up mainly of the landholding farmers (honbyakusho); later, it seems, the small farmers and the landless came generally to take part as well.
  • No page we read gives the size of a meeting hall.
    The dictionary entries on the village meeting are silent on it.
