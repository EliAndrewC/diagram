Before 1868, how big did a farmhouse persimmon grow?

Sources: youyang-zazu-juan18 (the persimmon's seven virtues, long life and much shade among them), oomachi-saijogaki (an old persimmon's crown, measured today)

Where the persimmon stands and on how many farmsteads are at Why does a persimmon stand beside so many farmhouses?, which gives the crown the map draws, about 23 ft, from a modern horticultural reference. This question asks whether anything from before 1868 says how big the tree grew.

A long-lived tree with wide shade. A Chinese miscellany of the ninth century records the common saying that the persimmon has seven virtues, the first two "long life" and "much shade", with no nesting birds and no insects among the rest (translated from the Classical Chinese by this project). That is how the tree was thought of long before modern times: a tree kept for generations that shades the ground around it. It gives no size.

How big an Edo-period tree grew. The largest persimmon in Okayama, at Omachi in the town of Kagamino, said to be the largest in Japan, has an estimated age of 400 years, so it was growing through the Edo period; it stands 17 m tall, and its crown is given as 20.17 m east to west and 14.65 m north to south. That is the extreme, measured now, not an ordinary dooryard tree as it stood before 1868.

Not found. No record from before 1868 gives the crown of an ordinary farmhouse persimmon: no farm manual, no house-plan register and no picture read gives a measurement.

What this means for the map. The persimmon beside the farmhouse is attested before 1868, and its long life and wide shade are an old saying; its size is not. The crown of about 23 ft stays as a GUESS: a full-grown tree's spread from a modern reference, well inside the 20 m crown of the one Edo-planted tree measured, and no premodern figure to calibrate it to instead.
