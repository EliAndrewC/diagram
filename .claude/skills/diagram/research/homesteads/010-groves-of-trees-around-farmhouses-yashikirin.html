Groves of trees around farmhouses (yashikirin)

Across the farming country of Japan, from the Sendai plain to Okinawa, a farmhouse often stood inside a small wood of its own, the yashikirin or homestead grove. On the Tonami plain, where they are called kainyo, more than 7,000 farmhouses scattered across the plain each stand in one. A 1987 survey there found dozens of good-sized trees around each farmhouse, nearly half of them cedar (sugi), and every homestead kept a stand of bamboo as well. The grove was the household's store as much as its shelter: a farm manual of 1789 lists what it gave - shelter from wind and cold, a guard against thieves and a neighbor's fire, firewood from its branches, timber from thinning its core trees, and fallen leaves for the hearth and for manure.

First of all it was a windbreak, so it stood on the side the wind came from. On the Sendai plain the winter wind blows mostly from the north-northwest, often and hard, so the groves there stand on the north or west of the house and often leave the south or east open. On the Tonami plain the groves kept off the cold seasonal winds and snowstorms of winter.

  • Farming communities have had these groves for centuries.
    Our maps show the countryside as it was before Japan began to modernize in 1868, at the end of the Edo period, and the grove is far older than that. The Kaga (Kanazawa) domain, which ruled the Tonami plain, treated the homestead grove - written 垣根 (kakine, "hedge, fence") in its documents, though how the domain read the word is not known, and spelled kaine in village papers - as a stand of timber and bamboo under its control: a 1658 notice for the Tonami district put the cutting of the domain's reserved trees and bamboo around homesteads and on field ridges under permit. A 1750 document calls a grove kept thick a firebreak and windbreak, and in 1782 a villager whose rice tax fell short sold four large cedars from his own grove to his headman to make it up.
  • How many trees stood around a farmhouse?
    The best count is a 1987 survey of Kashima, a hamlet of 46 households on the Tonami plain. It found 1,542 trees with trunks at least 10 cm (~4 in) across - about 33 a household, which is this page's arithmetic on the two figures rather than a number the survey states.
  • What does that count leave out?
    Smaller trunks went uncounted. The survey also sorts the grove into four layers by height and finds 44, 76, 80 and 153 species from the tallest layer down, so beneath the counted trees grew an understory of saplings and shrubs rich in species. The survey also does not say whether its count takes in the bamboo stand every homestead kept. A typical grove, then, was some 30-40 mature trees with a bamboo stand and an understory beneath them.
  • Were the groves as large before 1868?
    The counts above are from 1987, and no Edo document read counts a grove's trees: the one number is the four large cedars sold in 1782, which is not the grove's total, so the 30-40 mature trees above have no earlier figure to set against them. What the Edo record does show is a grove kept thick, so the 1987 count is taken here to hold for the grove before 1868 - a GUESS.
  • About half the trees were cedar, and the rest were of many kinds.
    Of the 1,542 trees counted, 735 - 48% - were cedar, about 16 a household. The rest belonged to 83 other species, pine and persimmon among them. One household's grove held anywhere from 1 to 14 species, about 6 on average. A 2004 study of groves at Tonami likewise found cedar, persimmon and urajirogashi the most frequent trees.
  • How tall were they?
    11 to 28 m (~36-92 ft), about 15 m (~49 ft) on average.
  • How large would these groves get?
    No survey read gives the largest. On the Sendai plain a survey published in 2020 counted 356, 122 and 126 trees at three homesteads, bamboo not included - 201 a household on average; the 47 species it found in all include the bamboos and a palm. The passage does not say how small a trunk it counted, while the Tonami count took only trunks of 10 cm (~4 in) and up, so the two are not directly comparable. A big Tonami homestead's grove is put here at 100-200 or more mature trees, and 200-plus at the very top - a GUESS: the Sendai survey shows one homestead of 356 trees, but no Tonami page gives one so large.
  • How much ground did one homestead's grove cover?
    Roughly 6,000 to 28,000 sq ft, in the one Edo-period measurement read. A 1684 register of hill land in the Mito domain, quoted in a 2019 paper by Miura, lists homestead woods household by household - 30 by 10 ken at 1 tan, 9 by 9 ken at 5 se 21 bu, and 40 by 20 ken at 2 tan 6 se - and from their areas the author takes them for homestead groves (yashikirin). A tan is about 991.7 square meters (~10,675 sq ft), so the three woods come to about 10,700, 6,100 and 27,800 sq ft. The two whose sides come near their stated areas are about 60 by 180 ft and 120 by 240 ft; the 9 by 9 ken entry does not match its own area as printed.
  • Homestead groves varied in form, size and kinds of tree.
    An encyclopedia account of homestead groves says their form, size and species were not fixed but varied, so the three woods measured in one Edo-period register of 1684 are a sample, not a survey.
  • A forestry paper of 1910 describes the woods around a farmhouse on the Musashino upland, from cedar and bamboo by the house out to oak woods beyond the fields.
    Written some forty years after the Edo period ended, it puts cedar, bamboo, evergreen-oak and zelkova woods around a farm's main house and its other buildings, then the cultivated fields, and woods of konara oak on the outer edge. The oak woods lie beyond the fields, so they are not the homestead's own grove.
  • Where farmhouses stood close together, did the village keep a wood of its own among them, apart from each farm's grove?
    No page we read records one. The Japanese word itself, yashikirin, names the wood formed on the lot where the house stands: the grove is that homestead's own. No page we read gives a size for a wood the whole village shared among its houses, or counts its clumps of trees.
  • On the Tonami plain, the tallest trees stood on the south and west of each house, and the groves were thin at the east front.
    Tall trees - mainly cedar, with oak and zelkova - stood from the south face of the house round to the west. Hackberry and alder, with a little bamboo, stood from the west round to the north. On the east grew flowering trees, persimmon and fig. The east was the front of the house, with its entrance and its garden, and there the grove was thin.
  • Did the groves go all the way round their houses?
    In some regions they did, and in others they did not: which sides a grove took differed from region to region.
    • All four sides, on the Izumo plain. The pine groves there went the whole way round the house before the Meiji era, and afterward took a hook shape covering only the north and west, as flood damage lessened and building changed. The pines had been planted in the first place to strengthen an earth bank built round the house against floods.
    • Cedar around the homestead, in the Takada domain. An order of 1625 had farmers plant camellia and bamboo grass on the south side and cedar around the homestead. Read as written, it does not say the cedars closed a ring, and it plants the south side with something else.
    • Two sides, on the Sendai plain. When the plain's new fields were settled, the first lord of the Sendai domain urged farmers to plant trees in many layers on the north and west against the cold wind that blows down off the mountains in winter.
    • Three sides, on the Tonami plain - every side but the thin front, as above. Neither Tonami survey dates this planting.
  • Windbreak groves sit on different sides in different regions.
    Which sides are windward depends on where the damaging wind comes from, so the same reasoning gives a different grove elsewhere. In Okinawa more of a homestead grove's large trees stand to the north of the house than on any other side, then the east, then the south, because there the greatest threats are the monsoon winds from the north and the typhoons from the east - so there the grove faces north and east, where on the Tonami plain the tall trees face south and west.
