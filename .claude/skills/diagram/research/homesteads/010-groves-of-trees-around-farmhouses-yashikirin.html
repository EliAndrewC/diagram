Groves of trees around farmhouses (yashikirin)

Across the farming country of Japan, from the Sendai plain to Okinawa, a farmhouse often stood inside a small wood of its own, the yashikirin or homestead grove. On the Tonami plain, where they are called kainyo, more than 7,000 farmhouses scattered across the plain each stand in one. A 1987 survey there found dozens of good-sized trees around each farmhouse, nearly half of them cedar (sugi), and every homestead kept a stand of bamboo as well. The grove was the household's store as much as its shelter: a farm manual of 1789 lists what it gave - shelter from wind and cold, a guard against thieves and a neighbor's fire, firewood from its branches, timber from thinning its core trees, and fallen leaves for the hearth and for manure.

Above all it was a windbreak, so it stood on the side the wind came from. On the Sendai plain the winter wind blows mostly from the north-northwest, often and hard, so the groves there stand on the north or west of the house and often leave the south or east open. When the plain's new fields were settled, the first lord of the Sendai domain urged farmers to plant trees in many layers on those two sides against the cold wind that blows down off the mountains in winter. On the Tonami plain the groves kept off the cold seasonal winds and snowstorms of winter, and the east side was the front of the house, with its entrance and its garden.

  • Was the grove there before 1868, in the Edo period?
    Yes. The Kaga (Kanazawa) domain, which ruled the Tonami plain, treated the homestead grove - written 垣根 in its documents, spelled kaine in village papers - as a stand of timber and bamboo under its control: a 1658 notice for the Tonami district put the cutting of the domain's reserved trees and bamboo around homesteads and on field ridges under permit. A 1750 document calls a grove kept thick a firebreak and windbreak, and in 1782 a villager whose rice tax fell short sold four large cedars from his own grove to his headman to make it up.
  • How many trees stood around a farmhouse?
    The best count is a 1987 survey of Kashima, a hamlet of 46 households on the Tonami plain. It found 1,542 trees with trunks at least 10 cm across - about 33 a household, which is this page's arithmetic on the two figures rather than a number the survey states - and 735 of them, 48%, were cedar, about 16 a household.
  • What kinds of trees were they?
    About half were cedar. The rest belonged to 83 other species, pine and persimmon among them. One household's grove held anywhere from 1 to 14 species, about 6 on average.
  • How tall were they?
    11 to 28 m, about 15 m on average.
  • What does that count leave out?
    The survey counted only trunks 10 cm across or more. It also sorts the grove into four layers by height and finds 44, 76, 80 and 153 species from the tallest layer down, so beneath the counted trees grew an understory of saplings and shrubs rich in species. The survey also does not say whether its count takes in the bamboo stand every homestead kept. A typical grove, then, was some 30-40 mature trees with a bamboo stand and an understory beneath them.
  • How large would these groves get?
    No survey read gives the largest. A big homestead's grove is put here at 100-200 or more mature trees, and 200-plus trees across 31 species at the very top - both a GUESS, resting on no source.
  • Was a grove as big before 1868?
    No page from before 1868 says. No Edo document read counts a grove's trees: the one number is the four large cedars sold in 1782, which is not the grove's total, so the 30-40 mature trees above are a 1987 count with no earlier figure to set against it.
  • Which trees stood on which side, on the Tonami plain?
    Tall trees - mainly cedar, with oak and zelkova - stood from the south face of the house round to the west; hackberry and alder with a little bamboo from the west round to the north; and on the east, flowering trees, persimmon and fig. The east was the front of the house, and there the grove was thin. The tall cedars on the west and south stood against wind and snow.
  • Did the grove go all the way round the house?
    In some regions it did, and in others it did not: which sides a grove took was a regional custom.
    • All four sides, on the Izumo plain. The pine groves there went the whole way round the house before the Meiji era, and afterward took a hook shape covering only the north and west, as flood damage lessened and building changed. The pines had been planted in the first place to strengthen an earth bank built round the house against floods.
    • Cedar around the homestead, in the Takada domain. An order of 1625 had farmers plant camellia and bamboo grass on the south side and cedar around the homestead. Read as written, it does not say the cedars closed a ring, and it plants the south side with something else.
    • Two sides, on the Sendai plain, since the domain's first lord urged it - the north and west, as above.
    • Three sides, on the Tonami plain - every side but the thin front, as above. Neither survey page dates this planting.
  • Windbreak groves sit on different sides in different regions.
    Which sides are windward depends on where the damaging wind comes from, so the same reasoning gives a different grove elsewhere. In Okinawa most of a homestead grove's large trees stand to the north of the house, then the east, then the south, because there the greatest threats are the monsoon winds from the north and the typhoons from the east - so there the grove faces north and east, where on the Tonami plain the tall trees face south and west.

How our maps draw the grove

  • Our maps draw every canopy tree.
    Each crown is drawn at its real size on real spacing - a mean crown radius of 8.5 ft, about 13 ft from one trunk to the next, no crown seated under another (Forest density and crown size) - so a grove appears as the dense stand of dozens of trees the survey counted. Bamboo, whose stems are too small to see at this scale, is drawn by a convention (How is bamboo drawn). The dense stand is what the Edo record attests; its tree count is a GUESS set from the 1987 survey.
  • Which way does a map's grove face?
    Since the windward side changes with the region, each map chooses it as a knob. The maps put the deep stand on the northwest by default, as on the Sendai plain; a map with a Tonami-like wind puts it on the south and west faces. That the Tonami plain's damaging wind comes from the south and west is a GUESS read off its planting, since no page read names the wind's quarter.
  • How deep is the stand?
    Knowing how many trees stood in a grove still leaves how they were arranged - a single line along the house, or a deep block. No page read gives a grove's depth, so the maps draw the windward stand 1.57 house depths deep - about 44 ft at a farmhouse 28 ft deep, some two and a half crowns - sized so the whole grove covers about six times the ground the house stands on. That depth and that six-to-one ratio are a GUESS, calibrated on the tree counts and crown sizes above.
  • How many sides does a map's grove take?
    Two, three or four, chosen at random once for each settlement. On the record read, the sides were a regional custom. The GM ruled on 2026-09-29: because it does sound as if groves completely surrounding farmhouses was a thing, then that should indeed be a tunable knob. It seems like two sides is the minimum. Three sides would sometimes be the case, and all four sides would also sometimes be the case. Offered the split and the flood-ground rise below, the GM accepted both: Your suggestions sound great, so yes, please go with all of that. Both the percentage split and the flood ground adjustment.

The rule the map follows: a settlement's farmstead groves take their sides from one random choice per settlement, the same at every farm, because the shape is reported as a regional custom. Two sides half the time, three sides three times in ten, all four sides twice in ten. Two sides are the windward pair - the form reported in the most regions, and the only one with a general statement and a date, the Sendai domain's first lord's time. Three leave open only the front, as on the Tonami plain; the maps put that front on the side away from the wind, where the yard and the way in are, as the Tonami plan does - this project's decision. Four close the ring, as on the Izumo plain. Where the farms stand on flood-prone ground - fields of reclaimed low ground behind dikes, or houses on a dike - four sides rise to four times in ten and the other two keep their 5 : 3 ratio (two sides 37.5%, three sides 22.5%), because the Izumo ring's own stated cause was flood; a map may declare its ground either way. Every one of these shares is a GUESS: no page read counts how many farmsteads took each shape, on any ground or in any period. The windward sides are the deep stand; the other planted sides are a thinner band of lesser trees, as on the Tonami plain. That band is one tree deep: 17 ft, two mean crown radii (Forest density and crown size). Carrying the Tonami pattern round a full ring is a GUESS, and so is the band's depth, since no page read gives it. A grove on all four sides is broken once, at the middle of its front, for a way in about 12 ft wide: the break is a physical necessity, since the farm must be reached, and its place at the middle of the front and its width are a GUESS, since no old page read gives where in a ring its opening stood or how wide it was. The village's shelter belt is a different planting and not this roll: it stays on one or two windward sides (Does a shelter belt wrap the settlement?).
