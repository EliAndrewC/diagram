Where did landless and tenant households live, and in what?

Sources: kotobank-mizunomi (who the landless villagers were), kotobank-nago (the tenant who rented a house as well as fields), kotobank-kadoya (the dependents housed inside a master's enclosure), sukumo-shishi-kenchi (the cultivators with no house plot of their own), takizawa-sonshi-yashiki (where a domain let new houses go, and the least a branch house got), l7r-merchant-families (the setting's canon)

Who they were. In an Edo village the landholding farmers stood against the mizunomi, who held no assessed land: they were not counted as full villagers, took no part in village government, and lived as tenants, by migrant or day labor, or by a craft. A tenant of this kind rented fields; a dependent of the kind called nago rented the house plot and the house as well. In the setting, most farmers are tenants, so a tenant's house is an ordinary house there, not a poor one.

Many lived on someone else's plot. The dependents called kadoya took the name from the gate-hut on their master's plot where they lived. Many registered house plots held, inside the plot's enclosure, huts, kadoya and retirement houses beside the main house, where weak dependent farmers lived while the master's family lived in the main house. In the land surveys of Sukumo, in Tosa, many registered cultivators held no house plot; most were kin, retainers or nago of a powerful farmer, living in a separate building or a hut on the head household's plot, and in the village of Yamana only 120 of the 360 registered cultivators held a house plot of their own. The Nanbu domain, in the north, gave a nago no house plot as a rule.

A new house went to the edge. Nanbu's order of 1715 forbade a branch household to take its house plot out of the cultivated land, and allowed it on a vacant plot, at the edge of the fields, or along the roads at the foot of the hills. In Shiwa district the least a branch household was given was three buildings - a main house, a stable and a privy - as one hearth. A landless household with a house of its own is placed by no page read.

How small. The eighteenth-century count of house sizes near Utsunomiya, with many houses under 10 tsubo and some of only 4 or 5, is in How big was an ordinary farmhouse, and how much bigger was a well-off one? No page read ties those small houses to landless or tenant households by name.

What this means for the map. The poorer households take two forms, and both are attested, so it is a KNOB: a hut or gate-hut standing inside a larger farm's plot, drawn as a small outbuilding that is lived in, or a small house of its own on the settlement's edge, at the field margin or the hill foot. How many of a map's households go each way, and how many are drawn at all, is a GUESS.
