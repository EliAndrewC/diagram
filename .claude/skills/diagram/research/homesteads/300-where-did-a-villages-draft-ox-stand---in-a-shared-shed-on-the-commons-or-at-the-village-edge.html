This question asks where in the village it stood: out on common ground among the houses, where several households could reach one beast, or beyond the last house at the village edge.

What was found: the beast lived with its household. In the Okayama region before the modern era the cattle shed was mostly inside the house itself. In the mountain district of Mikata, in Hyogo, many households kept their cattle in a stall inside the house - about 4,000 such houses at the peak in the 1950s - partly, the local cattle museum says, because there was too little flat land to spare for a separate cattle shed. A general encyclopedia's article on the stable, by an architectural historian, divides a farmhouse's stable into two forms, the inner stable inside the main house and the outer stable standing on its own, and says that the inner stable, taking a corner of the house's earth-floored work space, was widespread across the country, though few in regions such as southern Kanto; it gives no date or count.

A household without a beast borrowed or hired one; it did not share a shed. In mid-seventeenth-century Bizen province more than 80% of the cattle and horses had been bought by the farmers who kept them, 14% were beasts placed in their care, and 4% had come through the domain. Not every household had one: from the early eighteenth century only about half the farm households of Bizen kept an ox or a horse at all - the same share as in Mimasaka - and fewer as time went on. Large owners lent their cattle out to tenants, and one headman's household of 1818-43 had cattle placed with borrowers as far away as other provinces. Where a region ran short of draft cattle at plowing time, it could hire them from another for the season: Sanuki took them from the mountain villages of neighboring Awa, whose grassland let them keep more cattle than their paddies needed, for one koku of rice in return, and at times as many as several hundred came. Every arrangement read ties the beast to one keeper - its owner, or the tenant or region it was lent to; none has several households keeping one beast in a shed in common.

What was not found. No page read describes a byre shared by several households on the village's common ground, and none a byre set at the village edge, under the trees planted against the wind or out in the grass beyond the last house. Both remain GUESSES. So the choice between the commons and the edge is not a knob: the record supports neither place, and it does support a third place, the household's own.

The rule the map follows: a byre is drawn in a homestead, against or close beside its farmhouse, and belongs to the household there, whether it owns the beast or has it on loan. Both of its attested forms are drawn, as a knob rolled per settlement: on the inner roll the byre is drawn against the farmhouse as part of it, since a stall under the house's own roof cannot be seen from above (a map drawing convention); on the outer roll it is a small shed standing on its own in the homestead near the house. The inner stable is the more common roll; how much more is calibrated liberty, since no page read counts them. No byre is drawn alone beyond the last house at the village edge. A shed on the common ground among the houses, reached by several households, is a GUESS kept only until the record finds it or rules it out.
