This question asks where in the village it stood: out on common ground among the houses, where several households could reach one beast, or beyond the last house at the village edge.

What was found: the beast lived with its household. In the Okayama region before the modern era the cattle shed was mostly inside the house itself. In the mountain district of Mikata, in Hyogo, many households kept their cattle in a stall inside the house - about 4,000 such houses at the peak in the 1950s - partly, the local cattle museum says, because there was too little flat land to spare for a separate cattle shed.

A household without a beast borrowed one; it did not share a shed. In mid-seventeenth-century Bizen province more than 80% of the cattle and horses had been bought by the farmers who kept them, 14% were beasts placed in their care, and 4% had come through the domain. Large owners lent their cattle out to tenants, and one headman's household of the 1820s and 1830s had cattle placed with borrowers as far away as other provinces. Where a region ran short of draft cattle at plowing time, it could borrow them from another for the season: Sanuki borrowed from neighboring Awa, at times as many as several hundred head. Every arrangement read ties the beast to one keeper - its owner, or the tenant or region it was lent to; none has several households keeping one beast in a shed in common.

What was not found. No page read describes a byre shared by several households on the village's common ground, and none a byre set at the village edge, under the trees planted against the wind or out in the grass beyond the last house. Both remain GUESSES. So the choice between the commons and the edge is not a knob: the record supports neither place, and it does support a third place, the household's own.

The rule the map follows: a byre is drawn in a homestead, against or close beside its farmhouse, and belongs to the household there, whether it owns the beast or has it on loan. The attested place is inside the house; drawing it as a small shed beside the house, so that a reader can see the beast is kept there, is a map drawing convention. No byre is drawn alone beyond the last house at the village edge. A shed on the common ground among the houses, reached by several households, is a GUESS kept only until the record finds it or rules it out.
