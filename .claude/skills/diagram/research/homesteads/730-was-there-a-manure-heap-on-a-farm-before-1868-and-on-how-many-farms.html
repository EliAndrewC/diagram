Was there a manure heap on a farm before 1868, and on how many farms?

Sources: chenfu-nongshu-juanshang (the manure house of Song China), env-hakusho-2008-nogyo-zensho (the Nogyo Zensho's manure house and heap), kodaira-shishi-hiryo (a village's compost in 1754), sugiura-1973-fuzoku (the 1972 count of compost sheds)

What the heap was made of, and where the map draws it, is at The farmstead's fixtures. This question asks whether it is older than the modern counts.

The heap is premodern. In Song China, Chen Fu's farming manual of 1149 says a manure house must stand beside every farm dwelling, low-eaved to keep off wind and rain, because manure left out under the sky loses its strength. In it a deep pit is dug and lined with brick, and sweepings, ash, chaff, broken straw and fallen leaves are heaped, burned and soaked with liquid manure. In Japan the Nogyo Zensho of 1697 tells the farmer to fit out a manure house to store night soil. It says to pile the dung of cattle and horses layer on layer, as high as a small mountain where there are many beasts. It is read here in a modern Japanese rendering by Japan's Environment Ministry. Ogawa village's record of 1754, in what is now Kodaira city, has leaves, barley straw and turf mixed with night soil, bran and ash for the dry fields. The city's historian says fallen leaves were made into compost.

Its share is not counted before 1868. Both manuals are advice, and they put the manure house beside every farm. Neither says how many farms had one. Neither premodern village count in the record lists a manure shed (see What stood on a farmstead before 1868?). The one count, Sugiura's 0.24 compost SHEDS per household in 1972, is modern, and the drawn share does not rest on it. The GM ruled on 2026-09-28 that the maps drop what is attested only in modern times. The heap itself is premodern, so the maps keep it. Its share of 40-70% of homesteads is a GUESS below the manuals' every farm, and no count of any date sets it.
