Place names that describe the land (chimei)

Many Japanese place names (chimei) describe the ground the place stands on, such as a valley or a hill, and this is most common in the names of small places and in old names. Two such words, tani or ya for a valley and sawa for a mountain valley or a marsh, each appear in about four percent of Japan's indexed place names. The valley word is mostly read differently in eastern and western Japan, and sawa names different ground in each.

  • Naming a place from its terrain was common, most of all for small places and old names.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on place names calls it one of the many common ways of naming, especially for small-scale terrain and for names given long ago: a valley is named X-tani, a hill X-ga-oka.
  • Many names' origins are disputed or wholly unknown.
    The same article says many names have several competing explanations with none settled, or can only be called entirely unknown, so a name is a cue to its ground, not proof of it.
  • The characters for a valley and for a mountain valley or marsh each make about four percent of Japan's indexed place names.
    A geography paper counts 20,422 names in 谷 (tani or ya, "valley"), 3.95% of the names indexed nationwide, and 21,746 in 沢 (sawa, "mountain valley or marsh"), 4.21%.
  • Names with the valley character mostly read it tani in the west and ya in the east.
    The 13,484 names that read it tani or dani (tani voiced after another syllable) are mostly in western Japan. Names that read it ya are commonest on the Pacific side of eastern Japan, where shallow valleys cut into uplands or low mountains, though 22% of them are in western Japan.
  • Names ending in sawa are almost all in eastern Japan.
    Of the names ending in sawa, 95.7% are in eastern Japan, counting as far as the four eastern prefectures of the Chubu region, and 97.5% with Toyama, Aichi and Gifu added.
  • Today sawa means a mountain valley in eastern Japan and a marsh in western Japan.
    The same paper gives the word's present-day meaning in each half of the country; it does not say what the word meant in earlier centuries.
