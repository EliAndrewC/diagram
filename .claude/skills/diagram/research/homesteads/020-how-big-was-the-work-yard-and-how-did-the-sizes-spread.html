How big was the work yard, and how did the sizes spread?

Sources: kitamoto-mushiro-niwa, yonekura-mushiro, tobunken-mushiro, irri-drying-floor, ndl-kokumori, kotobank-momisuri, inekari-jawiki, hasa-jawiki

The GM, on the map's own modal: "I see the threshing yard size is listed as a rendering convention. How large WERE these yards?" A figure of 100-300 tsubo (330-992 sq m) that is easy to mistake for the yard is the homestead LOT (a band the record once took from the Akishima city history, whose page did not load when read: farms need a wide front yard, so lots run 100-300 tsubo) - a real number that belongs to a different thing.

The size, as households themselves stated it. Kitamoto (Saitama) city history, folk volume: 「At harvest time straw mats (mushiro) were spread over the whole of the yard, and the size of the yard was often expressed by the number of these mats. It was related to the area under barley cultivation; ordinarily forty to sixty mats, and among them there were households that exceeded a hundred. Incidentally, two mats correspond to one tsubo.」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「収穫期には庭一面にムシロが敷かれ、しばしば庭の広さはこのムシロの枚数で表現された。麦の耕作面積と関係 していて、普通四〇〜六〇枚、中には百枚を越す家もあった。ちなみに、ムシロ二枚が一坪にあたる。」) - the yard was measured in straw mats, 40-60 usually and past 100 for a few, two mats to the tsubo: 20-30 tsubo (66-99 sq m) ordinarily, past 50 tsubo (165 sq m) at the top, and tied, in the history's words, to the household's barley acreage. Two independent lines agree: an Okayama museum records ~50 mats per farm, and the measured mat is 90 x 180 cm, so ~80 sq m; a yard at Kodaira, from a city-history page that did not render when read, is given as 70 tsubo (231 sq m) - a large Musashino holding, the upper end. No survey found tabulates yards as areas; these are the readable figures.

Kitamoto's yard follows barley, and this generator draws wet rice. The history ties its yard to the barley (mugi) acreage. Rice is field-dried on hazakake racks before it reaches the yard, so a paddy household might need less standing floor. The GM ruled on 2026-08-28: "I agree with option two" - the sourced SHAPE everywhere, the sourced SCALE appropriate to what the household dries. Working a rice yard out from the crop - the middle-paddy standard yield of 1.3 koku a tan (中田 石盛, chuden kokumori), the hulling ratio, and a depth to spread the unhulled grain - needs that depth, and the only one found is the International Rice Research Institute (IRRI)'s 2.5 cm, modern advice written for tropical farmers of today. How thick an Edo household spread its grain, and how big a rice district's yard was, no page read gives, so by the GM's ruling of 2026-09-28 against what is only modern, the maps do not size a rice yard from that depth. What there is for rice is the Okayama museum's count: about 50 mats a farm, kept for sun-drying the unhulled rice, which at two mats to the tsubo is about 25 tsubo (83 sq m), the middle of Kitamoto's band. A rice hamlet's yards are therefore centered at 25 tsubo, the same as the dry-field yard. Both counts are local memory with no date - they may be of the Meiji years or later - and resting the yard on them is a calibration the GM may revisit.
