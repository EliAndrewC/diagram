How big was the work yard, and how did the sizes spread?

Sources: kitamoto-mushiro-niwa, kamikanai-1771-houses, kikoba-kenchi, santome-shinden-allotment, yonekura-mushiro, tobunken-mushiro, irri-drying-floor, ndl-kokumori, kotobank-momisuri, inekari-jawiki, hasa-jawiki

The GM, on the map's own modal: "I see the threshing yard size is listed as a rendering convention. How large WERE these yards?" A figure of 100-300 tsubo (330-992 sq m) that is easy to mistake for the yard is the homestead LOT (Akishima city history: farms need a wide front yard, so lots run 100-300 tsubo) - a real number that belongs to a different thing.

The size, as households themselves stated it. Kitamoto (Saitama) city history, folk volume: 「At harvest time straw mats (mushiro) were spread over the whole of the yard, and the size of the yard was often expressed by the number of these mats. It was related to the area under barley cultivation; ordinarily forty to sixty mats, and among them there were households that exceeded a hundred. Incidentally, two mats correspond to one tsubo.」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「収穫期には庭一面にムシロが敷かれ、しばしば庭の広さはこのムシロの枚数で表現された。麦の耕作面積と関係 していて、普通四〇〜六〇枚、中には百枚を越す家もあった。ちなみに、ムシロ二枚が一坪にあたる。」) - the yard was measured in straw mats, 40-60 usually and past 100 for a few, two mats to the tsubo: 20-30 tsubo (66-99 sq m) ordinarily, past 50 tsubo (165 sq m) at the top, and explicitly sized by the household's cropped acreage. Two independent lines agree: an Okayama museum records ~50 mats per farm, and the measured mat is 90 x 180 cm, so ~80 sq m; a directly measured yard at Kodaira is 70 tsubo (231 sq m) - a large Musashino holding, the upper end. No survey found tabulates yards as areas; these are the readable figures.

But Kitamoto is a BARLEY district, and this generator draws wet rice. Its yard is sized by the mugi crop, which the household spreads whole. Rice is field-dried on hazakake racks for 10-14 days before it reaches the yard and is threshed in batches over days, so a paddy household needs less standing floor. Deriving it from the crop instead - 1.3 koku/tan (中田 石盛, chuden kokumori, the middle-paddy standard yield) -> 244 kg momi at an 80% hulling yield -> mats at the International Rice Research Institute (IRRI)'s 2.5 cm spread, batched - gives 55-100 sq m for a full cho and 35-65 for five tan. The generator therefore centers a rice hamlet's yards at 18 tsubo (59.5 sq m) and keeps Kitamoto's 25 tsubo as the dry-field figure. The GM ruled between the two readings on 2026-08-28: "I agree with option two" - the sourced SHAPE everywhere, the sourced SCALE appropriate to what the household dries.

The spread is lognormal, and it is right-skewed. No survey gives a histogram of yards, so the shape comes from what the cadastres do tabulate, and every one is right-skewed: Kamikanai (1771) gives a complete main-house histogram whose 31 commoner houses fit a lognormal of median 22.5 tsubo and sigma 0.46 in log space, with the headman detached at 3.1x; Kikoba's Genroku 検地帳 (kenchicho, the land-survey register) puts homestead lots at 20-100 bu with two peaks, at 30 bu and 50 bu (the page: 「Homestead lot areas are distributed between 20 bu and 100 bu」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「屋敷反歩は20歩から100歩の間に分布している」)「with 30 bu and 50 bu showing the peaks」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「30歩と50歩がピークを示し」); no comparable figure for great holders was found - the page's only "2倍" (twofold) concerns house COUNT, not lot area). Kitamoto's own band-and-tail implies sigma 0.35-0.45 - that convergence is what the drawn sigma of 0.40 rests on. A floor exists but is not zero: early registers carry a no-homestead class (無屋敷登録人, muyashiki torokunin), but by Genroku 「all peasant households, including those holding no fields, came to have a house plot」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「田畑を保有しない百姓も含め全百姓が屋敷を持つようになり」) - the landless simply occupy the small end, so the roll is floored at 8 tsubo rather than allowed to vanish.

The yard follows the household, but not proportionally. The proportional coupling is ADDITIVE - 「if the holding increases by about 5 koku, the tsubo count increases by about 10 tsubo」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「持高が5石ほど増加すれば坪数が10坪ほど増加する」) - so a 20x holder does not get a 20x yard (the sentence is from the same paper's paragraph on Kyoho-era Iwahara village; of Kamikanai itself the paper says the relation to holding size "is not marked", and holdings inside the village span landless to about 30 koku). The generator gives the yard the household's own deviation in log space, amplified and then perturbed by an independent positional draw: a large household is overwhelmingly likely to have a large yard, a mismatch is possible and rare - the GM's own reading of what the record implies.

One attested form is NOT skewed, and is a knob. A planned shinden colony issued every settler an identical homestead - Santome 1696, "the size of the house lots is an entirely equal allotment" (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「屋敷の規模はまったくの均等配分」) - so an "allotted" yard setting draws uniform yards. Two attested forms become a per-settlement knob.

What this means on the map. The yard is rolled per household from the distribution above, so every hamlet that dries rice has to find room for it beside the house.
