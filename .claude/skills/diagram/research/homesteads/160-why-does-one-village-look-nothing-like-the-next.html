Why does one village look nothing like the next?

Sources: tameike-jawiki, shizen-teibo-jawiki, kaison-jawiki, shuson-jawiki (a nucleated village's forms, and what they follow), sonraku-jawiki (the lump, the levee line and the road line), caoshi-zhwiki (the Chinese market that grew from a periodic rural one), jori-jawiki (the surveyed grid, and how often it was tilted), santome-shinden-jawiki (long-strip allotment); the geomantic siting question below; this project's reading of the drawn villages; no work is cited for the Chinese allotment grid

A village is built from a handful of choices rolled from the map's own seed, each carrying a rule about when that value is even possible, so that a combination comes out coherent instead of merely various. The choices are rolled independently rather than drawn as whole village "types": independent draws give the widest variety and make it natural to fix one thing by hand and roll the rest, and what keeps the result honest is the rule on each choice, not the bundle it came in.

What each choice rests on. WHERE the cluster sits: a village took whatever higher and drier ground, unfit for paddy, lay beside its paddy, and "back to the hill, face the water" says which way it turns (the question below) - but which corner of a valley that is depends on the valley, so the high windward margin is one case and not the only one (how common it was is a GUESS). A nucleated village takes different forms with its density, its ground and its roads. Its SHAPE followed the dry ground it had: a hill's foot or a terrace gives a lump, a levee a string, a field edge a crescent, two flanking patches a split village (the crescent and the split village are this page's extension of the attested two). Settlement geography names the attested ones: houses gathered irregularly in a lump, houses in a line along a natural levee or a spring line at a hill's foot, and houses in a line along a road. Its LANES followed its site: a spine along a ridge, a T where a spur met the field track, a Y where two approaches merged, a cross at a small market node, and a lane along the water in a stream village - these lane forms are this page's GUESS. In China the periodic rural market was such a node, and from the Song dynasty some grew into the 草市 (caoshi, "grass market"), a market quarter outside the city walls, mostly at a waterway, a ford or a post station. Its WATER entered from wherever the catchment could deliver it above the fields, which is not always the same corner, and a small village might have a chain of little ponds - the stacked pond is a named form - rather than one reservoir. Its PLOTS took their size and their regularity from how the land had been divided: tiny hand-shaped plots in fields that grew up organically (the shape a GUESS); rectilinear blocks where a survey had laid a grid over them - in Japan the 条里 jori system, squares 1 cho (about 109 m) on a side; and long strips where a planned new-field colony was laid out, each farmhouse along the road with its field and woodlot running behind it in a strip of equal area. A Chinese counterpart to the surveyed grid, remnants of an allotment system, is a GUESS. And the GRAIN of the paddy drifts off the compass and the fall line, because real valleys let it: even the surveyed grid, usually laid north-south and east-west, was not seldom tilted with the region or the ground.

The biggest difference between two villages is the settlement's FORM. Compared on eight coarse structural axes - which side and which kind rather than which pixel - two villages that share a water direction and vary only the internal detail of a shared archetype still look like one place twice. A pair could differ on four of the eight and still read the same, because a cross of lanes against a spine of lanes is a detail, while a ribbon against a blob is not. That is why the form of the settlement - clustered, strung along a line, scattered, or built on the water - is an axis of its own and counts as the largest structural read there is. The GM's own eye confirmed the calibration: a ribbon along its field margin with a drifted field grain, against a clustered blob and its geomantic pond, differs on five axes and reads as two places.

The rule the map follows (village tier): a village rolls its cluster's position and its shape, its lane skeleton, the position of its water source, its plot size and regularity, and the drift of its field grain from its own seed, and a map may fix any one of them by hand. A rolled value its own rule forbids is never drawn: a lane along the water needs water running ALONG the cluster; a split village needs room for two hamlets to read apart, so a hamlet never splits; a gridded plot pattern needs a surveyed field; water entering where a stream crosses the valley's edge needs a stream, and water drawn from a pond - in a corner, midway along a margin, or a chain of ponds - needs a pond; and a water source below the fields it feeds is refused outright, because water does not run uphill. A hamlet rolls none of these.
