Where did the village meet?

Sources: kotobank-yoriai (the officials' houses, and the meeting hall in the shrine precinct), kotobank-mura-yoriai (the temples, shrines and halls used, who came and how often), citang-zhwiki (the ancestral hall as a lineage's meeting place)

Often in the headman's house. The village meeting was often held in the house of the headman or another village official; in the Kinki region, around Kyoto and Osaka, halls called kaisho were set up from early times, and the kaisho often stood inside the precinct of the village's tutelary shrine. In older times the officials' house, or a temple, a shrine or a hall for chanting the Buddha's name, served; some villages met at the festival's host house after the festival; and meeting halls gradually became the usual place.

Who came, and how often. The members were the heads of the village's households, and the regular meeting was usually held twice a year, with more when something urgent arose. The meetings of the Edo period were called by the village officials and made up mainly of the landholding farmers; later, it seems, the small farmers and the landless came generally to take part as well.

In a south-China village, the ancestral hall. A lineage's ancestral hall served, besides its rites, for the lineage's meetings and feasts. Where it stood is taken up in Where did a south-China village's ancestral hall stand, and how big was it?

What this means for the map. The meeting place is a KNOB with three attested forms: the headman's house, which draws nothing new; the village temple or shrine, which the map already draws; and a kaisho of its own, standing inside the shrine precinct. Only the third adds a building. Its size is a GUESS, and so is which villages build one, since the attested case is a regional one.
