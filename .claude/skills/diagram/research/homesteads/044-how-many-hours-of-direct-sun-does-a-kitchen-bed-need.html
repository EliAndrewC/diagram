How many hours of direct sun does a kitchen bed need?

Sources: atariya-nisho (the hours, crop by crop), jasaga-hiatari (the same classes, from a farm cooperative)

What the research found. Vegetables fall into three classes by the direct sun they need. Sun crops want about six hours or more a day and will not grow in shade; half-shade crops do with three to four hours; shade crops grow on one or two. Daikon, eggplant, cucumber and the beans are sun crops; turnip, komatsuna, taro, negi and ginger are half-shade crops; mitsuba, shiso and myoga are shade crops - and a farm cooperative's own list sorts the same crops the same way. Both are modern advice for modern cultivars; no Edo farm manual that says where a household's vegetable bed stood for the sun could be read.

The rule a sheet is checked by. A kitchen bed is a bed of sun crops - daikon above all, in the ground in the autumn shoulder month, the season the record's rules for a garden's open ground are set by (under "How much open ground does a kitchen garden keep to its south and east?") - so it needs six hours of direct sun a day in that month (accurate as the crops' need; that the shoulder month is the one counted is this project's reading, a guess). A bed that holds half-shade crops instead is a knob, needing three hours: the sources attest both kinds of bed, and a sheet declares which it draws, sun by default. The sun is the one the record already casts shadows with, at 38 degrees north in the shoulder month, and an hour counts when at least half the bed is lit (a guess: no source says how much of a bed must be in sun). The shade comes from everything drawn that stands up, at the least height the record gives it, so a bed the check fails is surely shaded: a building at the farmhouse ridge's 20 ft (under "How much open ground does a kitchen garden keep to its south and east?"), a compound wall at the 1.6 m of the surviving earth wall (under "How thick was a compound's earth wall?"), a tree at the 10 m of a working, pruned belt (under "The garden's sun, and how far the windbreak shades") - an untended or sacred wood stands taller. The scripted maps keep the placement rules those entries give; this rule checks what a person draws by hand.
