Did an ordinary rice village in south China keep pigs and ducks? Pigs on most farms, ducks out in the fields

Sources: zhang-guo-2019-nongshu and daizhige-bunongshu (the Shen family's farm manual on pigs, and its rice-and-mulberry calendar), buck-1930-farm-economy (how many farms kept hogs, and where the ducks fed), weilongwu-zhwiki (the pigsties inside a Hakka house)

This question is about the plain paddy hamlet, which has no fish ponds. The chicken coop has its own question, Did a farmstead keep chickens?

Pigs: kept on rice farms before the empire's end. The Shen family's farm manual, of the late Ming or early Qing, is written for a farm whose calendar turns the seedling beds and sows the rice, tends the mulberries and buys its manure in Suzhou and Hangzhou. It tells that farm that, with bought manure and hired labor both dear, keeping pigs and sheep is the simplest way to get manure, and that pigs fed on the grain left from distilling pay twice, in the liquor's profit and in manure that costs nothing to haul. The sty on a rice farm is therefore a premodern form, not a modern one.

How many farms: the only count is from after the empire. A survey of 2,866 Chinese farms in the 1920s found hogs on 45 per cent of them - but on nearly two-thirds of the farms in the rice-growing east-central provinces, against a third in the north. No count of pig-keeping farms from before 1912 was found; the manual says a rice farm should keep pigs but not how many did, so the survey's share is read back here into the late imperial village as a calibration, not as a premodern figure.

Ducks: fewer, and in the fields. The same survey found ducks kept by fewer farms than hogs or chickens, and much more commonly in the rice country, where they fed in the fields after the harvest on the fallen grain; in one county of Jiangsu, duck-herders drove their flocks over the farmers' fields, paying for the privilege by meeting the cost of repairing the community's bridges and roads. This page reads that as a duck of the reaped paddy, herded; no page read says whether a rice farm also penned ducks in its yard.

Where the sty stood: attested only for one house type. The survey says nothing about where hogs or ducks were housed. The Hakka hall-and-wing house kept its pigsties inside its own walls, in the rear ring of rooms with the kitchens, the stores, the privies and the hen coops. How an ordinary farmhouse in a paddy hamlet placed its sty no page read says.

What this means for the map. A pig sty belongs on a plain paddy hamlet as well as on a dike-pond one, as the seventeenth-century manual has it. About two households in three keep pigs, the share varying from hamlet to hamlet around the survey's figure - a calibration against the only count there is, labeled as one, since no premodern share was found. The sty is drawn small, against the farmhouse or its outbuildings on the household's own plot, and that placement is a GUESS kept until the record finds how an ordinary farm placed it. No duck pen is drawn in the yard: the paddy hamlet's ducks are out on its fields after the harvest, which a summer map does not show.
