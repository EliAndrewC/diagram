What did a farmhouse roof look like from above?

Sources: kotobank-minka (the forms, the materials, the regions), irimoya-jawiki (where the hip-and-gable roof was used), nihonminkaen-kanagawa and nihonminkaen-kanto (the roofs of real houses), kotobank-nihon-no-minka (roofs re-covered), huipai-zhwiki and minnan-architecture-zhwiki (south China)

Three shapes, one of them the plainest. A Japanese farmhouse roof took one of three shapes: gabled, hipped, or irimoya, a hipped roof with a small gable at each end of the ridge. The basic Edo farmhouse was a rectangle under a hipped or a gabled roof. Where the others stood is regional: ordinary houses were roofed irimoya mostly in the hill country from around Kyoto to Kai, Sagami and Musashi; gabled thatched farmhouses were common around Enzan in Yamanashi (old Kai), and from southern Kawachi into Nara the gabled thatch was held down at each end by a strip of tile. The farmhouses of the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries at one museum show all three, every one of them thatched - hipped, gabled and hip-and-gable, the ordinary houses and the headmen's alike. What share of a village's houses took each shape no page read says.

Thatch, with other covers beside it. The roofing of a folk house was thatch of grass or straw, cedar bark or boards held down with stones, or tile of two kinds. Thatch was not a mark of poverty (this page's reading): the headmen's houses above are thatched too. Some roofs now of metal were thatch first. In south China the look of a house in one well-known region is white walls under dark tile, and the showy upswept "swallowtail" ridge is common on temples, mansions and ancestral halls, and the page read does not put it on a farmer's house. Whether a poor Chinese farmer's house was thatched rather than tiled no readable page was found to say.

How each reads from above. Seen from overhead, a gabled roof is a rectangle split by a ridge running its whole length; a hipped roof has a ridge shorter than the house, with a slope running down from each end of it to the corners, so it reads as a long rectangle with a line down its middle and a diagonal into each corner; an irimoya reads like the hipped roof with a small triangle standing at each end of the ridge.

What this means for the map. The roof shape is a regional form, so it is a choice rolled per settlement - hipped, gabled or irimoya - and not per house; how often each comes up is a GUESS. The ordinary cover is thatch, for the headman as for the tenant; tile belongs on the storehouse (see How big was a farm storehouse) and, in a Chinese-leaning village, on the houses themselves, as in the one region read; whether the poorest there went thatched is unread, so a thatch-or-tile split in such a village is a GUESS.
