Where was the firewood stacked, and how big was the pile?

Sources: boso-no-mura-kigoya, sugiura-1973-fuzoku (the rate), 326woods-stack (the stack's height), suido-ishizue-kizuma (the firewood fence under the windbreak), sugiura-1977-tohoku (the roofed stack), sato-1962-haichi (the woodshed of the cold north)

The woodpile is one of the farmstead's fixtures the GM asked to see drawn; the whole set, and how many of each the map draws, is at The farmstead's fixtures.

The woodpile - the shed attested, the stack's wall a GUESS. The Boso-no-Mura reconstructed farmstead lists a 木小屋: "a building for storing the charcoal, firewood and the like that are used as fuel" (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「燃料として使う、炭やたきぎなどを収めておく建物です」); where the pile stood relative to the house the page does not say. Sugiura counts 0.76 firewood SHEDS per pre-1944 household; the open stack under the eaves is the simpler form and one of the three the map draws (that it is cheaper and older is this page's reading): 10 x 3.5 ft in plan, split logs stacked head-high - the 1.5 m height is modern stacking practice (326-woods, a modern page; that the older stack stood as high is a GUESS). Wall: the back wall or the kura's outer wall, out of the rain - a GUESS; drawn on 75-95% of homesteads.

Two more forms, and the one place a stack is attested. On the Shonai plain of the Japan Sea coast, a woodshed to store the winter's fuel was one of the marks of a farmstead in that cold country. On the Isawa plain in Iwate the firewood was stacked along the windbreak: each homestead's windbreak grove stood on its northwest side, and the firewood was stacked beneath it as a kizuma, some roofed with straw so that from afar it looked like a wall, filling the gaps under the grove's lower branches against wind and snow. At Yahaba, also in Iwate, some firewood was simply stacked with a roof set over it, which Sugiura reads as a small kizuma. So the record holds three forms - a woodshed, a stack along the windbreak on the windward side, and the open stack against the house wall - and only the first two are attested; the stack against the house wall is the GUESS the map draws. These are distinct forms, so the woodpile's form is a knob.

The rule the map follows: per hamlet, the woodpile is rolled among a woodshed, a stack laid along the inside of the windbreak on its windward side, and the open stack against the back wall or the kura; the windbreak stack is drawn only where the homestead has a windbreak. The weights are a guess.
