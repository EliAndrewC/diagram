Where was the firewood stacked, and how big was the pile?

Sources: boso-no-mura-kigoya, koshigaya-shishi-noumin-jukyo (the firewood shed in 1824), oamishirasato-choshi-kaoku (how many farms had one, and its size), sugiura-1973-fuzoku (the 1972 rate), 326woods-stack (the open stack, modern), suido-ishizue-kizuma (the firewood fence under the windbreak), sugiura-1977-tohoku (the roofed stack), sato-1962-haichi (the woodshed of the cold north)

The woodpile is one of the farmstead's fixtures the GM asked to see drawn; the whole set, and how many of each the map draws, is at The farmstead's fixtures.

In a wood shed. The Boso-no-Mura reconstructed farmstead lists a 木小屋: "a building for keeping the charcoal, firewood and the like that are used as fuel" (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「燃料として使う、炭やたきぎなどを収めておく建物です」) The shed is premodern: in the house-by-house record of Hasuda village of 1824, many houses had a firewood shed or a storage shed standing apart from the main house on the plot. In Kakimochi village, whose count of 1885 its historian reads back to the last years of the shogunate, 6 of the 16 households had a wood shed, 7 sheds in all. They ran from 1.5 by 1.5 ken (about 9 by 9 feet) to 5 by 2 ken (about 30 by 12 feet), three of the seven 4 by 2 ken (about 24 by 12 feet). A headman's homestead plan of 1849 from Seinagoya (清名幸谷村), another village in the same town history, has a firewood barn among its buildings. Sugiura's 0.76 firewood SHEDS per pre-1944 household is a count of 1972, modern, and higher than the premodern one. Where on the plot the shed stood none of these pages says.

Two more forms in the north. On the Shonai plain of the Japan Sea coast, a woodshed to store the winter's fuel was one of the marks of a farmstead in that cold country. On the Isawa plain in Iwate the firewood was stacked along the windbreak: each homestead's windbreak grove stood on its northwest side, and the firewood was stacked beneath it as a kizuma, some roofed with straw so that from afar it looked like a wall, filling the gaps under the grove's lower branches against wind and snow. At Yahaba, also in Iwate, some firewood was simply stacked with a roof set over it, which Sugiura reads as a small kizuma. So the record holds three forms - a woodshed, a stack along the windbreak on the windward side, and the open stack against the house wall - and only the first two are attested; the open stack is taken up below. The woodshed and the windbreak stack are distinct forms, so the woodpile's form is a knob.

The open stack under the eaves is found only in modern practice. An open firewood stack under the eaves appears on a present-day page on keeping firewood, which stacks it to about 1.5 m in a rack and prefers a spot under an eave soffit; no page read places the open stack, or its height, before 1868. The stack the map drew against the back wall or the kura's outer wall was 10 x 3.5 ft in plan, split logs stacked head-high, the height taken from that modern page. By the GM's ruling of 2026-09-28 the maps drop what is attested only in modern times, so they do not draw the open stack. The firewood is drawn in its shed, or along the windbreak.

The rule the map follows: per hamlet, the woodpile is rolled between a woodshed and a stack laid along the inside of the windbreak on its windward side; the windbreak stack is drawn only where the homestead has a windbreak, and the weights are a guess. The firewood stands in a wood shed of its own on about four farmsteads in ten, the larger houses first. The shed is 12 feet deep by 24 feet long, the common size in the Kakimochi count, and a small farm's can be as little as 9 by 9 feet. Where on the plot it stands is a GUESS: behind or beside the house, clear of the yard and the garden. The share and the size are a calibration against one village's count.
