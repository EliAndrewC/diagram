Pigs and ducks in south-China rice villages

Rice farmers in the lower Yangzi were told to keep pigs well before the empire ended in 1912: a farm manual of the seventeenth century tells a rice-and-mulberry farm that pigs are the simple way to get manure when bought manure and hired hands are dear. The one count of pig-keeping farms we found is a survey of the 1920s, which found hogs on nearly two farms in three in the rice-growing provinces of east-central China.

Ducks were fewer, and after the rice harvest they were turned out, or driven by herders, over the reaped paddy to pick up the fallen grain.

  • A seventeenth-century farm manual tells a rice farm to keep pigs for their manure.
    The Shen family farm manual dates from the late Ming or early Qing. Its calendar is a rice-and-mulberry farm's: it turns the seedling beds and sows the rice, plants mulberry seedlings, and buys its manure in Suzhou and Hangzhou. It tells that farm that, with bought manure and hired labor both dear, keeping pigs and sheep is an especially simple and convenient way to get manure, and that pigs fed on the grain left from distilling pay twice, in the liquor's profit and in manure that costs nothing to haul. The 2019 paper that quotes the passage reads it as northern dryland farming, which the manual's rice calendar does not bear out. The sty on a rice farm is therefore a premodern form, not a modern one.
  • Whose farm was the Shen family farm manual written for?
    A well-off one, by its own calendar: it hires hands for the busy months and calls in workers to cut the mulberry leaves.
  • How many farms kept pigs?
    Nearly two in three in the rice country, by the only count we found, made after the empire ended in 1912. A survey of 2,866 Chinese farms, made in 1921-1925, found hogs on 45 percent of them - on nearly two-thirds of the farms in the rice-growing east-central provinces, against a third in the north. No count of pig-keeping farms from before 1912 was found: the manual says a rice farm should keep pigs, not how many did.
  • In the 1920s, fewer farms kept ducks than kept hogs or chickens.
    A 1920s survey of Chinese farms finds hogs and chickens common in both north and east-central China, and ducks and geese to a more limited extent. Ducks were much more common in east-central China, where water is everywhere.
  • In the rice country, ducks fed on the reaped paddy.
    After the rice harvest, the fallen grain in the fields fed them. In one county of Jiangsu, duck-herders drove their flocks over the farmers' fields, paying for the privilege by meeting the cost of repairing the community's bridges and roads.
  • No page we read says whether a rice farm also penned ducks at the house.
    The 1920s survey counts the farms with ducks and finds them feeding in the reaped fields, but says nothing of where they were housed.
  • No page we read says where an ordinary paddy farm kept its sty.
    The 1920s survey says nothing about where hogs or ducks were housed.
  • The one house type whose sties we found placed, the Hakka enclosed house, kept them inside its own walls.
    They stood in the house's rear ring of rooms, with the kitchens, the stores, the privies and the hen coops. The enclosed house brought its well, drying ground, vegetable plots, livestock houses, fruit trees and fish ponds within its compass, so that it was self-sufficient.
