Pigs and ducks in south-China rice villages

Rice farmers in the lower Yangzi kept pigs well before the empire ended in 1912: a farm manual of the seventeenth century tells a rice-and-mulberry farm that pigs are the simple way to get manure when bought manure and hired hands are dear. The one count of pig-keeping farms is a survey of the 1920s, which found hogs on nearly two farms in three in the rice-growing provinces of east-central China.

Ducks were fewer, and lived off the fields more than the farmhouse: after the rice harvest they were turned out, or driven by herders, over the reaped paddy to pick up the fallen grain. This is the plain paddy village, with no fish ponds.   • A seventeenth-century farm manual tells a rice farm to keep pigs for their manure.
    The Shen family's farm manual dates from the late Ming or early Qing. Its calendar is a rice-and-mulberry farm's: it turns the seedling beds and sows the rice, plants mulberry seedlings, and buys its manure in Suzhou and Hangzhou. It tells that farm that, with bought manure and hired labor both dear, keeping pigs and sheep is an especially simple and convenient way to get manure, and that pigs fed on the grain left from distilling pay twice, in the liquor's profit and in manure that costs nothing to haul. The sty on a rice farm is therefore a premodern form, not a modern one.
  • The manual was written for a well-off farm, not a smallholding.
    The same calendar hires hands for the busy months and calls in workers to cut the mulberry leaves.
  • How many farms kept pigs?
    Nearly two in three in the rice country, by the only count there is, from after the empire. A survey of 2,866 Chinese farms, made in 1921-1925, found hogs on 45 percent of them - on nearly two-thirds of the farms in the rice-growing east-central provinces, against a third in the north. No count of pig-keeping farms from before 1912 was found: the manual says a rice farm should keep pigs, not how many did.
  • Ducks were kept by fewer farms than hogs or chickens.
    The same survey finds hogs and chickens common in both north and east-central China, and ducks and geese to a more limited extent.
  • In the rice country, ducks fed on the reaped paddy.
    Ducks were much more common in east-central China, where water is everywhere and the fallen grain in the fields fed them after the rice harvest. In one county of Jiangsu, duck-herders drove their flocks over the farmers' fields, paying for the privilege by meeting the cost of repairing the community's bridges and roads.
  • Did a rice farm also pen ducks at the house?
    No page we read says so, one way or the other.
  • The Hakka enclosed house kept its pigsties inside its own walls.
    They stood in the house's rear ring of rooms, with the kitchens, the stores, the privies and the hen coops; the enclosed house brought its well, drying ground, vegetable plots, livestock houses, fruit trees and fish ponds within its compass, so that it was self-sufficient.
  • Where did an ordinary paddy farm keep its sty?
    No page we read says. The 1920s survey says nothing about where hogs or ducks were housed, and the enclosed house is the only house type whose sties we found placed.
