Did a village keep a meadow for fodder and green manure? Yes - common grass ground more than ten times its fields, on the hills and riverbeds beyond them

Sources: kotobank-magusaba (what the fodder ground was, and who held it), fuchu-magusaba-jawiki (what it supplied, and its scarcity), biodic-kusayama (more than ten times the fields; open grass or thin low pine), takamori-karishiki (the manure a paddy took, the same ratio, villages borrowing hill ground), kotobank-karishiki (wide grass ground around the paddies), satoyama-jawiki (the grass mountain, and the small one tied to a paddy), kitamoto-magusaba (a river's floodplain as fodder ground), aso-kayaba (the thatch meadow a settlement kept)

What it was. The magusaba was grass ground where fodder was cut, most of it a commons the inhabitants used together, and it went by many names - the kusakariba, the kayaba, the "fertilizer-grass hill". In the Edo period it was a moor for fodder and for fertilizer, and the ground where green manure (karishiki) was cut was called by the same name; it was usually the commons of one village or hamlet. It supplied fodder, the paddy's green manure and fuel, and as paddies spread and the grass ground shrank, villages fought each other over what was left. The same ground, or ground beside it, gave the thatch: in Aso a settlement of 50 to 60 households had to keep 20 to 30 hectares (about 50 to 75 acres) of thatch meadow, cut in February and March.

How large, against the paddy. Far larger than the fields. To get enough green manure and fodder, grass ground more than ten times the area of the fields is said to have been needed; a Nagano lecture repeats the same calculation, beside one household's record of about two horse-loads of green manure on every tan of paddy. So wide grass ground was needed around the paddies, and the manure was cut on the hill and moor commons too.

Where it stood, and what it looked like. Three forms are attested:

  • The grass mountain (kusayama) - a hill whose trees were deliberately felled and kept down so that it carried only the grass the village's paddies needed. Its cover varied, from open grass to a thin wood of pine three or four meters (10 to 13 ft) tall.
  • The small grass plot tied to one paddy - a grass mountain set aside for a particular paddy, called a tazuke kusayama.
  • The riverbed - on the Kanto plain, villages whose own fodder ground ran short as new fields were opened came to depend on the floodplain of the Arakawa river.

A village with no hills of its own asked a village that had them, and borrowed the ground. How the hill ground lies beyond a village's fields is taken up with the fuel wood, in its own question on this page.

What this means for the map. A village's meadow is not a plot among its fields. At more than ten times the fields it cannot fit on a hamlet or village sheet, so what the sheet shows is its NEAR EDGE: open grass, or grass with thin low pine, on the hill ground past the fields, running off the frame. Which form a village has is a knob rolled per settlement - the hill grass mountain where the sheet has hills, the riverbed where it has a river flat, and now and then a small grass plot beside one paddy. The ratio is a DEGREE, not a form: the map need not show all of it, only that the grass ground begins where the fields end and does not stop at the sheet's edge.

The rule the map follows: on a sheet with hill ground beyond its fields, the first ground past the fields on the hill side is drawn as open grass or grass with scattered low pine, and it meets the frame; on a sheet with a river flat and no hills, the grass ground is the floodplain beside the river. A tazuke plot, when rolled, is one grass patch of about the paddy's own size on the slope above it. The plot's size and how often it is rolled are a GUESS.
