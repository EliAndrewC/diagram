Pines in residence gardens (kuromatsu)

The Japanese black pine (kuromatsu) is the lead tree of the Japanese garden, and a garden pine is a tended one: its new shoots are pinched and further worked twice a year, and a pine left untended loses its worth as a garden tree. Left to grow, it is a tall tree of 15 to 40 m (~49-131 ft). A pine had a set place at a garden's edge too, planted at the foot of the wall so that it showed over it. The garden itself is at Ornamental gardens, ponds and stone lanterns (teien).

  • Left to grow, the black pine reaches 15 to 40 m (~49-131 ft).
    A Japanese guide to garden trees gives the species that height.
  • The black pine is the lead tree of the Japanese garden.
    The same guide says it is used as the lead tree of a Japanese-style garden and to decorate celebrations.
  • A garden pine is tended twice a year.
    Its care needs midori-tsumi, the pinching of the new shoots that stick up in early spring, and the further work called momiage, twice a year; a pine whose care is neglected loses its worth as a garden tree. One trained form is called mon-kaburi, "gate-covering".
  • A pine was planted at the wall to be seen over it.
    The mikoshi no matsu is a pine planted at the edge of a wall so that it can be seen from outside. The phrase is already in a Japanese-Portuguese dictionary of 1603-04, at the start of the Edo period.
  • How big was a pruned garden pine?
    Pruning likely held a garden pine well below the height the species reaches in the wild, by an amount the record cannot put a number on; this is a GUESS. No page we read gives how wide the crown of a pruned garden pine was, or how tall it was kept.
