How big was the village's dooryard copse? It was the homesteads' own woods - about 6,000 to 28,000 sq ft each in a 1684 register

Sources: yashikirin-jawiki (the grove stands on the house lot), miura-2019-yashikiyama (the 1684 register's three homestead woods, and the 1910 account of the woods round a farmhouse group), shakkanho-jawiki (the tan in square meters), kotobank-yashikirin-heibonsha (form, size and species varied), takehara-2004-yashikirin (persimmon among the commonest trees)

What the record says. In the Japanese record the grove that stands with a house is that homestead's own, the yashikirin, formed on the house lot. The one period measurement read is a 1684 register of village woods in the Mito domain, quoted by Miura, which lists homestead woods by household - 30 by 10 ken at 1 tan, 9 by 9 ken at 5 se 21 bu, and 40 by 20 ken at 2 tan 6 se - and from their areas the author takes them for woods inside the homestead. A tan is about 991.7 square meters, so the three woods come to about 10,700, 6,100 and 27,800 sq ft; the two whose sides come near their stated areas are about 60 by 180 ft and 120 by 240 ft (the 9 by 9 ken entry does not match its own area as printed). An encyclopedia account of homestead groves says their form, size and species were not necessarily fixed but varied.

What grew in them. A 1910 forestry paper on the Musashino upland puts cedar, bamboo, evergreen-oak and zelkova woods round the group of farmhouses, then the fields, then the konara woodland (zokibayashi) on the outer edge; a survey of surviving groves on the Tonami plain found persimmon among the three commonest trees. So the bamboo and fruit trees the map puts in its copse are the homestead grove's own trees. How many trees one grove held is answered on the homestead page, under the real scale of homestead groves.

What the record does not say. No page read gives a size for a copse shared by the whole village apart from the homesteads' own woods, or a number of clumps in one.

What this means for the map. The copse is sized by the homesteads, not by the gaps the other features leave: a village's copse is its homesteads' woods taken together, so a copse smaller than one household's wood - one clump 30 ft across among a dozen houses - draws less than the record gives any single homestead. The size is rolled per homestead within the register's range, because the record gives a range and says size varied; that is a degree along a continuum, so calibrated liberty rather than a knob.

The rule the map follows: each homestead that keeps a wood has between about 6,000 and 28,000 sq ft of trees on and beside its lot, counting its windward grove and its share of the copse together. The range is a calibration against one 1684 register of three households, not a survey of villages; counting the grove and the copse as one wood is this project's decision, because the record knows them as one.
