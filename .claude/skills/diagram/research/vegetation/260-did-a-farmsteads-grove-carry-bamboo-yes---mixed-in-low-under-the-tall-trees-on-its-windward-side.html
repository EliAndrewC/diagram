Did a farmstead's grove carry bamboo? Yes - mixed in low under the tall trees, on its windward side

Sources: tonami-yashikirin-haichi (the bamboo stands of the Tonami grove, the side they stood on, and their uses), sendai-igune-modelplan (bamboo filling the low part of the grove against the wind), furusato-ikiru-tsuijimatsu (pine and bamboo binding the Izumo flood bank), yashikirin-jawiki (the north and west as the grove's windbreak sides)

This question is about bamboo growing INSIDE a farmstead's grove of tall trees. A farmstead's separate bamboo patch, and which side of the house it stands on, is Did every farmstead keep its own bamboo?; the village's own bamboo thicket is Bamboo: how common, where it stood.

Bamboo was one of the grove's own plants, not a separate thing beside it. The Tonami plain's research institute, describing the farmstead groves (kainyo) there, says the grove was built round cedar with many other trees, and that there used to be many bamboo stands in it - madake, moso, hachiku and yadake - though few are left today. It also says where in the grove they stood. From the west round to the north of the house, cedar, hackberry and alder were often mixed with bamboo; the tall cedar, oak and zelkova stood from the south to the west, and the east side, where the house fronts, had little grove; the same page says the grove held off the strong south wind of early spring and the cold seasonal wind of winter. The igune of the Sendai plain stands on the north and west of the house to break the wind and the snow, so there the bamboo under the tall trees stood on the windward side. The Tonami page does not say which of its two winds the west-to-north side met, so at Tonami the bamboo side is read as windward from the Sendai groves, not stated.

It had a job there, low down. The same page says moso and other bamboos are very good against strong wind, that the shoots were eaten, and that the culms went into building, river-bank works and barrel hoops. Sendai City's plan for replanting the igune of the Sendai plain describes the traditional grove it replaces as having the same makeup: a main body of tall evergreens such as cedar and black pine, bamboo filling the bare lower part of the trees where there are no branches, to stop the wind there, and a few fruit trees. On the Izumo plain the clipped pine grove (tsuijimatsu) is said to have begun as pines and bamboo planted to bind the earth bank a house was raised on against floods.

How much of the grove was bamboo is not known. No page read gives a share, either of farmsteads whose grove held bamboo or of a grove's ground under bamboo. The Tonami account says only that there were many bamboo stands once. The share is a guess.

What it means for the map. A farmstead grove's bamboo goes on its windward back, mixed in with the tall trees rather than standing apart from them, and it fills the low layer under their crowns. A tree crown is drawn from above, so bamboo under it does not show; where it shows is in the gaps between crowns and along the grove's edge. How much bamboo to draw is a guess, and a small one: a grove is mostly trees with bamboo among them.
