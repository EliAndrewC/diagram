The crop margin - scrub stands 6 ft off every field edge

Sources: aze-standard, nougyoudoboku-keihan (the bund), pmc7538448-levee (levees walked and mowed periodically), kotobank-karishiki, conservation-headland-enwiki, meadow-enwiki (cleared land goes to scrub when the cutting stops)

  • What was found. A paddy levee (keihan/aze) is a narrow earthen ridge - about a foot wide across its top and a foot tall - and wider, this record guesses up to ~3 ft, where it doubles as a footpath (azemichi). The levee grass was CUT several times a season - for green manure and, this record guesses, for fodder and thatch. Constant cutting is what keeps woody scrub from establishing; this record takes the cut ground to reach about 1-2 m past a field's edge, a width no page read gives. No page read gives a traditional clean-strip width between a crop and the growth beyond it either. The wide "conservation headlands" of modern British farming are a MODERN wildlife intervention, introduced in the 1980s, not the historical norm, and this record reasons that East Asian land hunger kept margins at the narrow end of the range.
  • The decision it drove. A crop margin of 6 ft (~1.8 m) - the bund plus the cut strip beside it, a figure this record chose, since no page gives one. Scrub of every kind stands off every paddy and dry plot by this margin, and the tall glyphs (the scraggly pines and the woodland crowns) stand back by as far as their drawn crowns spread as well, so nothing leans over a crop.
  • Disclosed departures. (1) Grass-tuft blade TIPS may lean up to a few real feet past the margin line at coarse tiers - accepted deliberately: grass overhanging a bund is real, and the overhang is too small to see on the drawn map. (2) The reed marsh gets NO margin at all - wet ground genuinely starts at the polder dike, so reeds abutting a field's low bund is honest.
