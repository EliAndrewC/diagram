Where does the scrub stop at a marsh? Grass grades into the reeds; brush and pines stop at the edge

Sources: none - the GM's ruling on the drawn edge and measurements on our own maps; what grows at a marsh's margin, which the edge follows, is at The marsh margin

The form the edge takes, and the two forms it is not (the GM, 2026-08-26: "the marshland is not supposed to overlap with the scrubland rendering"). Dry-ground scrub - brush discs, blades, pines - scatters, with no rule, through the reeds in the same pattern as outside them; under the rule as first measured, on one hamlet only four scrub marks stood inside its marshes, all of them on the marsh's own edge. Cutting the scatter dead at the marsh's boundary exposed a ruled line and a bare strip along the straight western edge of the toe, because the reeds then feathered inset from that boundary. Letting every kind of scrub thin INTO the marsh across that feather closed the line, and left a line of pines standing in the bog along some 1,600 ft of the northern edge, which the GM read as the overlap still being there: "it looks like it is still overlapping!" What the map keeps from that: no pine or dry brush stands in a marsh - because, as The marsh margin records, a marsh's margin grades through sedge and grass into reed, and no pine or dry brush stands in it. The reverse rule is not needed: the marsh is drawn after the scrub and its own reeds already keep off crops, water and buildings.

How the grading is drawn. The scrub's grass and the marsh's reeds are each one small block repeated across their ground, and where the two meet a narrow band either side of the boundary is drawn with a third block that mixes them - grass tufts and reed tufts at about half their own densities, with a little of the marsh's wet haze - so the grass thins out into the reeds and the reeds into the grass, as the margin itself grades. The band follows the marsh's edge, which is drawn rounded at its corners and gently waving along its length rather than as the straight strips it is laid out in. Both are map drawing conventions, the GM's (2026-10-01: "a more gradual transition ... between the two"; a corner "would be more like a rounded curve"). Where a stream or a ditch crosses the marsh, the reeds reach the water's edge, and a narrow band of denser, slightly darker reeds lines each bank so the water stays easy to follow - a map drawing convention too; that reeds stand at the water is the record's own finding for a reservoir's shore.

The rule the map follows: no dry-ground scrub is drawn inside a marsh beyond its mixed edge. Grass thins into the reed edge through that band and is gone beyond it; brush and pines stop at the marsh's edge, and every patch of rough ground a map lays down after the marsh keeps out of it the same way: its grass grades through the band, its brush and pines stop at the edge.
