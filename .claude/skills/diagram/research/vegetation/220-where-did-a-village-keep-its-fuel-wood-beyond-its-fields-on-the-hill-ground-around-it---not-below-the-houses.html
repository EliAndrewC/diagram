Where did a village keep its fuel wood? Beyond its fields, on the hill ground around it - not below the houses

Sources: kotobank-murazakai (houses, then fields, then hill land), satoyama-jawiki (the nearest, lowest hills are the slopes round the settlement), miura-2019-yashikiyama (the fuel wood on the outer edge beyond the fields), satoyama-enwiki (between the foothills and the flat farmland), iriaichi-jawiki (forest commons and grass commons)

What the record says. The village of the Japanese record is, as an ideal type, its houses at the center, its fields around them, and hill and wild land beyond. In the Yoshino mountains the hills are ranked from the nearest and lowest outward, and the nearest - the satoyama - is the fields and mixed woodland on the slopes around the settlement; the satoyama is the border zone between the foothills and the flat farmland. A 1910 forester's account of the Musashino upland gives the same order on flat ground: groves round the houses, then the fields, then the konara woodland on the outer edge. The commons themselves were of two kinds: forest, cut for fuel, timber and leaf litter, and open grass and riverbank land, cut for fodder and thatch.

What this means for the map. A village's wood stands beyond its fields from the houses, on the rising ground where the fields give out - the nearest hill slope. Ground below the houses is where the fields run down to the flat; a wood seated there, past the fields and beside a reed marsh, stands where the record puts the grass and riverbank commons, not the forest. Nearness counts too - the satoyama is the NEAREST hill ground - so the wood is sought on the nearest slope above the fields, not on the nearest open ground in any direction.

The rule the map follows: a woodland commons is seated beyond the fields from the houses and on ground higher than the fields it adjoins; where the map has no such ground, the wood goes on the far side of the fields on the level, never downslope of the houses. Low wet ground by a marsh or river is left to grass and reeds. The order of houses, fields and wood is the record's; reading "beyond the fields" as "higher than the fields next to it" where the ground slopes is this page's reading of "the slopes around the settlement".
