Did a village keep a great old tree at its entrance? An enoki beside the boundary shrine, and one on each highway milestone mound

Sources: hanamoku-enoki (the enoki at a village's entrance), ichirizuka-jawiki (the milestone mound, its size, pairing and trees), kotobank-ichirizuka (the enoki as the mound's tree)

The shrine's own sacred tree is a question of the shrine precinct, on the religion and death page; the Chinese village's mouth is marked by its water-mouth grove. This question is the Japanese village's single great tree.

At the village entrance. In former times a boundary god (dosojin) or a small shrine was kept near a village's entrance, and an enoki was planted there - it is thought, because the enoki had long been believed to ward off evil spirits, because it spreads its branches wide and gives deep shade, and because its fruit can be eaten, among other reasons. How many villages kept one is not recorded.

On the highway. A traveler on the way to a village met the other great roadside tree. In the Edo period an earth mound, the ichirizuka, was raised beside the main highways at every ri (about 2.4 miles) counted from Nihonbashi in Edo: a mound 5 ken (about 30 ft) square and 1 jo high (about 10 ft - a jo is ten shaku; the article converts it to 1.7 m, which is an error), with an enoki or another tree planted on top so that travelers could rest in its shade. In its original form the mounds stood in a pair, one each side of the road; on the five main highways their trees were 55% enoki, 27% pine and 8% cedar, and a standard dictionary gives the enoki as the rule.

At a crossroads or a well. No readable source says a village kept a great tree at its crossroads or its well. A tree there is a GUESS and is not drawn.

What this means for the map. The entrance tree is a knob: a village has one great enoki beside its boundary god where its lane leaves for the road, or it does not, and the share of villages that do is a GUESS. Where an Imperial road crosses a sheet, it may carry a milestone pair - two mounds facing each other across the road, each with one tree, the tree rolled among enoki, pine and cedar in the recorded shares. A milestone stands only every 2.4 miles, so most sheets have none.

The rule the map follows: an entrance enoki is one broad crown, larger than a farmstead's trees, beside the boundary god's stone (dosojin) or small shrine at the lane's exit from the settlement, rolled for about one village in two (a GUESS). A milestone pair is two 30 ft square mounds, one each side of the road, each carrying one crown; a sheet carries one only when a ri mark falls on its stretch of road.
