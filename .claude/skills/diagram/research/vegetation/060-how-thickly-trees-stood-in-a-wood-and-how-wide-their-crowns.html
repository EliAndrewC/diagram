How thickly trees stood in a wood, and how wide their crowns

A wood in old Japan was seldom left to grow as it would. A village's fuel wood was cut every twenty to thirty years or so and grew back from the stumps, as relatively thin, low trees with several stems to a stump, and timber was planted, most often about one seedling to the tsubo. Measured in the twentieth century, young konara woods of this kind carried a few thousand stems a hectare.

No page we read gives the width of a tree's crown in a worked wood, and no record we read from before 1868 counts the trees of a grown wood. The figures below for a mixed hill wood and for a crown are this project's guesses, and say so where they stand.

  • A village's fuel wood was cut every fifteen to thirty years and grew back from the stump.
    The Forestry Agency describes the village woods as cut and regrown from the stump about every twenty to thirty years, woods of relatively thin, low trees growing several stems to a stump. The konara woods in particular were cut on a short cycle of about fifteen to thirty years.
  • A cut stump sent up dozens of shoots, so a worked wood stood as clumps of thin stems.
    In a trial in Nagano, a konara stump cut in autumn sent up about thirty-three shoots the next spring, and about fourteen were still standing three years on. A worked wood stood as clumps of stems, not as spaced single trees.
  • A worked wood held much less timber than one left uncut.
    A Toyama survey puts a konara wood cut every twenty years at about 100 cubic meters of timber a hectare (~1,430 cu ft an acre), against about 265 (~3,790 cu ft an acre) in today's uncut woods of fifty to sixty years.
  • How thickly did a young konara wood stand?
    About 2,500 to 5,000 konara stems a hectare (~1,000-2,000 an acre), the younger the thicker. Trial stands in Nagano stood, at 15 to 23 years, at 2,560 to 5,030 konara stems a hectare, counted as found, not thinned. The report says the prefecture's konara woods had been fuel woods cut and regrown from the stump for generations, though it does not give the trial stands' own history. At 2,560 a hectare each stem has about 42 sq ft, a mean spacing near 6.5 ft; at 5,000, about 22 sq ft, near 4.6 ft.
  • How thickly did an older, overgrown konara wood stand?
    About 1,500 to 2,000 stems a hectare (~600-800 an acre). Konara-rich woods the same Nagano report felled at 26 to 31 years held 1,460 to 2,063 stems a hectare of every kind. A planted konara stand at Tsukuba, 29 years old, had 1,700 stems a hectare, 14.3 m (~47 ft) tall on average, its trunks 14.8 cm (~6 in) thick at breast height - one stem to about 63 sq ft, a mean spacing near 8 ft.
  • The older stands had grown past the age a worked wood was cut.
    The Tsukuba stand was planted, not regrown from cut stumps, and at 29 years it stood at the old end of the fifteen-to-thirty-year cycle. The prefecture's fuel woods had been left alone since oil and gas replaced wood as household fuel from the early 1960s, and had begun to grow old and thick-trunked. The report does not say its own felled woods had that history; that they did is this project's reading.
  • How thickly did a worked wood stand when it was cut?
    Probably thicker than 1,700 a hectare, nearer the 2,560 of the 23-year Kiso stand. In the younger stands of the Nagano report the stems stood thicker the younger the wood, and the older stands had grown past the cutting age. That is this project's reading of three stands, which the report does not draw itself.

The village coppice was one kind of wood. The mixed wood of a settled valley's back slope, cut over for fuel and timber in turn, was another, and no page we read measures it. Where a village's own fuel wood stood is at Village fuel woods and their coppice (satoyama).

  • How thickly did a mixed hill wood stand?
    About 500 to 800 canopy trees a hectare (~200-320 an acre), a GUESS on no page read. The wood meant is the mixed broadleaf and conifer cover of a settled valley's back slope, and that makeup is itself a GUESS. At 600 a hectare each canopy tree has about 180 sq ft, a mean spacing near 13 ft. The overgrown konara stands above held about two to four times as many stems.
  • How wide was a canopy tree's crown?
    About 5 to 8 m (~16-26 ft) across in a hill wood, a GUESS: no page we read gives a crown's width in a coppiced hill wood. Nor does any give one for a konara, a kunugi or any tree of a worked fuel wood, or for a cedar windbreak.
  • Did a few trees spread wider above the rest?
    Now and then, this project guesses, an emergent tree stood above the canopy with a wider crown. No page we read gives such a tree a crown width.
  • Did the crowns of a closed wood touch?
    Some classifications divide woods into open and closed by their crown cover, without saying at what cover a wood counts as closed. Reading a closed wood as one whose crowns meet is this project's reading. In some forests the crowns of fully stocked trees do not touch, but hold a channel-like gap between them, called crown shyness.

Every count above was taken in the twentieth century. Before 1868, in the Edo period, the record we read counts trees only as they were planted.

  • Edo documents counted planted timber by the tsubo.
    They mostly gave a planting as so many to the tsubo, sometimes as a fixed spacing in shaku or ken; a count to the chō was extremely rare. A tsubo is a square six shaku a side, about 3.3 square meters (~36 sq ft), and a chō about 9,917 square meters (~2.5 acres), close to a hectare.
  • How thickly was Edo timber planted?
    Usually about one to the tsubo, some 3,000 a hectare (~1,200 an acre): planted thick where small thinnings had a value, and thin where timber was hard to carry out. Among thick plantings about three to the tsubo was the commonest, some as many as nine, and so thick a planting could not reach its end without thinning. Three to the tsubo is about 9,000 a hectare (~3,600 an acre).
  • Some domains planted thinner, so that every tree would grow on.
    In Morioka domain sugi planted in the 1830s and 1840s went about nine to a ken square; since four of the nine were thinned out and wasted, it was later ruled that they be set about a ken apart, so that every tree planted would grow on. In Hida in the 1850s one rule set fir about two ken apart each way, chestnut four shaku and other seedlings six; another set sugi, fir and chestnut about six shaku apart and hinoki four or five.
  • The sparsest Edo planting found was 700 to 800 trees to the chō, in part of the Owase district of Kii.
    There sugi and hinoki were set at 800 to the chō and pine at 700, which the historian calls extremely sparse, where neighboring villages set 7,000 to 10,000. A chō being close to a hectare, that is about 700 to 800 a hectare (~290-330 an acre).
  • How thick a planting went depended mostly on whether the timber could be carried out.
    The historian finds the density more often set by economic conditions than decided on technical grounds.
  • On the coast, sugi was planted thicker against the wind.
    A rule for raising sugi in coastal districts, recorded in a village of Rikuchū from the 1830s on, set four or five to seven or eight to the tsubo: thick where the wind and spray were fierce, to hold off wind damage, and thin in a sheltered valley. The rule sets how many trees were planted, and says nothing of their crowns.
  • No record from before 1868 counts the trees of a grown wood, a worked coppice or a windbreak, or gives a crown's width.
    The Edo record counts timber on the day it was planted, and counts plantations of sugi, hinoki and pine, not a village's fuel wood or its windbreak. No record we read counts a worked fuel wood's stems, or gives a crown's width in a wood or a windbreak.
  • Could a grown wood have stood as thin as 500 to 800 trees a hectare?
    Possibly. The sparsest Edo planting found, Owase's 700 to 800 to the chō, is about that thin, and a thickly planted wood was thinned as it grew, so a grown wood stood thinner than it was planted. That a wood of 500 to 800 a hectare is no thicker than the sparsest Edo planting found is this project's reading.

In China, a sixth-century farm manual, the Qimin Yaoshu, gives spacings for timber trees rather than counts.

  • The Qimin Yaoshu sets catalpa and qiu two paces apart each way.
    Catalpa goes one tree to every two paces square, not thick, because the tree must grow large, and qiu the same, six hundred trees in five rows. A pace, the bu, was five or six Chinese feet, about 1.3 or 1.5 m (~4-5 ft) on the foot the nearest table gives, so two paces is about 2.5 to 3 m (~8-10 ft).
  • Its oak wood was a coppice, cut and left to sprout, with no count of its stems.
    The oak is sown broadcast on the bend of a hill and never moved; it is cut for rafters at ten years and for purlins at twenty, and once cut it soon sprouts again.
