The marsh margin: reed -> sedge/grass -> dry ground; woody at a reed edge is alder or willow, never pine

What the record says. The textbook hydrosere - the zonation from open water to woodland that lies along a spatial gradient from the shore - runs open water -> littoral -> reed swamp (Phragmites, ヨシ) -> sedge / wet meadow (Carex, Calamagrostis, スゲ) -> alluvial / swamp WOODLAND -> dry ground (Packer et al. 2017, Biological Flora of Phragmites australis, which names open water, littoral, marsh and alluvial forest and ends the transition at woodland - the dry ground past it is this record's own last step - the forest in British examples a wet woodland of alder, birch and willow where the reed thins out among saplings and trees; the sedge and wet-meadow step rests on the Northeast-China source below, the northern-Japan study that names it having no public copy). In Japan the woody stage is alder (ハンノキ林, alder woodland, as the sources below name it), and the classification below also lists a willow (タチヤナギ群集), but on the sand and mud of lower reaches - no Japanese source read names it as a step of the reed-to-dry-ground sequence: the Kushiro Mire, mainly reed, is being invaded by alder as it dries - reed held about 87% of the mire in 1977 and alder about 45% by 2004 - and what resists the alder is STANDING water rather than a fluctuating table: the reed holds the points that are inundated the whole time, alder very probably can hardly establish itself where the water stands 0.4 m or more above the ground, and it is around the rivers and the old channels, where the groundwater lies below the surface and swings widely, that the paper expects it to go on spreading; a northern-Japan wetland classified into reed swamp, Carex lyngbyei marsh, and reed/Calamagrostis grassland with Spiraea shrubs (Otanoshike, Ecological Research 2004, which has no public copy); the national river-vegetation classification lists the reed community of the lower reaches, the ukiyagara-makomo (wild-rice) association and the tachiyanagi-willow association of lower-reach sand-and-mud deposits (ヨシ群落, ウキヤガラ－マコモ群集, タチヤナギ群集) and the alder community (ハンノキ群落) as one of excessively wet ground (過湿地), and a review of wetland classification lists alder woodland among the vegetation of eutrophic mires in a Central European scheme and the alder swamp (ハンノキ林湿地) among the wetland types named in Japan. Northeast-China floodplains show the same ladder: emergent Carex marsh streamside, meadow marsh at intermediate flooding, Calamagrostis wet meadow on the terrace. Red pine (Pinus densiflora, アカマツ) - the scraggly pine our scrub draws - grows even on arid, poor soil along ridges and - in southern Ussuriland, where it is rare - on rocky slopes, sandy soils and seashores - that it is absent from wet lowland is this record's reading of those habitats, not a finding of either page.

The decision it drives. (1) Pines and dry brush inside the reeds: unsupported in every source - the hard exclusion stands. (2) Grass grading into the reeds over the margin: it is the sedge / wet-meadow zone of the hydrosere, drawn with the commons' grass-blade glyph. (3) Woody plants DO stand at a reed margin in the natural state - but they are alder and willow (the British wet woodland above; the Japanese sources read put alder there, and willow only on lower-reach sand and mud). Where a village's shelter belt runs down into the marsh, the map draws its trees there as alder, in a blue-gray green set apart from the belt's own - a drawing convention. For the rest of the toe the record supports two forms, and the map should roll between them rather than pick one; so far it draws only the first: a managed toe (reeds and sedge cut for thatch and fodder, the margin kept open - the form drawn here) and an alder-willow carr along the toe, which the map does not draw. Cutting is what keeps a reed bed one: it stops old reed building up into soil on which willow then grows. That reed and thatch grass were cut every year from common thatch fields, wetlands among them, and that the cutting is what held such ground back from forest, is at Were the reed beds cut?; that a village cut its own marsh the same way is read across from those sources, not said of a village marsh by any of them.
