The marsh margin: reed -> sedge/grass -> dry ground; woody at a reed edge is alder or willow, never pine

Sources: mlit-vegetation-classes, hotes-wetland-diversity, lou-2016-floodplain-zones, gymnosperm-densiflora, takao-densiflora, packer-2017-phragmites, haneishi-2011-kushiro-alder (what the alder takes in a drying mire, and the standing water that holds it off); the reed cutting behind the managed margin is its own question

Grass alone grades into the reeds, and no pine or brush stands in the marsh; the managed margin below rests on the reed cutting recorded in its own question.

What the record says. The textbook hydrosere - the zonation from open water to woodland that lies along a spatial gradient from the shore - runs open water -> littoral -> reed swamp (Phragmites, ヨシ) -> sedge / wet meadow (Carex, Calamagrostis, スゲ) -> alluvial / swamp WOODLAND -> dry ground (Packer et al. 2017, Biological Flora of Phragmites australis, which names open water, littoral, marsh and alluvial forest; the sedge and wet-meadow step rests on the Northeast-China source below, the northern-Japan study that names it having no public copy). In Japan the woody stage is alder (Alnus japonica, ハンノキ林), and the classification below also lists a willow (タチヤナギ群集), but on the sand and mud of lower reaches - no source read names it as a step of the reed-to-dry-ground sequence: the Kushiro Mire, mainly reed, is being invaded by alder as it dries - reed held about 87% of the mire in 1977 and alder about 45% by 2004 - and what resists the alder is STANDING water rather than a fluctuating table: the reed holds the points that are inundated the whole time, alder very probably can hardly establish itself where the water stands 0.4 m or more above the ground, and it is around the rivers and the old channels, where the groundwater lies below the surface and swings widely, that it spreads (Haneishi, Nakatsugawa and Kudo 2011, on groundwater and the spread of the alder in the Kushiro Mire); a northern-Japan wetland classified into reed swamp, Carex lyngbyei marsh, and reed/Calamagrostis grassland with Spiraea shrubs (Otanoshike, Ecological Research 2004 - no public copy of it was found, so it is named here, not relied on); the national river-vegetation classification lists the reed community of the lower reaches, the ukiyagara-makomo (wild-rice) association and the tachiyanagi-willow association of lower-reach sand-and-mud deposits (ヨシ群落, ウキヤガラ－マコモ群集, タチヤナギ群集) and the alder community (ハンノキ群落) as one of excessively wet ground, and a review of wetland classification lists alder woodland among the vegetation of eutrophic mires in a Central European scheme and the alder swamp (ハンノキ林湿地) among the wetland types named in Japan (MLIT river-environment vegetation classes; Hotes, wetland ecosystem diversity). Northeast-China floodplains show the same ladder: emergent Carex marsh streamside, meadow marsh at intermediate flooding, Calamagrostis wet meadow on the terrace (PLOS One 2016). Red pine (Pinus densiflora, アカマツ) - the scraggly pine our scrub draws - is a tree of ridges and arid, poor soil and - in southern Ussuriland, at the edge of its range, where it is rare - of rocky slopes, sandy soils and seashores - that it is absent from wet lowland is this record's reading of those habitats, not a finding of either page (Gymnosperm Database; Mt. Takao museum).

The decision it drives. (1) Pines and dry brush inside the reeds: unsupported in every source - the hard exclusion stands. (2) Grass grading into the reeds over the margin: it is the sedge / wet-meadow zone of the hydrosere, drawn with the commons' blade glyph. (3) Woody plants DO stand at a reed margin in the natural state - but they are alder and willow, a wet woodland this map does not draw. The record supports two forms, and the map should roll between them rather than pick one; so far it draws only the first: a managed toe (reeds and sedge cut for thatch and fodder, the margin kept open - the form drawn here) versus an alder-willow carr along the toe (a distinct wet-woodland glyph, deliberately unlike the dry scrub and the fengshui grove). That reed and thatch grass were cut every year from common thatch fields, wetlands among them, and that the cutting is what held such ground back from forest, is at Were the reed beds cut?; that a village cut its own marsh the same way is read across from those sources, not said of a village marsh by any of them.

The form the edge takes, and the two forms it is not (the GM, 2026-08-26: "the marshland is not supposed to overlap with the scrubland rendering"). Dry-ground scrub - brush discs, blades, pines - scatters, with no rule, through the reeds in the same pattern as outside them; under the rule, on one hamlet only four scrub bases stand inside its marshes, all of them on the marsh's own edge. Cutting the scatter dead at the marsh's boundary exposed a ruled line and a bare strip along the straight western edge of the toe, because the reeds themselves feather inset from that boundary. Letting every kind of scrub thin INTO the marsh across that feather closed the line, and left a seam of pines standing in the bog along some 1,600 ft of the northern edge, which the GM read as the overlap still being there: "it looks like it is still overlapping!" The form that holds: grass alone grades into the reeds across that feather, a wild edge with no line in it, while brush and pines stop dead at the marsh's edge - because a bog's margin is sedge into reed, and woody cover stands on the dry ground above it. The reverse rule is not needed: the marsh is drawn after the scrub and its own reeds already keep off crops, water and buildings.

The rule the map follows: no dry-ground scrub is drawn inside a marsh. Grass thins into the reed edge and is gone beyond it; brush and pines stop at the marsh's edge, and every patch of rough ground a map lays down after the marsh keeps out of it the same way.
