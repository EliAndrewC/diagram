The marsh margin: reed -> sedge/grass -> dry ground; woody at a reed edge is alder or willow, never pine

Sources: opal-biwa-yoshi-hara (cutting and burning keep a reed bed open and willow out), mlit-vegetation-classes, hotes-wetland-diversity, lou-2016-floodplain-zones, gymnosperm-densiflora, takao-densiflora, packer-2017-phragmites, haneishi-2011-kushiro-alder (what the alder takes in a drying mire, and the standing water that holds it off); the rest of the managed-margin reasoning is this page's own

Grass alone grades into the reeds, and no pine or brush stands in the marsh; the managed-margin reasoning below is unsourced except that cutting keeps a reed bed open, and is marked as such where it is given.

What the record says. The textbook hydrosere - the zonation from open water to dry land that every lowland wetland shows in space - runs open water -> littoral -> reed swamp (Phragmites, ヨシ) -> sedge / wet meadow (Carex, Calamagrostis, スゲ) -> alluvial / swamp WOODLAND -> dry ground (Packer et al. 2017, Biological Flora of Phragmites australis). In Japan the woody stage is alder (Alnus japonica, ハンノキ林) and willow (タチヤナギ群落): the Kushiro Mire, mainly reed, is being invaded by alder as it dries - reed held about 87% of the mire in 1977 and alder about 45% by 2004 - and what resists the alder is STANDING water rather than a fluctuating table: the reed holds the points that are inundated the whole time, alder can hardly establish itself where the water stands 0.4 m or more above the ground, and it is around the rivers and the old channels, where the groundwater lies below the surface and swings widely, that it spreads (Haneishi, Nakatsugawa and Kudo 2011, on groundwater and the spread of the alder in the Kushiro Mire); a northern-Japan wetland classified into reed swamp, Carex lyngbyei marsh, and reed/Calamagrostis grassland with Spiraea shrubs; the national river-vegetation classification names a reed community of the lower reaches (ヨシ群落), a tachiyanagi-willow association on the sand-and-mud deposits of lower rivers (タチヤナギ群集) and a wild-rice community listed with ukiyagara (ウキヤガラ－マコモ), and an alder community (ハンノキ群落) of excessively wet ground (過湿地); a review of Japan's wetlands counts alder woodland (ハンノキ林) among the plants of the eutrophic mire (MLIT river-environment vegetation classes; Hotes, wetland ecosystem diversity). Northeast-China floodplains show the same ladder: emergent Carex marsh streamside, meadow marsh at intermediate flooding, Calamagrostis wet meadow on the terrace (PLOS One 2016). Red pine (Pinus densiflora, アカマツ) - the scraggly pine our scrub draws - is a tree of ridges and arid, poor soil and, at its Russian northern edge, of rocky slopes, sandy soils and seashores; that it is absent from wet lowland is this record's reading of those habitats (unsourced) (Gymnosperm Database; Mt. Takao museum).

The decision it drives. (1) Pines and dry brush inside the reeds: unsupported in every source - the hard exclusion stands. (2) Grass grading into the reeds over the margin: it is the sedge / wet-meadow zone of the hydrosere, drawn with the commons' blade glyph. (3) Woody plants DO stand at a reed margin in the natural state - but they are alder and willow. Where a village's shelter belt runs down into the marsh, the map draws its trees there as alder, in a blue-gray green set apart from the belt's own - a drawing convention, since the real foliage is a plain dark green. For the rest of the toe two forms are open: a managed toe (reeds and sedge cut for thatch and fodder, the margin kept open - the form drawn here) and an alder-willow carr along the toe, which the map does not draw. Cutting is what keeps a reed bed one: it stops old reed building up into soil on which willow then grows. Unsourced, and labeled so: that Edo-period reed beds were mown on thatch fields (茅場), and that sedge was cut for fodder - plausible, searched, not found.

The form the edge takes, and the two forms that failed on the way to it (the GM, 2026-08-26: "the marshland is not supposed to overlap with the scrubland rendering"). Dry-ground scrub - brush discs, blades, pines - had been scattering through the reeds in the same pattern as outside them; on one hamlet only four scrub glyphs stand inside its marshes, all of them on the band's own edge. The first repair cut the scatter dead at the marsh's boundary, which exposed a ruled line and a bare strip along the straight western edge of the toe, because the reeds themselves feather inset from that boundary. The second let every kind of scrub thin INTO the marsh across that feather; it closed the line, and left a seam of pines standing in the bog along some 1,600 ft of the northern edge, which the GM read as the overlap still being there: "it looks like it is still overlapping!" The form that holds is the third: grass alone grades into the reeds across the fade, a wild edge with no line in it, while brush and pines stop dead at the marsh's edge - because a bog's margin is sedge into reed, and woody cover stands on the dry ground above it. The reverse rule is not needed: the marsh is drawn after the scrub and its own reeds already keep off crops, water and buildings.

The rule the map follows: no dry-ground scrub is drawn inside a marsh. Grass thins into the reed edge and is gone beyond it; brush and pines stop at the marsh's edge, and every patch of rough ground a map lays down after the marsh keeps out of it the same way.
