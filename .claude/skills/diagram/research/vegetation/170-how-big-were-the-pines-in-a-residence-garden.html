How big were the pines in a residence garden?

Sources: uekipedia-kuromatsu (the Japanese black pine as a garden tree), kotobank-mikoshi-no-matsu (the pine planted to be seen over the wall)

The Japanese black pine, left to grow, is a tall tree of 15 to 40 m, about 50 to 130 ft. It is also the lead tree of the Japanese garden, and a garden pine is a tended one: it needs its new shoots pinched (midori-tsumi) and its old needles thinned (momiage) twice a year, and a pine left untended loses its worth as a garden tree. One trained form is grown to arch over a gate. A pine had a set place at the edge of the garden too: the mikoshi no matsu is a pine planted at the foot of the wall so that it can be seen from outside, a phrase already in a Japanese-Portuguese dictionary of 1603-04.

How wide the crown of a pruned garden pine was, or how tall it was kept, no page read says. Pruning holds a garden pine far below the height the species reaches in the wild, by an amount the record cannot put a number on.

What this means for a plan: pines in a residence garden are ACCURATE, and so is a pine at the wall showing over it to the lane. Their crown size is a GUESS. The drawn crowns of 4 to 6 ft read as young or closely pruned trees; a garden kept by generations of magistrates would hold older trees whose crowns, even pruned, would likely be wider, and a crown of 15 to 30 ft, the figure the map's own notes give a mature garden pine, is no less a guess.
