How thickly were woods stocked before modern times? Timber was planted about three to the tsubo, but no old record counts a grown wood's trees or its crowns' width

Sources: tokugawa-1941-zorin (how thickly Edo documents had timber planted), shakkanho-jawiki (the tsubo, the ken and the chō in metric), qimin-yaoshu-enwiki (the farm manual's date), qimin-yaoshu-zhongzhu (the manual's spacing for timber trees and its oak wood)

The map stocks its woods, and sizes their crowns, from modern measurements: the hill wood and every stand's crowns in the forest density entry, the belt in the belt's crowns, the village coppice in how thickly a worked coppice was stocked. Before modern times:

  • Planted timber, counted: attested. Edo documents mostly gave a planting as so many to the tsubo, sometimes as a fixed spacing in shaku or ken; a count to the chō was extremely rare. The commonest was about three to the tsubo, some plantings as many as nine, and so thick a planting could not reach its end without thinning. Some went thinner. In the 1830s and 1840s Morioka domain moved its sugi from about nine to a ken square to about one a ken apart, so that every tree planted would grow on. In Hida in the 1850s fir went about two ken apart each way, chestnut four shaku and other seedlings six. And in part of the Owase district of Kii, sugi and hinoki were set at 800 to the chō and pine at 700, which the historian calls extremely sparse, where neighboring villages set 7,000 to 10,000. How thick depended mostly on whether the timber could be carried out. A tsubo is a square six shaku a side, about 3.3 square meters; a chō is about 9,917 square meters, close to a hectare; so three to the tsubo is about 9,000 trees a hectare, one to the tsubo about 3,000, and Owase's sparse planting about 700 to 800.
  • Planted thicker against the wind: attested. A rule for raising sugi in coastal districts, recorded in a village of Rikuchū from the Tenpo era on, set four or five to seven or eight to the tsubo, planted thick where the wind and spray were fierce, to hold off wind damage, and thin in a sheltered valley.
  • In China: attested, as a spacing. A sixth-century Chinese farm manual sets catalpa two paces apart each way, not thick, because the tree must grow large, and qiu the same, 600 trees in five rows. Its oak is sown broadcast on the bend of a hill and never moved; it is cut for rafters at ten years and for purlins at twenty, and it sprouts again once cut - a worked coppice, with no count of its stems.
  • Not found, old or new. No record read from before modern times counts the trees of a grown wood, a worked coppice or a windbreak, and none gives the width of a crown.
  • What it means for the map. The old record counts timber on the day it was planted, not the grown wood, and it counts plantations of sugi, hinoki and pine, not a village's fuel wood or its belt. So the densities the map draws for a grown hill wood, a belt and a coppice, and every crown width it draws, rest on modern measurements or on none, and each stays labeled so in its own entry. The one old figure as sparse as the hill wood's 500-800 stems a hectare is Owase's planting of 700 to 800 to the chō; a thickly planted wood was thinned as it grew, so a grown wood stood thinner than it was planted, and a wood drawn at 500-800 is no thicker than the sparsest Edo planting found. That is this page's reading. Whether the maps are stocked on the old planting figures instead is left to the GM.
