Did a village keep a bamboo thicket before modern times? Yes - round its houses, on dry ground

Sources: nagaokakyo-take-nishiyama (an early-Edo screen painting settlements ringed by bamboo), nagaokakyo-take-takenoko (the Edo-period bamboo tax and village bamboo groves), ogura-kyoto-ezu-shokusei (bamboo groves beside shrines and temples in the 17th century), qimin-yaoshu-zhongzhu (bamboo on high ground near hills, not in wet low fields)

A hamlet's bamboo stands in two places (Bamboo: how common, and where it stood): the farmstead's own strip, and the take-yabu, a thicket of its own at the edge of the settlement. The page that first placed the thicket where the plain meets the worked hills describes the present day. Before modern times:

  • A thicket round the settlement: attested. An early-Edo screen of the countryside round Kyoto paints settlements ringed by bamboo groves, which the historians of a city there take to be madake or hachiku. The villages of that district owed bamboo as a tax through the Edo period and managed their bamboo groves. On the hills round Kyoto around 1658-1661, bamboo groves right beside the shrines and temples were not rare. All three are of one bamboo-rich region near a great city.
  • On dry ground, not in a wet field: attested. A sixth-century Chinese farm manual wants bamboo on high, level ground, near hills best, and says it dies in a low field once water reaches it.
  • Not found, old or new. No record read says a village held its thicket in common, or sets one at a paddy's margin.
  • What it means for the map. The thicket at the settlement's edge, on dry ground, is accurate, and the maps keep it. A seat for it at a paddy's margin is a guess, and so is its being held in common.
