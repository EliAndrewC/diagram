Were the reed beds cut? Yes - every winter, for thatch, and the cutting kept the marsh edge open

Sources: kayabun-kayabuki (thatch fields cut every year, in wetlands among other places, and held open by the cutting), ohmi-yoshi (the reed cut in winter from old times, and the burning after it), biwako-visitors-yoshi-hiire (winter cutting and spring burning kept the reed beds)

The marsh at a village's edge is drawn as reed grading through sedge and grass to dry ground, with no scrub in it; the zones themselves are in The marsh margin. This question is whether villagers kept that edge open by cutting it.

Reed and grass for thatch were a crop, cut every year from ground kept for it. The Japan Thatching Culture Association says that the plants used for thatch - susuki grass most of all, and reed among others - can be gathered year after year by cutting them every year; that a thatch field was a place kept for that; that grassland left alone turns into forest, and that the thatch fields were kept from doing so by cutting every year and by burning; and that these fields lay in the hills around a settlement or in its wetlands, used in common by the settlement.

Reed in particular was cut in winter and burned in spring. On Lake Biwa the dead stems above ground have been cut in winter from old times and made into reed goods; after the cutting, sedge and other weeds from the same bed are laid on the stubble, dried and burned. Even now, where a reed bed on dry ground is not cut, it seems often to go to ruin. Shiga's tourism office puts it in one line: the reed was part of daily life from old times, and the beds were kept healthy by cutting in winter and burning in spring.

What it means for the map. A village's reed marsh is a worked field of reed, cut each winter, and the cutting is part of why woody growth does not stand in it - which is the managed form the map draws, grass grading into the reeds and no brush or pine inside them. The sources speak of thatch fields in general and of Lake Biwa's reed beds; that a small farming village cut its own marsh the same way is an interpolation from them, not a thing any page says of a village marsh. The other form, alder and willow along the reed's edge where nobody cuts, is recorded at The marsh margin.
