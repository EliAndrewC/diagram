Bamboo: how common, where it stood, and how to show it

Sources: satoyama-enwiki, phyllostachys-enwiki, pmc5723622-bamboo-range, bamboo-growers-hardiness, tsuijimatsu; the Shirakawa farmstead grove (Kids Web Japan) has no readable page, chikurin-jawiki, take-jawiki, yashikirin-jawiki

The GM: "is there supposed to be bamboo on the reference hamlet? Why or why not? how common was it for there to be bamboo in settlements such as this?" - and the rendering problem behind it: a culm is a few inches across, so at 1 px = 1 ft a bamboo stand has nothing to draw at true scale.

How common: ubiquitous below the frost line. From Wikipedia "Satoyama": satoyama "contains a mosaic of mixed forests, rice paddy fields, dry rice fields, grasslands, streams, ponds, and reservoirs for irrigation", and by the 1960s were used as "rice fields, plowed fields, shifting cultivation, grasslands, thatch fields, secondary forests for fuel, and giant bamboo forests". From Wikipedia "Phyllostachys bambusoides": madake's "long internodes and equally long fibres ... make it ideal for traditional basket-weaving and the production of fans"; its sheaths wrapped food and covered geta; it made shakuhachi. From PMC 5723622, the moso/madake range study: bamboo "distribution remained south of 41°N, in areas that included plains and hilly regions of central Honshu, as well as the coasts of northern Honshu" (the paper's warming-scenario projection, not its present-day survey result); stands "were not found at study sites where ... mean annual minimum temperature was below -16.8°C", and "moso and madake bamboo distribution in northern Japan depends primarily on temperature", agreeing with prior work "that moso bamboo cannot withstand temperatures around or below -18 to -20°C". From the growers: madake "Minimum Temp: 5°F (-15°C), Hardiness Zone: 7" (completebamboo.com), with other readable growers giving -18 to -23 C - a spread, not a number; and no read source says new SHOOTS take frost, only that "colder weather will likely cause frost damage to leaves and canes" (bambubatu), so the record says only "frost-tender at the margin". So: a lowland paddy hamlet in a temperate province has bamboo as a matter of course, and a cold upland one may have none - that half is sourced. Two attested forms, so the map varies it from settlement to settlement.

Where it stood. Two places, both already in this record: (1) the homestead's own yard - on the Tonami plain the bamboo stood on the SOUTH side with the storehouses and fruit trees, its roots planted to hold the soil; no page read puts it on the shady north-west service strip (the homestead research); and the dooryard's "persimmon and bamboo" stand IN the sunlit yard; (2) the take-yabu (bamboo thicket) as its own stand at the village edge, harvested like a coppice. Bamboo was NOT scattered one culm at a time through a mixed wood: a stand is a clonal thicket, dense and monospecific, with a hard edge.

Why bamboo cannot be drawn as trees. Bamboo mixed at 20% through a cedar windbreak is not how bamboo grows (a stand, not a seasoning), and a glyph the size of one culm - about 6 ft across at true scale - reads as nothing at fit zoom, pale dots in a dark belt: a map drawn that way has bamboo and no reader can tell.

How to show it. Japan's own topographic maps solved exactly this: the legend of the GSI (the Geospatial Information Authority of Japan, the national mapping agency) has a distinct bamboo-grove symbol (竹林), separate from the broadleaf and conifer symbols, because a reader of a map must be able to tell the three apart at map scale.

As built (the GM, 2026-08-27: "make that change in the manner that you had previously proposed"). A bamboo stand is drawn as a polygon whose position and extent are to scale, marked with paired culm strokes and a leafy fork in pale yellow-green; bamboo is not mixed into the windbreak or the dooryard copse; whether a hamlet has bamboo at all, and where, varies by settlement - at the cluster's shady side (drawn as its NORTH, the side the house shades), as a thicket at the field margin's shady end, or both; a stand narrower than 14 ft on its short axis is not drawn, and no stand overlaps a paddy. The stand glyph is a map drawing convention - the marks are symbolic, the extent is not - and so are the stand sizes and the mark pitch; north is drawn as the shady side, the side the house shades - this record's own reading.

Household bamboo, per farmstead - which side, and how common (the GM's question, 2026-08-27). The GM asked whether a farmstead's own bamboo always stood to the north-west and whether every farmstead had one.

  • WHERE (ja.wikipedia "屋敷林"): on the Tonami plain 「on the south side there were storehouses and barns, and plants bearing fruit, such as figs, grapes and persimmons, and bamboo were planted」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「南側には蔵や納屋などがあり、無花果や葡萄、柿などの果実がなる植物や竹などが植えられていた」) - storehouses and barns on the SOUTH side, and there the fruit trees and the bamboo; and separately, 「at houses near rivers and in flood-prone areas, alder and root-spreading bamboo are planted as flood protection to keep the soil from washing away」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「河川の近い家や水害の多い地域では、防水用としてハンノキや、根を張る竹を植えて土壌の流出を防いでいる」) - by rivers and in flood-prone ground, bamboo (and alder) planted for its roots. From tsuijimatsu.com: the kainyo 「is centered on cedar, with ate (asunaro), zelkova, oaks, bamboo, persimmon, chestnut and others besides」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「はスギが中心で、ほかにアテ（アスナロ）・ケヤキ・カシ類・竹・柿・栗などで」) - cedar central, bamboo among the secondary species. A GUESS as far as the table goes: the grove as a whole faces the local harmful wind - north and west (Isawa, Iwate; Hikawa, Shimane), west (Iide, Yamagata), south and west (Tonami) - a regional table seen only in a search snippet, on no page that could be read. Not confirmed: the "N/W strip ... kitchen drain" sentence (the 1996 Tonami model homestead, the homestead research) - no readable source carries it. So the SIDE is two-formed at least (with the storehouses; at the wet edge; on the wind side) and is ROLLED per farmstead, weighted toward the back of the house and the shed's side (two supportable forms are rolled, here per farmstead).
  • HOW COMMON (Visit Toyama): on the Tonami plain "each house has been surrounded by homestead woodland ... over 7,000 houses", with "cedar, Japanese zelkova, and bamboo ... used as materials for building new houses as well as materials for various everyday tools"; and tsuijimatsu.com: 「bamboo was an important material for everyday life」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「竹は日常生活の資材として重要なものでした」). Not found: any share of farmsteads keeping bamboo, and any source distinguishing household bamboo from the communal take-yabu by number. So the presence rate is a GUESS: about three farmsteads in five keep a stand, "one of several secondary species" read as common but not universal.
  • As built: a 22 x 16 ft strip stands at a farmstead that has one, most often behind the house or beside the shed; on Inashiro 3 of 15 farmsteads keep one.

On the GSI legend (ridgelineimages.com "Reading GSI Topographic Maps") the bamboo forest (竹林) symbol is listed as its own category beside broad-leaved (広葉樹林) and coniferous (針葉樹林) forest, and distinct from the bamboo-grass (笹地, sasa) symbol.
