Which fruit trees stood on a farmstead? Persimmon, chestnut and plum in the yard, tea on the field bunds - how many is a guess

Sources: yashikirin-jawiki (plum and persimmon planted for food; fruit on the Tonami farmstead's south side), kotobank-yashikirin (fruit trees in part of the yard), esj-yashikirin-regions (chestnut and persimmon among the commonest trees of four regions' farmsteads), chaen-jawiki (tea in one row along the field bunds), kotobank-shiboku-sanso (tea and mulberry as crops of the field), biwa-jawiki (the belief that kept loquat out of the homestead)

The farmstead's grove as a whole - how big it was and how many farmsteads had one - is at the homestead grove's scale; its bamboo is at Did every farmstead keep its own bamboo? This question is about the fruit and useful trees beside the house.

What the record says. Besides shelter from wind and fire, a homestead grove carried trees that could be eaten from, such as plum and persimmon; and in part of the yard fruit trees were planted. On the Tonami plain the storehouses and barns stood on the south side, and there fig, grape and persimmon were planted, with bamboo. A survey of homestead groves in four districts found chestnut among the three commonest trees in the two northern ones (Isawa and Iide) and persimmon among them on the Tonami plain, and between about nine and eighteen kinds of woody plant at the average homestead - a small sample of four to ten homesteads a district.

Tea stood out on the fields rather than at the house: bund tea (keihan-cha) was tea planted in a single row along the banks between fields, which also marked the boundary with the neighbor, broke the wind and kept the soil from washing off, and a household sometimes sold what it did not drink. Tea and mulberry were otherwise two of the "four trees" of the early-modern period, crops of the field grown for sale and processing, restricted by the shogunate's rules on what a field might grow and grown as local specialties from the middle of the period; the mulberry planting a village may carry is the mulberry overlay, not a dooryard tree. The loquat was kept out: a folk belief is recorded that a loquat planted in a garden or a homestead brings unending sickness.

How many. No readable source counts the fruit trees of a farmstead. The number is a GUESS: one to three fruit trees near the house, as the sources speak of them as a part of the yard rather than an orchard.

What this means for the map. The species is a knob, since three are attested at farmsteads: each village rolls its fruit trees from persimmon, chestnut and plum, weighted toward chestnut in a cold northern district and persimmon elsewhere. Bund tea is a second knob - a village has it along some of its dry-field bunds or it does not - since the form is attested but how many villages kept it is not. No loquat is drawn at a farmstead, and no mulberry outside the overlay.

The rule the map follows: a farmstead that rolls fruit trees stands one to three single crowns in its yard within about 30 ft of the house, most often on the side with the storehouse and shed rather than the grove's windward side; the count, the distance and the side are a GUESS. A village that rolls bund tea draws a single row of low tea bushes along some dry-field bunds.
