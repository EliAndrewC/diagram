Does scrub stand under a village wood? No - the floor was worked clear; grass fringes the edge

Sources: satoyama-enwiki, takeuchi-2010-satoyama (the coppice as the source of fuel, fertilizer and fodder), jiao-2019-satoyama (litter gathered for the paddies, the oaks cut on a cycle, sunlight on the floor), uehara-2009-agris, waldrand-dewiki, pmc7898781-fukugi; no publicly readable source for the EUNIS mantle-and-fringe wording or the 2025 Okinawa forest-edge study, satoyama-jawiki, coppicing-enwiki

The GM: "Should scrubland overlap with forests? It seems like it shouldn't. Like, visually, it looks weird. but maybe what is being represented is more accurate than what I am imagining." It was not more accurate. Two findings, one from land use and one from vegetation structure:

  • A satoyama wood's floor was kept clear, on purpose. From Wikipedia "Satoyama": "During the Edo era, young and fallen leaves were gathered from community forests to use as fertilizer in wet rice paddy fields";29 "succession to dense and dark laurel forest is prevented by farmers that cut down these trees for firewood and charcoal every 15 to 20 years";30 satoyama declined with "the drastic shift ... from charcoal and firewood to oil and the change from compost to chemical fertilizer".31 The coppice was the village's source of fuel, fertilizer and fodder: forest litter (ochiba) and compost were gathered from it for the paddies,32 its deciduous oaks - konara and kunugi (Quercus serrata and Quercus acutissima) - were cut on a cycle for firewood and charcoal, every 20 to 40 years by one review against the 15 to 20 above,33 and the cutting and the litter-gathering together let sunlight reach the floor - so the floor's cover was herbs and flowers under a managed canopy, not brush.34 From Uehara et al. 2009, AGRIS: a managed coppice stand held 42 plant species against 23 in a stand left uncared for35 50 years,67 the neglected one poorer in herbs36 - the abandoned wood is the different one. So brush and young pine under the crowns of an inhabited village's grove is the form the read record does not show; the floor is litter-raked ground under a canopy cut every 15-20 years.
  • The edge is a gradient, and it is grass. From German Wikipedia "Waldrand": 「An ideal, fully grown forest edge is divided, from the outside inward, into a herb fringe (Krautsaum), a shrub belt (Strauchgürtel) and a forest mantle (Waldmantel)」 (translated from the German by this project; original: 「Ein idealer, ausgewachsener Waldrand gliedert sich von außen nach innen in Krautsaum, Strauchgürtel und Waldmantel」)37 - an ideal mature forest edge runs, outside to inside, herb fringe, shrub belt, forest mantle (the EUNIS habitat factsheet's own wording for the outer two layers has no publicly readable source - searched 2026-08-28: the E5.2 factsheet page was gone). On a worked wood the shrub belt is among what gets cut - the undergrowth was cut and carried off beside the leaf litter; the grass fringe is what remains, and it thins out under the first crowns.112

Rule: brush and pines are kept out of every village grove; grass blades thin into it over the same fade the marsh edge uses and are gone beyond it; the crowns are the grove. Inashiro draws 1,263 grass blades, all within the fringe, and no brush or pines inside the belt. The coppice patches take the same rule - the coppice IS the worked wood - so every woodland commons keeps scrub out too. The fade itself is a map drawing convention shared with the marsh - one ramp for every soft edge, so two edges never read differently for no reason. An abandoned-coppice shrub layer is a real second form for a DESERTED settlement; that an inhabited one never shows it is our inference from the managed-against-uncared contrast above, not a finding.

The homestead windbreak is the same kind of managed wood, from "Distribution and utilization of homestead windbreak Fukugi trees", PMC 7898781: "Homestead windbreaks are managed as part of a residence and are strips of trees planted and maintained to alter wind flow and microclimate38 ... designed based on Feng Shui concepts in the Ryukyu Kingdom, around 300 years ago".
