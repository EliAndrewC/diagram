The fengshui forest - real scale, and why ours is honest

Sources: forests-2020, hu-2011-fengshui-patches, afcd-ncsc-9-06, hk-herbarium-fsw, afcd-hkbio-8, yuan-liu-2009, kweon-youn-2021, ma-2017-fengshui-carbon, coggins-minor-2018, fengshui-woodland-enwiki, zhongshan-fsw-2016

  • Scale - the real numbers. How many: Fujian villages keep about two fengshui forests each (the province-wide figure the Forests 2020 paper reports from Xu, Qiu and Wang 2012) and two Buyi villages in Guizhou hold two parcels each, so the map's back grove plus water-mouth cluster is the attested count. How dense: stem density ~3,400 stems/ha (「3403 plants/ha」), basal area ~49 m²/ha in well-protected Pearl River Delta patches (one 1200 m² transect each; values the authors call consistent with well-developed evergreen broadleaved forests), and a basal area of 30.0-52.5 m²/ha in five Guangzhou fragments. How large - the back grove: patch area is highly variable. The one large sample readable with each wood's area measured is Hong Kong's territory-wide survey of 115 village woods with GIS-drawn boundaries - the wood 「seen today behind many villages, typically in a crescent shape」: median about 1 ha, mean 1.1 ha, half of them under a hectare, four in ten between one and two, one in ten at two or more, the range 0.06-5.3 ha (the Herbarium's summary: 「The average area of a fung shui wood is 1 hectare」, 600 m² to 6 ha). Across the delta in Zhongshan, Guangdong, a 2016 survey of the city's fengshui groves counts 66 of them in 66 natural villages totaling 269.85 ha - about 4.1 ha each on average, the record's own arithmetic; the quoted passages give no per-grove areas, so the spread behind that mean is not known here. Villages in Fujian are the tail: fengshui forests listed as 「A forest over three hectares」 and 「A forest over six hectares」 for two Youxi County villages, and Lingtou's over 20 ha in total, kept for over 800 years, a 22-hectare houlongshan grove in Nanjing County saved in 1958-59 and gazetted by 1963 as the Letu Subtropical Rain Forest Nature Reserve, and a 133 ha lineage forest at Letu - probably the same forest, sized differently by the two papers (a GUESS from the shared name). A TYPICAL village grove is nevertheless a small forest patch, and the ~1-2 ha back grove drawn here sits in the upper half of the measured band - a deliberate choice, recorded: the belt must read as a forest at two feet to the pixel. How large - the water-mouth cluster: no Chinese one has been measured, and the ~0.1-0.5 ha drawn is a guess between Korean figures, at How big is a water-mouth grove? How many trees: no readable source counts a Chinese grove's trees; the Korean groves hold 「less than 10 to more than 100」 each, and at the Pearl-delta density a 1-2 ha belt would carry some 3,400-6,800 stems of all sizes - so the 100-300 mature canopy trees over a dense bamboo/shrub understory drawn for the belt, and a few dozen big old trees at the water mouth, remain a GUESS bracketed by those figures. The back belt reads as a real small FOREST (clearly the largest vegetation feature, a wall of dozens-to-hundreds of crowns - the grove fills its polygon with overlapping dense clumps so a big belt does not read as a handful of lone trees), the water-mouth as a distinct smaller cluster, plus the bamboo/fruit scatter through the village. Hoshigaoka draws a ~1-2 ha embracing windward belt + a ~0.3 ha water-mouth + the leafy scatter.

  • Why the sizing is honest at this scale. At two feet to the pixel a ~1-2 ha grove is genuinely large relative to the ~11 ha built cluster (~10-18%), so the belt reads as the dominant feature without being cartoonishly oversized. Sources: Fengshui woodland (Wikipedia) (the concept only - the page carries no grove count and no grove size); Coggins & Minor, "Fengshui Forests as A Socio-Natural Reservoir in the Face of Climate Change and Environmental Transformation" (Asia Pacific Perspectives 15(2), 2018); Hu et al., "Values of village fengshui forest patches" (Pearl River Delta, 32 patches, the density/basal-area figures); "Village Fengshui Forests as Forms of Cultural and Ecological Heritage: Interpretations and Conservation Policy Implications from Southern China" (Forests 2020).
