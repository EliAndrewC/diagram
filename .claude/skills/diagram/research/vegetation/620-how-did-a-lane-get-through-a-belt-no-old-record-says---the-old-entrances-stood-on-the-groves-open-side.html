How did a lane get through a belt? No old record says - the old entrances stood on the grove's open side

Sources: yashikirin-jawiki (the grove round the whole house before Meiji, the 1625 order, and the Tonami entrance), wang-zhenzhong-shuikou (the road and bridge at a Huizhou water mouth, and a path round a planted dam)

Where the belt stands, and why its open flanks are not gaps, is at Does a shelter belt wrap the settlement? This question is the lane that meets it.

A planted run was continuous. On the Izumo plain, before the Meiji era, the homestead grove enclosed the whole circuit of the house - that region's form, not every region's, for most groves stood on one or two sides (above); and elsewhere, in 1625, an order issued in the Takada domain directed that cedar be planted round the homestead. Neither says how the way in passed the trees.

The way in went by the open side. On the Tonami plain the homestead's east side was the entrance, with the garden, while tall cedar stood against wind and snow on the west and south; the page gives that layout no date. In Huizhou the water mouth was where a village met the outside world, so its road and its bridge were there; and at Fangkeng, in a villager's account the chapter does not date, the grove planted on the water mouth's earth dam stood like a city wall, with only the stream and the flagstone path going round it.

How a lane was cut through a planted run is on no page read - neither an opening's width nor its angle to the wind. The angled crossing set back from the belt's end is a modern extension service's design rule, quoted at Does a shelter belt wrap the settlement?, and nothing earlier, so the maps do not draw it.

What this means for the map. The maps close a hole in the belt's planted run: the continuous run is old, and a bare stretch wider than about 30 ft across the wind is filled, the 30 ft a map drawing convention. Where one of the maps' lanes crosses the belt, the planting resumes on both sides of it; that crossing is a GUESS, since the old entrances found stood on the grove's open side or came in along the stream. A farmstead's own grove, when its roll closes all four sides (Which sides of the house did a homestead grove take?), is broken once, at the middle of its front, for a way in about 12 ft wide: the break is a physical necessity, since the farm must be reached, and its place and its width are a GUESS, since no page read gives where a ring's opening stood or how wide it was.
