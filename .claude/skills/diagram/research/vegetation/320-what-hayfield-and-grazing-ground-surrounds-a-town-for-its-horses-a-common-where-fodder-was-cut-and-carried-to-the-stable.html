What hayfield and grazing ground surrounds a town for its horses? A common where fodder was cut and carried to the stable

Sources: kotobank-magusaba (fodder ground where pack horses and farm animals were kept), kitamoto-magusaba (fodder cut, fed in the stable and trodden into manure)

What a village's meadow was, how large it was against the fields, and the forms it took are at Did a village keep a meadow? This question is the town's: the ground that fed the horses of a road town, and how it looks on the map.

What the record says. In the Edo period the war horses grew fewer and much fodder ground was turned to green manure, but the pack horses carrying travelers and goods along the highways never ceased and grew more in demand as trade grew, so where those pack horses and the farm horses and cattle were kept, the ground was used as fodder ground in the true sense. The fodder was cut, not grazed where it grew: it was grass and young shoots from the undergrowth of woods and moors, put into the stable as feed and trodden by the horse into stable manure. So the "hayfield" of a Japanese road town is a cut meadow, and the horse's place is the stable in the town.

How large, and how bounded. No readable source gives the size of a road town's fodder ground, or says how a fodder ground was marked off on the ground - by a bank, a ditch, a hedge or nothing but the lie of the land. What is recorded is who held it: most often one village or hamlet, as its commons. The size and the edge are a GUESS.

What this means for the map. The ground a town keeps for its horses is drawn as open grass, not as pasture with fences or animals on it, standing beyond the town's fields on its hill or river side as a village's meadow does. A caption reads it as a fodder meadow; "grazing" in a caption is a GUESS, since the record found cutting and not grazing. Its edge follows the lie of the land - a stream, a slope, the foot of a hill - because nothing readable says it was marked off by anything built.

The rule the map follows: a road town's fodder meadow is a band of open grass past its fields on the side away from the road, meeting the frame; it carries no fence, no pen and no grazing animals. Its width on the sheet and the line of its inner edge are a GUESS, set to read as a meadow beside the fields rather than as a size.
