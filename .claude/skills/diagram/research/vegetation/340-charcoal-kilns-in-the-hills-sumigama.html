Charcoal kilns in the hills (sumigama)

Charcoal burning was once an important income for Japan's hill villages. Some burned it for a living, and farming households burned it as a side job in the slack season of the farming year, in the winter months between the autumn harvest and the spring planting. The kiln was dug into a hillside: a firing chamber about 10 by 7 ft, with a chimney trench rising steeply up the slope from its back.

A kiln stood in the wood it burned, the village fuel wood on the hill ground beyond the fields (Village fuel woods and their coppice (satoyama)). The charcoal a town bought and stored is at Charcoal yards and charcoal stores.

  • Some burned charcoal for a living, others as a side job in the farming slack season.
    A Shikoku town's history says charcoal burning was formerly an important source of income for hill villages, with those who made it their sole trade and those who did it as a side job in the slack season.
  • In that district, hill villages had more burners by trade and field villages more who burned on the side.
    Villages with little field and much hill, such as Hashikami, had more who made charcoal their trade; villages with many fields, such as Yamana and Hirata, had more side-job burners.
  • The side-job villages burned most from about December to April.
    The town's figures for 1936 show Yamana and Hirata burning little in the months of rice planting and harvest, and most in the slack season from about December to about April.
  • How many kilns did a village have?
    At the end of 1936 the district counted its kilns village by village: 35 in Hirata, a field village, and 159 in Hashikami, a hill village. These are whole administrative villages of the twentieth century, each many hamlets, so they give no count for one hamlet before 1868.
  • The period's charcoal was soft black charcoal, cooled in a closed kiln.
    From the late Muromachi into the Edo period, black charcoal was made by letting the kiln cool before it was opened. Rough charcoal had been mostly white charcoal, drawn red-hot from the kiln and smothered with ash outside it. The firm distinction between the two is said to be modern.
  • A kiln was dug into a slope.
    At a site on the southern slope of a river valley in Date, in Fukushima, kilns dated to the late 16th or early 17th century - around the start of the Edo period - were built underground into the sloping ground, though the excavators could not rule out that some were half sunk.
  • How large was a kiln's firing chamber?
    The largest at the Date site measured 2.92 m (~10 ft) by 2.10 m (~7 ft). Two kilns of the same type elsewhere in Fukushima were somewhat larger: 3.29 m (~11 ft) by 2.06 m (~7 ft), and 4.00 m (~13 ft) by 2.60 m (~9 ft), each chamber a horizontally long oval.
  • A chimney trench rose steeply from the chamber's back.
    At the Date kiln it ran 2.36 m (~8 ft), rising at 40 to 60 degrees. Chamber and chimney together run about 4.5 m (~15 ft) along the slope, by this page's sum of the two.
  • In the setting, charcoal burners are hinin.
    The setting's notes list charcoal burners among the hinin, the castes outside the people. In the Japanese hill villages of the record, farming households burned charcoal too; whether farmers also burn in the slack season in the setting, the notes do not say.
