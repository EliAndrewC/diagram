What are the village's three groves - the windbreak belt, the water-mouth cluster and the dooryard copse?

Sources: coggins-minor-2018 (where each grove stands), forests-2020 (the entrance and water-tail groves); the GM's two corrections of 2026-07, and their ruling of the same month on a clump against a house wall; this project's measurement of the scripted hamlets, umca-windbreak-manual, kotobank-yashikirin, tsuijimatsu-jawiki, fengshuilin-zhwiki

A nucleated village's greenery is not one wood but three, and each has its own place. The field survey of southern Chinese villages maps the groves at named topographic points rather than as a ring, so the map draws them the same way:81

  • The back-village belt (后龙林) - the big one: a dense crescent embracing the high windward back of the cluster, nestled against the houses and wrapping the flanks.126 Where the high side is the windward side, and under beishan mianshui (背山面水) it usually is, the back grove is also the wind wall.
  • The water-mouth cluster (水口林) - a smaller, irregular stand of big old trees at the LOW entrance, where the stream and the track leave the valley, locking in the water and marking the way in.82
  • The dooryard copse - bamboo and fruit trees filling the open gaps THROUGHOUT the cluster, so the houses read as embedded in green rather than dusted with it.125

Why one communal grove and not a grove per house. The per-farmstead belt is the DISPERSED settlement's answer; a nucleated cluster shelters itself and cannot fit a house-sized grove bundle onto every farm, so its windbreak is a single village-scale wood - which we take to be the dominant southern Chinese pattern, the grove protected by taboo, and the reason those stands survived as the most mature forest in the region (this rests on general reading; no source is cited).83

The village is LEAFY, not bare (the GM's correction). A southern village was not tan ground with a few tree blobs on it: houses stood among scattered bamboo and dooryard fruit - litchi, longan, persimmon, citrus, mulberry - and some villages were effectively ringed in bamboo (this rests on general reading; no source is cited).84 But the greenery is STRUCTURED. It does not line the main road and it does not shade the paddies or the drying yards: the field and downslope side stays open, which is the same crop-and-drying logic the farmstead grove follows.124

Shape: organic and embracing, never a ruled wall (a second GM correction). A real grove hugs the terrain and wraps the settlement, so the belt is an irregular, terrain-following footprint filled with clumps, deepest in the middle and tapering into horns that wrap the corners; a rectangle and a square read as machine-planted.123 The raggedness of that crescent is a map drawing convention, drawn so the belt does not read as a planted line.

A clump stands against a house, never on it (the GM, 2026-07). Trees grow right up against a wall - directly next to is correct and common, and a few inches of canopy over the eaves is fine - but a clump drawn ON a roof, a threshing yard or a garden reads wrong, because the map loses the building it exists to show.122 The keep-out therefore reserves each house, yard, garden, byre and shed by its own circumscribing radius plus the clump's FULL drawn radius.

The belt is DEEP, and its outline stands back off the houses. The copse, not the belt, is what fills the inner gaps - and the belt is drawn 80-120 ft deep, which packs three or four rows of overlapping canopy; a 30 ft band holds one row and reads as scattered blobs rather than a wall. The belt's SIZE is the one-to-two-hectare figure of the entry above; the depth is what makes that size read as a wall.

The belt keeps out of the gardens' afternoon sun. A working belt about 10 m tall throws some 50 ft of shadow eastward in the late afternoon of the shoulder month, so no windbreak clump stands within 50 ft west or southwest of a yard's or a bed's west edge, and the belt's near face moves out by that lane wherever the wind puts the belt west of the houses.121 The geometry, and the 75 ft lane that was priced and declined with it, are in the homestead research. The lane binds the WINDBREAK's clumps only: the copse is the dooryard's persimmon and bamboo, which the record puts in the yard.

The rule the map follows: a nucleated hamlet or village draws three groves and no more - a windbreak belt on the windward, high side of the house cluster, embracing it and wrapping its flanks; a smaller water-mouth cluster at the low entrance where the stream or track leaves; and a loose copse of bamboo and fruit trees in the gaps between the houses. The field side, the drying yards and the main road stay open, and no grove clump stands on a house, a yard, a garden or a crop.

The rule the map follows: the windbreak belt is drawn 80-120 ft deep and its footprint is set back clear of the front rank of houses, so the clumps inside it are not thrown away for landing on roofs; the gaps left between the houses are the copse's to fill. A belt shallower than about 30 ft reads as a row of blobs and is not drawn.
