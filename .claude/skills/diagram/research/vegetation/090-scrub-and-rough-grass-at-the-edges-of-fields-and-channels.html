Scrub and rough grass at the edges of fields and channels

A paddy field was ringed by a bund, a low earth ridge about a foot tall and a foot or two across its top, which held the water in and was walked as a path. Its grass was cut: today several times a season, and before Japan began to modernize in 1868 in early spring, for green manure plowed into the fields. Cleared land that is no longer cut goes over to scrub, so the cutting kept a band of grass between a crop and the scrub beyond it.

How wide that band ran, no record before 1868 that we read gives; the one old figure is a land survey's reckoning, in 1726, in the Edo period, of about 1.5 ft of bund and strip taken out of each field. On flat ground the bund was most of it; on terraced hill ground the slope face below each field was bund ground too, and could be larger than the field itself.

  • A standard paddy bund today is a foot tall and one to two feet wide across its top.
    An Aomori prefecture standard sets a bund 30 cm (~1 ft) above the higher field, its slopes one to one (the outer face one to 1.2 on its two higher types), and draws its top 40 cm (~16 in) or 60 cm (~2 ft) wide; a guide to farm engineering gives the standard bund a top 30 cm (~1 ft) wide. The bund widths read for older fields are at Bunds between the paddies (aze).
  • Bunds are walked as paths.
    A paddy levee is built and kept up to hold the water in and to let people and their tools pass, and the bund is the footing and passage for inspection rounds, pest control, weeding and fertilizing. A wide one that doubles as a passage for farm work is called an azemichi, a bund path.
  • How wide was a bund walked as a path?
    Up to about 3 ft, a GUESS: no page we read gives a path-bund's width. The figure is near the base of the standard bund, about 90 cm (~3 ft) where its sloping sides meet the ground.
  • How often is bund grass mown today?
    Several times a season. Weeds on a bund harbor pests and disease, so regular mowing is indispensable. Farmers keep their bund grass by mowing or herbicide, two to five times a year on the paddies of one study, and the collective farms of Hiroshima prefecture, surveyed in 2007-08, mowed their bunds with brush cutters 3.4 times a year on average.
  • Before 1868 bund grass was cut for green manure.
    A historical dictionary describes karishiki as the green grass and young shoots gathered in early spring on hills, fields, paddy bunds and the like and plowed straight into the fields as fertilizer. It says the method spread from antiquity and still held an important place in the farming of the Edo period, so that paddies needed wide grass-cutting ground around them. Another dictionary on the same page places it before the Meiji period, when bought fertilizer became general.
  • This record guesses that bund grass was cut for fodder and thatch as well as for green manure.
    It is a GUESS: no page we read says it.
  • How often was a bund cut before 1868?
    As often as today, two to five times a year, a GUESS. The one old timing we read is the early-spring cutting for green manure, and no record before 1868 that we read gives a count a year.
  • Cleared land that is no longer cut or grazed goes over to scrub.
    An encyclopedia's account of meadows says such land first grows lush, and then the grasses are shaded out as scrub and woody plants take hold, on the way back to woodland. Its examples are European and North American, and it says nothing of a field's edge.
  • An Edo-period land survey took about 1.5 ft of bund and strip out of each field's area.
    A historical dictionary defines azebiki as surveying a field with a bund of one shaku (about 1 ft) and a one-shaku strip at each side of it taken out of the field's area; its earliest example, a shogunate order of 1726, reckons the bund and the land beside it at one and a half shaku (about 1.5 ft) to each field. It was a reckoning for tax, not a measured strip, and whether any crop stood on it the order does not say.
  • How far past a field's edge did the cut grass reach?
    About 1-2 m (~3-7 ft), a GUESS. It is wider than the one old figure, the bund and strip of about 1.5 ft a field that the 1726 order reckoned, and a historical dictionary says only that the paddies needed wide grass-cutting ground around them. No record before 1868 that we read gives the width of the grass-cutting ground beyond a bund, no page gives a traditional clean strip between a crop and the growth beyond it, and none gives the width one stroke of a scythe cuts.
  • How much of the ground round a paddy was bund and slope face?
    Little on flat ground, much more on hill ground. The share of farmland that cannot be cropped because it is bund or slope face is set at 2.8% in Nonoichi City, which this record takes to be flat ground, a GUESS. Across hilly Hiroshima it averages 9.5%, and in mountain paddies the slope faces below the fields can be larger than the flooded area itself. Both figures are modern.
  • This record reasons that land hunger kept East Asia's field margins narrow.
    It is our reasoning: nothing we read links scarce farmland to narrow margins, and no page gives a width for any historical margin.
  • Britain's conservation headlands, field-edge strips sprayed only selectively for wildlife, date only from the 1980s.
    A conservation headland is a strip along a field's edge sprayed with pesticide only selectively, a wildlife measure introduced in Britain in the 1980s, so it is no guide to an old field's margin.

Irrigation channels ran beside many fields, and natural streams through the country around them.

  • Was a channel's bank cut like a bund?
    Where a channel runs beside a paddy, this record reads its bank as the bund of that paddy (Where a field meets its ditch), so its grass was cut the same way. No record we read speaks of a channel's bank in particular.
  • A natural stream's banks are grown with water-loving plants.
    The plants along a river's margins and banks are riparian vegetation, and they change from kinds suited to wet ground near the channel to kinds suited to the drier upland.
