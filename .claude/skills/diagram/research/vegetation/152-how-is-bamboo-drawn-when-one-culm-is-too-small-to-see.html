How is bamboo drawn, when one culm is too small to see?

Sources: ridgelineimages-gsi

A culm is only inches across, so at 1 px = 1 ft a bamboo stand has nothing to draw at true scale. Whether a hamlet has bamboo at all, where it stood, and that it grows as a stand of its own are at Bamboo: how common, where it stood; a farmstead's own strip is at Did every farmstead keep its own bamboo?

Why bamboo cannot be drawn as trees. Bamboo scattered through a cedar windbreak is not how bamboo grows (a stand, not a seasoning: where it stood says how it grows), and a glyph the size of one culm reads as nothing at fit zoom, pale dots in a dark belt: a map drawn that way has bamboo and no reader can tell.

How to show it. Japan's own topographic maps solved exactly this: the legend of the GSI (the Geospatial Information Authority of Japan, the national mapping agency) has a distinct bamboo-grove symbol (竹林), separate from the broadleaf and conifer symbols, so the three can be told apart at map scale.

As built (the GM, 2026-08-27: "make that change in the manner that you had previously proposed"). A bamboo stand is drawn as a polygon whose position and extent are to scale, marked with paired culm strokes and a leafy fork in pale yellow-green; bamboo is not mixed into the windbreak or the dooryard copse; whether a hamlet has bamboo at all, and where, varies by settlement - at the cluster's shady side (drawn as its NORTH, the side the house shades), as a thicket at the field margin's shady end, or both; a stand narrower than 14 ft on its short axis is not drawn, and no stand overlaps a paddy. The stand glyph is a map drawing convention - the marks are symbolic, the extent is not; north is drawn as the shady side, the side the house shades - this record's own reading.

On the GSI legend (ridgelineimages.com "Reading GSI Topographic Maps") the bamboo forest (竹林) has its own entry, and its symbol is distinct from the bamboo-grass (笹地, sasa) symbol.
