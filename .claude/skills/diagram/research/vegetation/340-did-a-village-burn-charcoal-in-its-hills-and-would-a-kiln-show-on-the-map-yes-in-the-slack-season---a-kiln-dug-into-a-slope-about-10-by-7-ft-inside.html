Sources: sukumo-charcoal (who burned charcoal, and when; kilns counted per village), fcp-gyoaido-kiln (a measured early-modern kiln), mokutan-jawiki (the closed-kiln black charcoal of the period), l7r-castes (charcoal burners in the setting's castes)

Where a village's fuel wood stood - beyond its fields, on the hill ground - is taken up in its own question on this page, and a kiln stands in that wood. The charcoal a town bought and stored is at the charcoal yards.

Who burned it, and when. Charcoal burning was once an important income for hill villages, done both by those who made it their trade and as a side job in the farming slack season; in one Shikoku district, villages with little field and much hill had more burners by trade, villages with much field more who burned as a side job, and those side-job villages burned most from about December to April. In 1936 the same district counted its kilns village by village - 35 in Hirata, a field village, and 159 in Hashikami, a hill village. The kiln of the period was a closed one: from the late Muromachi into the Edo period, soft black charcoal was made by letting the kiln cool before it was opened, though the firm distinction between black and white charcoal is said to be modern.

What a kiln looked like. At a site on a hill slope in Fukushima, kilns of the late 16th to early 17th century were dug into the slope, the firing chamber of the first measuring 2.92 m by 2.10 m (about 9.6 by 6.9 ft), with a chimney channel 2.36 m (about 7.7 ft) long running up the slope behind it; the excavator leaves open whether some were fully or half sunk. The whole works - chamber and chimney - runs about 15 ft along the slope.

In this setting. The GM's notes list charcoal burners among the hinin, the non-people castes. That is a DEVIATION from the Edo village, where a farming household burned charcoal in the slack season: here a kiln in a village's wood is a charcoal burner's. Whether farmers also burn in the slack season is not in the notes.

What this means for the map. A kiln shows: at 15 ft long it is larger than a shed, and a village or hamlet sheet with hill wood beyond its fields may carry one, dug into the slope inside the wood, its chimney end uphill. How many is a GUESS, rolled per settlement from how much hill wood the sheet has - none on a sheet with no hill ground, one or two where the sheet's edge is wooded hill. The 1936 counts are for whole administrative villages and a later century, so they set no number for one sheet.

The rule the map follows: a charcoal kiln is a mound about 10 ft by 7 ft with a chimney trench about 8 ft long running up the slope from its back, set inside hill woodland with its long axis down the fall line and a path to it from the nearest lane. Zero kilns on a sheet with no hill wood; otherwise one, and a second on about one sheet in three - the count and the rate are a GUESS.
