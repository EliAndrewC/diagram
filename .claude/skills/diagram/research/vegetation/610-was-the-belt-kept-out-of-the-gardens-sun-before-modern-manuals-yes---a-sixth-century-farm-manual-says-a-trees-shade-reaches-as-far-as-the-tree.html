This question is whether a planter before modern times kept trees off the crops' sun at all.

Yes, and the rule is written down. The Qimin Yaoshu, the Chinese farm manual of the sixth century, says that the elm shades the ground and that the five grains do not grow beneath its shade, gives how far the shade reaches - on the east, the west and the north, as far as the tree itself, according to its height and breadth - and tells the planter to set the elms on the north edge of the garden ground, the side from which no noon shadow falls on it.

What this means for the map. Keeping the belt out of the gardens' sun is an old rule, and the maps draw it as accurate. The manual's reach, read as one tree's height, is about 33 ft for the belt's working height of 10 m; the maps keep a wider 50 ft, worked from where the sun stands in the mid-afternoon of the months either side of summer, and that geometry holds in any century. The manual's figure is the narrower of the two and the older; the maps keep 50 ft.
