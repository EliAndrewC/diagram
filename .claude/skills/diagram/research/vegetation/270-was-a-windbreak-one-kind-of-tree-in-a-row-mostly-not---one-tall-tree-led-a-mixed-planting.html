Was a windbreak one kind of tree in a row? Mostly not - one tall tree led a mixed planting

Sources: takehara-2004-yashikirin (the species counts, the dominant cedar, the rows and the clipped hedge in four regions), tonami-yashikirin-haichi (the Tonami grove built round cedar), udworks-igune (the igune's four tall trees), yashikirin-jawiki (the Izumo grove mainly black pine), furusato-ikiru-tsuijimatsu (the pines clipped to one height), hu-2011-fengshui-patches (the Chinese village grove as mixed broadleaf forest)

A map could draw a windbreak as a wood like any other, or as a line of one kind of tree - darker, taller and in rank - so that it reads apart from the looser woods near it. Which is right depends on what the windbreaks were made of. What they were for, and which sides they stood on, is in What are the village's three groves? and Does a shelter belt wrap the settlement?

The Japanese farmstead grove: one tall tree in the lead, many others with it. A 2004 survey of homestead groves in four regions of Japan - Isawa in Iwate, Iide in Yamagata, Tonami in Toyama and Hikawa in Shimane - counted on average 13.3, 9.8, 17.8 and 8.8 kinds of tree per homestead. In the three regions of summer-green forest, cedar grew at every homestead and was the dominant tree. At Iide and Tonami the grove was planted in rows, at Iide thin enough to see the house through, at Tonami thick enough to hide it. The Tonami research institute puts it the same way: the grove is built round cedar, with many kinds of tree besides. Around Sendai the igune has four tall trees as its frame - cedar, zelkova, alder and black pine, over 20 m high - with many middle and low kinds. So the planted windbreak was ranked and led by one tall conifer, but it was not a single species.

The one pure line: the clipped pine wall of Izumo. On the Izumo plain the homestead grove (tsuijimatsu) is mainly black pine, planted on the west and north of the house and clipped to a set height every four or five years, so that it stands as a trimmed wall. Even there the survey found two other trees, mochinoki and matebajii, among the three commonest with the pine, and it describes the grove as clipped like a hedge.

The Chinese village grove: a mixed broadleaf wood. The fengshui woods behind villages in the Pearl River Delta held some 47 kinds of tree per sampled patch on average, and their measures match those of a well-developed evergreen broadleaf forest rather than a conifer planting.

So there are two forms, and the map rolls between them. A village's windbreak belt is drawn either (1) conifer-led: darker, taller crowns set in rank along the windward side, with lesser broadleaf crowns among them - the Japanese grove's form, drawn at village scale, which is an interpolation, since every survey here is of a single farmstead's grove; or (2) mixed broadleaf: an irregular wood of rounded crowns like the woods around it - the Chinese village grove's form. The clipped single-species pine wall is a third form, but it belongs to one house, not to a village, and the map does not draw it at village scale. Neither form is a line of one kind of tree, and neither is drawn as one. The share of conifer in the first form is not measured by any source read here; it is a guess that the conifer is the most common crown in the belt.
