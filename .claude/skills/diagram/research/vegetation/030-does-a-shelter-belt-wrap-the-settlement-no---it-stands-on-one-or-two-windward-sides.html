Does a shelter belt wrap the settlement? No - it stands on one or two windward sides

Sources: coggins-minor-2018 (the separate patches at named topographic points), yashikirin-jawiki (the homestead grove enclosing the whole house before Meiji, and on the north and west sides after), irie-2020-igune (the grove formed on the north or the west and lacking the south or the east, and kept that way for centuries), sendai-igune-hozen (the city's encircling definition of the word), umca-windbreak-manual (what a gap does to the wind, and how a crossing that cannot be avoided is cut); the GM's rulings of 2026-08-29. No readable source was found for what the grove's open flank is used for, kisetsufu-jawiki, katabatic-wind-enwiki

The ruling that opened the question (the GM, 2026-08-29): "The actual ruling that I have on whether or not it is okay for a windbreak forest to have gaps is that we should just do whatever was historically true. If we believe that windbreak forests would have had such gaps based on our historical findings, then we can give them gaps. Otherwise, we should not have any gaps." The research pass that followed changed the shape of the rule rather than settling a number.

The question had been posed wrongly. It asked whether a belt is an unbroken wall or a wall with holes, and neither record describes a single continuous perimeter in the first place. In China, at village scale, the grove is a SYSTEM OF SEPARATE PATCHES: the survey maps them at named topographic points - the back or master-mountain grove, the water-head grove, the water-mouth grove downstream, and groves at the mountain gaps where the winds are strongest - with the paddies lying in the open ground between them, and that open ground is not narrow. In Japan, at farmstead scale, the grove is built on ONE OR TWO SIDES by design: the northeastern igune stands on the homestead's north and west, the winter-monsoon-facing sides, and a 2020 study of the igune states the rule with its reason: because the winter winds of the Sendai plain are mainly north-northwesterly and strong, the grove is formed on the north or the west side of the homestead plot and often LACKS the south or the east side. Groves built that way have been kept as windbreaks for several hundred years. That the open flank is the one the entrance, the garden and the yard take is on no page read. A city conservation page defines the WORD the other way, as trees planted so as to encircle the homestead, but that is a gloss on the term rather than a description of built form: the scholarly record gives the two-sided grove as the long-standing shape and the ring as the loose modern reading, which is the way round a pre-Meiji setting wants.

So the distinction that matters is between an ABSENT FLANK and a HOLE IN A PLANTED RUN, and only the second is a defect. A belt that does not wrap the settlement is correct and historical. A bare 40 ft run in the middle of the side the belt DOES occupy is attested nowhere, and shelter planting holds it to be actively harmful: a gap funnels the wind, and downwind of it the air often moves FASTER than it does over the open field. The attested remedy for a crossing that cannot be avoided is geometric rather than a screen across the opening: the opening is cut at an ANGLE to the prevailing wind, and a lane through a belt is put 100 to 500 ft from the belt's END rather than through the middle of its run. The physics of air through a constriction holds in any century; the angled crossing is a modern extension service's design rule, borrowed here because nothing read says how a Chinese or Japanese planter cut an access through a belt.

What that makes of the 30 ft threshold: a map drawing convention, not a finding. No source reached - Chinese or Japanese, historical or agronomic - gives a WIDTH for an opening in a shelter belt, neither for the open flank, nor for an access gate, nor for the water-mouth passage the stream and the road already run through. The 30 ft bar at which a bare run across the wind counts as a hole is ours, and is labeled ours. What IS sourced is the direction of the rule: within the planted run, close the holes.

A knob the record handed us, and the ruling that declined it. Both forms are reported of the Japanese homestead grove: the Izumo tsuijimatsu enclosed the entire circumference of the house before Meiji and was later reduced to a hook covering only the north and west sides as flood risk fell and building styles changed. Where the record supports two forms, the map ordinarily rolls between them from settlement to settlement rather than picking one. The GM ruled for the hook and against the knob (2026-08-29): "I think that having a north and west hook is what we want because that is where things ended up, and Rokugan has been around for a long time. So I think they would have ended up in the same place and for the same reason. It also communicates that wind tends to blow from the northwest to the southeast, which is an interesting geographical fact about China and Japan, which we have decided is also true for Rokugan in most places when the local geography does not override the regional geography."

Two reasons, and the second is the one worth keeping. The first is that a long-settled country converges on the same answer for the same reason, so the END STATE is the one to draw. The second is that the belt's shape is communicative: a reader who sees belts on the north and west of most settlements is being told a real fact about the regional wind, and a per-settlement full-ring roll would scramble that signal for variety's sake. This is the rare case where two supportable answers are answered with a ruling instead of a knob - not because the second form is unsupported, but because the first carries information the map is trying to convey. The full-ring form stays recorded here and undrawn.

The regional-against-local rule the GM describes is already how the maps are built - northwest by default, because at the scale of a province that is the right answer, while any one map's windward quarter is its upslope bearing, cold air draining downhill making the high side and the windward side the same fact. And the belts already draw as hooks. Measured over the scripted hamlets, the arc each belt subtends around its own cluster: 102 degrees, 146, 97, 136 and 129 - one side or two sides apiece, none past 200 degrees, none wrapping its settlement.

The rule the map follows: a shelter belt stands on the windward one or two sides of the settlement and is never carried round it. Its absent flanks are not gaps and need no explanation. Within the side it does occupy the planting is continuous: a bare run wider than about 30 ft measured across the wind is a hole and is filled, and where a lane or a track must cross the belt the planting resumes on both sides of the crossing rather than stopping for it.
