Why is the hillside past the grove open scrub rather than more forest?

Sources: fan-2003-forest-floods (the stripping of China's hill forests), satoyama-jawiki (red pine, grass hill and bare hill; the 1894 estimate; the grass hill kept treeless), rinya-meiji-chisan (the hills degraded by the Edo period), biwahaku-hageyama-2024 (one village's common hill cut bare), satoyama-enwiki, coggins-minor-2018

The grove is green because it is protected, and the hills around it are not. China's forests were lost to fire clearing and fire hunting, farming, war, building and fuel, and under the Qing a fast-growing population sent great numbers of "shed people" into the mountain forests of central China to clear and plant the slopes. The taboo on cutting the grove is exactly WHY it is lush while the slope behind it is meadow and scrub, so drawing more forest out there would undo the point of the grove.

How far the stripping went: Japan's record measures it. By the early modern period most of Japan's village hills had become red pine wood, grass hill or bare hill. In 1894 a professor at Tokyo Imperial University's College of Agriculture reckoned that of the land counted as forest, only 30% was under trees and the other 70% was bare red hill. A village's grass hill was clear-cut on purpose and kept free of trees for the dry grass it gave. The Forestry Agency says the hills were considerably degraded by the Edo period, and names one in the south of Shiga known as "the bald of Tanakami". One village's own papers show how it happened. At Minami-Tsuda, on the outskirts of Omihachiman, felling standing trees was forbidden but cutting brush was free, and brush cutting is thought to have taken the common hill down to bare ground, a shortage the documents trace back to the Genroku era (1688-1704); brush ran so short that over eight tenths of the year's brush was bought in, and the map drawn at the start of Meiji draws most of the hill as bald. So the hill past a village's protected wood ran from grass and brush through thin red pine to bare ground, and the map draws the middle of that range - open scrub with a few scraggly pines, neither forest nor bare earth. Where on that range a given map sits is calibrated liberty, not a finding.

The land runs in an order, and the commons sits outside the grove. The sequence away from the houses is village, then back grove, then fuel-and-fodder commons, then whatever lies past the map - so the commons stands on the windward, high side and FURTHER out than the belt, never between the belt and the houses. The hill country around a village was a mosaic of woods, fields and grassland, and this record guesses that the ordering is the fuel commons' alone: rough hill grazing, pasture and the managed woodland patches are general hinterland land and stand wherever the ground is marginal.

How it is drawn, and why it has no outline. The commons is deliberately OPEN and SPARSE - coarse grass tufts, low brush and a few scraggly pines - so that it reads apart from the dense dark closed-canopy grove at a glance. It carries no solid fill and no hard outline, because a filled four-sided patch reads as a literal rhombus lying on the hillside. Instead the scatter feathers out to nothing at its margin, so the ground has no boundary at all, and the band is drawn as a ragged strip paralleling the grove's back at a jittered width and UNDER the grove, so the canopy laps its inner edge and wood and scrub abut with no bare seam between them. The open degraded cover is the finding above, drawn at the middle of its range, and the feathered edge and the absent outline are map drawing conventions.

The rule the map follows: the fuel-and-fodder commons is drawn beyond the windbreak on the high, windward side - village, grove, commons, off-map - and never between the grove and the houses. Rough grazing, pasture and woodland patches are not held to that order and clothe any dry marginal ground. No commons of any kind is drawn on the flooded rice fields, and the dry margins are filled as one continuous band around the settlement rather than as isolated corner patches, because this record guesses that in a valley every edge of the map is marginal land of the same sequence.
