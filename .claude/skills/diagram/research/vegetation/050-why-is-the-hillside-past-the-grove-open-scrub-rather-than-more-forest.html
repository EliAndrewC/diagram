Why is the hillside past the grove open scrub rather than more forest?

Sources: satoyama-enwiki; none cited - the claim below rests on general reading of southern China's deforestation and of forest-rehabilitation writing, neither of which the record names by work, coggins-minor-2018

The grove is green because it is protected, and the hills around it are not. Southern China's hills were progressively stripped for firewood and timber over roughly a thousand years, so past the village's protected band the ground is cut over and open: a monotonous succession of hills either deforested or carrying thin open stands of masson pine and China fir, with severe erosion on the worst of them (this rests on general reading; no source is cited). The taboo on cutting the grove is exactly WHY it is lush while the slope behind it is bare, so drawing more forest out there would undo the point of the grove. How far that stripping went is a guess, pending a source; the direction of it - protected band, degraded hinterland - is what the map draws.

The land runs in an order, and the commons sits outside the grove. The sequence away from the houses is village, then back grove, then fuel-and-fodder commons, then whatever lies past the map - so the commons stands on the windward, high side and FURTHER out than the belt, never between the belt and the houses. That ordering is the fuel commons' alone: rough hill grazing, pasture and the managed woodland patches are general hinterland land and stand wherever the ground is marginal.

How it is drawn, and one form that failed. The commons is deliberately OPEN and SPARSE - coarse grass tufts, low brush and a few scraggly pines - so that it reads apart from the dense dark closed-canopy grove at a glance. It carries no solid fill and no hard outline: an early version drawn as a filled quad read as a literal rhombus lying on the hillside. Instead the scatter feathers out to nothing at its margin, so the ground has no boundary at all, and the band is drawn as a ragged strip paralleling the grove's back at a jittered width and UNDER the grove, so the canopy laps its inner edge and wood and scrub abut with no bare seam between them. The open degraded cover is the same guess as above, and the feathered edge and the absent outline are map drawing conventions.

The rule the map follows: the fuel-and-fodder commons is drawn beyond the windbreak on the high, windward side - village, grove, commons, off-map - and never between the grove and the houses. Rough grazing, pasture and woodland patches are not held to that order and clothe any dry marginal ground. No commons of any kind is drawn on the wet crop, and the dry margins are filled as one continuous band around the settlement rather than as isolated corner patches, because in a valley every edge of the map is marginal land of the same sequence.
