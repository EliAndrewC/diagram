Sloping ground and hillsides

Wet rice is grown in level basins of standing water, so a farmed slope is broken into level steps, and the water that floods each step enters on the high side and leaves on the low. On a steep hillside the steps are terraces you can see; on a gently tilted valley floor they are too low to notice, and the fall of the land shows only in which way the water runs. What follows rests on general reading, not on a source we cite.

  • On a steep slope the paddies become terraces.
    They are long, narrow, curved strips that follow the contour, with a riser between one and the next. This rests on general reading; no source is cited. The sourced account of hillside rice land is at Terraced and valley paddies (tanada, yachida).
  • On a gently tilted valley floor the steps cannot be seen.
    The paddies read as a flat patchwork, the level steps between them too slight to notice. This rests on general reading; no source is cited.
  • Water enters the paddies high and leaves them low.
    The source and the pond lie on the high side, the ditches that share out the water fan down from there, and a drainage ditch leaves from the low corner. This rests on general reading; no source is cited.
  • Wet rice needs a way for water out as well as in.
    So a drainage outlet is an expected feature of rice land in its own right. This rests on general reading; no source is cited. Where a field's drains ran, and why, is at Field drains (akusuiro).
