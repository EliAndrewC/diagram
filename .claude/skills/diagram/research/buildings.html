Historical grounding: the "why" behind the Mode A realism checks

The research behind the compound plans - the magistrate's manor and its kind: what was found, the decision it drove, and any deliberate departure from literal reality.

Administrative culture is JAPAN-first for compound interiors

Sources: takayama-jinya-city, takayama-jinya-jawiki (Japan); neixiang-yamen-zhwiki, henan-neixiang (China)

The primary models for a magistrate's manor are the Edo-period daikansho/jin'ya (rural intendant's office; Takayama Jin'ya is the surviving anchor)21 and machi-bugyōsho (urban magistracy). The Chinese county yamen corroborates the same compound grammar - walled enclosure, office-front/residence-rear,3 raised hall over kneeling litigants,4 in-compound shrines,5 granary + archive + jail6 - and is drawn on for enrichment, but where the two traditions differ (jail scale, clerk counts, codified axial symmetry)7 the Japanese civilian form leads, scaled to L7R demographics.

Office in front, residence behind - the two-court split is universal

Sources: neixiang-yamen-zhwiki ("前衙后邸…前朝后寝制度" - office in front, residence behind, as regulation); takayama-jinya-jawiki (the office block rebuilt as one range in 1816)

Chinese regulation mandated it9 outright8 ("front yamen, rear residence"); Japanese compounds did the same without codification (Takayama's office wing vs residence wing;1110 the Edo south magistracy's street-side office half and rear private half - unsourced). The chief's household living inside the working compound is the institution's point: the office IS a household, and the guarded internal gate between the courts is the hinge between state and home.

Every administrative compound keeps a shrine

Sources: neixiang-yamen-zhwiki (the jail-god shrine; the yamen-god and earth-god shrines are on the Henan page). The Jōge jin'ya excavation plan with its two shrines has no readable source (searched 2026-08-28: the Fuchū city 上下代官所 pages, k-yagumo.sakura.ne.jp/web5/daik.htm, daikanpanfu2.pdf - not found)

Even a 3-man Japanese branch office (Jōge jin'ya, its excavated plan - unsourced: the plan was searched for and not found again) had two small shrines inside its walls, one of them an Inari-sha; a full Chinese yamen had three (yamen-god, earth-god, jail-god).1312 So the shrine is generic equipment, not a particular; what varies is scale and dedication. Ochiba's two-altar hall is the deliberate exception, justified by its priest-magistrate. The rule (a modest shrine, Inari default) does not depend on the count.

Cells are remand, not punishment

Sources: tenmacho-jawiki, chuo-royashiki; neixiang-yamen-zhwiki, henan-neixiang (the southwest jail). The "west is the death direction / western ghost gate" gloss is unsourced (searched 2026-08-28, not found)

Edo jails held the accused pending judgment;14 sentences were exile, flogging, fines, or execution - not, in the ordinary case, prison time15 (ja.wikipedia 伝馬町牢屋敷 names 永牢 and 過怠牢 as real, if exceptional, prison-as-sentence categories held there alongside the remand prisoners23) - and light offenders were consigned to their home village (unsourced). Purpose-built prisons existed as separate metropolitan compounds, the largest being Edo's Tenmachō (there were also jails at daikan offices and other magistracies)72 (~8,850 m2,1716 its own moated block).1918 Cell PLACEMENT is deliberately a free variable: Chinese regulation put the jail in the southwest corner2120 (Neixiang;22 Pingyao "west side"; the gloss that west is the death direction and that the condemned left by the western "ghost gate" is unsourced), but Rokugan has no equivalent directional rule, so no placement check exists (GM decision, 2026-07). The rule (small remand cells, no prison blocks) does not depend on the exceptions: the ordinary sentence was not confinement.

Clerks are few, local, and heimen

Sources: the setting's own caste notes (scribes are heimen artisans) and budgets; tedai-jawiki (who the tedai were)

Both traditions ran the paperwork on locally-hired commoners under a tiny elite staff: Japanese tedai24 were hired from the peasants and townsmen who knew the district, most of them the children of village and town officials;26 Chinese counties ran on dozens of statutory (hundreds of actual) local clerks and runners.25 Scaled to an L7R county (~6,800 inhabitants, vs ~100,000-200,000 for a Chinese county), that becomes ~3-4 clerks at ~4-5 koku/yr. They are scribes by caste (heimen artisans, in the setting's caste notes), live in town, commute to the manor, and as permanent locals are the office's institutional memory under successive magistrates - and its natural tax-farming pressure point.

Staff housing spans a real spectrum

Sources: hatchobori-jawiki (the yoriki/doshin 組屋敷 apart from the office), takayama-jinya-jawiki (the storehouse-keepers' rowhouse; it names neither the staff nor their housing). The rural half - ~30 staff in on-grounds rowhouses at a jin'ya - is unsourced (searched 2026-08-28, not found for Takayama)

Rural jin'ya housed staff on-grounds in rowhouses70 (the ~30 figure is unsourced); it was the big urban magistracies that externalized the workforce to a separate constables' district (Edo's Hatchōbori),27 keeping only the magistrate's household inside the walls. Both ends are historical; the county-manor default is on-grounds, per the setting's staffing (a working platoon, mostly without dependents).

A compound wall is a building, not a boundary line

Sources: hei-jokaku-jawiki (the wall's thickness and its roof), kojodan-dobei (the frameless dobei and the rammed-earth tsuijibei)

Japanese compound walls of this class - the neribei plastered earth wall over a post core, or a dobei on a stone plinth - are a foot or more thick at the base (1 shaku, ~30 cm, or more;30 a frameless neribei about 30 cm29), the rammed-earth tsuijibei up to ~1 m (~3.3 ft),64 and they carry their own tiled coping,28 which is why the vocabulary draws them as a thick stroke rather than a hairline - at 3 ft, a tsuijibei-class figure kept as a map drawing convention (the GM's ruling below). Thickness is what makes the wall a wall: it is what stops a determined man,32 what carries the roof tiles that keep the earth core dry, and what the compound spends real money on. So the ground under it is occupied ground. Buildings ringing a jin'ya court back onto that wall - eaves nearly touching, rear wall a foot or two off it so the two roofs shed separately and the wall stays reachable for patching (unsourced) - which is exactly "abut, do not overlap." Note the direction of the departure from literal reality: we do NOT thin the wall to buy a building an extra foot. A drawn overlap is not a small inaccuracy either, because the wall is painted last and eats the structure's outline, so the map actively shows the building dissolving into the masonry.

GM ruling (2026-08-28) - a MAP DRAWING CONVENTION, disclosed: the 3 ft wall stays as a rendering convention for legibility. The precedent for a wall that thick is the tsuijibei (築地塀, rammed earth on a plinth, up to ~1 m - kojodan-dobei); the ordinary neribei / dobei of a county compound was ~1 shaku31 (hei-jokaku-jawiki). The interactive map tells its reader exactly this: drawn thicker than the typical wall so the stroke reads, with the tsuijibei as the real form it resembles.

The granary is a staging node, not the terminal store

Sources: takayama-jinya-city (the 御蔵 moved from the castle third bailey in 1695,34 and among the largest grain kura in the country35), takayama-jinya-jawiki (the numbered kura rows). "Staged for all of Hida" and the hired-boat transport are unsourced (searched 2026-08-28, not found in the pages read)

Tax rice flowed from village granaries through the administrative compound toward central stores; the office kura held rice in transit plus a local reserve. Remote, high-transport-cost territory kept large on-site granary rows instead (Takayama's onkura,33 staged for all of Hida - the staging is unsourced). Which end a county sits on is readable from its settlement map - water access means through-flow, isolation means storage. The rice moved on HIRED commoner boats under the office's seals (unsourced) - the magistracy owns no hulls; its control is documentary (a tally office at a river landing models this, per Hayakawa).

The courtroom is a room of the office hall, not a freestanding stage

Sources: takayama-jinya-jawiki (the 1816 rebuild lists 玄関・吟味所・御役所・大広間 as one block),36 takayama-jinya-city (the 吟味所 and 白州 as one paired feature)37

At Takayama Jin'ya the office wing - reception rooms, day office (goyōba), official study - is the dominant public building,38 and the hearing court is one room of that block.39 A magistracy drawn with only a narrow ceremonial dais-pavilion has nowhere for the magistrate and samurai staff to do the daily paperwork that is most of the job; the residence's private study is the wrong side of the state/home hinge for official business. So the dais band is the FRONT of a deeper office hall (day office + official study behind it).

No interrogation room

Sources: takayama-jinya-city ("吟味所、白州はグリ石敷で屋根のあることが特徴的である" - the examination room beside the roofed court); the Rokugan decision is the GM's (2026-07)

Edo justice used codified judicial torture,41 and Takayama preserves its interrogation room (ginmisho) beside the hearing court.40 In Rokugan torture is unusual though not strictly forbidden, so magistracies keep no room purpose-built for it - questioning happens in the day office or the hearing court like any other business.

Poverty texture is historically genuine

Sources: none found (searched 2026-08-28: 代官 借財 役所経費 不足, daikan debt - neither the "roughly 90% of intendants carried personal debt" figure nor the snow-on-the-verandas complaint was located in any page read; search syntheses agree only that daikan offices were chronically under-resourced) - a GUESS

Office funding chronically undershot real costs - the Edo office surcharge never covered expenses, roughly 90% of intendants carried personal debt, and one administrator complained in writing of snow drifting onto his verandas (both specifics unsourced). A poor magistracy should LOOK underfunded: patched walls, weathered timber, a hall too small for its ceremonies. The shabby end of the posting-wealth knob is qualitative and does not rest on the 90%.

Guest doors feed courts, not flanks

Sources: the GM's rule (2026-07); the historical staging (gate -> court/garden -> genkan) is unsourced

Historical arrival was STAGED: gate, then court or garden, then the formal stepped entrance (genkan) - a visitor of rank never stepped from the street into a room or against a building's side. Service circulation is the deliberate inverse (the kitchen postern opens into work space). On the Hayakawa sheet the guests' river door opens into the guest garden, not the sliver beside the guest house's flank.

An ancestral alcove holding office-predecessor tablets is proper only at a LINEAGE-HELD posting

Sources: GM canon 2026-07 (the Nagahara Reiji as a provincial lineage); mingguanci-zhwiki (the 名宦祠 at the Confucian temple - a STATE hall for meritorious local officials, not a shrine inside the yamen).42 The Chinese yamen practice of venerating predecessors in office is unsourced

Where a lineage holds the magistracy across generations, the past magistrates ARE the current one's forebears and the alcove is literally ancestral - Hayakawa is such a posting (the Nagahara Reiji are a provincial lineage; GM canon 2026-07), which its guest-wing "9th magistrate" accretion also reflects. At an exam-appointed magistracy, predecessor veneration belongs in the office hall if anywhere, and the residence alcove holds the magistrate's own family tablets.

The Mode A scale is 3 px = 1 ft

Sources: takayama-jinya-city (the designated site, "員数 11,219.05平方メートル" - ~9,800 m2 without the front plaza65); the Jōge ~1,900 m2 figure is unsourced (see "Every administrative compound keeps a shrine")

WHY this number (GM decision, 2026-07): the real anchors bracket a county magistracy neatly - the excavated 3-man Jōge branch office was ~230×88 ft (~1,900 m2; unsourced) and Takayama Jin'ya's site ~9,800 m2 without its front plaza (11,219 m2 with it), so a 15-samurai county seat belongs between them; at 3 px/ft both pool manors land there (Hayakawa 247×200 ft ≈ 4,600 m2, Ochiba 267×200 ft ≈ 4,950 m2) and the feature sizes come out real (office hall 113×28 ft, hearing court 113×38 ft, cell 23×17 ft, walls 3 ft thick). At 2 px/ft the same drawing claims Takayama-gross area for a mere county post; at 4 px/ft it pinches below what the drawn program occupies. The scale also extends the settlement ladder cleanly: compound 1/3 ft/px, hamlet/town 1, village 2, city 3.

The granary holds grain, not just rice

Sources: hatakata-men-jawiki (dry-field tax commonly in coin, 関東畑永法).66 The "~30-40% of tax value as coin" share, the soybean-bale regions and the Chinese mixed-grain regulation are unsourced (searched 2026-08-28: the NTA 租税史料 page returned unreadable text and zh.wikipedia 常平仓 is silent on grain composition)

What the research found: Edo paddy tax arrived as hulled brown rice in straw bales (unsourced) and dominated a wet-rice county's kura - but the DRY-FIELD share of the tax usually arrived as CASH (Kanto dry-field tax paid in money outright;73 ~30-40% of tax value empire-wide came as coin (unsourced)), with SOYBEANS as the recurring in-kind exception (several regions collected the dry-field tax in soybean bales - the state wanted horse fodder; unsourced); a corner of unhulled rice was kept as famine reserve (stores longer than hulled) (unsourced); and in mountain districts the tax arrived as cash and local goods, the granary shrinking toward a strongroom (unsourced). Chinese county granaries were mixed-grain by regulation (rice south, millet north, plus wheat and soybeans; unsourced). The Rokugan-specific decision (GM canon 2026-07): Rokugan is wealth-rich but coin-poor, so cash commutation is LESS common than in Edo Japan - the dry-field share of a Rokugani county's tax arrives IN KIND (soybean and barley bales beside the rice) more often than its Japanese model, and an upland county's tax arrives as goods rather than coin, making its granary part warehouse-of-goods, part strongbox. So: a wet-rice county's granary is rice-dominant with a visible minority of other grain; the label is "granary".

Fire discipline: halls burn, kura endure

Sources: sado-bugyosho-fires (the Sado magistracy burned and was rebuilt five times)

Administrative halls were ordinary wooden buildings44 and burned repeatedly (the Sado magistracy burned and was rebuilt over and over);43 only thick-walled earthen kura were fireproof,45 which is exactly why tax grain and documents got dedicated storehouse buildings instead of rooms in the hall.

Fire-water is distributed to the halls, not the kura

Sources: tfd-hongou-fire-history (tubs at the doorway and on the roof as routine practice),67 thepaper-taipinggang (308 vats, placed where water was far, per-courtyard by regulation). Not confirmed in the pages read: hand-bucket stacks and the iroha-gumi brigades as part of the same regime, and "EVERY building" as a rule rather than a custom

What the research found: Edo fire precaution kept standing water at EVERY building - tensuioke (rain-fed "heaven-water" tubs) at the doorway and on the roof of each wooden structure, plus hand-bucket stacks, a mandated-plus-customary regime ("keep water ready at each frontage," reinforced by the iroha-gumi town-brigade system). The placement logic is distribution to the point of RISK: priority to the flammable wooden buildings, weighted hardest to the kitchen (open kamado, live flame all day). Ming/Qing China corroborates exactly - the Forbidden City's 308 taiping-gang4746 / men-hai ("gate-sea", unsourced) water vats sat per-hall / per-courtyard / per-gate, graded by importance48 and deliberately placed to fill coverage gaps away from the wells.49 The decision: draw ~8-12 fire-water tubs distributed one per major wooden building, 2 at the kitchen, and the fireproof plaster kura carries none - a token pair clustered at one storehouse is both too few (below the per-building custom) and, if placed at the fireproof kura, backwards (defending the one building engineered not to burn). The tubs are drawn at ~2.5 ft - true tensuioke scale (unsourced), and one may sit at the vented WOODEN granary as yard coverage since - unlike the plaster kura - it is flammable. These tubs are a compound-plan feature only. At settlement scale a town/city map draws buildings as footprints and renders NO per-building fixture - no per-house well, privy, hearth, or fire-tub (the settlement map keeps even the rain barrels feeding a well's shaft off the map, showing only the shared wellhead) - so per-building fire-water is PRESUMED present, not a rendering gap. Fire defense still appears on those maps at the right grain: the communal fire-watch tower (hinomi-yagura), which walled towns and cities carry by default. Same concern, two scales: the tubs fight the fire at the building, the tower spots it for the town - tub glyphs are not scattered onto a settlement map (they would be 1-3 px noise across hundreds of buildings).

The compound has a size HIERARCHY, not just individual sizes

Sources: the three anchors are unsourced - Izumo's 二間四方 (searched 2026-08-28: genbu.net unreadable; ja.wikipedia 出雲大社 and ja.wikipedia 大社造 give only the 24 m height), the ~67 tsubo mid-rank house (not found; a yoriki house at 20-30 tsubo is the nearest figure seen, and a 13-class Edo residence scheme runs 70-2,300 tsubo) and the 2-4 horse county stable (not found; the stable page gives a European 3.5 x 3.5 m stall)

What the research found: in a lord's walled compound the domestic footprints rank in a fixed order - the residence is the dominant DOMESTIC building (the administrative office hall may exceed it, being the institution's working core, but no service/utility/storage/sacred building should). Anchors: an elite daidokoro is large but a fraction of the living quarters, not their equal (a whole mid-rank house ≈ 67 tsubo, and a kitchen that size is a tell); a county stable held ~2-4 horses (~55-70 sq ft/stall) so it is a fraction of a ~10-20-man barracks; a Shinto worship hall is small even when grand (Izumo, the national outlier, is ~2×2 ken; a provincial haiden is 3-5 ken (unsourced)), so a shrine hall out-footing the residence is impossible at county tier. The decision: each absolute size gets a ceiling AND the ordering gets its own sweep, because "each building is individually within tolerance" silently permits an impossible ranking (a kitchen at or above the living block, a stable over the barracks, a shrine over the residence). The deliberate departure: the office hall is allowed to be the largest building - it is not domestic, and a magistracy's public working block genuinely dominated (Takayama).50

Packing: a jin'ya is mostly open, and the fix for "too much empty space" is consolidation, NOT a smaller envelope

Sources: the 37-42% coverage was measured on plans in 2026-07 and has no readable source (searched 2026-08-28, not found on the web - the primary is 上嶋善治「Historic Site: the Remains of the Takayama Jinya」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「史跡高山陣屋跡」), 岐阜県文化財保護センター調査報告書1, cited by ja.wikipedia) - a GUESS pending that report; the 3,000 tsubo site / 1,000 tsubo built figure (~33%, just under the 37-42% band) rests on a search synthesis that no page read carries;71 the 12.6% plaza share is derived from the designated-site register

What the research found: courtyard compounds (Edo jin'ya/daikansho, buke-yashiki, Chinese yamen/siheyuan) were organized AROUND open courtyards52 - building footprint covered only ~37-42% of the walled site for a jin'ya (unsourced) (Takayama's front plaza alone is ~12.6% of its site74), and a large open forecourt + the sanded oshirasu (~12-18% of the site) is a load-bearing FEATURE, not slack. So a compound that "feels" empty at ~37% coverage is NOT oversized: the envelope is right (the size-audited Joge<->Takayama band) and shrinking the walls is the wrong fix. The real defect, when there is one, is FRAGMENTATION - real jin'ya CONSOLIDATED functions into a few connected ranges (Takayama's main building is one mass: entrance + offices + official rooms + residence + kitchen under continuous roofs; the storehouses are an abutting ROW),51 whereas a schematic plan tends to scatter a dozen free-standing labeled pavilions with loose slack between them. The decision: keep the envelope and coverage; measure packing (coverage, the largest vacant rectangles, aligned building gaps); tighten only GENUINE loose slack - two ordinary wooden SERVICE buildings on a shared edge should abut into a range or sit at a ~6-8 ft fire-gap, not 12-16 ft apart; a plaster KURA keeps a ~6-10 ft fire-gap; and the forecourt / oshirasu / garden / court-divider spaces are features to preserve, not fill. Deliberate departure: our sheets keep buildings somewhat MORE separated than a real consolidated jin'ya (each stays a distinct labeled footprint for legibility), but the gaps are held to fire-gap width, not ahistorical slack - a documented legibility divergence, not an accident. On the sheets: Hayakawa's west service column (archive/clerks/stables) stands at fire-gaps (9 ft / 6.7 ft), both manors sit at 37-38% coverage, in-band, and Ochiba's cell and barracks form an abutting guard range under the granary with a real 16 ft loading apron beside it. An empty region kept as a feature needs a QUANTIFIED justification - a cart-and-draft-animal loading apron is ~15-20 ft, not 24 (unsourced) - because a plausible name ("cart apron") can launder a void the way a tolerance can launder an oversized building; and packing is judged per region as well as overall, since a locally sparse quadrant hides inside an in-band average (a region that reads sparse because it holds the oshirasu, a garden or the forecourt is a FEATURE).

Rendering / layout is checked automatically, because it is geometry not judgment

Sources: not applicable - a rendering and tooling decision with nothing physical behind it

Seven defects that a reader spots instantly but the author's eye skips are hard checks on every compound sheet - the KEY LESSON being which checks belong in a tool and which in a reviewer's judgment: a rule with an objective geometric answer (draw order, adjacency, overlap) is cheaper and surer as automated code; a rule needing a feature-vs-slack judgment (is this open ground a courtyard or a void?) belongs to the reviewer. The seven: 1. Foreground on the TOP LAYER - anything painted over by a shape drawn LATER in the file reads as buried (a label under a building, a tub under a garden). Convention: draw labels, point glyphs and furniture-scale features near the end of the drawing so their ink sits above every fill. Both sides of this check are deliberately broad: the OCCLUDER side is fill-blind - any later shape counts, not just the fills the tool classifies as building or garden - and the OCCLUDED side spans labels, tubs, wells and every sub-building rect, because a palette grows, so enumerating occluders by fill silently rots, whereas "drawn later and covering it" cannot. One exemption is needed and only one - a shape drawn wholly INSIDE the item it overlaps is that item's own detailing (a well-mouth circle in its curb, a hearth's fire in its hearth), not burial. 2. Rain barrels above gardens - same draw-order rule for tubs specifically (a tub sits ON the grass, not under it). Handled by the same top-layer move. 3. Glyph-group labels beside their glyphs - a label that NAMES a point-glyph group ("fire-water tubs", "well") must sit next to a glyph it names (building labels sit on their rect, so they are exempt; only the small floating groups drift). The check flags one >9 ft from its nearest glyph. 4. Notice board at a gate - a kosatsu is read where people pass, so it must sit within ~15 ft of a wall gate opening (detected as a gap in the wall segments). This auto-satisfies the GM's "between the main gate and the service gate" when both are on one wall. 5. No black-on-black - a dark-ink label laid over a dark BLOCK or the thick wall stroke loses contrast; the check flags it and reports the smallest nudge (tested in each direction) that lands it on clear ground. Tiny dark markers (a kura door, an altar square) are excluded - only a real dark block or wall hurts legibility. 6. No label overlaps - two labels whose (estimated) boxes overlap smear together; flag the pair, move one apart. Relocating a label to satisfy another check - moving the notice board or a fire-water label near a gate or kitchen - easily lands it on a neighboring label. 7. Doors sit ON a wall - a door is an opening in a wall, so its glyph must be flush to / straddle a building edge, not float inside with white space to the wall. The check flags a small dark door-rect fully contained in a building whose nearest edge is close (clearly meant to be that wall's door) but not flush. This is fill-scoped: door glyphs are small dark rects, so the kura's single door is checked; the raised granary's stilts straddle its base edge (not contained) and pass, and the kitchen hearth is too big to be a door. Text bounding boxes are ESTIMATED (font-size x string length), so the checks run conservatively (a small overlap threshold) to avoid nitpicking a 1 px graze. The bold width factor is SPLIT - all-caps band labels run ~0.72 of font-size per character, mixed-case bold only ~0.60 - and both figures were calibrated by rendering real pool strings and reading the ink extents, not guessed: a single 0.72 figure over-measures mixed-case labels by ~26%, enough to report a phantom overlap. Estimator error shows up as CHECK noise, so when a label check fires on something that looks fine in the render, measure the ink before loosening the check.

A dojo is a city institution; county training is courtyard keiko

Sources: hanko-jawiki, edo-three-dojos-jawiki (the commercial fencing dojos of late Edo); jinya-jawiki (a small domain's drill ground - the scope note below)

The city tier (GM 2026-07-25): every provincial city draws exactly one state martial hall plus a count of private dojos rolled from its samurai cohort, with the sizes and the per-capita reasoning in the settlements rule file. The county finding below is the floor of that same ladder. What the research found: the standalone dojo with a resident sensei and enrolled students is an URBAN institution in both anchor cultures. In Edo Japan, formal martial instruction lived in castle towns and cities - the domain (han) school's martial hall,53 and the famous commercial fencing dojos of late-Edo metropolitan life;54 a rural daikansho/jin'ya (the county magistracy's direct model) had NO bugeijo in its program, and rural samurai trained at home in an earthen yard, a hall cleared for practice, or on shrine grounds (unsourced). The Chinese county yamen is starker still: no training hall at all - military drill happened at separate garrison grounds (unsourced). The L7R numbers force the same conclusion from inside the setting: a county town holds ~15 samurai total - no student body, no living for a sensei - while the provincial city (~225 samurai) and capital (~800) are where the setting notes' own dojo references all sit (Toshi Ranbo's "many dojos", the train-at-a-dojo-in-every-clan pilgrimage vow). A county samurai's FORMAL schooling happened in their youth at a clan school in a city; the county posting only maintains that edge with daily keiko, and ambition rides to the provincial city. The decision: no magistracy draws a dojo by default. Every magistracy still TRAINS - the working platoon and the duty watch keep daily practice - but the venue is multi-use open ground in the compound, not a dedicated building. A magistracy WITH a true dojo is an Ochiba-slot particular that characterizes the resident (a noted swordsman, a retired sensei) and should read as a local distinction, the kind that draws young samurai from neighboring counties. The MARKING convention (GM 2026-07-24): every magistracy draws a practice ground - the swept patch + weapon rack + striking-post markers of the vocabulary entry - beside the watch's lodging, sized at ~90-135 sqft per samurai actually drilling (a solo kata footprint runs ~8-10 ft square; paired work roughly doubles it) (unsourced). Marking the GROUND and its GEAR rather than labeling a building is the honest form of the finding - rural practice left equipment, not architecture - and it gives outer-court open ground a quantified packing justification.

The scope of what is read: ja.wikipedia 陣屋 lists a 調練場 drill ground in a small DOMAIN's jin'ya program.55 That is a domain seat, not the bakufu daikansho this entry models, and it is ground rather than a hall - so it CONFIRMS the practice-ground convention drawn here, but it does NOT confirm the negative claim about a daikansho specifically: the page describes no bakufu daikansho program at all, so "a rural daikansho had no bugeijo" rests on no page read either; the word "jin'ya" covers both institutions. The "boom of 1830-1860" date range was found in no page read.

Privies attach to the house; night-soil drives their placement

Sources: guernica-night-soil (tsuke-tsubo;56 farmers paying estates); kotobank-benjo, sinyoken-madori (the privy built into the house; the farm case). The sand box for inspection under the master's privy is unsourced (searched 2026-08-28: matsue-bukeyashiki.jp lists 雪隠 only by name; 房総のむら silent)

What the research found: the samurai-residence kawaya/setchin was built INTO the house6159 (at the rear of the guest parlor,60 off the engawa; the pull-out sand box under the master's for health inspection is unsourced), not a free-standing block in the yard; and a well-appointed house had more than one (a guest privy by the reception suite, a family privy toward the back).62 Night-soil was a contracted, premium commodity57 - farmers PAID daimyo estates for the right to empty their privies68 - so collection was by outside carters emptying cesspits,58 which means pits sit toward a service edge/wall/gate for cart access, never so deep that a collector crosses the inner court. The decision: privy count scales with occupancy (~1 per functional zone, ~3-4 at a county manor), the residence privy attaches to the house with its cesspit to the rear/service wall, and servants'/outer privies line service walls near a gate - "at least one inner, one outer" under-provisions a 40-60-person compound and lets the residence privy float as a detached block.

The shady rear is the service strip

Sources: shoinzukuri-jawiki (the garden south of the formal rooms;69 the north side unaddressed). The north service strip is unsourced (searched 2026-08-28: ja.wikipedia 武家屋敷, 房総のむら 武家屋敷, matsue-bukeyashiki.jp - none discusses garden orientation or a south-formal / north-service split) - reasoning from the sun rule

What the research found: a buke property's prized formal garden sat on the sunny SOUTH side facing the reception rooms, so the shady NORTH rear carried the household's service economy - a vegetable/kitchen garden "in the back," storehouses, a well, the family privy, and the servants'/retainers' quarters (often in the nagayamon range,63 but service functions filled the back yard). The decision: the north band behind the residence is drawn as a service strip (servants' nagaya + utility + attached privy) or narrowed to a ~6-10 ft cart/servant alley; a wide empty band there is internal dead space (the interior counterpart of an over-loose crop) and also leaves the ~10 on-grounds servants - who exist in the staffing anchors - drawn nowhere. Both pool manors draw a servants' quarters and an attached family privy in that band.
