The market-day flophouse - who actually stays over

Sources: kichinyado-jawiki, kichinyado-kotobank (3 mon in 1611, 6 mon in 1658, 24 + 16-24 mon in 1843), thepaper-night-gates (ward gates shut at the dusk drum, city gates barred by night)

A county seat exists partly as the periodic-market center for its hinterland: most peasants come from within a day's round-trip, but those at the far edge of the catchment stay over on market eve in cheap communal lodging - a kichin-yado ("firewood-fee inn"), where you get a roof and the cooking fire for a few mon a night in the seventeenth century (a few tens by the 1840s), the bedding your own or rented for nearly as much again (16 to 24 mon on top in Edo in 1843).

How often the market meets, and how far its customers walk in, is under how often a county town held its market.

The rule the map follows: every town draws one market-day flophouse unless it declares otherwise, and a busy hub draws more. It is deliberately large and plain, and drawn barn-like - no awning, a long row of plain doorways - by this map's own convention, since no page read describes the building's outside; so it reads as nothing like a shophouse, because its whole value is volume at rock-bottom price: it beds dozens on market eve. It stands where travelers arrive: in the gate market outside a walled town's gate (what a gate market was and where it stood: What is in a town's gate market), since the gate shuts at dusk and a peasant arriving late cannot get in at all - this page's inference from the night closing the record attests for Tang and Ming cities, since no page read says a county town's gate shut at dusk - or along the road of an unwalled one, as the kichin-yado stood at the edge of a post town.
