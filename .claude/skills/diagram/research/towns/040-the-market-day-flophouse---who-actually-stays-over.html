The market-day flophouse - who actually stays over

Sources: kichinyado-jawiki, kichinyado-kotobank (3 mon in 1611, 6 mon in 1658, 24 + 16-24 mon in 1843), hiroshige-minakuchi-nheritage (a kichin-yado as an Edo print shows it from the road), huc-journey-chinese-empire (a Qing capital's paid night shelter of one great hall), huc-journey-archive-record (the year of that account's English edition), thepaper-night-gates (ward gates shut at the dusk drum, city gates barred by night)

A county seat exists partly as the periodic-market center for its hinterland: most peasants come from within a day's round-trip, but those at the far edge of the catchment stay over on market eve in cheap communal lodging - a kichin-yado ("firewood-fee inn"), where you get a roof and the cooking fire for a few mon a night in the seventeenth century (some two dozen by the 1840s), the bedding your own or rented for nearly as much again (16 to 24 mon on top in Edo in 1843).

How often the market meets, and how far its customers walk in, is under how often a county town held its market.

The cheap lodging with one shared room is older than modern times in both countries the setting draws on. The kichin-yado of the Edo period and before was the lowest grade of inn, its lodgers in one large shared room. In China Evariste Huc's account, in its English edition of 1855, describes the capital's "House of the Hens' Feathers", a paid night shelter that was "simply composed of one great hall", its floor one layer of feathers, where men, women and children all slept together for a coin a night. What such a house looked like from the road is thinner. The one period picture read is a Hiroshige print of the Minakuchi post station, which shows a kichin-yado as a thatched house, marked by its name written large on its paper doors. How many a house bedded on one night is on no page read, in any period.

The rule the map follows: every town draws one market-day flophouse unless it declares otherwise, and a busy hub draws more. It is drawn plain: a thatched house with one shared room, like the one in the Edo print, with no awning or shop front, so it reads as nothing like a shophouse. The maps do not draw it as a barn with a long row of doorways sized to bed dozens: no source of any period shows that building or that count, and by the GM's rule of 2026-09-28 a form no premodern source shows is not drawn. Its size is a guess: no bigger than a large house, since no page read measures one. It stands where travelers arrive: in the gate market outside a walled town's gate (what a gate market was and where it stood: What is in a town's gate market), since the gate shuts at dusk and a peasant arriving late cannot get in at all - this page's inference from the night closing the record attests for Tang and Ming cities, since no page read says a county town's gate shut at dusk - or along the road of an unwalled one, as the kichin-yado stood at the edge of a post town.
