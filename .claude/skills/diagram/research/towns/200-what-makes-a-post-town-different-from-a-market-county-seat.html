What makes a post town different from a market county seat?

Sources: kotobank-shukubamachi (the post town's form, size, center and ends, from three Japanese reference works), kotobank-shukueki (the even lots and the bent entrance), fujisawa-shukuba-kibo (the household and inn counts of nine stations), kameyama-sekijuku (one station's counts), visitbeijing-jimingyi (a walled Chinese relay town)

A post town was a town that lived by the road. In Japan it was a commercial settlement grown up around a relay station on the highways, its townhouses usually running in a long narrow band along the road, and the relay duty was laid on castle towns, ports and rural market towns alike - the name post town was kept for the ones whose living depended most on forwarding men and horses and on lodging travelers. That is the difference from a market county seat, as this record frames it: the seat exists for its district and its market day (see the gate market), the post town for the traffic passing through, and it is drawn out along the road accordingly.

How big. On the Tokaido, a highway of heavy traffic, an ordinary post town had about 300 to 600 houses and 30 to 60 inns. The official survey of 1843 gives the real spread: along one prefecture's stretch of that road, from 197 houses at Hakone to 1,542 at the castle town of Odawara, and 632 houses with 42 inns at Seki. A great station could run a ri (about 2.4 miles) or more along the road. How long the ordinary ones were is under how long and how deep a town's built core is.

What it must keep standing. The office that forwarded official men and horses, and the inns where traveling lords and the like lodged, stood at about the middle of the town, with the inns, teahouses and cookshops for ordinary travelers and the porters' and pack-horse offices before and behind them, and the other merchants and craftsmen further out. The road through the station was comparatively wide, 2 to 4 ken (about 12 to 24 ft), with a water channel down its middle or along its edge, for fighting fire and for watering men and horses. At each end of a town on the five great highways stood a gate-mound of stone or earth, and the road bent, or turned through a masugata, at the entrances, so that the street could not be seen down from outside.

Its lots. The lots along the road were laid out to a plan as long strips, of nearly constant depth and differing frontage; the station's households were allotted lots of roughly even size and were often freed of ground rent in return for supplying horses or porters. Apart from the station's officials the residents usually farmed as well, and the back of each lot was often a vegetable garden.

Two looks, by traffic. On the busy highways each main house was built across the full width of its lot, and by the later Edo period the two-story, board-roofed inns stood eave to eave. On side roads and in the mountains the inns were often little different in plan from the thatched farmhouses round about, each with a front yard toward the road and a gap to its neighbor. So a post town on a trunk road has a continuous street front, one on a lesser road a row of detached houses with yards.

The Chinese relay town. A Chinese relay station could be a walled town of its own. Jimingyi, the largest surviving one, covers about 56 acres inside a wall about 2,000 m (6,600 ft) round and 11 m high, with gates east and west; five roads divide it into twelve blocks, the relay office at the center, the stables in the northwest, the granary in the northeast, shops and houses among them, and the post road passing east-west along the south side of the town. Whether an ordinary relay station was walled like this one is on no page read.

The rule the map follows: a post town is drawn as a strip along its road rather than around a square: the relay office and the lords' inn at about the middle of the strip, ordinary inns and teahouses on either side of them, other shops beyond; the road through it 12 to 24 ft wide with a water channel along it; the lots even strips of one depth running back from the road, each ending in a garden; a bend or a squared kink in the road at each entrance. The street front is continuous on a trunk road and broken by front yards on a lesser one, the choice rolled per town.
