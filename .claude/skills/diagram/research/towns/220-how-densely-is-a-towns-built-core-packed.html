How densely is a town's built core packed?

Sources: toriimoto-jawiki, nakatsugawa-jawiki and itabashi-jawiki (three post towns' houses over their Edo-period lengths), naraijuku-jawiki (a preserved townscape's breadth today), kotobank-shukubamachi (full-frontage houses and garden backs), kotobank-gaison (the street town as dense)

Dense along the road, open behind it. A town strung along a road is dense by definition: its houses stand close along one or both sides of the street, and on a busy road each main house filled the whole width of its lot. But the density is a band. Behind the houses the lots commonly ran back as gardens, since most of a post town's households farmed as well, so a town's commercial core and its farm ground meet inside the same lot, the house at the street end and the vegetable plot at the back.

The numbers. The Edo period's own counts and lengths give the measure. Toriimoto put 293 houses along about 1.4 km of road in 1843, and Nakatsugawa 228 along about 1.1 km, each about 21 houses to every 100 m of road, both sides counted, or about 32 ft of street front to a house. Busy Itabashi, at the edge of Edo, put 573 along a built street of about 1.7 km, about 20 ft to a house. Each count is of the station's houses, so these are road per house, not the width of a lot; whether every counted house fronted the road is on no page read.

What is not known. How many houses a town held to the acre is not known for the Edo period. The one breadth read, Narai's 200 m across, is the extent of the townscape preserved today, and no dated lot depth was found, so the record gives no houses-to-the-acre figure and the maps do not use one. What share of a town's ground stood under roofs, as against yards, gardens and road, is on no page read either. Nor is a Chinese county seat's density: the pages read give the wall's length but not the households inside it. How many live in a town, and in how many houses, is a separate question (under who lives in a town); this one is only how tightly they are packed.

The rule the map follows: a town's street front is packed - on a busy road the houses fill their lots' frontage - at about 32 ft of street to a house in a post town of two or three hundred houses, down to about 20 ft on a busy great road, and the ground behind each row is its lots' gardens, not open waste. No houses-to-the-acre figure is used. The frontage is a calibration from post towns' Edo-period counts and lengths; the share of the ground under roofs is a guess.
