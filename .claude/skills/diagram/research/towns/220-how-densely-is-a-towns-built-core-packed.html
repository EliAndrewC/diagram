How densely is a town's built core packed?

Sources: naraijuku-jawiki (one post town's houses over its measured area), denken-sekijuku and kameyama-sekijuku (another's length and houses), kotobank-shukubamachi (full-frontage houses and garden backs), kotobank-gaison (the street town as dense)

Dense along the road, open behind it. A town strung along a road is dense by definition: its houses stand close along one or both sides of the street, and on a busy road each main house filled the whole width of its lot. But the density is a band. Behind the houses the lots ran back as gardens, since most of a station's households farmed as well, so a town's commercial core and its farm ground meet inside the same lot, the house at the street end and the vegetable plot at the back.

The numbers. Two post towns give a measure. Seki put 632 houses along about 1.8 km of road, about 35 houses to every 100 m of road, both sides counted, or an average of about 19 ft of street front to a house. Narai put 409 houses on a strip about 1 km long and 200 m across, about 49 acres, or about 8 houses to the acre over the whole strip, road and gardens included. That sits inside the band the record sets for a city's residential quarter, 1.5 to 11 dwellings to the acre (under how densely a city quarter is built), toward its dense end.

What is not known. What share of a town's ground stood under roofs, as against yards, gardens and road, is on no page read. Nor is a Chinese county seat's density: the pages read give the wall's length but not the households inside it.

The rule the map follows: a town's street front is packed - on a busy road the houses fill their lots' frontage, averaging about 19 ft of street each - and the ground behind each row is its lots' gardens, not open waste. Over the whole built strip, road and gardens included, a town carries about 8 households to the acre. Both figures are calibrations from two post towns; the share of the ground under roofs is a guess.
