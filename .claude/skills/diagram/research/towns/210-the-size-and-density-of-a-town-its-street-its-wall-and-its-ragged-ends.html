The size and density of a town: its street, its wall and its ragged ends

A Japanese town that lived by its road was a strip along it: a row of houses on each side of the street, each house on a lot running back from the road, with the house at the street end and often a vegetable garden behind. The Edo period (1603-1868) wrote down its post towns' lengths, and they run from under 900 ft of street at Tsumago to about 5,600 ft at busy Itabashi, with roughly 20 to 30 ft of road to each house counted.

A Chinese county seat was measured by its wall instead, and most were in the smallest class, under 2 km (~6,562 ft) round. At its ends a town did not stop square: along the roads that carried its traffic, the houses ran on.

  • The shogunate's survey of the 1840s recorded the Nakasendo's post towns with their lengths and their houses.
    The survey, the Shukuson Taigaicho, gives the stations on the Nakasendo highway their lengths and their counts of houses; Toriimoto's and Itabashi's figures below come from it.
  • Tsumago, the post town with the shortest street found, ran about 270 m (~886 ft).
    Its street apparently did not change through the Edo period.
  • The post town of Nakatsugawa ran about 1.1 km (~3,609 ft), with 175 houses in the 1790s and 228 in 1843.
    The length held from 1663 to the end of the shogunate, so the town grew denser inside the same street.
  • The post town of Toriimoto ran about 1.4 km (~4,593 ft), with 293 houses in 1843.
    The shogunate's survey of 1843 measured it from one neighboring village's boundary to the next.
  • Itabashi, a busy station at the edge of Edo, had a built street of about 1.7 km (~5,577 ft) and 573 houses.
    It was the first station out of Edo on the Nakasendo, and one of the four stations of Edo. Of its road of about 2.2 km (~7,218 ft), the shogunate's survey of 1841-1844 counted about 1.7 km (~5,577 ft) as built street.
  • A great station could stretch a ri, about 3.9 km (~12,795 ft), or more.
    Stretching long along the highway was the post town's mark, and some of the large stations reached that length.
  • Two post towns preserved today are about as long as the Edo-period records.
    Narai's townscape runs about 1 km (~3,281 ft) along its river and Seki's preserved district about 1.8 km (~5,906 ft). These are the extents of what is preserved today, so the Edo lengths above are the better measure of the period.

Along the street a town was close-built; behind its houses it was not.

  • A post town was two rows of lots deep, with the highway between them.
    The lots on both sides of the highway were laid out as strips of nearly one depth, differing in their frontage. How the post town was laid out and who lived in it is at Towns: the county seat and the post town, and who lives in them (machi).
  • A street town's houses stood close along its street.
    The street town (gaison) is defined by its dwellings standing densely along one or both sides of a street.
  • At the stations of busy highways, each main house filled the whole frontage of its lot.
    At stations with heavy traffic, such as those of the Tokaido and the Nakasendo, the main house was built along the highway across the full frontage of its plot.
  • Behind the houses, the lots commonly ran back as vegetable gardens.
    Apart from the station officials, a post town's households usually farmed as well, and in many cases the back of the lot was a vegetable garden.
  • How much street front did a house have?
    About 32 ft of road to each counted house at an ordinary station, about 20 ft at a busy one. Toriimoto's 293 houses over its 1.4 km (~4,593 ft) and Nakatsugawa's 228 over its 1.1 km (~3,609 ft) each come to about 21 houses to every 100 m (~328 ft) of road, both sides counted; Itabashi's 573 over 1.7 km (~5,577 ft) to about 33. Each count is of the station's houses, so these are road per house, not the width of a lot: whether every counted house fronted the road is on no page we read.
  • No Edo-period figure gives a town's houses to the acre.
    The one breadth read, Narai's 200 m (~656 ft) across, is the extent of the townscape preserved today, and no lot depth from the period was found.
  • No page we read gives the share of a town's ground that stood under roofs.
    The entries and articles read give lengths, house counts and the lot pattern, but no built share against the yards, gardens and road. How closely a city's quarters were built is at How densely is a quarter built, and what counts as empty ground?

A Chinese county seat was most often walled, and its size is recorded as the length of its wall.

  • The Ming and Qing ranked city walls in five classes by the length of their circuit.
    The smallest class, under 2 km (~6,562 ft) round, was the most numerous, and most county seats were in it.
  • Every prefectural city stood in the class above, 2 to 5 km (~6,562-16,404 ft) round.
    The same ranking puts all the cities of prefectures and departments in its fourth class.
  • The small county walls were found especially on the western frontier.
    Many county seats of the eastern coast were larger than the prefectural cities of the west.
  • Jimingyi, a walled relay town, held about 56 acres inside a wall about 2,000 m (~6,562 ft) round.
    It is one measured example at the small end; its wall stood 11 m (~36 ft) high, with east and west gates.
  • Leping, a prosperous county seat, was walled 4.3 km (~14,108 ft) round.
    It is one measured example at the large end, a class above most county seats. The first of the six county seats of its prefecture, it was walled in 1562, the wall about 985 m (~3,232 ft) north to south and 1,270 m (~4,167 ft) east to west.
  • A square wall under 2 km (~6,562 ft) round encloses under about 62 acres.
    It is under 500 m (~1,640 ft) on a side; a wall of the same length that is not square encloses less.
  • No page we read gives the households inside a county seat's wall.
    They give the wall's length, but no household count against the walled area.

Where a town's roads left it, its houses did not stop at a line. The marked ends a post town set on its highway are at Towns: the county seat and the post town, and who lives in them (machi).

  • Japanese geographers call a long, narrow settlement that faces a highway and lives by its traffic a street village (gaison, the street town above).
    They set it against a road village, whose houses along the road live mainly by farming. The definition names no town; that a town's edge takes this form where its roads leave it is this record's reading.
  • After the great fire of 1657, Edo's town ground spread out along its highways.
    Firebreaks were cut through the town, and town ground formed in great numbers along the suburban highways. It is the clearest case the record has, though it is a great city's, not a small town's.
  • In 1662 the built-up ground around Edo was taken in as town ground.
    The surrounding districts that had been built up were put under the town magistrate, so the building came first and the government caught up with it.
  • Outside a walled city, the biggest gate suburbs lay along the main traffic route.
    Inns and services for travelers and traders grew outside the gates that led to the long-distance roads, and the suburb that grew large enough to be walled in its turn lay in the direction of the main traffic. A town's strip outside its gate is at What is in a town's gate market, and how far out along the road does it run?, and a city's at What stands outside a city gate?
  • No page we read says how far a town's houses ran on along its roads.
    None gives the length of the strip beyond a town's last houses or how quickly its houses thinned, and the study of walled cities measures no suburb, so any length is a GUESS.
