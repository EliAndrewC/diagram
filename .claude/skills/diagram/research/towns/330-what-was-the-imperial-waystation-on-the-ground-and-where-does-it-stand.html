What was the Imperial waystation on the ground, and where does it stand?

Sources: l7r-budgets (the setting's waystation: its staff, horses, buildings and spacing), ekika-jawiki (the ancient relay station's horses by class of road, and the stations that shared a district office), fuse-ekika-himeji-dokkyo (the one excavated station's walled compound and its buildings), ekika-hyogo-museum (the spacing, the horses and the look of the excavated buildings), jimingyi-visitbeijing (the Chinese walled post town, recorded and set aside)

The waystation is the setting's own institution, and the canon gives most of it. A typical station has 15 to 20 permanent staff and a busy trunk-road station 25 to 30, a smith among them; it keeps 8 to 10 horses on average, more where the couriers are busier; its buildings are a main hall, barracks, stables, gates and courtyard walls; and the stations stand about ten miles apart, irregularly, as the route allows. What the canon does not give is how much ground the complex covers and where it stands against a town.

The nearest history is the ekika, the relay station of Japan's ancient highways. A station on a great road kept 20 horses, one on a middle road 10 and one on a small road 5, more or fewer as the ground required - the same order as the setting's. On the Sanyodo there was in principle one station every 16 km, about ten miles, with 20 horses. The first station ever confirmed by excavation, at Koinumaru, was a compound walled with rammed earth about 80 m (about 260 ft) a side, holding at least seven tile-roofed buildings on stone footings; the buildings dug up there and at a second site had tiled roofs, white walls and red-lacquered pillars, rare for their day. A walled square of about 260 ft holding a hall, barracks and stables is exactly the complex the canon lists, for a staff of 15 to 30.

Where it stood took two forms. Some stations were compounds of their own, as Koinumaru's was; but a good many were run by the district office, or kept in the station master's own house. So a waystation may stand alone on the road between towns, or at a county seat beside the magistrate's compound.

Jimingyi, the Chinese post station looked at here, is a different thing and does not fit. It is a walled town some 2,330 m around, holding the station's offices, granary, horse compound and guest court, and in the Qing it held at its height a garrison of more than 300 and 82 post horses. That is a fortress with a town inside it, ten times the canon's staff; the setting's waystation is the smaller institution.

What it means for a map: the waystation is drawn as a walled compound about 260 ft square on the Imperial road, with its hall, barracks and stables inside. Its siting is a knob - its own compound on the road, or at a county seat beside the magistrate's compound - because both forms are attested; which towns carry one is set by the canon's spacing of about ten miles, not by anything here.
