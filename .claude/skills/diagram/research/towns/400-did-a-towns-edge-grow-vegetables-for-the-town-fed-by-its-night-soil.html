Did a town's edge grow vegetables for the town, fed by its night soil?

Sources: thepaper-city-gardens (a Chinese farm manual's advice for land near a city wall, and the gardens round Kaifeng), katsushika-edo-yasai (why Edo's vegetables were grown close to it, and the night soil that came back), ja-tokyo-edo-yasai (the ground opened east and west of Edo to feed it, and how the produce went in), kotobank-shimogoe (Osaka's supplying villages and the city's night soil), king-forty-centuries (vegetable gardens inside a Japanese city's limits, and the night soil carted out to the fields)

Near a wall, vegetables. A sixth-century Chinese farm manual, the Qimin yaoshu, gives the rule in a sentence: land near a city wall should be planted thick with melons, vegetables and eggplant, for the household and to sell what is left over. A memoir of Kaifeng at the end of the Northern Song says the capital was ringed with gardens, with no idle land within a hundred li.

Why close. The Japanese cases give the reason and the return flow. In the Edo period Katsushika, east of Edo, took to growing daikon, eggplant, cucumber, leeks and turnips as well as rice: fresh vegetables could not be kept long, so they were grown close to the city. The city's night soil came back by boat, and the farmers bought it because the crops grew well on it. The marsh and tideflats east of Edo were reclaimed into paddy and fields and, fed with the city's night soil, grew rice and vegetables sold in it; to the west new upland fields were opened for the city's food, their produce going in by handcart, ox cart and river boat. Around Osaka the villages that supplied the city with greens came to lean heavily on its night soil once other fertilizers grew dear, in the middle of the early-modern period. A visitor to Yokohama in 1909 rode a tram between fields and gardens of wheat, barley, onions, carrots and cabbage before he was out of the city limits, and saw the night soil going out along the roads to the fields by the cartload.

A town, not a city. Every case here but the manual's is a great city's. The manual's advice is for any land near a city wall, and a county town's wall is one; that a town's own edge carried a belt of vegetable plots for its market, fed by its own night soil, is this record's reading of the manual and of the cities' cases, not something a page says of a town.
