Which way does a shelter belt lie, and does its compass bearing matter?

Sources: kikori-bofurin (a planted windbreak laid at right angles to the wind); naro-bofu-haichi (the main belt against the prevailing wind, the secondary belts mostly at right angles to it); yashikirin-jawiki (the Japanese homestead grove described by the sides of the house it stands on); this project's measurements across every nucleated map in the pool; the shelter-belt agronomy itself is on the vegetation page

The belt nestles against the windward fringe of each farm cluster, as a Japanese homestead grove stands on the sides of the house the wind and snow come from, and it has to be wide enough to reach open ground: clumps are refused wherever they would fall on a house, a yard or a garden, so a band drawn only over the packed rows thins to a scatter, and a band has to bridge from the rows out into clear ground to take at all. Corner masses of forest by themselves are decoration rather than a wind wall, and a town with more than one farm cluster needs a hugging band for each, merging into the corner woods where they meet.

Neither a canopy fraction nor a compass bearing measures the rest of that. A canopy fraction - how much of the windward arc within some radius is under canopy - cannot separate the approved forms from the bad ones, and it ranks them backwards: the ribbon-form belt that hugs a cluster scores 4 to 18 per cent while the corner-blob layouts it should reject score 29 to 45, so the question the maps are held to is instead whether a substantial belt actually stands beside a farmhouse.

Nor does the belt's bearing discriminate. The textbook shelter belt lies across the wind - planted belts are laid at right angles to the wind they stop, the main belt set against the prevailing wind and the secondary belts mostly at right angles to it - so on a northwest wind it runs northeast-southwest, and a nearly north-south belt on a declared northwest wind looks as though it faces the wrong quarter; measured against every nucleated map in the pool, it does not. Measured as the face presented to the wind - the clumps' extent across the wind over their extent along it - such a belt scores 1.46 against 1.02, 0.90 to 1.13, 1.04, 1.25 and 1.33 for the approved maps: a wider face than most of the pool, so any threshold that failed it would fail half the approved maps too. Measured as the principal axis of the clumps against the ideal across-wind axis, it is 31 degrees off, the second-best alignment in the whole pool - only a ribbon-form belt at 5 degrees and another at 14 beat it - while every approved map runs 33 to 49 degrees off. The pool's real convention is therefore that a belt follows the cluster's windward fringe, and the wind decides which SIDE it lies on, not which bearing it runs at - which is how the Japanese homestead grove is described as well: by side, not by bearing, the grove of one Tonami district planted tall on the west and south, and the pine hedge of Izumo once a ring round the whole house, before the Meiji period, and later a hook over the north and west alone. No bearing rule is drawn from this, and a belt whose only fault is its compass angle is not a fault at all.

The rule the map follows: each farm cluster carries its own band of trees along its windward fringe, deep enough to stand in open ground rather than only over the rows. At least one substantial grove - a dozen clumps or more - stands within 150 ft of a farmhouse ; on the approved maps the belt stands much closer than that limit, between about 37 and 262 ft from the nearest farmhouse depending on the tier. A town whose shelter is a real wood is exempt: one drawn settlement nestles against the forest itself, which is the same property arrived at by other means. The 150 ft reach and the grove's size are a calibration against the drawn maps.
