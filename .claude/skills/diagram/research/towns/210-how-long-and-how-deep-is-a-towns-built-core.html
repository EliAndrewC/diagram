How long and how deep is a town's built core?

Sources: nakatsugawa-jawiki (one post town's length and houses in the 1790s and 1843), toriimoto-jawiki (a small post town's length and houses in 1843), itabashi-jawiki (a busy post town's built street and houses in the 1840s), tsumago-aisurukai (the smallest street length found), kotobank-shukubamachi (the great stations' length), naraijuku-jawiki and denken-sekijuku (two preserved townscapes' lengths today), city-wall-grades-zhwiki (the Ming-Qing wall grades by circumference), visitbeijing-jimingyi (one walled relay town's area), leping-archina (one large county seat's wall)

A road town is measured along its road. The Edo period wrote its post towns' lengths down in its own units, and four give the scale. Tsumago's street ran about 270 m (890 ft) and apparently did not change through the period. Nakatsugawa's ran about 1.1 km (3,600 ft) from 1663 to the end of the shogunate, and held 175 houses in the 1790s and 228 in 1843. Toriimoto ran about 1.4 km (4,700 ft) with 293 houses in 1843. Itabashi, a busy station at the edge of Edo, had a built street of about 1.7 km (5,700 ft) and 573 houses in the 1840s. A great station could reach a ri, about 3.9 km (2.4 miles), or more. Two post towns are of the same order today - Narai's townscape about 1 km (3,300 ft) along its river, Seki's preserved district about 1.8 km (5,900 ft) - but those are the extents of townscapes preserved today, so the scale below rests on the Edo lengths instead. A town's depth is its two rows of lots and the road between them, since a post town's lots ran back from the road as strips of one depth (see Towns: the county seat and the post town).

A walled seat is measured round its wall. Ming and Qing city walls are divided into five classes by the length of the circuit, and the smallest class, under 2 km (6,600 ft) round, was the most numerous - most county seats among them - while every prefectural and departmental city stood in the class above, 2 to 5 km. The page qualifies that the small county walls were found especially on the western frontier, and that many county seats of the eastern coast were larger than the prefectural cities of the west. A walled relay town - a station on the imperial post road, where couriers changed horses - gives one measured example at the small end: about 56 acres inside a wall about 2,000 m round. A prosperous county seat, the first of the six in its prefecture, gives one at the large end: a wall 4.3 km round, about 985 m north to south and 1,270 m east to west (3,200 by 4,200 ft). A square wall under 2 km round is under 500 m (1,640 ft) on a side and encloses under about 62 acres. What share of that ground the town actually built on is under how densely a town's built core is packed.

Scaled to this setting's town. A county seat here holds about 238 households, about 80 of them not farming (see who lives in a town). The two small stations, Toriimoto and Nakatsugawa in 1843, both put about 21 houses on every 100 m of road, both sides counted; busy Itabashi put about 33. At the small stations' rate the 80 would line about 390 m (1,270 ft) of road, and all 238 about 1,150 m (3,800 ft); at Itabashi's, about 240 m (790 ft) and 720 m (2,350 ft). Each count is of the station's houses, and whether every one of them fronted the road is on no page read, so the rates are houses per length of road, not lot widths. A road town of this size is a strip about a kilometer long, the size of a small post station, and a walled one, by this project's guess, a ring toward the small end of the smallest class - no source found ties a town's household count to the length of its wall.

The rule the map follows: an unwalled road town's built strip - the houses of its households that do not farm, lining both sides of the road - runs about 800 to 1,300 ft along the road, and about 2,400 to 3,800 ft where its farmhouses stand on the road as well; its depth is two rows of lots and the road. A walled town's rampart runs no more than about 6,600 ft round. These are calibrations from post towns' Edo-period lengths and house counts and from the Ming-Qing wall grades, scaled by this setting's household count, not measurements of a town of this size.
