How long and how deep is a town's built core?

Sources: naraijuku-jawiki (one mountain post town's length, breadth and houses), denken-sekijuku (one Tokaido post town's length), kameyama-sekijuku (the same town's houses), kotobank-shukubamachi (the great stations' length), city-wall-grades-zhwiki (the Ming-Qing wall grades by circumference), visitbeijing-jimingyi (one walled relay town's area), leping-archina (one large county seat's wall)

A road town is measured along its road. Two surviving post towns give the scale. Narai, on the Nakasendo, runs about 1 km (3,300 ft) along its river and 200 m (660 ft) across, and held 409 houses in 1843. Seki, on the Tokaido, runs about 1.8 km (5,900 ft) from the fork at its east entrance to the fork at its west, and held 632 houses. A great station could reach a ri, about 3.9 km (2.4 miles), or more. Its depth is its two rows of lots and the road between them, since a post town's lots ran back from the road as strips of one depth (see what makes a post town different); that Narai's 660 ft across is the whole of it is this project's reading of the figure, which the source does not break down.

A walled seat is measured round its wall. Ming and Qing China graded its city walls in five classes by the length of the circuit, and the smallest class, under 2 km (6,600 ft) round, was the most numerous - most county seats among them - while every prefectural and departmental city stood in the class above, 2 to 5 km. The page qualifies that the small county walls were found especially on the western frontier, and that many county seats of the eastern coast were larger than the prefectural cities of the west. A walled relay town - a station on the imperial post road, where couriers changed horses - gives one measured example at the small end: about 56 acres inside a wall about 2,000 m round. A prosperous county seat, the first of the six in its prefecture, gives one at the large end: a wall 4.3 km round, about 985 m north to south and 1,270 m east to west (3,200 by 4,200 ft). A square wall under 2 km round is under 500 m (1,640 ft) on a side and encloses under about 62 acres. What share of that ground the town actually built on is under how densely a town's built core is packed.

Scaled to this setting's town. A county seat here holds about 238 households, about 80 of them not farming (see who lives in a town). At Seki's rate - about 35 houses to every 100 m of road, both sides counted - the 80 would line about 230 m (750 ft) of road, and all 238 about 680 m (2,200 ft); at Narai's, about 200 m (650 ft) and 580 m (1,900 ft). So a road town of this size is a strip well under a kilometer long, the size of a small post station, and a walled one, by this project's guess, a ring toward the small end of the smallest class - no source found ties a town's household count to the length of its wall.

The rule the map follows: an unwalled road town's built strip - the houses of its households that do not farm, lining both sides of the road - runs about 650 to 750 ft along the road, and about 1,900 to 2,200 ft where its farmhouses stand on the road as well; its depth is two rows of lots and the road. A walled town's rampart runs no more than about 6,600 ft round. These are calibrations from two post towns and the Ming-Qing wall grades scaled by this setting's household count, not measurements of a town of this size.
