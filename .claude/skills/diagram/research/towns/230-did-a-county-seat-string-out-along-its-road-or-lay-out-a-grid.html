Did a county seat string out along its road, or lay out a grid?

Sources: kotobank-gaison (the street town, its lots and its one-sided form), zaigomachi-jawiki (the rural town's plan, and its planned exceptions), kotobank-shukubamachi (the street town that grows back streets), leping-archina (one county seat's cross street and lanes), visitbeijing-jimingyi (one walled relay town's roads)

Both, and the history says which by region. The forms are distinct, so on these maps the plan is a knob rolled per town, not one shape for all.

The street town. In Edo Japan the post towns, market towns and temple-gate towns almost all took a linear form, their houses lined up on both sides of a central street, with the Tokaido stations the typical case; their lots were mostly neat strips at right angles to that street. The rural town that was a town in all but law was the same: in most cases one or two rows of townhouses along a highway or a river on strip lots, without the castle town's separate quarters for samurai, townsmen and temples. Two variants are attested. Along a road squeezed between mountains and the sea the town is often built on one side of the street only. And a post town that was also its district's economic center could grow back streets and blocks behind the main street.

The planned town. Some Japanese rural towns that grew out of temple towns (jinaicho) or medieval towns had planned street layouts inside moats and earth banks, Imai's a regular grid. The two Chinese towns read here were both walled and both laid out on planned streets inside the wall. One county seat, walled in 1562 (its wall is measured under how long a town's core is), has a plan that turns on a cross street, forming a main east-west commercial street with a tortoise-shell net of lanes on either side, 14 across and 9 along; a walled relay town - a station on the imperial post road, where couriers changed horses - is divided by five crossing roads into twelve blocks. The planned axis from the gate to the magistrate's office is under Chinese towns were planned.

How often. For Japan the reference works say almost all. For China no page read counts how many county seats took a cross-street plan, a full grid or a single street.

The rule the map follows: a town's plan is one of four forms, rolled per town from the map's seed: a street town built along both sides of its road; a one-sided street town where the site is squeezed between slope and water; a street town with back streets and blocks behind its main street, for a seat that is its district's market center; or a walled town laid out with a main avenue and cross streets (the rule under Chinese towns were planned). An unwalled town takes one of the first three and a walled town the fourth. The weights among the three street forms are a guess; that the plain street town is the most common of them follows the Japanese reference works.
