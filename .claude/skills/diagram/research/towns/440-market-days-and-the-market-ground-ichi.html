Market days and the market ground (ichi)

In the setting, the county town holds a market day about once a week, when farmers from all over the county come in to buy, sell and barter, since the town is the lowest tier of settlement with merchants. The Japanese periodic markets it is drawn from kept a calendar of days in the month instead of a week: three market days a month, and later six, every fifth day. A rural market in Qing China drew its customers from a walk of an hour or two.

On the day, the market met along a town's main street, on a strip kept clear between the street and the house lots, or before the gate of a temple or shrine. No page we read says what its stalls were built of, or whether anything of them stood between market days.   • The setting's county town holds its market about once a week.
    The setting's notes say the town in the center of the county has its market day approximately once per week, and that farmers come in for it because the town is the lowest level to have merchants.
  • Japan's periodic markets met three times a month, and later six.
    The older periodic market met three times a month; with the growth of trade, markets came to be held six times a month, every five days - the rokusai-ichi - on pairs of days such as the 1st and 6th or the 2nd and 7th, two days in each ten.
  • The six-day market lasted in Tohoku and Kanto, while around Kyoto and Osaka it grew into country towns.
    In the Edo period the six-day market stayed the center of a region's trade in Tohoku and Kanto; in the Kinai, the region around Kyoto and Osaka, many such markets grew into country towns and the name died out.
  • Neighboring markets set their days so as not to compete.
    In each region the market days were adjusted, for instance by setting them against the days of the region's central market.

China's periodic markets kept a schedule of their own, and the record has measured their reach where it has none for Japan's.

  • A Chinese periodic market opened every few days, for the villages around it.
    It opened one day in every two or several, or three times in ten days, and its buyers and sellers were chiefly the villagers nearby.
  • Is the setting's weekly market a historical schedule?
    Not in its count: about four market days a month falls between the three-day and the six-day market, so the weekly market is a deliberate deviation in its count and not in its kind - a market held by a town that is the only one its villages have.
  • How far did a Chinese market's customers walk?
    In the mid-Qing, a 2000 study of the market network finds, China's rural markets stood one or two to every 100 square kilometers (~24,711 acres). A market drew on 40 to 60 square kilometers (~9,884-14,826 acres) on the plains and over 100 (~24,711 acres) in the mountains. Its customers walked 3 to 5 km (~9,843-16,404 ft) on the plains and 5 to 7 km (~16,404-22,966 ft) in the mountains, a trip of one or two hours, there and back in half a day.
  • Are those Chinese figures measured walks, or averages?
    They read as averages, not paced roads: the study does not state its method, and each area looks like a province's land, hill and water included, divided by its count of markets, with the walk worked out from it - a GUESS. They are the reach of the lowest tier, the rural market, not of a larger commercial town.
  • A twentieth-century model of rural China lands inside those figures.
    In G. William Skinner's typical case, a market town's area was just over 50 square kilometers (~12,355 acres), market towns stood less than 8 km (~26,247 ft) apart, and the longest walk in was about 4.5 km (~14,764 ft), for some 1,500 households in eighteen or so villages.
  • How far did a Japanese market town's customers walk?
    No page we read says. The nearest figure is a spacing: in Settsu and Kawachi near Osaka, a country of cotton and other commercial crops, where the Kinai's country towns grew up in the late seventeenth century, a town had formed every few kilometers. That is of the same order as the Chinese reach, though it is a dense case, not a typical one.

Where the market met took two forms in Japan: along the main street of a market town, and before a temple's or shrine's gate.

  • In the market towns of Edo-period Kanto, the market met in a strip along the main street.
    The market ground was a strip one to two ken deep (about 6 to 12 ft), set between the town street and the house lots and kept for putting up stalls on market day.
  • Stalls stood both inside the house lots and out in the street.
    The stalls inside the lots, which took in the market strip, were under the lot's owner; the stalls out in the street, which served as the selling floor, were under the market's head.
  • A market god's stone marked the market's edge.
    Most market sites were on old highways, and the market god, the ichigami, was worshipped at the roadside, sometimes at the boundary of the market or of its ward, or between the market settlement and its neighbors; its body was most often a round natural stone. It often stands at a place that marks an edge - a village boundary, a landing, a bridge foot, a crossroads - and a market is inferred from where one stands.
  • The medieval market met before temple and shrine gates.
    The medieval three-day market was held mainly before the gates of temples and shrines, at hubs of land and water transport, and near an estate's office or its steward's residence.
  • In China, the temple fair met at a temple and took in trade.
    The temple fair, the miaohui, gathered near a temple for offerings to the gods, contests and performances, first on its religious festivals and later on fixed dates, and gradually took in market trade.

Earlier Chinese law kept the market behind a wall of its own, and the record finds that wall only in the capitals.

  • Under the Tang, a market could be set up only at a prefecture or county seat.
    A 2009 essay on the Tang market quotes the rule. The market opened at midday to three hundred drumbeats and dispersed before sunset to three hundred strokes of the gong, and even a county's market was divided into rows by goods - the rice row, the silk row, the iron row.
  • The Tang capital's two markets each took the ground of two wards.
    Each measured about 1,025 m (~3,363 ft) north to south and 927 m (~3,041 ft) east to west.
  • The Han capital's markets were walled and gated squares, with a tower for their officials.
    Each was walled and gated on four sides, with a five-story tower at its center from which the market's officials looked down over it, and storehouses behind the shops against the market wall.
  • Was a county seat's market walled?
    No page we read says whether a county seat's market had a wall of its own, or how big it was.
  • The Song dynasty ended the walled market.
    The Song abolished the Tang's ward-and-market system and its night curfew, and commercial streets, entertainment quarters and night markets took its place.

The market day brought a crowd, and a festival brought another; the record looks at what a town kept for each.

  • The setting's acting troupes make most of their money on market day.
    The rest of the week they frequently travel through the county's village districts to perform. Where a town's stage stands is at Where does a town's theater stage stand, and which way does it open?
  • A Japanese shrine's procession rests at an otabisho.
    A shrine's festival generally carries the god out in a portable shrine, which rests or stays the night on its way at, or sets out to reach, a place called the otabisho. That may have a shrine building of its own, a temporary altar, or be the house of the representative of the shrine's ujiko.
  • The market's own festival was the market god's, at the first market of the New Year.
    A Japanese encyclopedia says it was customarily held then.
  • Did a town keep a square or festival ground open for the crowd?
    No page we read describes one, Japanese or Chinese. The pages place the festival at the shrine, the temple and the route of the god's procession, and the temple fair near its temple.

A provincial city keeps the same market on a larger scale, and meets the road's traffic at its gates as well.

  • How many draft animals did a caravan bring to a city's gate?
    Many dozens, oxen and packhorses, with their guards, porters and drivers - a GUESS: no page we read counts the animals of one train or names oxen among them. The nearest documented order of magnitude is what a Japanese post station had to keep standing for the road's traffic: each Tokaido station was first made to keep thirty-six horses, and from 1638 a hundred post horses and a hundred porters.
  • When a post station's horses ran short, the villages around it made up the rest.
    A post station was itself a town, and a hundred horses was not always enough. The answer the sources record is not a bigger yard: the neighboring villages supplied men and horses, from 1694 at two men and two horses for every hundred koku of assessed yield, and the ring of villages, at first two or three ri around the station, was widened past ten.
