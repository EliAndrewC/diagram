What else stood just outside a city - kilns, lime works, and the villages it swallowed?

Sources: bunka-imado-kawarayaki (tile firing as the specialty of a riverside place in Edo), imado-yaki-jawiki (where Imado stood, and how many households fired there), liulichang-zhwiki (a capital's tile works outside its wall, moved out when a new wall took it in), higashiyamato-nariki-lime (where a city's lime came from), kotobank-edo (the built-up ground around a city taken into it)

What grew along the roads out of a city is What stands outside a city gate?; its gardens are Did a town's edge grow vegetables for the town, fed by its night soil?; its gentry's country estates are Gentry estates are dispersed, not clustered at the wall. This question is the rest.

Tile kilns, at the edge and by water. A city's roof tiles were fired close to it. At Imado on the west bank of the Sumida river, tile firing had become the place's specialty, as the period's guidebook to Edo says; Imado and Hashiba lie northeast of Asakusa, and at the end of the shogunate about fifty households there fired Imado ware of every kind, tiles among it. Beijing's glazed-tile works reached north to the moat of the Inner City, and when the Outer City's wall of 1553 took that ground into the city, the kilns (by this account; the same article elsewhere puts the move in the Qianlong years, in the eighteenth century), being unsuited to firing inside a city, moved out to Liuliqu village, in what is now Mentougou district. So a city's kilns stood just beyond its built ground, and moved out again when the city grew over them.

Lime, from the hills. Lime was not a suburban works. In 1606 the lime for Edo's castle was ordered from the villages of Kami-Nariki and Kita-Osoki, burned there, and carried in by relays of horses from the districts along the road; a survey of the road from Tanashi to Ome some forty years later counts a stretch of six ri across the moor with no houses on it. Lime was burned where the stone was, and the city's share came in along a road.

The villages it swallowed. The ground next to a city was in time built over and taken in: in 1662 the ground around Edo's old core that had been built up was put under the town magistrate as town ground. The ribbon of houses along a road out of a town is Where does a town's built edge stop, and does a ribbon of houses run on along the road?.

What it means for a map: a city may draw a small tile works just beyond its built ground, on water, as a cluster of kiln households rather than one shop; how many a provincial city had is on no page read and is a guess. It draws no lime kiln in its near hinterland, since lime came in from where the stone was; this rests on Edo alone, and that a provincial city's lime also came from away is a guess by extension.
