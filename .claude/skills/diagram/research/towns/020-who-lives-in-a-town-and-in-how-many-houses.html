Who lives in a town, and in how many houses?

Sources: l7r-budgets, the setting's own budget notes (the town's caste table, its wealth rows and its samurai note); dechi-jawiki (a merchant's apprentices living in the shop); buke-hokonin-wiki (a samurai household's live-in servants); jinya-jawiki (retainers' official houses inside a small lord's seat). The household counts are the setting's own, not history's; the history behind the drawing conventions is cited where it stands.

A county seat holds about 1,200 inhabitants in about 238 households, and unlike a hamlet it is not one caste. The campaign notes give a town roughly 24 merchant households, 29 laborer dwellings, 13 servant households, 12 burakumin households and 4 samurai families beside its ~156 farming households. Because those numbers are documented rather than invented, the map places the actual count of each, one building to a household, instead of a token sample of each caste - a town whose merchants are a picturesque half-dozen is a town that has quietly lost two thirds of its trade. The notes give the village a headman's household and name none for the town, whose working samurai are the magistrate and their staff; read from those tables, the map draws no peasant headman at this tier, the state being present in person, as the magistrate.

Three map drawing conventions stand between that census and what is actually drawn, and they are the same reasoning as the manor glyph below - the sheet draws a household where it stands, and a household that lives inside someone else's walls is presumed to be there rather than drawn twice. Servants mostly live inside the samurai and merchant compounds they serve - a merchant's apprentices lived in the shop from about the age of ten, and a samurai household's private servants, the komono, lived in and did its odd jobs - so only the five or so miscellaneous servant households stand alone and the rest are not drawn. Shops are drawn as business premises counted separately from the merchant dwellings: on the map a shop building stands for no household (whoever sleeps in it is already counted with its merchant), so adding one perturbs no population figure. Samurai show as five to ten houses, the roughly fifteen-strong working platoon being barracked inside the manor and not drawn; a small lord's seat could hold its retainers' official houses inside its own bank and moat, beside the residence and the offices.

Wealth is not flat either. The town wealth tiers put a minority of merchants in the very-rich and rich brackets and a few wealthier master laborers above the rest of their caste, so a town is never one uniform house size - a row of identical boxes is the tell that the tiers were ignored.

The rule the map follows: a town map draws about 24 merchant houses, 29 laborer dwellings, 5 free-standing servant households, 12 burakumin dwellings, 5 to 10 samurai houses and about 156 farmhouses; shops are extra and count against no household. The farmhouses must remain the largest single group on the sheet, which caps the laborers - small and large together - below them. Four or five of the 24 merchants live in a large house and two or three of the 29 laborers in a master's, the rest in small or standard dwellings; the large houses stand just behind the street frontage, in gaps left among the packed rows.
