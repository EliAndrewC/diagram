Gate markets outside a town (guanxiang)

Outside the gates of a walled Chinese city, the streets just beyond the gate, the guanxiang, grew into a district of their own. A gate funneled the traffic of one sector of the countryside, so the ground just outside it drew markets and businesses serving the country people, and the gates on long-distance routes drew inns for travelers and traders. By the nineteenth century few walled cities lacked such a suburb outside at least one gate. The study these findings come from covers walled cities of every rank and speaks of them in general, not of the county seat by name.

In the setting our maps draw, a town's gate market is there for its traffic, not for taxes: the setting collects its import tariffs at the gates of provincial cities and capitals, not at a town's. The city's strip outside its gate is at What stands outside a city gate?, and the periodic market the villages come in for, and where in the town it meets, at Market days and the market ground (ichi).

  • The ground outside a gate drew markets for the country people.
    Since a gate channeled all the traffic to and from one sector of the city's hinterland, the area just outside it was a favored site for markets and for businesses serving the rural population.
  • China's periodic markets met every few days, for the villagers nearby.
    A periodic market opened every other day or every few days, its buyers and sellers chiefly the villagers around it; it arose because a long journey to a distant city to trade did not pay. That a town's gate was where the villages' market-day trade met the town is this project's reading: the article on the periodic market places it in the countryside.
  • Gates on long-distance routes drew inns for travelers.
    Inns and other services for travelers and traders were set up outside the particular gates that opened onto long-distance routes. That travelers on the through road, carters and peddlers among them, bought there without going inside the walls is this project's reading. A town's own inn for the road is at Where does a caravan inn stand, and what does it need beside it?
  • Commercial districts outside China's city walls grew from the Song dynasty onward.
    The Chinese Wikipedia dates the districts of trade outside the walls, the caoshi, from the Song, and traces them to the rural periodic markets of the Tang. The study of walled cities dates the growth of suburbs from the later Tang and the Southern Song.
  • Outside the busiest gates, the suburb often grew before the ground inside the wall had filled.
    The study says so of many, if not most, walled cities.
  • The largest suburb lay toward the main traffic.
    Where a suburb grew large enough to be walled itself, it lay in the direction of the main flow of traffic on an important route.
  • Did a town's gate shut at dusk?
    Probably, though no page we read says so of a county town. In Tang Chang'an, the capital, the ward gates closed at dusk to the drum, and at night checkpoints barred passage through the outer wall's gates; a Tang city's ward gates opened at sunrise and closed at sunset. That a county town's own gate shut at dusk too, leaving late arrivals outside it, is this project's inference.
  • What shops stood in a county town's gate strip?
    Within two kinds: the market and the shops that sold to the villagers, at every gate that carried traffic, and, at a gate on a through road, inns and services for travelers. No page we read lists a county town's gate strip trade by trade, so which shops stood in it is a GUESS.
  • How many buildings did the strip hold, and how far along the road did it run?
    No page we read counts the buildings of a county town's gate strip or says how far it ran; the study gives no count and no length for any suburb. The one counted strip the record has is a capital's, at What stands outside a city gate?
