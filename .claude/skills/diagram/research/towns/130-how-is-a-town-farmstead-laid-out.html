How is a town farmstead laid out?

Sources: chengchi-zhwiki; the GM's decision of 2026-07; the homestead group's own sources are on the homesteads page, chinese-city-wall-enwiki, yashikirin-jawiki, visit-toyama-sankyoson (farmers building amid the fields they work); forestry-jp-tarama-hogo (the Ryukyu kingdom's village tree belts, Tarama's dated 1742); miyakomainichi-tarama-hogo (the Tarama belt's course round its settlement and its size today); jpgreen-byobuzan (the Tsugaru domain's windbreak of 1682 on the windward side of its new fields)

A town's farmhouses are not a looser version of a hamlet's. They stand in a nucleus and run the same to-scale homestead group the villages do - house, threshing yard strictly to its south, a garden set where the sun reaches it, a storehouse plot reserved to the north - and the whole group is tested against the paddies by its footprint.

That footprint test is the GM's direction of 2026-07, and what it guards against is the reason to keep it. Placing the house first and testing the fields by their centers is a 14 ft error in the wrong direction at a town's scale: a 44 ft town house passes such a test with a corner already sunk into the crop. Testing what the building actually occupies, against what the field actually occupies, is the only version of the rule that holds at this scale.

The nucleus also changes the greenery. A dispersed farmstead is sheltered by its own grove; these maps give a town's farms no per-farm groves and draw the green instead as one communal windbreak on the windward side, with a leafy copse scatter through the rows.

A windbreak planted for a whole settlement's houses and fields is older than modern practice. In the eighteenth century the Ryukyu kingdom, under its chief minister Sai On, encouraged inland belts of trees called hogo, or village hogo, as windbreaks among other things, and the one on the island of Tarama, made in 1742, still shelters the settlement and its farmland; today it runs about 1.8 km, 10 to 15 m wide, round the settlement. On a far larger scale, the Tsugaru domain began in 1682 to plant the hills and the coastal dunes on the side the sea wind came from, to shield the fields it was opening behind them, and when the belt was felled for food in the famines of the 1780s those paddies nearly went back to waste. The communal windbreak is drawn on that authority. Tarama's belt rings its settlement where these maps set one belt on the windward side, as Tsugaru's stood; drawing it on one side only is this project's decision.

The other half of the rule, that a nucleated village's farms kept no groves of their own, is still a guess: no source this record has read says either way, and the single windbreak is the same rule the homesteads page gives a nucleated hamlet.

The rule the map follows: town farmsteads use the nucleated to-scale homestead group - house, threshing yard, garden and storehouse plot - footprint-tested against the fields, with no per-farm grove (a guess); the shelter is one communal windbreak on the windward side, the form of a village's planted belt in the eighteenth-century Ryukyus and a domain's in seventeenth-century Tsugaru. They stand outside the rampart, ringing the paddies at roughly village density, because a farmer builds close to the ground they work, and these maps keep a town's fields outside its walls - a drawing decision, since the Chinese walled city listed farmland among its basic intramural land uses and Suzhou's wall still enclosed farmland in the twentieth century.
