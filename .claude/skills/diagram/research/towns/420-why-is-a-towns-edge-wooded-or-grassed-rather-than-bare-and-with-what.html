Why is a town's edge wooded or grassed rather than bare, and with what?

Sources: kotobank-zokibayashi (what the wood nearest a settlement was made of), rinya-satoyama (what such woods were for, and how they were cut and regrew), maff-musashino-giahs (woods planted beside new fields for their leaves and to hold the soil), townnews-zama-magusaba (the grass ground where thatch and fodder were cut)

Because it was used. The ground just beyond a settlement's fields was neither wild nor bare: it was worked, for what a settlement burned and what its fields ate. It took two forms.

A cut-over wood. The wood nearest habitation was the zokibayashi: young, secondary broadleaf wood - konara oak, hornbeam, ryobu, alder - fuel woods among it. Woods of this kind, mostly konara and kunugi oak, were used since long before Meiji for firewood and charcoal, wild food, and fertilizer and farm materials, and they were cut and let regrow from the stumps every twenty to thirty years, so that they were made of thin, low trees growing several stems from one stump. Such a wood could be planted on purpose: when the Musashino upland was opened from 1654 to feed a growing Edo, trees were planted on the grass plain beside the new fields, their leaves raked for compost, and the woods held the light soil against the wind.

A cut grass ground. The other cover was grass: the villages' shared ground where roofing material and fodder for the animals were cut. A road town's ground of this kind, kept for its horses, is the hayfield of What are the barns in a town's hayfield, and how big are they?.

Not a marsh. Why the edge of a town or a city carries no wet toe marsh below its fields is No toe marsh at town/city scale - the drainage-investment gradient.

What it means for a map: a town's margin beyond its fields is clothed, and with worked ground, and which of the two covers a town has, and how much of each, is a knob rolled per town: a cut-over broadleaf wood, drawn young and low - many thin stems, not a closed canopy of old trees - or an open cut grass ground with no fence and no animals. The share of each is a guess: no readable page says how much of a town's margin was wood and how much grass.
