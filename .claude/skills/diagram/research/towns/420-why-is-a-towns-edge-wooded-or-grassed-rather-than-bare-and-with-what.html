Why is a town's edge wooded or grassed rather than bare, and with what?

Sources: kotobank-zokibayashi (what the wood near a settlement was made of), rinya-satoyama (what such woods were for, and how they were cut and regrew), maff-musashino-giahs (woods planted on the grass plain for their leaves and to keep the soil from blowing away), jfs-inagawa-daiba-kunugi (a charcoal coppice dated to the Muromachi period, and its cycle today), hattori-ikedazumi (its rotational cutting in documents from 1145 to 1833), ffpri-kunugi (a late-Edo farm book on the branches cut in winter for charcoal),townnews-zama-magusaba (the grass ground where thatch and fodder were cut)

Because it was used. The ground near habitation was worked, for what a settlement burned and what its fields ate. The sources below show it in two forms, a cut-over wood and a cut grass ground; that these were the only two, and that they lay just beyond the fields rather than anywhere near the houses, is this record's reading of them, not a source's statement.

A cut-over wood. The wood near habitation was the zokibayashi: young, secondary broadleaf wood - konara oak, hornbeam, ryobu, alder - fuel woods among it. Woods of this kind, mostly konara and kunugi oak, were used since long before Meiji for firewood and charcoal, wild food, and fertilizer and farm materials, and they were cut and let regrow from the stumps every twenty to thirty years, so that they were made of relatively thin, low trees growing several stems from one stump; the page gives that cycle without a date. Such a wood could be planted on purpose: when the Kawagoe domain opened part of the Musashino upland from 1654 to feed a growing Edo, trees were planted on the grass plain to make woods on the flat, their fallen leaves gathered as compost for the fields, and the woods kept the soil from blowing away.

The young, cut-over wood is older than modern times. A wood cut back on a short cycle, so that it stands as many thin stems, is attested well before the Meiji period. The kunugi woods of the upper Ina River, on the Hyogo-Osaka border, produced Ikeda charcoal back to at least the Muromachi period, and Edo-period documents record the charcoal's use for the tea ceremony; documents and traditions from 1145 to 1833 record the rotational cutting of those woods, a land survey of the Enpo era names kunugi hills in the district's villages, and the old records agree with today's practice on the rotation period among other points. Today that rotation is eight to nine years. The Koeki Kokusanko, a farm book of the late Edo period, describes these daiba kunugi: from about one jo up the tree puts out hundreds of branches, and those grown half a shaku to one shaku around are cut in winter and burned into the Ikeda charcoal used for the tea ceremony. This is one charcoal district, famous for a fine charcoal; how short an ordinary fuel wood near a town was cut in the setting's period, the record does not say.

A cut grass ground. The other cover was grass: the villages' shared ground where roofing material and fodder for the animals were cut. A road town's ground of this kind, kept for its horses, is the hayfield of What are the barns in a town's hayfield, and how big are they?.

Not a marsh. Why the edge of a town or a city carries no wet toe marsh below its fields is No toe marsh at town/city scale - the drainage-investment gradient.

What it means for a map: a town's margin beyond its fields is clothed, and with worked ground, and which of the two covers a town has, and how much of each, is a knob rolled per town: a cut-over broadleaf wood, drawn young and low - many thin stems, not a closed canopy of old trees, a form the record dates to before modern times - or an open cut grass ground with no fence and no animals. The share of each is a guess: no readable page says how much of a town's margin was wood and how much grass.
