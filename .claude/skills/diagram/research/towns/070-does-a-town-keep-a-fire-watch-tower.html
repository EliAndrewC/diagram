Does a town keep a fire-watch tower?

Sources: kotobank-hinomi-yagura; no work of record - the fire argument rests on general reading and is not cited; the walled-only scoping is the 2026-07-24 decision below, jishinban-jawiki, edo-no-kaji-jawiki

Only a walled town stands a fire-watch tower. The reasoning is the wall: a packed wooden core is where a fire is catastrophic, and the rampart that keeps raiders out also traps the blaze inside with the town, so the seat pays for a watch.40 An open town - a road town strung along the highway, or an unwalled county seat whose houses sit at detached village grain - has field gaps between its blocks that act as natural fire breaks, and gets none. That the gaps do that work is a guess: it rests on general reading, and no source is cited for it.23 The walled-only scoping is the decision of 2026-07-24. The tower itself is a hinomi-yagura: a tall timber frame (open and braced, in this page's reading) with an alarm bell. In Edo it was the neighborhood's watch as much as the state's - each town paid for its own watch house and most carried a ladder and a bell, the shogunate's towers standing one to ten blocks - and the setting makes it the magistrate's.39

The rule the map follows: a walled town stands one fire-watch tower among the laborer and merchant blocks - in the dense commoner quarter, not the samurai one - standing clear of the rampart, and distinct from the guard towers that sit on the rampart itself. An open town draws none. The tower is placed last, after the housing and the farmsteads, onto a seam the finished map actually has, so that adding it moves nothing else.
