Does a town keep a fire-watch tower?

Sources: kotobank-hinomi-yagura (one tower to ten wards, and the ladder on the watch house), jishinban-jawiki (who paid for the watch house), hinomi-yagura-jawiki (how the tower spread from Edo to the country), edo-no-kaji-jawiki (the packed core); the walled-only scoping is the 2026-07-24 decision below

Only a walled town stands a fire-watch tower. The reasoning is the wall: a packed wooden core is where a fire is catastrophic, and the rampart that keeps raiders out also traps the blaze inside with the town, so the seat pays for a watch. An open town - a road town strung along the highway, or an unwalled county seat whose houses sit at detached village grain - has field gaps between its blocks that act as natural fire breaks, and gets none. That the gaps do that work is a guess: no source read says it. The walled-only scoping is the decision of 2026-07-24.

What the history adds. The tower is a hinomi-yagura, a tall frame carrying an alarm bell. In Edo it was the neighborhood's watch as much as the state's - each ward paid for its own watch house, one tower stood to about ten wards, and a ward without one set a fire ladder on the roof of its watch house instead - and the setting makes it the magistrate's. So there were two forms, a free-standing tower and a ladder with its bell on the watch-house roof, and by Edo's own ratio a town of a few wards falls below one tower. The tower began in Edo and spread outward with the fire brigades, reaching almost every region of the country only in the early Showa era, which is the history's side of drawing none on an open country town. How tall a town's tower was and where it stood is the urban features' question.

The rule the map follows: a walled town keeps one fire watch among the laborer and merchant blocks - in the dense commoner quarter, not the samurai one - and its form is a knob rolled per settlement between the two the history shows: a free-standing fire-watch tower, standing clear of the rampart and distinct from the guard towers that sit on the rampart itself, or a watch house carrying a ladder and bell on its roof. An open town draws none. The watch is placed last, after the housing and the farmsteads, onto a seam the finished map actually has, so that adding it moves nothing else.
