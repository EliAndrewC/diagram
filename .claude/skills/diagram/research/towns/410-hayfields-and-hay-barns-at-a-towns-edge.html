Hayfields and hay barns at a town's edge

A town on a highway kept horses - the pack horses that carried travelers and goods, and the farm horses and cattle - and in Japan their fodder was grass cut on a fodder ground, usually a village's or hamlet's common land, and carried to the stable. Dried grass was named as a fodder in Japan centuries before the Edo period.

Where hay was kept in bulk, the record shows it kept in stacks in the open: a sixth-century Chinese farm manual stacks it inside round fences, and a Ming novel's army fodder depot counts its stacks. No page we read describes a hay barn standing in a field, and the pressed rectangular bale is the product of a nineteenth-century Western machine.

  • Dried grass was named and collected as fodder in ancient Japan.
    The Wamyo Ruijusho gives one of the two old characters for fodder the reading karakusa, "dried grass", and the Engishiki had it collected from the provinces of the Kinai for the court's horse bureaus.
  • Where pack horses and farm animals were kept, a fodder ground stayed a fodder ground through the Edo period.
    As the wars ended and fewer war horses were kept, much fodder ground was turned to cutting green manure, but the pack horses carrying travelers and goods along the highways never ceased and grew more in demand as trade grew, so where those pack horses and the farm horses and cattle were kept, the ground was used as fodder ground in the true sense.
  • The fodder was cut and carried to the horse in its stable.
    It was the undergrowth and young branches of woods and moors, put into the stable as feed and trodden by the horse into stable manure.
  • A fodder ground was usually one village's or hamlet's commons.
    The encyclopedia entry on the fodder ground says so, and a lecture notice on Sagamino, around Zama, calls it the villages' common land, where roofing material for houses and fodder for the animals were cut.
  • No page we read gives the size of a road town's fodder ground, or says how a fodder ground was marked off.
    No source read says whether it was bounded by a bank, a ditch, a hedge or nothing but the lie of the land.
  • In some regions the stable floor was dug deep and wild grass thrown in.
    The floor was dug 60 cm (~2 ft) or more deep and wild grass thrown in load after load, part of it eaten and the rest trodden with the dung into manure.
  • A farmhouse stable could stand apart from the house, with a shed built on.
    A farmhouse's outer stable, standing by itself, usually had a privy and a shed built on with it, and a stable usually had a ceiling, with a servants' room or a storeroom above it. Neither article says what the shed or the storeroom held.

The Chinese record shows how hay kept in bulk was stored.

  • A sixth-century Chinese farm manual stacks a flock's hay inside round fences.
    The Qimin Yaoshu, finished in the sixth century, has the hay cut in the eighth and ninth months and stacked on high, dry ground inside two round fences of mulberry and thorn, each about five or six paces, where a stack a zhang high does no harm and the sheep pull their feed through the fence all winter. The fence stands around the stack, not around a field.
  • A Ming novel's army fodder depot kept its hay in stacks beside thatched storehouses, inside an earth wall.
    The Water Margin, a novel of the Ming set in the Northern Song, describes an earth wall with a two-leaved gate, seven or eight thatched buildings inside used as storehouses, the horse fodder in stacks all round, and two thatched halls in the middle, where the keeper handing it over counted the stacks. It is fiction, and it does not say what the storehouses held.
  • No page we read describes a hay barn standing in a field.
    The record shows where a horse's fodder came from and where the horse lived, but not a building in between: no readable page, Japanese or Chinese, premodern or later, describes a large building standing by itself in a field to store hay.
  • The nearest measured thing is a Han post station's stable yard, 30 m (~98 ft) square.
    Xuanquanzhi, near Dunhuang, was a walled compound 50 m (~164 ft) square with a stable area 30 m (~98 ft) square projecting at one corner, and the stations of its commandery kept about forty horses each. It is a stable yard for a large relay, two thousand years before the setting's period and on a desert frontier, not a hay barn. The stables a road town kept are at Relay stations: the Imperial waystation, the relay office and their stables (umaya, toiyaba).
  • When did hay first come in bales?
    A rectangular block of hay, pressed and tied, is what a baling machine makes, and the machine is a nineteenth-century Western one: mechanical cutting devices were developed in the 1860s, and from them came mowers and balers; a reaper whose knotter bound hay into bundles was invented in 1872 and sold from 1874. The article rests those dates on one twine maker's page and gives no date for the first hay press, so they bound the machine rather than fix it. No readable page shows hay pressed into blocks before the machine, in Japan or in China.
  • In the setting, one clan's lands set farmland aside by law for hay.
    Stockpiles of it are held against war, so hay stored in bulk is the setting's own; how it was housed is not in the setting's notes.
