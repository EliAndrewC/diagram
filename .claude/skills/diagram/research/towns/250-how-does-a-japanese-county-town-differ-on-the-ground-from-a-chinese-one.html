Four things tell them apart at a glance.

The plan. A Japanese county town is a street: one or two rows of townhouses along a highway or a river on strip lots, without the separate quarters for samurai, townsmen and temples that a castle town has. A Chinese walled seat is laid out inside its wall around crossing streets. The forms and how the map chooses among them are under did a county seat string out along its road.

The house. The Japanese townhouse faces the street: on a busy road each main house filled the whole width of its lot, and the houses stood eave to eave. The Chinese house of any standing faces inward: the siheyuan is a courtyard with buildings on its four sides - the main hall, the east and west side halls and the reverse-facing gate range - closing the court in, and a large one is walled round from outside with high walls and no windows, for privacy; the two-court and larger houses were the homes of officials and gentry. The rule the map follows: a town on the Japanese model draws its commoners' houses as street-facing rows with no gap along the frontage, the magistrate's compound as a walled or banked enclosure with the samurai houses beside it, and no town wall; a town on the Chinese model draws a wall, its wealthier houses as inward-facing courtyard compounds behind blank outer walls, and the magistrate's compound on the main avenue.
