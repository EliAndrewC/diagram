How big is a stable, and what is its yard like?

Sources: umaya-kotobank (a Japanese stable: inside the house or standing apart, how it is closed and walled, what is above it, and how big a stall is), l7r-budgets (the setting's waystation horses)

The one stable the record can measure is the farmer's. A Japanese farmhouse kept its horse either in an inner stable, a corner of the house's own earth-floored work room, or in an outer stable standing apart. Its entrance was closed with removable wooden bars and its other sides were plank walls; it usually had a ceiling, with a servant's room or a storeroom above. In the long-wintered north it was roomy, and in the Kinki region (around Kyoto and Osaka) narrow, about 3 m (about 10 ft) square; an outer stable usually had a privy and a shed built on with it.

The bigger stables the maps draw are the record's other questions: the wagon inn's long stable with its single feeding trough is in Where does a caravan inn stand, and what does it need beside it?, and the Chinese station's horse compound in What did a post station keep for its relay horses and porters? Neither is measured on any page read. A stable for the waystation's 8 to 10 horses, at about 10 ft a stall, would run 80 to 100 ft of stalls; that length is a guess built on the one measured stall, not a measured relay stable.

What a stable yard's ground was like is no better known. No page read describes the ground of an inn's, a relay's or a gate's stable yard; the beaten earth, the hitching rails and the litter the maps draw are this record's reading of a picture, set out in Stable yards - beaten earth, hitching rails, and watering by relay.
