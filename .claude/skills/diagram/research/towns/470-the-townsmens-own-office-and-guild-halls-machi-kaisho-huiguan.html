The townsmen's own office and guild halls (machi-kaisho, huiguan)

In the Japanese cities of the Edo period, a ward's commoners kept an office of their own, the machi-kaisho, where the ward's officials did the ward's business with its residents. Sakai and the other self-governing towns, Kyoto and Osaka kept one in every ward, but few castle towns did, some kept none at all, and a post town with one was unusual. The nearest Chinese counterpart the record finds, the huiguan of the Ming and Qing dynasties, belonged not to a town but to an association: a club of people from one home region, and later, in some, of the merchants of one trade.

  • A ward office (machi-kaisho) was where a ward's officials worked and met its residents.
    It served the ward as an office and a meeting place. Its officials gathered the residents there to pass on rules such as the magistrate's notices, and drew up the papers the residents submitted.
  • Some ward offices held a court or a small jail.
    An office's work covered the whole of the town's government, and usually reached beyond administration and civil justice into policing - searching for and arresting criminals among the townspeople - so it sometimes had a white-sand court or a small jail.
  • Some ward offices lodged the domain's guests and the shogunate's officials.
    A Japanese encyclopedia entry on the ward office says there were places where the office served as their lodging and reception.
  • Sakai and the other self-governing towns, Kyoto and Osaka kept an office in every ward.
    A Japanese encyclopedia names Sakai, Imai, Hiranogo and Kaizuka, which had governed themselves at the end of the middle ages, Kyoto, and Osaka, the great commercial city of the Edo period: each had a ward office in every ward, with a general office above them for a group of wards, a street or the whole town.
  • Few castle towns kept an office in every ward.
    Utsunomiya had one in each ward, but the Japanese encyclopedia entry on the ward office calls it an exception.
  • Some castle towns had no ward office at all, their officials working at the town magistrate's office.
    The same encyclopedia entry says there were castle towns where the town's officials were posted at the town magistrate's office and worked there, and no ward office was set up.
  • A post town with a ward office was unusual.
    Sukagawa on the Oshu road and Yokkaichi on the Tokaido kept one, but they are named as unusual examples.
  • In Edo, the ward headman's house served as the ward office.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on the early modern ward office says that in Edo the headman's official residence doubled as it.
  • Edo's three town elders worked from their own houses, on corner lots facing Honcho street.
    The shogunate granted the lots, and the elders' houses stood on corners of Honcho street in its first, second and third blocks; as with a samurai, the residence doubled as the office.
  • No page we read gives a ward office's size or where on the street it stood.
    The pages say what the offices did and in which kinds of town they were kept; the only place on a street they give is Edo's elders' corner lots.

In Ming and Qing China, the pages we read describe the huiguan: when it began, and whom it served.

  • The huiguan, also called the gongsuo, was a Ming and Qing institution.
    It is described as a commercial and trade organization of the Ming and Qing dynasties, and the earliest known was built in Beijing in the Yongle reign of the Ming, early in the fifteenth century.
  • A huiguan began as a club for people from one home region, and some became guilds of one trade.
    It was set up at first for the fellowship, entertainment and lodging of people from the same home region; later some came to unite the merchants of one trade.
  • No page we read places a huiguan in a county seat.
    The earliest known huiguan stood in the capital, Beijing, and the pages read name none in a county seat.
