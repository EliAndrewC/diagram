Which way does a town house's door face, and how deep do the rows stack?

Sources: kotobank-shukubamachi (the strip lots, the house on the road, the vegetable garden behind), kotobank-machiya (the ridge parallel to the street, or the gable to it), hirairi-tsumairi-jawiki (the two names for which face holds the entrance)

The door faces the street. A post town's lots on both sides of the road were planned strips of nearly one depth, and apart from the station's officials its households usually farmed as well, the back of the lot often a vegetable garden; the house stood on the road, across its frontage on a busy highway, behind a front yard on a quiet one (the two forms are under does a town's street front stand wall to wall). A machiya usually ran its ridge parallel to the street, but some turned the gable to it; the two forms are named for which face of the building holds the main entrance, the long side under the ridge or the gable end, so either way the door is on the street.

The rows are one deep. Behind the house is often the garden, not a second rank of houses. That a town grows a back row only where it grows a back lane is a guess: no page read counts the depth of a town lot or its back rows, and the garden behind a post-town house is attested only as often, not always. It follows the city's pattern, where the tenement rows stand one rank or two along a lane behind the shops, under which way a city house's door faces - and where a town's laborers rent at all is under where a laborer lives.

The rule the map follows: a town house on a street faces that street, its door on the street side whether its ridge runs along the street or its gable is turned to it; behind it lies its own garden plot, and the row is one house deep. A second rank appears only on a back lane (a guess), and follows the city rule for that lane.
