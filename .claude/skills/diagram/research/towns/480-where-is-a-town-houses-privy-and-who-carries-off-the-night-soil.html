Where is a town house's privy, and who carries off the night soil?

Sources: kotobank-benjo (the town house's privy at the back, the tenement's shared privies, the farmers who emptied them), townhouse-jawiki (the town house's plan, and the tenement's privy at the alley's end), culturepro-nagaya-benjo (the landlord's right to the night soil, and who collected it), shimogoe-jawiki (the dealers, their contracts and their boats), taiwancn-beijing-waste (the Chinese capital's night-soil men), l7r-castes (the setting's owaiya)

A farmstead's privy is under the farmstead's fixtures, and a samurai residence's under privies attach to the house, with how far the night soil traveled and what the farmers paid for it. This question is the town house and the tenement.

The town house: at the back, inside the lot. In an early-modern town house one urinal and one or two stools stood side by side where the through-passage reached the back yard. A Kyoto town house's long plan runs from the shop room on the street through the middle room, the kitchen and the back room to the washroom, privy and bath, and then the small garden and the storehouse. The privy is the back of the house, not a building in the street.

The tenement: shared, at the alley's end. A back tenement had no privy for each unit; in one or two places within the block where the tenements stood, one urinal and several stools stood in a row. In Edo the shared well and privy stood at the far end of the alley. The landlord held the rights to what the shared privy collected, and its sale was a real income.

Who carried it off. These were pit privies, emptied by nearby farmers for fertilizer. At first the farmers came themselves, dealing directly with the samurai residences and the town houses, and in time specialist dealers appeared. In a great source of night soil like Edo, the dealers contracted with landowners and landlords and moved it by boat and by ox and horse, and by one late account the sale of the rights made up most of a tenement landlord's income. In Beijing from the late Qing, night-soil men collected every day from households and dry latrines - some scrubbing the households' chamber pots - and carried it by shoulder pole or cart to a night-soil yard, each yard with its own fixed routes, most of the work done at dawn. No page read says where such a yard stood.

In the setting. The owaiya, who handle night soil, are a burakumin profession. That is a deviation: in Japan the collectors were farmers and then commoner dealers. Whose privies they empty in a town, the campaign notes do not say; either way nothing drawn changes.

What marks the map is the tenement's shared privy, a small shed of its own at the far end of the alley. The street-front house's privy is inside the house's own footprint, and the collection leaves nothing standing in town.

The rule the map follows: a block of back tenements draws one shared privy shed, or two in a long block, at the far end of its alley beside the shared well, about 6 by 9 ft - one ken by a ken and a half, a guess, since no page read measures one. A street-front house draws no privy outside its own footprint.
