One story or two, and thatch, board or tile - what does a town's street front look like from above?

Sources: machiya-shoka-jawiki (the low half-story and the ban behind it, early Edo's board roofs, the 1720 tile policy, Kyoto's storehouse at the back), kotobank-machiya (tile in the west, board in the east, the low storage story, plastered fronts in Edo), kotobank-shukubamachi (a busy post town's two-story board-roofed inns), sekijuku-jawiki (one post town's mix of heights), naraijuku-jawiki (the Kiso valley's stone-weighted board roof), ouchijuku-jawiki (a thatched station), qixian-cnr (a Chinese county seat's two-story shops and gray tile), dianwu-zhwiki (the southern shophouse of two or three stories)

Stories: a low half-story, then two. A machiya usually carried a low second story used for storage; at first only the street side had one, built with a low eave - and it was low because the domains restricted it by ban. Later came the full second story, as large as the ground floor. In Kyoto the half-story stayed usual through the whole early-modern period except for the large merchants. A post town on a busy highway had two-story inns by the late Edo period; one such town, Seki on the Tokaido, still mixes two-story, half-story and one-story houses along its street.

Roofs: tile in the west, board in the east, thatch where the town was half a village. Western Japan roofed its machiya in tile and plastered the walls over the posts; eastern Japan roofed in board, with deep eaves and a light, low-pitched roof weighted with stones; the gable-fronted machiya of the west is thought to carry on the form of the thatched house. The Kiso valley's traditional roof was board weighted with stones; the busy highway's late-Edo inns were board-roofed; and a half-farming station such as Ouchi lined its road with thatched houses. Even Edo was mostly shingle and board in its early years.

Fire law shaped the city, not the town. After the great fire of 1657 Edo tried covering thatch and shingle with earth, which did not last; in 1720 the shogunate recommended tile roofs - which it had for a time forbidden to townsmen - and walls plastered thick like a storehouse's. In late-Edo Edo the plastered storehouse front spread to the main house itself. No page read gives a fire law for a small town's roofs. Where the merchant's storehouse stood differed too: Kyoto never built it on the street but at the back of the lot, the opposite of Edo's plastered street-front shops.

In China, two stories and tile. In the county seat of Qixian the building on the street was mostly two stories, the shop or workshop below and a storeroom above, under the small gray tile of its old houses; the southern shophouse was two or three stories, the shop below and the family above.

From above. A map seen from overhead shows the roof, not the stories: a second story does not change a house's footprint, so it changes nothing drawn in plan. The roof's material does - thatch, board and tile read differently in color and texture.

The rule the map follows: a town's street-front roof is a knob rolled per town among three forms: tile, on the Chinese model and in a western-style Japanese town; board (weighted with stones), in an eastern-style or hill town; thatch, in a quiet town that is half a village. A storehouse stands at the back of its lot, never on the street, unless the town is a great city's. Stories are not drawn. How the three roofs split across towns is on no page read, so the weights are a guess; so is giving a rich merchant's house tile in a board-roofed town, which no page read says.
