How often did a county town hold its market, and how far did its customers walk?

Sources: l7r-market-day (the setting's weekly market day), kotobank-rokusaiichi (the six-day market, and where it lasted), kotobank-sansaiichi (market days staggered between neighbors), jishi-zhwiki (the Chinese periodic market's schedule and its villagers), xutan-2000-market-network (a mid-Qing market's area and walk), skinner-2002-etudes-rurales (how far a Chinese market town's customers walked, in a twentieth-century model), zaigomachi-jawiki (the spacing of Japanese market towns near Osaka)

How often. In the setting the county town holds a market day about once a week, when farmers from throughout the county come in to buy, sell and barter, because the town is the lowest tier that has merchants. The Japanese markets it is drawn from met on a fixed calendar rather than a week. The older periodic market met three times a month; with the growth of trade, markets came to be held six times a month, every five days - the rokusai-ichi - on day pairs such as the 1st and 6th or the 2nd and 7th, two days in each ten. In the Edo period it stayed the center of a region's trade in Tohoku and Kanto, while around Kyoto and Osaka many such markets grew into country towns and the name died out. Neighboring markets set their days so as not to compete: in each region market days were adjusted, for instance by setting them against the central market's. A Chinese periodic market opened one day in every two or several, or three times in ten days, and its buyers and sellers were chiefly the villagers nearby.

So the setting's weekly market is a deviation in its count and not in its kind: about four market days a month, between the three-day and the six-day market, held by a town that is the only market its villages have.

How far. In the mid-Qing, xutan-2000-market-network finds, China's rural markets stood one or two to every 100 square kilometers; a market drew on 40 to 60 square kilometers on the plains and over 100 in the mountains, and its customers walked 3 to 5 km on the plains and 5 to 7 in the mountains, a trip of one or two hours, there and back in half a day. These read as averages, not paced roads (a guess: the page does not state its method, and each area looks like a province's land, hill and water included, divided by its count of markets, with the walk worked out from it); and they are the reach of the lowest tier, the rural periodic market, the market the setting's county town holds for its farmers, not of a larger commercial town. G. William Skinner's model of rural China (skinner-2002-etudes-rurales) lands inside those figures: in its typical case a market town's area was just over fifty square kilometers, market towns stood less than eight kilometers apart, and the longest walk in was about 4.5 km, some 1,500 households in eighteen or so villages. In Japan, no page read gives a market town's reach, but in Settsu and Kawachi near Osaka, a country of cotton and other commercial crops, where the Kinai's country towns grew up in the late seventeenth century, a market town had formed every few kilometers, a spacing of the same order, though a dense case, not a typical one. The setting brings its farmers in from the whole county, which is its own measure; a household at the far edge of the reach stays over on market eve, under the market-day flophouse.
