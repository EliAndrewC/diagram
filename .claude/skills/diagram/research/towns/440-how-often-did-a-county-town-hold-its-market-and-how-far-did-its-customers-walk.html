How often did a county town hold its market, and how far did its customers walk?

Sources: l7r-market-day (the setting's weekly market day), kotobank-rokusaiichi (the six-day market, and where it lasted), kotobank-sansaiichi (market days staggered between neighbors), jishi-zhwiki (the Chinese periodic market's schedule and its villagers), skinner-2002-etudes-rurales (how far a Chinese market town's customers walked)

How often. In the setting the county town holds a market day about once a week, when farmers from throughout the county come in to buy, sell and barter, because the town is the lowest tier that has merchants. The Japanese markets it is drawn from met on a fixed calendar rather than a week. The older periodic market met three times a month; with the growth of trade, markets came to be held six times a month, every five days - the rokusai-ichi - on day pairs such as the 1st and 6th or the 2nd and 7th, two days in each ten. In the Edo period it stayed the center of a region's trade in Tohoku and Kanto, while around Kyoto and Osaka many such market towns became permanent towns and the name died out. Neighboring markets did not meet on the same day: each region set its market days against its central market's, so that the markets would not compete. A Chinese periodic market opened one day in every two or several, or three times in ten days, and its buyers and sellers were chiefly the villagers nearby.

So the setting's weekly market is a deviation in its count and not in its kind: about four market days a month, between the three-day and the six-day market, held by a town that is the only market its villages have.

How far. In the modal case of G. William Skinner's analysis of rural China (skinner-2002-etudes-rurales), a market town's area was just over fifty square kilometers, market towns stood less than eight kilometers apart, and the longest walk in was about 4.5 km, some 1,500 households in eighteen or so villages. No page read gives a Japanese market town's reach. The setting brings its farmers in from the whole county, which is its own measure; a household at the far edge of the reach stays over on market eve, under the market-day flophouse.

A map shows the town on an ordinary day, and nothing on it is drawn from how often the market meets. What an ordinary day still shows of the market is under where a town's market was held.
