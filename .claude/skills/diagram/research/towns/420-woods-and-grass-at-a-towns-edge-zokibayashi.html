Woods and grass at a town's edge (zōkibayashi)

The ground just beyond a settlement's fields was not wild. It was worked for what the settlement burned and what its fields ate, in two forms: a cut-over broadleaf wood, the zōkibayashi, cut for firewood, charcoal and fertilizer and left to regrow from the stumps, and a cut grass ground, the magusaba, where thatch and fodder were cut. Such woods were cut back every few decades, so they stood as thin, low trees, several stems to a stump. One charcoal district has made its charcoal from kunugi oak woods since at least the Muromachi period, its old records note the rotational cutting, and today it cuts them every eight or nine years.

That these two covers were a town's, and its only two, lying just beyond its fields rather than near its houses, is this record's reading of the sources below, which describe them near settlements in general. How a village kept its own fuel wood is at Where did a village keep its fuel wood?, and how thickly a worked coppice was stocked at How thickly was a worked coppice stocked?.

  • The wood near a settlement was young broadleaf wood that had grown back after cutting.
    The zōkibayashi was a secondary wood of deciduous broadleaf trees - konara oak, hornbeam, ryobu and alder among them - including woods for firewood and charcoal.
  • Such woods gave fuel, food and fertilizer long before Japan began to modernize in 1868.
    Woods of this kind, mostly konara and kunugi oak, were used since long before the Meiji period for firewood and charcoal, wild greens, and fertilizer and materials for farming.
  • How often was such a wood cut?
    About every twenty to thirty years, by a modern account that gives no date for the cycle. The woods were cut and left to sprout again from the stumps, so they stood as relatively thin, low trees growing in clumps from the stump.
  • Such woods were planted on purpose on the Musashino upland, opened for farming from 1654 in the Edo period.
    When the Kawagoe domain opened part of the upland to feed a growing Edo, trees were planted on the grass plain to make woods on the flat; their fallen leaves were gathered as compost for the fields, and the woods kept the soil from blowing away.

A wood cut back on a short cycle, so that it stands as many thin stems, is attested well before the Meiji period in one charcoal district, famous for a fine charcoal, on the upper Ina River on the Hyogo-Osaka border.

  • The kunugi woods of the upper Ina River made Ikeda charcoal from at least the Muromachi period.
    The district's charcoal is traced to at least the Muromachi period by old documents and tradition, and Edo-period documents record its use for the tea ceremony.
  • Records from 1145 to 1833 note the Ikeda charcoal district's rotational cutting.
    A study of the district found 27 records of its charcoal in old documents and traditions from those years, its rotational cutting among them.
  • A land survey of the Enpo era names kunugi hills in the Ikeda charcoal villages.
    Its registers record kunugi hills at Hitokura, Kurokawa, Kunisaki and Yoshikawa.
  • A late-Edo farm book describes the cut-back kunugi and its winter cutting.
    The Koeki Kokusanko says the tree puts out hundreds of branches from about one jo up, and those grown half a shaku to one shaku around are cut in winter and burned into the Ikeda charcoal used for the tea ceremony.
  • The Ikeda charcoal district cuts its kunugi every eight or nine years today.
    The rotational cutting makes a patchwork landscape. These figures are one charcoal district's, not an ordinary fuel wood's near a town.
  • The old records agree with today's practice on how often the kunugi is cut.
    Comparing the old documents and books with the district's charcoal making today, the same study found them in agreement on the rotation period, the planting of kunugi and the cut-back daiba kunugi, among other points.

Beside the wood, the other worked cover was grass.

  • The grass ground near a settlement was the villages' common, cut for thatch and fodder.
    Roofing material for houses and fodder for the animals were cut on it, on ground the villages held in common. A road town's ground of this kind, kept for its horses, is at What are the barns in a town's hayfield, and how big are they?.
  • No page we read says how much of a town's margin was wood and how much grass.
    The pages on both covers say what each was cut for, not how much ground each took.
