Where does a town's built edge stop, and does a ribbon of houses run on along the road?

Sources: kotobank-ressen (the settlement that grows as a strip along a highway because it lives by the traffic), kotobank-edo (Edo's town ground running on along its highways, and taken into the town), chang-morphology-walled-capitals (the walled city's biggest suburbs lying along its main route)

It does not stop square. A place that lives by a road grows along it. Japanese geographers name the form: a long, narrow settlement facing a highway and living by transport and trade is a street village, as against a road village, whose houses along the road are farmers'. A town's edge takes that form where its roads leave it. The clearest case the record has is a great city's: after the fire of 1657 Edo's town ground spread along the highways, and many towns formed along the suburban highways; in 1662 the ground around the old core that had been built up was taken under the town magistrate as town ground. The strip came first and the government caught up with it.

A walled town. Outside a wall the same growth is the gate suburb, and the biggest of those - the ones that came in time to be walled in their turn - lay along the main traffic route. On a town map that strip is the gate market, What is in a town's gate market, and how far out along the road does it run?.

How far. No readable page gives the length of the ribbon beyond a town's last block, or how quickly its houses thinned; the Edo case is the country's largest city, and the study of walled cities measures no suburb. The length is a guess.

What it means for a map: an unwalled town's built edge does not stop square at the last block where a road leaves it; along each road that carries traffic, houses run on out as a ribbon one lot deep on either side, thinning with distance, and the road carrying the most traffic has the longest ribbon. On a walled town the ribbon outside the gate is the gate market. Its length is a guess.
