Does a town's street front stand wall to wall, or house by house?

Sources: kotobank-shukubamachi (the busy road's town built eave to eave, the side road's town with gaps), machiya-shoka-jawiki (the machiya built to the full width of its lot, the fire wall against the neighbor), unnojuku-jawiki (a post town's fire walls), ouchijuku-jawiki (a half-farming station's thatched houses), matouqiang-zhwiki (the Chinese fire gable between house and house), qixian-cnr (a county seat's shops set close along the street)

In Japan, both - and the traffic decided which. A machiya was built to the full width of its lot; in a post town on a busy highway such as the Tokaido or the Nakasendo the main house stood on the road across the full frontage of its lot, and by the late Edo period two-story inns lined the road eave to eave, planned like a big city's machiya. Houses that close were built against each other's fire: some machiya raised a small roofed wall on the roof at the boundary with the neighbor, to stop a fire spreading from next door, and the post town of Unno on the Hokkoku road still shows such fire walls on its roofs. On side roads and in mountain stations the other form was usual: inns whose plans differed little from the thatched farmhouses around them took a front yard on the road side and often stood with a gap between them and the next house. Ouchi, a "half farming, half station" stop of the Aizu western highway, still lines the old road with thatched houses. How many towns took each form is on no page read.

In China, close-set. Because traditional houses were mostly of wood, and to save land, the lanes between houses were very narrow, and a gable wall rising well above the roof was put between one house and the next to stop fire spreading - the "horse-head wall", a hallmark of the Hui (Huizhou) style. In the county seat of Qixian the shop signs on both sides of the street stood close-set as fish scales. Neither page says in so many words that a county seat's street front shared its walls; the walls of a city's commoner terraces are under urban commoners built in continuous street walls.

The rule the map follows: whether a town's street front stands wall to wall is a knob rolled per town. A busy town, on a great road or at a market, builds each house across the full width of its lot, touching its neighbors along the street; a quiet town on a side road or in the hills sets its houses back behind a front yard with a gap between one and the next, farmhouse fashion. A town on the Chinese model is close-set. The weight between the two Japanese forms is a guess, since no page read counts them; the drawn joint between two touching shops is the convention described under machiya row density.
