Towns: the county seat and the post town, and who lives in them (machi)

A town in this setting is one of two kinds. A market county seat exists for its district and its market day (see the gate market). A post town lives by the road: in Japan it was a commercial settlement grown up around a relay station on the highways, its townhouses usually running in a long narrow band along the road, and it took its name from forwarding men and horses and lodging travelers. An ordinary post town on the Tokaido had a few hundred houses and a few dozen inns.

A Japanese county town and a Chinese one also differ at a glance. The Chinese seat was usually walled, laid out around crossing streets, its wealthier houses turned inward behind blank walls, its magistrate in a hall on the main axis. The Japanese seat was usually an unwalled street of shopfronts, its magistrate in a banked compound around which the town grew. Each of those points is told in full in its own section: the wall, the street plan, the Chinese town's axis and where the magistrate's compound stood.

  • A post town was a town that depended on the road's traffic.
    The relay duty was laid on castle towns, ports and rural market towns alike; the name post town was kept for the ones whose living depended most on forwarding men and horses and on lodging travelers.
  • How big was an ordinary post town on the Tokaido?
    On the Tokaido, a highway of heavy traffic, an ordinary post town had about 300 to 600 houses and 30 to 60 inns, by one Japanese historical dictionary's reckoning.
  • How far did the real post towns spread around that figure?
    The shogunate's survey of the Tokaido in 1843, late in the Edo period, gives the spread: along one prefecture's stretch of that road, from 197 houses at Hakone to 1,542 at the castle town of Odawara, and 632 houses with 42 inns at Seki.
  • A great station could run a ri (about 2.4 miles) or more along the road.
    Stretching long along the highway was the post town's mark, and some of the large stations reached a length of one ri or more. How long the ordinary ones were is at how long and how deep a town's built core is.
  • The relay office and the lords' inns stood at about the middle of the town.
    The office that forwarded official men and horses, and the inns where traveling lords and the like lodged, stood at about the middle, with the inns, teahouses and cookshops for ordinary travelers and the porters' and pack-horse offices before and behind them, and the other merchants and craftsmen further out.
  • The road through a station was comparatively wide, with a water channel along it.
    It ran 2 to 4 ken (about 12 to 24 ft), with a channel down its middle or along its edge, for fighting fire and for watering men and horses.
  • A town on the five great highways had a gate mound at each end.
    The mound was of stone walling or of earth.
  • The road bent at a post town's entrances, so that its street could not be seen down from outside.
    The highway curved at the entrances, or turned through a squared kink, a masugata.
  • A post town's lots were even strips of one depth.
    The lots along the road were laid out to a plan as long strips, of nearly constant depth and differing frontage; the station's households were allotted lots of roughly even size, and were often freed of ground rent in return for supplying horses or porters.
  • Most of a post town's households farmed as well.
    Apart from the station's officials the residents usually farmed, and the back of each lot was often a vegetable garden.
  • On a busy highway the houses stood eave to eave; on a side road they stood apart, each with a front yard.
    On highways such as the Tokaido and the Nakasendo each main house was built across the full width of its lot, and by the later Edo period the two-story, board-roofed inns stood eave to eave. On side roads and in the mountains the inns were often little different in plan from the thatched farmhouses round about, each with a front yard toward the road and a gap to its neighbor.

China's relay stations were organized differently.

  • A Chinese relay station could be a walled town of its own.
    Jimingyi, the largest and best-preserved surviving one, covers about 56 acres inside a wall about 2,000 m (~6,562 ft) round and 11 m (~36 ft) high, with gates east and west. Five roads divide it into twelve blocks, the relay office at the center, the stables in the northwest, the granary in the northeast, shops and houses among them, and the post road passes east-west along the south side of the town.
  • Was an ordinary Chinese relay station walled like Jimingyi?
    No page we read says how many relay stations were walled towns, or describes an ordinary one's plan.

A county seat, Japanese or Chinese, is told apart from its neighbor first by its wall, then by its street, its houses and its seat of government.

  • A Chinese county seat was usually walled, and a Japanese one was not.
    The history and the exceptions are at Was a county seat walled?
  • A Japanese county town is usually one street, without the separate quarters of a castle town.
    It is one or two rows of townhouses along a highway or a river on strip lots, without the separate quarters for samurai, townsmen and temples that a castle town has. A Chinese walled seat is laid out inside its wall around crossing streets; the forms are at Did a county seat string out along its road or lay out a grid?
  • The Japanese townhouse faces the street.
    At a busy post station each main house filled the whole width of its lot along the highway, and by the later Edo period its inns stood eave to eave. So a Japanese street is lined with shopfronts.
  • The Chinese courtyard house faces inward, behind blank walls.
    The siheyuan is a courtyard with buildings on its four sides - the main hall, the east and west side halls and the south range, its back wall on the lane and its doors opening north into the court - closing the court in. A large one is walled round from outside with high walls and no windows, for privacy, and the two-court and larger houses were usually the homes of officials and gentry. So a Chinese residential lane is lined with blank walls and gates; the row of shops along a Chinese commercial street is at Chinese towns were planned.
  • The Japanese magistrate's seat was the jin'ya, a compound around which a town grew.
    It held the intendant's residence, the office for public business, the staff's houses, the rice and levy stores, the judgment yard and the jail, and around it samurai houses lined up, and merchants and craftsmen who traded with the jin'ya's warriors gathered. The Chinese seat's magistrate sat in the yamen on the town's main axis, a reading for an ordinary county seat at Chinese towns were planned.
  • Where did the jin'ya stand in its town?
    It had no one siting: a small town sometimes formed as one with it, but at the seats of outlying holdings and the like many were built beside a settlement already there. The rest is at Where does a magistrate's manor stand?
  • A small lord's seat could house its retainers inside its own bank and moat.
    In the seats of small domains one sees a layout with earthworks and moats but no high stone walls, holding the residence, the offices, retainers' official houses, storehouses and a drill ground.

Who lives in a town on our maps is this setting's own canon, not history's: the counts below are from the setting's budget notes. The same census for a provincial city is at Who lives in a provincial city?

  • A county seat holds about 1,200 inhabitants in about 238 households.
    Unlike a hamlet it is not one caste: beside its roughly 156 farming households, the setting's notes give a town about 29 laborer households, 24 merchant households, 13 servant households, 12 burakumin households and 4 samurai families.
  • About 20 samurai live in a county town, about 15 of them the magistrate and their staff.
    The rest, about five, are not working.
  • The setting's notes name no headman for a town.
    They give the village a headman's household, and name the magistrate and their staff as the town's working samurai.
  • Wealth varies within a caste.
    The town's wealth rows put a minority of merchants in the very rich and rich brackets, and a few master laborers above the rest of their caste.
  • A merchant's apprentices and a samurai household's servants lived in.
    A merchant's apprentices lived in the shop from about the age of ten, and a samurai household's private servants, the komono, lived in and did its odd jobs.
