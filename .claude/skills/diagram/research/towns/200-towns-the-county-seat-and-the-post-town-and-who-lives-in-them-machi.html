Towns: the county seat and the post town, and who lives in them (machi)

A town in this setting is one of two kinds. A market county seat exists for its district and its market day (see Gate markets outside a town (guanxiang)). In this setting one holds some 1,200 inhabitants. A post town lives by the road: in Japan it was a commercial settlement grown up around a relay station on the highways, its townhouses usually running in a long narrow band along the road. An ordinary post town on the Tokaido had a few hundred houses and a few dozen inns.

A Japanese county town and a Chinese one also differ at a glance. The Chinese seat was usually walled, laid out around crossing streets, its wealthier houses turned inward behind blank walls, its magistrate in a hall on the main axis. The Japanese seat was usually an unwalled street of townhouses, its magistrate in a banked compound.

  • Castle towns, ports and market towns all carried the relay duty, but only those that lived by it were called post towns.
    The relay duty was laid on castle towns, ports and rural market towns alike; the name post town was kept for the ones whose living depended most on forwarding men and horses and on lodging travelers.
  • How big was an ordinary post town on the Tokaido?
    On the Tokaido, a highway of heavy traffic, an ordinary post town had about 300 to 600 houses and 30 to 60 inns, by one Japanese historical dictionary's reckoning.
  • How much did the Tokaido's post towns vary in size?
    A survey of the Tokaido's stations in 1843, late in the Edo period, gives the spread: along one prefecture's stretch of that road, from 197 houses at Hakone to 1,542 at the castle town of Odawara, and 632 houses with 42 inns at Seki.
  • A great post town could run a ri (about 2.4 miles) or more along the road.
    Stretching long along the highway was the post town's mark, and some of the large stations reached a length of one ri or more. How long the ordinary ones were is at The size and density of a town: its street, its wall and its ragged ends.
  • The relay office and the lords' inns stood at about the middle of the town.
    The office that forwarded official men and horses, and the inns where traveling lords and the like lodged, stood at about the middle, with the inns, teahouses and cookshops for ordinary travelers and the porters' and pack-horse offices before and behind them, and the other merchants and craftsmen further out.
  • The road through a post town was comparatively wide, with a water channel along it.
    It ran 2 to 4 ken (about 12 to 24 ft), with a channel down its middle or along its edge, for fighting fire and for watering men and horses.
  • A post town on the Five Highways had a gate mound (mitsuke) at each end.
    The mound was of stone walling or of earth.
  • The road bent at a post town's entrances, so that its street could not be seen down from outside.
    The highway curved at the entrances, or turned through a squared kink, a masugata.
  • A post town's lots were even strips of one depth.
    The lots along the road were laid out to a plan as long strips, of nearly constant depth and differing frontage, and the station's households were allotted lots of roughly even size.
  • A post town's households often paid no ground rent, in return for supplying horses or porters.
    The people of a station were in many cases freed of ground rent in return for providing horses or porters.
  • Most of a post town's households farmed as well.
    Apart from the station's officials the residents usually farmed, and the back of each lot was often a vegetable garden.
  • On a busy highway the houses stood eave to eave; on a side road they stood apart, each with a front yard.
    On highways such as the Tokaido and the Nakasendo each main house was built across the full width of its lot, and by the later Edo period the two-story, board-roofed inns stood eave to eave. On side roads and in the mountains the inns were often little different in plan from the thatched farmhouses round about, each with a front yard toward the road and a gap to its neighbor.

China's relay stations were organized differently.

  • A Chinese relay station could be a walled town of its own.
    Jimingyi, the largest and best-preserved surviving one, covers about 56 acres inside a wall about 2,000 m (~6,562 ft) round and 11 m (~36 ft) high, with gates east and west. Five roads divide it into twelve blocks, the relay office at the center, the stables in the northwest, the granary in the northeast, shops and houses among them, and the post road passes east-west along the south side of the town.
  • Was an ordinary Chinese relay station walled like Jimingyi?
    No page we read says how many relay stations were walled towns, or describes an ordinary one's plan.

The Japanese and the Chinese county seat, point by point:

  • A Chinese county seat was usually walled, and a Japanese one was not.
    The history and the exceptions are at Town walls, and which county seats had one (chengqiang)
  • A Japanese county town is usually one street, without the separate quarters of a castle town.
    It is one or two rows of townhouses along a highway or a river on strip lots, without the separate quarters for samurai, townsmen and temples that a castle town has.
  • A Chinese walled seat was laid out around crossing streets.
    The forms, and their exceptions, are at Town plans: the street town (gaison), the planned grid and the castle town
  • The Chinese courtyard house faced inward, behind blank walls, not onto the street.
    The siheyuan is a courtyard with buildings on its four sides - the main hall, the east and west side halls and the south range, its back wall on the lane and its doors opening north into the court - closing the court in. A large one is walled round from outside with high walls and no windows, for privacy, and the two-court and larger houses were usually the homes of officials and gentry. So a Chinese residential lane is lined with blank walls; the row of shops along a Chinese commercial street is at Town plans: the street town (gaison), the planned grid and the castle town.
  • The Japanese magistrate's seat was the jin'ya, a compound of residence, offices, stores and jail.
    It held the intendant's residence, the office for public business, the staff's houses, the rice and levy stores, the judgment yard and the jail.
  • Did a town grow around the jin'ya, or did the jin'ya join a town already there?
    Where a town grew around one, samurai houses lined up, and merchants and craftsmen who traded with the jin'ya's warriors gathered. But the jin'ya had no one siting: a small town sometimes formed as one with it, while at the seats of outlying holdings and the like many were built beside a settlement already there. The rest is at The magistrate's manor in a town: where it stands and which way it faces (jin'ya and yamen)
  • Did the Chinese magistrate's yamen stand on the town's main axis?
    In the planned Chinese town it did; how far that holds for an ordinary county seat is set out at Town plans: the street town (gaison), the planned grid and the castle town.
  • A small lord's jin'ya could house the lord's retainers inside its own bank and moat.
    In the seats of small domains (han), where the jin'ya stood next in rank to a castle, one sees a layout with earthworks and moats but no high stone walls, holding the residence, the offices, retainers' official houses, storehouses and a drill ground.

Who lives in a town is this setting's own canon, not history's: the counts below are from the setting's budget notes. The same census for a provincial city is at Who lives in a provincial city?

  • A county seat holds about 1,200 inhabitants in about 238 households.
    Unlike a hamlet it is not one caste: beside its roughly 156 farming households, the setting's notes give a town about 29 laborer households, 24 merchant households, 13 servant households, 12 burakumin households and 4 samurai families.
  • About 20 samurai live in a county town, about 15 of them the magistrate and their staff.
    The rest, about five, are not working; at five to a household, these 20 are the town's 4 samurai families.
  • The setting's notes name no headman for a town.
    They give the village a headman's household, and name the magistrate and their staff as the town's working samurai.
  • Wealth varies within a caste.
    The town's wealth rows put a minority of merchants in the very rich and rich brackets, and a few master laborers above the rest of their caste.
  • A merchant's apprentices and a samurai household's servants lived in.
    The census counts its servants as households, but many did not keep a house of their own. A merchant's apprentices lived in the shop from about the age of ten, and a samurai household's private servants, the komono, lived in and did its odd jobs.
