Was a county seat walled?

Sources: xiancheng-zhwiki (how many county seats were walled, dynasty by dynasty), city-wall-grades-zhwiki (how high and how long a county wall was), kotobank-ikaku-toshi (walled towns common in China, rare in Japan), kotobank-jokaku-toshi (in Japan only the lord's castle was fortified), jokaku-toshi-jawiki (the Japanese towns that were enclosed), jinya-kotobank (the magistrate's seat itself banked and moated)

In China, mostly yes - by period. Whether a county seat was walled turned on the dynasty. The Han dynasty ordered every county town walled; the Song dynasty, wary of provincial warlords, discouraged walls away from the frontier, and many interior seats went long without; by the late Ming more than two thirds of county seats are estimated to have been walled, and by the end of the Qing the great majority of prefecture, department and county seats were. Walled towns were so common that a Japanese encyclopedia describes China as a society of enclosures, every county and provincial city and even villages walled. A county wall was modest: most were only 4 to 10 m (13 to 33 ft) high, and most county seats were ringed by less than 2 km (6,600 ft) of it. How a provincial city's wall worked - its moat, its gates and their barbicans, its towers and the patrol road inside it - is under the city defenses questions, from what keeps the moat full to where a neighborhood fence stops at the rampart. Which county seats of the late Ming made up the unwalled third, and why, is on no page read.

In Japan, almost never. In medieval and early-modern Japan only the lord's castle was fortified; the castle town's residents, ordinary samurai and townsmen alike, lived outside it, and that is what sets the Japanese town apart from the walled city of the continent. Town areas bounded by moats or rivers are found, but, apart from Kyoto's earth rampart, a wall around a whole town was rare. The exceptions are specific: self-governing trading towns such as Sakai, Hakata, Hirano and Imai ringed themselves with earth banks and moats, the temple towns of the Ikko sect were defended by moats and banks, and some warlords' castle towns of the civil wars were given an outer ring of river, moat and bank. The jin'ya - in the Edo period the seat of a lord without a castle, or the office from which a local official or a shogunal intendant governed - was itself often ringed with earth banks and a moat in the early years, but mostly without high stone walls, and no page read says the town that grew around it was enclosed.

What makes a town walled. In China, being an administrative seat in an age that built walls; in Japan, a town that had to defend itself - a self-governing merchant town, a sect's temple town, a warlord's castle town in a time of war. A Japanese county town under settled rule stood open.

The rule the map follows: whether a county seat is walled is a knob rolled per town. A seat on the Chinese model is walled about two times in three - the share attested for the late Ming - with a rampart no more than about 33 ft high and 6,600 ft round (the wall's own shape is under a rampart's cost scales with its length, its section under how high and thick is a town's rampart, its moat under did a walled county town have a moat); a seat on the Japanese model stands open, its magistrate's compound alone banked or moated, and only rarely - a town that governs and defends itself - is it ringed by an earth bank and moat instead of a wall. The two-in-three weight follows the Chinese finding; which model a seat follows is the setting's, not the history's.
