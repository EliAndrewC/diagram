Vegetable gardens at a town's edge, fed by its night soil

Land close to a city went to vegetables for the city's market. Fresh vegetables could not be kept for long, so they were grown near the people who ate them, and the city's night soil went back out to the same fields as their manure. A Chinese farm manual gives the rule, and the gardens around Kaifeng, Edo, Osaka and Yokohama show it in practice. Every case is a great city's; that a county town's edge did the same on a smaller scale is this record's reading.

  • A Chinese farm manual advises planting land near a city wall thick with vegetables.
    The sixth-century Qimin yaoshu says land near a city wall should be planted with plenty of melons, vegetables and eggplant, both for the household and to sell what is left over.
  • Is the city-wall advice as old as the sixth-century manual?
    Perhaps not, but it is at least medieval. The sentence stands in the "miscellaneous remarks" set before the manual's first book, which the eighteenth-century imperial editors suspected a later hand of adding, though an edition of the Song dynasty already had them.
  • Kaifeng, the Northern Song capital, was ringed with gardens.
    A memoir of the city at the end of the Northern Song says it was all gardens around the capital, with no idle land within a hundred li.

The Japanese cases give the reason for growing close, and show the night soil going back the other way.

  • Katsushika, close to Edo, grew vegetables for the city because they did not keep.
    In the Edo period Katsushika, east of Edo, took to growing daikon, eggplant, cucumber, leeks and turnips as well as rice, because fresh vegetables could not be kept long.
  • Edo's night soil went back by boat to Katsushika's vegetable farms.
    Katsushika's ward history says populous Edo gave off a great deal of night soil, carried there by boat, and the farmers bought it because the crops grew well on it.
  • East of Edo, marsh and tideflats were reclaimed into fields fed with the city's night soil.
    The ground lay in what are now three of Tokyo's eastern wards; reclaimed into paddy and fields, it grew rice and vegetables in quantity, sold in the city.
  • West of Edo, new upland fields were opened to feed the city.
    Their produce went in by handcart, ox cart and river boat.
  • Osaka's vegetable villages came to lean on the city's night soil.
    The villages around Osaka that supplied it with greens used night soil on a large scale once other fertilizers grew dear, in the middle of the early-modern (Edo) period.
  • In 1909, four decades after Japan began to modernize in 1868, Yokohama still had vegetable fields inside its city limits.
    A visitor rode a tram between fields and gardens of wheat, barley, onions, carrots and cabbage before he was out of the city limits.
  • Night soil still went out from Yokohama to the fields by the cartload in 1909.
    A visitor riding from Yokohama to Tokyo saw loads of night soil going out from early morning, within the city and along the roads to the fields, most often on carts pulled by men.
  • Did a town's own edge carry vegetable gardens for its market?
    Probably, but no page we read says so of a town. The manual's advice is for any land near a city wall, and a county town's wall is one; that a town's edge carried vegetable plots for its market, fed by its own night soil, is this record's reading of the manual and the cities' cases.
  • No page we read gives how far out from a town its vegetable gardens reached.
    The cases that give the gardens at all are great cities', and none of them measures the ring of gardens around the wall.
