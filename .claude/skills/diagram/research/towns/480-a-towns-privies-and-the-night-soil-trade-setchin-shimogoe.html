A town's privies and the night-soil trade (setchin, shimogoe)

A Japanese town house's privy stood at the back of the house, where its earth-floored passage reached the back yard, and a back tenement's units shared one or two privies on the lot, in Edo at the far end of the lane beside the shared well. The privies were pits, and what they held was fertilizer: nearby farmers emptied them, and in time specialist dealers contracted for it with the landlords, for whom its sale was real income.

A farm's privy is at Where did the privy stand, and where was its night soil kept?, and a samurai residence's, with how far the night soil traveled and what the farmers paid for it, at Privies (setchin).

  • A town house's privy stood at the back, where the passage met the back yard.
    In an early modern town house one urinal and one or two stools stood side by side where the through-passage reached the back yard.
  • In a Kyoto town house the privy came near the end of the long plan.
    The plan ran from the shop room on the street through the middle room, the kitchen and the back room to the washroom, privy and bath, and then the small court garden and the storehouse (Gardens behind a town house (tsubo-niwa)).
  • A back tenement shared its privies.
    No unit had its own: in one or two places within the block where the tenements stood, one urinal and several stools stood in a row.
  • No page we read gives the size of a tenement's shared privy.
    The reference entry on the privy counts its urinal and stools, not its measure.
  • In Edo the shared well and privy stood at the far end of the lane.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on town houses places them at the far end of the back tenements' lane (roji). The lane itself is at Laborers' housing: back-lot tenements (uradana) and the master laborer's house.
  • The landlord owned what a tenement's shared privy collected.
    Its sale brought an income not to be sneezed at.
  • In Edo, farmers came for the night soil first, then specialist dealers.
    At first farmers came to collect it, dealing directly with the samurai residences and the town houses, and in time specialist dealers appeared.
  • In Edo, dealers contracted with landowners and landlords and moved the night soil by boat and by ox and horse.
    A source of night soil as large as Edo brought specialist collectors and middlemen into being.
  • How much of a tenement landlord's income was night soil?
    By one late account, most of it: the Morisada manko is cited as saying that in the late Edo period the sale of the rights made up the greater part of a tenement landlord's income.
  • City privies were still emptied by nearby farmers after the Meiji Restoration.
    They were pit privies, emptied for fertilizer.

In Beijing the trade was an occupation of its own, with yards and fixed rounds.

  • In Beijing from the last years of the Qing dynasty, night-soil men collected every day on fixed routes.
    They went daily to each household and to the dry latrines and carried the night soil by shoulder pole or cart to a night-soil yard. Each yard had its own routes and districts, and most of the work was done at dawn.
  • Some of Beijing's night-soil men specialized in scrubbing households' chamber pots.
    The same account sets them apart from the men who carried the night soil.
  • No page we read says where Beijing's night-soil yards stood.
    The Beijing account names the yards and their routes, not their places.

The setting assigns the work to another caste.

  • In the setting, the owaiya, a burakumin trade, handle the night soil.
    The setting lists the owaiya, who handle night soil, among the burakumin's professions. That is a deviation: in Japan the collectors were farmers and then specialist dealers.
  • The setting does not say whose privies the owaiya empty in a town.
    It names them among the burakumin's professions and says nothing of where they work.
