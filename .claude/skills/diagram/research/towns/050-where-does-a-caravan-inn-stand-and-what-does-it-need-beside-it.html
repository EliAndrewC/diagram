Where does a caravan inn stand, and what does it need beside it?

Sources: dachedian-jilin-daily (the north-Chinese wagon inn: where it stood, its yard, its stable, and its single story), okabe-hatago-jawiki (a surviving Japanese post-station inn, two-story, and its silence on a yard or a stable), kotobank-umayado (the Japanese inn that kept travelers' horses, and the one picture of a stable behind one); the distances are calibrated against the drawn towns, jishi-zhwiki, shukuba-jawiki

A county seat sits on the trade route that made it worth administering, so it keeps lodging of a second kind, above the flophouse: one prominent caravan inn for the wagon-train merchants, their drivers and their guards, with a large stable beside it and open ground beside that for the oxen and horses. The closest attested analogue is the north-Chinese wagon inn, which stood on the main thoroughfare or in the belt around a city's outskirts and kept exactly that arrangement: open ground in the back yard for parking the carts, a long stable with one very long feeding trough and a tying rail above it, a lean-to beside the stable for the grooms, and a well in the yard. The inn takes one of two forms, because the record holds two analogues that disagree on the one thing a map can show. The wagon inn is single-story: that account puts the buildings in its yard uniformly single-story, of mud brick and reed thatch. The post-station inn is drawn two-story on its own analogue: a Tokaido hatago of about 1836 still stands, two stories, its upper floor the guest rooms. Neither is the better analogue; they are two answers the record supports, so - by the GM's ruling - a town rolls one, and both are drawn as their sources describe them. What stands beside a post-station inn is less sure. A Japanese inn that took a traveler's horse into its keeping was a named kind of place, the umayado, so a stable behind such an inn is not our invention - but the one picture of that arrangement the record has is a sentence from a 1913 novel, cited by a dictionary as the earliest example of that sense of the word, which dates the picture late rather than early; and nothing read describes a cart yard, a feeding trough or a grooms' lean-to at a lodging house of that kind. So under the post-station form the stable stands on thin ground and the cart yard is a guess, drawn because the town's layout rule wants it, not because a Japanese source shows it. The city tier keeps several of these at its gates; a town keeps one. The failure the rule guards against is an inn drawn correctly and then buried: a caravan pulls up to the road it is already on, so an inn behind two ranks of shops is an inn nobody can reach with a wagon.

The rule the map follows: a town's inn takes one of the two forms - the single-story wagon inn or the two-story post-station inn - rolled from the map's own seed like every other knob, with no bias between them, because the record says nothing about how common either was; a map may pin one.

The rule the map follows: one inn with its stable within about 150 ft , and open ground beside the stable - no more than four dwellings within about 75 ft of it. The inn fronts the route it serves: within about 115 ft of the roadbed with no building between it and the road, its entrance turned to the nearest point of the road and its long edge running along it, so a diagonal road gets a tilted inn. An unwalled town sites it along the road where the caravans pass; a walled town keeps inn and stable inside the rampart, fronting the main street, because the caravans come in through the gate.
