Where does a caravan inn stand, and what does it need beside it?

Sources: park-yeolha-ilgi-dianshe (a Korean envoy's account of the north-Chinese inn's yard, stable and troughs), yeolha-ilgi-zhwiki (the year of that journey), dachedian-jilin-daily (the wagon inn of northeast China from the end of the Qing to the 1970s: where it stood, its yard, its stable, its grooms' lean-to, its well and its single story), okabe-hatago-jawiki (a surviving Japanese post-station inn, two-story, and its silence on a yard or a stable), kotobank-umayado (the Japanese inn that kept travelers' horses, and the one picture of a stable behind one), jishi-zhwiki (a Chinese merchants' lodging that also warehoused its guests' goods), shukuba-jawiki (the grades of lodging at a Japanese post station); the distances are calibrated against the drawn towns

A county seat on these maps stands on a trade route, so it keeps lodging of a second kind, above the flophouse: one prominent caravan inn for the wagon-train merchants and their goods, which in its Chinese form keeps a long stable and open ground for the carts in its yard. The Chinese inn kept that arrangement before modern times: a Korean traveling with an embassy to the Qing court in 1780 found on the road to Shanhaiguan that an inn's courtyard was broad, "not less than several hundred paces", because otherwise it could not hold the carts, horses and crowds; its stable had long troughs of stone, or of brick built in the same form, and dozens of wooden troughs stood in rows in the yard as well. A newspaper's account of the wagon inn of northeast China, which began at the end of the Qing and lasted into the 1970s, describes the same arrangement - open ground in the back yard for parking the carts, a long stable with one very long feeding trough and a tying rail above it - and adds a lean-to beside the stable for the grooms and a well in the yard. The lean-to and the well are in that account alone, a newspaper's of 2026, and the 1780 account names neither; by the GM's rule of 2026-09-28 that nothing only modern sources show is drawn, the maps do not draw the grooms' lean-to or the inn's own well. The inn takes one of two forms, because the record holds two analogues that disagree on the one thing a map can show. The wagon inn is single-story on that account, which puts the buildings in its yard uniformly single-story, of mud brick and reed thatch; the 1780 account gives no height, so the single story is a guess for a premodern inn, carried from the later account. The post-station inn is drawn two-story on its own analogue: a Tokaido hatago of about 1836 still stands, two stories, its upper floor the guest rooms. Neither is the better analogue; they are two answers the record supports, so - by the GM's ruling - a town rolls one. What stands beside a post-station inn is not attested before modern times. A Japanese inn that took a traveler's horse into its keeping was a named kind of place, the umayado, but the dictionary's earliest example of that sense is a sentence from a 1913 novel, and it is the one picture the record has of a large stable behind a travelers' inn; the older senses of the word are a place that kept the post horses and the men who worked there, not an inn's stable. Nothing read describes a cart yard, a feeding trough or a grooms' lean-to at a lodging house of that kind. So the post-station inn is drawn without a stable or a cart yard behind it; the stable and yard belong to the wagon inn, whose premodern source shows them. The city tier keeps several of these at its gates; a town keeps one, a count this project calibrated against its drawn towns rather than took from a source - what a post town kept, and how big one inn was, is How many inns did a post town keep, and how big was one?, and how big a stable is, How big is a stable, and what is its yard like? The failure the rule guards against is an inn drawn correctly and then buried: a caravan pulls up to the road it is already on, so an inn behind two ranks of shops is an inn nobody can reach with a wagon.

The rule the map follows: a town's inn takes one of the two forms - the single-story wagon inn or the two-story post-station inn - rolled from the map's own seed like every other knob, with no bias between them, because the record says nothing about how common either was; a map may pin one.

The rule the map follows: one inn; a wagon inn with its stable within about 150 ft , and open ground beside the stable - no more than four dwellings within about 75 ft of it. The inn fronts the route it serves: within about 115 ft of the roadbed with no building between it and the road, its entrance turned to the nearest point of the road and its long edge running along it, so a diagonal road gets a tilted inn. An unwalled town sites it along the road where the caravans pass; a walled town keeps inn and stable inside the rampart, fronting the main street, because the caravans come in through the gate.
