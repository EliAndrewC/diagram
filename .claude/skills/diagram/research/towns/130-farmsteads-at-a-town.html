Farmsteads at a town

Farmers built their houses close to the fields they worked, and a Chinese walled city could keep fields inside its wall. A farm's house, yard, garden and storehouse were a farmer's anywhere, and are described at What stood on a farmstead.

A farmhouse standing alone among its fields was sheltered by its own grove of trees. Where a whole settlement needed shelter, a single belt of trees could be planted for all its houses and fields, as the Ryukyu kingdom did at Tarama in 1742 and the Tsugaru domain in northern Honshu did from 1682.

  • Farmers built close to the fields they worked.
    On the Tonami plain in Japan, farmers built their houses in the middle of their own rice fields so they could manage the water for them. That a town's farmers likewise lived out by its paddies, not among its shops, is this project's reading of that practice.
  • A Chinese walled city could hold farmland inside its wall.
    The Chinese walled city counted farmland among the ordinary uses of the ground inside its wall, and Suzhou's wall still enclosed large tracts of farmland in the twentieth century. Who worked such fields, and where they lived, is at Who works the fields inside a city's wall, and where do they live?.
  • A farmhouse standing alone among its fields had a grove of its own.
    Where the houses of a village stood apart, dotted among the paddies, homestead groves were planted around them as a windbreak. The groves themselves are at Groves of trees around farmhouses (yashikirin).

A belt of trees planted for a whole settlement is older than modern practice; which side of a settlement such a belt stood on is at Does a shelter belt wrap the settlement? and Which way does a shelter belt lie?.

  • The Ryukyu kingdom planted belts of trees around its villages in the eighteenth century.
    Its chief minister Sai On encouraged inland belts called hogo, or village hogo, as windbreaks among other things, and the one on the island of Tarama, made in 1742, still shelters the settlement and its farmland.
  • Tarama's belt rings its settlement.
    Today it runs about 1.8 km (~5,906 ft), 10 to 15 m (~33-49 ft) wide, around the settlement, protecting it and its farmland from typhoons and salt winds.
  • The Tsugaru domain planted a windbreak on the windward side of its new fields from 1682.
    In the far north of Honshu, the domain planted the hills and the western coastal dunes, the side the sea wind came from, to shield the fields it was opening behind them. When the belt was felled for food in the famines of the 1780s, those paddies nearly went back to waste.
