Farmsteads at a town

A farmstead at a town was a farming household's house with its yard, garden and storehouse, as in a village (What stood on a farmstead). Where it stood is less plain: on one Japanese plain farmers built in the middle of their own fields, and a Chinese walled city could keep fields inside its wall.

A farmhouse standing alone among its fields was sheltered by its own grove of trees. Where a whole settlement needed shelter, a single belt of trees could be planted for all its houses and fields, as the Ryukyu kingdom did at Tarama in 1742 and the Tsugaru domain did for its new fields from 1682.

  • Did a town's farmers live out among their fields?
    On the Tonami plain in Japan, farmers built their houses in the middle of their own rice fields so they could manage the water for them. That a town's farmers likewise lived out by its paddies, not among its shops, is this project's reading of that practice.
  • A Chinese walled city could hold farmland inside its wall.
    So a town's fields, and the farmers who worked them, did not always lie outside its wall. The Chinese walled city counted farmland among the ordinary uses of the ground inside its wall, and Suzhou's wall still enclosed large tracts of farmland in the twentieth century. Who worked such fields, and where they lived, is at Who works the fields inside a city's wall, and where do they live?.
  • A farmhouse standing alone among its fields had a grove of its own.
    Where the houses of a village stood apart, dotted among the paddies, homestead groves were planted around them as a windbreak. The groves themselves are at Groves of trees around farmhouses (yashikirin).

Instead of a grove at each house, a settlement could plant one shelter belt for all its houses and fields. The two below date from the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, long before Japan began to modernize in 1868. Which side of a settlement such a belt stood on, and which way it ran, is at Shelter belts on a village's windward side (bofurin).

  • In the eighteenth century the Ryukyu kingdom encouraged its villages to plant belts of trees.
    Its chief minister Sai On encouraged inland belts called hogo, or village hogo, as windbreaks among other things, and the one on the island of Tarama, made in 1742, still shelters the settlement and its farmland.
  • The belt on the Ryukyu island of Tarama rings its settlement.
    Today it runs about 1.8 km (~5,906 ft), 10 to 15 m (~33-49 ft) wide, around the settlement, protecting it and its farmland from typhoons and salt winds.
  • The Tsugaru domain planted a windbreak on the windward side of its new fields from 1682, in the Edo period.
    The domain planted the hills and the western coastal dunes, the side the sea wind came from, to shield the fields it was opening behind them.
  • When the Tsugaru windbreak was felled in the famines of the 1780s, its fields nearly went back to waste.
    In the crop failures of 1784 and 1786 the belt's trees were felled and sold for food, and the new paddies behind it nearly went back to waste.
  • No page we read says whether the farms inside a shared belt of trees also kept groves of their own.
    The pages on homestead groves describe them around houses standing apart, and the pages on the Ryukyu and Tsugaru belts say nothing of the houses inside them.
