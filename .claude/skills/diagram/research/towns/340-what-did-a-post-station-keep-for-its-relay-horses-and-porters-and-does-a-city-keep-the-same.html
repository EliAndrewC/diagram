What did a post station keep for its relay horses and porters, and does a city keep the same?

Sources: toiyaba-kotobank (the relay office: its staff, where it stood in the station, how many there were, and the crowd at a great passage), tenmayaku-kotobank (how many men and horses a station kept, and whose horses they were), jimingyi-visitbeijing (the Chinese station's horse compound), tenmacho-machi-jawiki (the castle town's relay quarter and where it stood)

The heart of a Japanese post station was an office, the toiyaba, where the relay of men and horses was run. Where it stood differed by station: one in the middle of the street, one at each end, or three along it; it was run by the station's headman with the headman's deputies, a clerk who logged every man and horse in and out, and dispatchers who assigned the loads. It was sometimes a part of the headman's own house and sometimes a building of its own, and a station with two headmen had two offices; one or two to a station was usual, and at Okazaki the porters and the horses were relayed from separate offices. When the station's own men and horses fell short, the sukego villages' men and horses were gathered at it, sometimes hundreds of horses and thousands of men, and the scene was like a battlefield.

The numbers were fixed from above. In 1601 the shogunate made the Tokaido's stations keep 36 post horses apiece, requisitioning the horses and the men from the station's own residents; the rule became 100 men and 100 horses a station on the Tokaido, 50 and 50 on the Nakasendo and 25 and 25 on the other highways, the Tokaido's hundred from 1638. No page read describes a stable at the office itself, nor a yard where a hundred horses stood waiting; since the horses were requisitioned from the residents, this record reads the Japanese station's relay stock as kept in its householders' own stables rather than in one place. Jimingyi, a Chinese station, did otherwise: it kept its post horses in one horse compound on its back street.

A castle town kept the same duty in a quarter of its own. The tenma-cho carried the relay horses and the labor that went with them for the lord, and it was placed, as a rule, close to the castle's main gate; as time went on only a few of its houses, such as the ward headman's where the relay office stood, actually handled the horses, and many of the quarter's landlords and householders paid their share in money.

What it means for a map: a relay office is a house on the street, not a yard. Where the relay horses stand is a knob - dispersed through the householders' own stables, as this record reads the Japanese station, or gathered in one horse compound, as at Jimingyi - because the record's one Chinese station and its reading of the Japanese one differ on it. A city that keeps a relay keeps it in a quarter near its lord's main gate. In this setting the relay horses are the Imperial waystation's (What was the Imperial waystation on the ground, and where does it stand?); whether a town or a city also keeps a relay office of its own is not something the history can decide.
