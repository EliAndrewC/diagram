Town plans: the street town (gaison), the planned grid and the castle town

In the Edo period, Japan's post towns, market towns and temple-gate towns almost all strung out along one street, their houses lined up on both sides of it, in many on strip lots. Chinese town planning looked to a written ideal: a classic of ritual draws the capital as a grid of avenues keyed to its gates; a walled town's streets chiefly joined its gates; and the government office, the yamen, faced south, built to the Qing regulations for such offices. So a town's plan differed by region, and within each region it took more than one form.

  • In the Edo period, Japan's post towns, market towns and temple-gate towns almost all took the form of a street town.
    The houses lined both sides of a central street, with the stations of the Tokaido the typical case, and in many the lots were neat strips at right angles to that street.
  • A Japanese townhouse filled the width of its street front and ran long toward the rear, because wide frontage took great wealth.
    Laying out a wide frontage took great means, so the townhouse was built across the full width of its lot and given a plan running long toward the rear.
  • Many of Japan's rural towns - towns in all but law - were one or two rows of townhouses along a highway or a river.
    Such a town belonged in law to the countryside but was in substance a town. In many cases it was one or two rows of townhouses along a highway or a river, on strip lots, without the castle town's separate quarters for samurai, townsmen and temples.
  • Along a road squeezed between mountains and the sea, a Japanese street town was often built on one side only.
    A Japanese encyclopedia (Nipponica) says such one-sided street towns are often seen on highways running along narrow coasts.
  • A Japanese post town that was its district's economic center could grow back streets and blocks.
    A post town often took the street-town form, but where it was the region's economic center it might form a townscape with back streets and blocks.
  • Some Japanese rural towns had planned layouts inside moats and earth banks.
    Rural towns that grew out of temple-precinct towns (jinaicho) or medieval towns sometimes did; Imai's was a regular grid.
  • Japan's castle towns were laid out in zones by status.
    The samurai lived by the castle, the higher their rank the closer. Merchants and artisans lived outside the samurai quarter, resettled there by trade.
  • A castle town's temples stood in rows on its outer edge, where they helped defend it.
    The temples stood in rows on the town's outer edge, and they had a part in its defense.
  • A castle town's streets were bent on purpose, for defense.
    Streets were bent into right-angle turns and given dead ends, lengthening the way to the castle.

Chinese towns started from a plan, and the plan changed as trade grew.

  • The classical plan of a Chinese capital is a grid of avenues keyed to its gates.
    The Rites of Zhou has the builder lay out the capital nine li square, three gates on each side, with nine roads running each way inside it.
  • Japan copied the Chinese capital's grid for its imperial capitals.
    Nara and Kyoto were modeled on the Tang capital Chang'an, on a more modest scale.
  • A walled Chinese town's streets chiefly joined its gates.
    The Chinese Wikipedia article on walled cities says the roads inside such a city were chiefly the streets linking its gates.
  • The Neixiang county yamen, built to the Qing regulations for local government offices, faces south.
    The yamen, the county's government office, sits at the north of its ground and faces south.
  • Did a main avenue run from a county seat's gate straight to its yamen?
    This page's GUESS is that it did. The planned grid is attested for a capital, and the gate-linking streets and the south-facing yamen for a county seat, but no page we read says a main avenue ran from an ordinary county seat's gate to its yamen.
  • Leping, a walled county seat in China, is laid out around a cross street.
    The town, walled in 1562 (its wall is measured at how long a town's core is), has a main east-west commercial avenue formed by its cross street, with a tortoise-shell net of lanes on either side, 14 across and 9 along.
  • Jimingyi, a walled Chinese relay town, is cut into twelve blocks by five crossing roads.
    Jimingyi was a relay town, a station on the imperial post road where couriers changed horses, walled round, with its relay office at the center.
  • How often did a Chinese county seat take each plan?
    For Japan the reference work says almost all post towns, market towns and temple-gate towns were street towns. For China no page we read counts how many county seats took a cross-street plan, a full grid or a single street.
  • Chinese city streets opened to shops in the Song dynasty.
    In the Tang dynasty a city was divided into residential and commercial wards, walled off from one another; by the Song, growing trade let shops line the streets of residential areas instead of standing behind ward walls.
  • Did trade gather outside a Chinese town's gates?
    The streets outside a city gate and the ground near them had a name of their own, the guanxiang, and so did the main street outside the gate. No page we read says trade clustered there. In both countries, the lesser streets of a town were made by the houses along them.

  • Beijing's hutong lanes were formed by rows of courtyard houses.
    Neighborhoods grew by joining one courtyard house to another to form a hutong, then one hutong to another, with small lanes running between the main hutongs for passage.
  • The poor of Edo and Osaka rented row houses on the back alleys behind the shops.
    The back tenement was a rented house whose landlord was the master of the shop on the street. It was a crude partitioned row house facing a back street, and in Edo and Osaka it housed petty traders, artisans and day laborers.
