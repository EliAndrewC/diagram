Where did lords and officials lodge on the road, and how big was it?

Sources: honjin-kotobank (who lodged at a honjin, and how many a station had), honjin-mlit (the honjin's building: one story, its rooms, gate, entrance and garden), kusatsu-honjin-jawiki (one surviving honjin measured), wakihonjin-jawiki (the second-rank lodging), jimingyi-visitbeijing (the official lodging court of a Chinese post station), l7r-budgets (the setting's waystation as a host)

On the Edo highways a traveling lord lodged in a house kept for persons of rank. The honjin was the great lodging house at a post station where daimyo on their journeys to and from Edo, princely houses, court nobles, shogunal officials and high priests rested and slept. A station had one or two on average, but some had none and some had five or six side by side. In principle it was a single-story building, with many matted rooms, board floors and earth floors, an imposing gate, a wide entrance, a raised audience room in the formal reception style, and a garden beyond it.

The one measured here is among the largest that survive. Kusatsu's surviving honjin stands on a site of about 4,719 square meters (1,427 tsubo, about 50,800 square feet) with about 1,547 square meters of floor (468 tsubo, about 16,650 square feet). Below it stood the waki-honjin, smaller but furnished to the same standard, raised audience room and all, which took ordinary travelers whenever no lord or envoy was using it. The ordinary inn was, in turn, a honjin with the gate, the formal entrance and the raised room taken away (How many inns did a post town keep, and how big was one?).

A Chinese post station kept its own lodging for officials. At Jimingyi, among the station's buildings, the official lodging court is a Ming building of brick and timber, three courtyards deep. What a county seat's government compound kept for a passing official, and where in a post town's street a honjin stood, no page read says.

In the setting, a busy trunk-road waystation occasionally hosts ceremonial Imperial travelers. What it means for a map: where a town lodges lords and officials, the lodging is a gated house of one story, the grandest in the street and set apart from the inns by its gate and entrance, and at most the size of Kusatsu's site of about 50,800 square feet, which was among the largest (no page read gives a waki-honjin's measured size); a waystation that hosts such guests may keep a lodging court of its own, as Jimingyi did.
