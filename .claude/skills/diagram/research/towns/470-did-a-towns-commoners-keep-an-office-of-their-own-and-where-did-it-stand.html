Did a town's commoners keep an office of their own, and where did it stand?

Sources: kotobank-machikaisho (which towns kept a ward office, and what it did), kinsei-kaisho-jawiki (the ward office's work, and the headman's house doubling as it), machidoshiyori-jawiki (where Edo's town elders worked), huiguan-zhwiki (the Chinese guild and native-place hall)

In the self-governing and great commercial cities, a ward office. In Sakai and the other towns that had governed themselves at the end of the middle ages, in Kyoto, and in Osaka, the great commercial city of the early modern period, each ward of the city had its own machi-kaisho, with a general office for the whole above them. The ward's officials were posted there; they gathered the residents to pass on the magistrate's notices and drew up the papers the residents submitted. Because its work reached into civil justice and policing, an office sometimes had a white-sand court or a small jail, and some lodged the domain's guests and the shogunate's officials.

In a castle town, seldom one in each ward. Among castle towns a ward office in each ward was the exception, and in some there was none at all, the town's officials working at the town magistrate's office instead; a post town with one was unusual. In Edo the ward headman's residence served as the ward office, and the city's three town elders worked from their own houses, on corner lots facing Honcho street, the residence doubling as the office as a samurai's did.

In China, an association's hall. The huiguan, also called gongsuo, was a Ming and Qing institution: the earliest known was built in Beijing in the early fifteenth century, first as a place of fellowship and lodging for people from one home region, and some later became associations of the merchants of one trade. It was an association of its members, not a town's government.

No page read gives a ward office's size, or where on the street it stood, or places a huiguan in a county town.

The decision: a county town is its magistrate's seat, and its nearest counterpart is the castle town, where in some the commoners' officials worked at the magistrate's office, and in Edo from their own houses. So a town map draws no separate commoners' hall: where a headman keeps the town's papers, it is their own house on the main street, drawn as a house. A ward office of its own belongs to a self-governing or great commercial city, and the guild hall to its members, whom no page read places in a county town.
