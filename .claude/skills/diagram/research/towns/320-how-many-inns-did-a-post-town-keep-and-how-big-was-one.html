How many inns did a post town keep, and how big was one?

Sources: tokaido53-jawiki (the inn counts of the Tokaido's stations, the largest and the smallest), hatago-kotobank (the average count on two highways, and the inn's form set against the lord's lodging), hatago-hiratsuka-museum (the three size classes by frontage, the average per class, and one inn measured), kusatsu-juku-jawiki (one station's houses, inns and population counted together)

By the counts below, a highway post station was, before anything else, a street of inns. The Tokaido's fifty-three stations are said to have held nearly three thousand hatago between them, and the count differed sharply from one station to the next. Miya, the ferry port, had 247 and Kuwana on the far shore 120, while Fujisawa had 45. On average a station held 55 on the Tokaido and 27 on the Nakasendo. How thick that is on the ground shows at Kusatsu, where in 1843 586 houses and 2,351 inhabitants kept 72 inns - about one house in eight, though the page elsewhere puts it at one in five or six, with as many as 132 inns at the busiest.

A hatago was built as the lord's lodging was, with the raised audience room, the gate and the formal entrance left out and the whole somewhat reduced, and it came in three sizes. The size is said to have been reckoned by the frontage: at Mishima an inn of 5 ken or more on the street (about 30 ft) counted as large, one of about 4 ken (about 24 ft) as middling and one of 3 ken or less (about 18 ft) as small. The small ones were the commonest: an average Tokaido station's 55 inns were just under 9 large, just under 18 middling and just over 28 small. One Hiratsuka inn was a two-story building 5.5 ken (about 33 ft) wide on the street, with a building area of 57.25 tsubo, about 2,040 square feet; if that area is its ground floor, it ran about 62 ft back from the street. None of the pages read gives a hatago a cart yard or a stable of its own.

Two things the record could not find. No readable source counts the inns of an ordinary county seat - a market town on a road that was not a relay station - so the one caravan inn a town map draws stays this project's calibration, labeled as such in Where does a caravan inn stand, and what does it need beside it? Nor has the record a readable account of a Chinese inn's size, rooms or stories beyond the wagon inn that question already describes.

What it means for a map: in this setting the relay horses belong to the Imperial waystation (What was the Imperial waystation on the ground, and where does it stand?), so a town is not a post station and the post-town counts are a ceiling, not a target. What they do settle is the inn's scale: a Japanese-form inn is a street-front house 18 to 33 ft wide, most of them at the small end, not a compound.
