Where does a laborer live, and how big is a laborer's house?

Sources: kotobank-uradana (the back tenement: its lane, unit, shared well and privy, and who lived there), machiya-shoka-jawiki (one row or two behind a shop by its frontage), homes-nagaya (the one-story tenement; the street-front row for small merchants), kotobank-tanagari (how many townsmen rented, in the great cities and by region), kotobank-shukubamachi (the post town's even plots for the households that found its porters and horses)

In a city, a back tenement. The laborer of a Japanese city rented. Behind the shops on the street, a lot's back ground held several row buildings on a lane about one ken (6 ft) wide, with a well, a privy and a rubbish heap shared by the residents always gathered in one corner; the standard unit was nine shaku of frontage by two ken deep (about 9 by 12 ft), an earth-floored kitchen at the lane door and one room of four and a half or six mats; and the residents were peddlers, craftsmen such as carpenters and plasterers, and the simple laborers called day laborers. Units ran from nine shaku to two ken wide and two to two and a half ken deep (about 9 to 12 ft by 12 to 15 ft). The common tenement was one story; behind a shop with about three ken of frontage the units lined one side of the lane, behind one with about five, both. The better-off small trader rented a unit of a row on the street itself, with two rooms and an earth floor. In Edo and Osaka about seven residents in ten are estimated to have been tenants. How the city's back lanes are laid out is under which way a city house's door faces.

In a town, fewer tenants, and many on their own plot. Among the urban residents of early-modern Japan the share who rented their house was 20 to 30% in the north-east (Ou) and on the Japan Sea coast (Hokuriku), and 50 to 60% around the Inland Sea (Setouchi). In a post town the households that made up the station were mostly allotted plots of about even size and, in return for finding horses or porters, were often exempted from ground rent - so a post town's porters could come from households on plots of their own along the road, not from a tenement behind it.

In China - not found. Where a Chinese county town's laborers lived, and in what, is on no page read. Nor does any page read give the ground a whole tenement block took per household, lane and well and privy included.

What it means for the drawn laborer house. The laborer glyph is drawn 34 ft by 24 ft, about 816 sq ft. One back-tenement unit is about 108 sq ft; so the glyph is the size of a row building of some seven or eight units, not of one family's home.

The rule the map follows: in a city a laborer lives in a one-story back tenement on a lane behind the street-front shops, about 9 by 12 ft a household, the row one rank or two by the width of the shop in front. In a town where laborers are few - the 20 to 30% end - most laborers have a small house on a plot of their own, and a back tenement appears only behind the larger shops; where they are many - the 50 to 60% end - the back tenement is the laborer's usual home, as in the city. Which end a town takes is a knob rolled per town. The tenant shares are of all urban residents, not of laborers alone, so giving the laborers of a low-share town their own plots is a calibration against those shares, not a finding; a town on the Chinese model is a guess until a source is found.
