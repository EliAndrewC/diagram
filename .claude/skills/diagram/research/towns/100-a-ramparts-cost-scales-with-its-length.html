A rampart's cost scales with its LENGTH

Sources: chinese-city-wall-enwiki, thepaper-city-walls (irregular plans set by topography and by taking high points in). The gully-and-quarter imagery is illustration; the cost argument is reasoning

The why is pure economics: a rampart is the single most expensive thing a town builds (rammed earth or stone, maintained for centuries),1615 and its cost scales with its length (this page's reasoning; no page read prices a wall), so a town's line is drawn to skirt what it must defend - though the important cities the record describes deliberately walled in far more ground than they had built, for growth and for timber and farmland in war, so no empty ground is a town-tier reading rather than the rule.35 Historically this is why town walls are irregular1817: they kink in to exclude a gully and bulge out around a quarter, following the settlement's actual footprint (and the terrain).

The rule the map follows: a walled town's rampart is drawn as an irregular thick line with a gate in it, and the irregularity is required: at least five sections, with the section lengths genuinely spread rather than merely unequal - a coefficient of variation of a quarter or more . That is a spread test on purpose, so a wall may hug a feature with several short segments while a lazily near-regular polygon still fails. Nothing is built on the rampart: it carries a no-build corridor about 46 ft wide , so even a large building's corner stays off it. A field may abut the wall but never crosses it. And the wall need not close a full ring - it may be an open arc whose two ends run partway up the flanks of a steep hill, the slope closing each side in place of a blunt wall end, with the magistrate's compound on the summit.

The rule the map follows: the wall hugs the built town. No continuous stretch of rampart longer than about 280 ft may run more than about 140 ft from the nearest building, feature or piece of terrain ; the two thresholds are calibrated against the drawn walled town rather than derived from a source. Two kinds of slack are legitimate and expected: terrain, since a wall climbs or skirts a hill rather than leveling it and the hill is itself what the rampart is standing on, and a modest gate forecourt, the open assembly square just inside the gate. A long empty margin running the whole length of one face is neither, and the fix is almost always to pull that face inward to ride just outside the last buildings, redrawing the cross-street and the quarter behind it to match, rather than to scatter buildings into the margin. This composes with the street rule above: a street earns its length from the buildings along it, and the wall earns its length from the whole built area - both reject infrastructure drawn around emptiness.
