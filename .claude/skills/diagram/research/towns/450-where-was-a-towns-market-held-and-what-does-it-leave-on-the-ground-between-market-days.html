Where was a town's market held, and what does it leave on the ground between market days?

Sources: okamura-1999-ichimachi (the market ground along a market town's street, and its stalls), ichigami-jawiki (the market on the highway, and the stone that marks it), kotobank-ichigami (where the stone stands), kotobank-sansaiichi (markets before temple and shrine gates), miaohui-zhwiki (the Chinese temple fair), xiao-2009-tang-fangshi (the Tang market, only at a county or prefecture seat), tang-changan-zhwiki (the size of the Tang capital's two markets), thepaper-changan-markets (what a walled market looked like), lifang-zhwiki (the end of the walled market under the Song)

This is the periodic market a town's villages come in for. The standing shops outside a walled town's gate, which serve the road's traffic every day, are a different thing, under the gate market; how often the market met is under how often a county town held its market.

Along the main street. In the market towns of Edo-period Kanto the market ground was a strip one to two ken deep, set between the town street and the house lots and kept for putting up stalls on market day. Stalls stood in two places: inside the house lots, taking in that strip, where the lot's owner let them, and out in the street itself, which served as the selling floor, under the market's head. Most market sites were on old highways, and the market god, the ichigami, was worshipped at the roadside, sometimes at the boundary of the market or of its ward, or between the market settlement and its neighbors; its body was most often a round natural stone. It often stands at a place that marks an edge - a village boundary, a landing, a bridge foot, a crossroads - and a market is inferred from where one stands.

Before a temple or shrine gate. The medieval three-day market was held mainly before the gates of temples and shrines, at hubs of land and water transport, and near an estate's office or its steward's residence. In China the temple fair, the miaohui, gathered near a temple for offerings, contests and performances, first on its festivals and later on fixed dates, and gradually took in market trade.

In a walled market. Under the Tang no market might be set up anywhere but a prefecture or county seat; it opened at midday to three hundred drumbeats and dispersed before sunset to three hundred strokes of the gong, and even a county town's market was divided into rows by goods. The capital's two markets each took the ground of two wards, about 1,025 m by 927 m; the Han-dynasty capital's markets were squares walled and gated on four sides, with a tower at the center from which the market's officials looked down over it, and storehouses behind the shops against the market wall. Whether a county town's market had a wall of its own, and how big it was, no page read says. The Song dynasty abolished the ward-and-market system and the night curfew, and commercial streets took its place.

What an ordinary day shows. No page read describes what a stall was built of, or whether anything of it was left standing between market days. So the map draws no stalls and shows only the ground (a guess): the clear strip before the house fronts where the market meets on the street, the market-god stone at its edge, and the ground before a gate, kept open, where it meets at a temple or shrine.

The decision: two places are attested for a town's market, so where it meets is a knob, rolled per town from the map's seed - along the main street, or before the gate of the town's temple or shrine. The walled market is a capital's, and under the Song dynasty it ended, so a town does not draw one.

The rule the map follows: a town rolls where its market meets. On the street, the house fronts along the stretch where it meets stand back from the street by a clear strip one to two ken deep, about 6 to 12 ft, and a market-god stone stands at one end of the stretch, at a crossroads or at a bridge foot - drawn as a small round mark, its size a guess, since no page read measures one. Before a gate, the ground in front of the temple's or shrine's gate is kept open. Nothing of the stalls is drawn.
