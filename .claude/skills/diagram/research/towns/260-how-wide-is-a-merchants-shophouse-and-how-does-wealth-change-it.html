How wide is a merchant's shophouse, and how does wealth change it?

Sources: machiya-shoka-jawiki (Kyoto's three frontage bands, the Edo lot and its front shop, the great stores), kotobank-uradana (the Edo lot's frontage and depth), kotobank-shukubamachi (a post town's strip lots, one depth and many frontages), qixian-cnr (a Chinese county seat's shops: five bays, deep, courtyard behind)

In a Japanese city, frontage was the measure of wealth. Kyoto's townsmen fell into three bands by the width of their house on the street: those with large houses of ten ken or more of frontage (about 65 ft), the ordinary shopkeeper with four or five (about 26 to 33 ft), and the small trader with one and a half to two (about 10 to 13 ft) or renting. Edo laid its townsmen's ground out in lots of five or six ken of frontage (about 33 to 39 ft) running twenty ken (about 130 ft) back from the street, and the shop that fronted the lot was kept to about five ken deep (about 33 ft), with the rented tenements behind it. Edo's great stores bought up the lots beside them: some had 36 ken of frontage (more than 200 ft), and some had 15 with ground that crossed the town boundary at the back.

In a Japanese town, the lots were even and the frontages were not. A post town's lots on both sides of the road were planned strips of nearly the same depth, differing in their frontage. No page read gives a small town's frontages in feet, nor how many of its merchants stood in each band.

A Chinese county seat's shop was wider and set on a courtyard. In the old county town of Qixian, a home of the Shanxi merchant houses, the shops on the old streets mostly followed the northern plan of one courtyard or several in a row behind the street; the frontage was generally five bays, each 2.5 to 3 m, which is about 41 to 49 ft in all, and the shops ran deep - one pharmacy more than 9 m (about 30 ft) deep. That is one town of wealthy trading houses; no page read gives the spread of shop sizes across Chinese county towns.

What it means for the drawn shop. The shop glyph is drawn 48 ft by 32 ft. Its depth is an Edo front shop's, about five ken. Its width is a Chinese five-bay shop's at the top of its range, and one and a half to nearly twice an ordinary Japanese shopkeeper's four or five ken; only Kyoto's large houses and Edo's great stores are wider. So the drawn shop sits in the rich band on the Japanese model and in the ordinary band on the Chinese one.

The rule the map follows: a merchant's frontage is set by wealth, in three bands, and which model a town follows is a knob rolled per town. On the Japanese model: a small shop about 10 to 13 ft wide, an ordinary one about 26 to 33 ft, a rich house 65 ft or more, each about 33 ft deep at the street, with its lot running on behind it. On the Chinese model: the ordinary shop about 41 to 49 ft wide with a courtyard behind. How many shops fall in each band is on no page read, so the split among the bands is a guess until one is found.
