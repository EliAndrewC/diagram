Theater stages on shrine ground (miyaji shibai)

In Edo-period Japan, the permanent roofed theater with seating belonged to the great cities, Edo, Kyoto and Osaka, and in Edo only a few licensed troupes were allowed one. Beside them, plays were put on in booths on the grounds of shrines and temples, under a temporary license, with nothing roofed but the stage. Such a play was a 宮地芝居 (miyaji shibai, "shrine-ground play").   • In Edo, only four licensed troupes were allowed to raise a yagura.
    By about the beginning of the Enpo era, the 1670s, the shogunate permitted only the Nakamura-za, the Ichimura-za, the Morita-za and the Yamamura-za to raise one.
  • Roofed theaters with seating arose late in the Edo period, centered on Edo, Kyoto and Osaka.
    The Japanese Wikipedia dates the kabuki theater with a roof and seating to the late Edo period, in those three cities.
  • A shrine-ground play was a booth play in a shrine's or temple's precinct, under a temporary license.
    A Japanese dictionary defines it so for the Edo period.
  • Only the stage of a shrine-ground play could be roofed.
    A playhouse without a yagura was held to a simple booth, with no roof over anything but the stage, and no draw curtain, revolving stage or hanamichi walkway.
  • Where did the audience stand?
    No page we read says. This project's reading, a GUESS, is that it stood on the open ground between the stage and the shrine's or temple's hall, inside the precinct the play was put on in.
  • Why were plays put on in a precinct?
    The pages we read give the license, not the reason. This project's reading, a GUESS, is that a precinct was where the festival crowd already gathered.
