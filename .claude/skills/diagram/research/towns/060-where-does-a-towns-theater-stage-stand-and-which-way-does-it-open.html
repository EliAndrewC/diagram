Where does a town's theater stage stand, and which way does it open?

Sources: kotobank-miyaji-shibai; the tier-scaling finding on how a capital's institutions scale down, where a permanent theater house is capital grammar and a provincial town's playing place stands instead on shrine ground; the distances are calibrated against the drawn towns, edo-sanza-jawiki, shibaigoya-jawiki

The town's playing place is not a building of its own trade: a permanent roofed theater house belongs to the capital, and below that tier the stage belongs to a religious precinct, where the festival crowd already gathers.42 So what a town draws is a roofed stage facing an open viewing ground within its temple or monastery precinct - the East Asian temple opera stage or shrine kagura stage, never a Greco-Roman seating bowl - with the audience standing on the ground between the stage and the hall.41 That is also why the stage's orientation is not free: a stage whose open side is turned away from the hall puts its audience outside the precinct, with its back to the thing that owns it.

The rule the map follows: every town draws one theater stage unless it declares otherwise, adjacent to a religious hall - within about 260 ft of the nearest one - with its open viewing side turned toward that hall. A walled town keeps the stage inside the walls unless it declares otherwise.
