Where does a magistrate's manor stand, and which way does its gate face?

Sources: neixiang-xianya-zhwiki (the surviving Qing county yamen, and the regulation that puts its gate to the south); the siting is calibrated against the drawn maps

A magistrate's manor stands at the edge of the settlement it administers rather than in the middle of it, and its gate faces what it fronts: the town itself, or the road it sits beside. There is no fixed compass default, because the answer depends on where the town is - one drawn manor faces west to the hamlet below it, another south down its hill to the town, and the third is tilted so that its front wall runs parallel to the diagonal road it fronts, its gate opening onto the roadbed. The fallback when nothing is said is south - the formal orientation, laid down for a Chinese local government office by regulation and by ritual propriety: the seat sits to the north and faces south, civil on the left and military on the right, the court in front and the sleeping quarters behind24 - never a blind west.

Tilting the compound is cheap to draw and expensive downstream. A tilt changes the manor's footprint and therefore the ground it blocks, which reshuffles the seeded blocks of housing around it and can knock a caste count off its band. The fix used is to re-roll the map's seed until the counts land back in band; each of the dense towns and the drawn city carries a hand-picked seed for exactly this reason. Hand-correcting the individual block counts instead is whack-a-mole: each correction perturbs the next block, and the map stops being the output of its own seed.

The rule the map follows: the manor stands at the edge of the town, its gate set deliberately to face either the dwellings it administers or the road it fronts, and the whole compound is turned where it fronts a road at an angle so that its front wall lies parallel to the roadbed.
