Where does a magistrate's manor stand, and which way does its gate face?

Sources: jinya-jawiki (a Japanese lord's seat had no one siting, and was often built beside a settlement already there); tamba-kashiwara-jinya (a domain seat at the highest point of its town, backed by its hill); sina-neixiang-xianya and bjd-qing-yamen (the Chinese county yamen inside its town, on the main street); neixiang-xianya-zhwiki (the regulation that puts a yamen's gate to the south); the gate's facing is calibrated against the drawn maps

Where the manor stands depends on which model the town follows. On the Japanese model it stands at the edge of the settlement it administers rather than in the middle of it. A Japanese lord's or intendant's seat had no one siting: some grew a small town of their own around them, but the seats of outlying holdings were often built beside a village or town that was already there, and one surviving domain seat stands at the highest point of its town, on a gentle slope backed by the hill behind it. On the Chinese model the county yamen stands inside the walled town and in its middle, on the main street: Neixiang's stands on East Main Street at the center of its county town, and a department's yamen stands inside its city on the north side of its own East Main Street. The edge is therefore the Japanese form; a town on the Chinese model puts its magistrate's compound on the main avenue, as under how a Japanese county town differs from a Chinese one.

The gate faces what the manor fronts: the town itself, or the road it sits beside. No one compass direction serves every manor, because the answer depends on where the town is - one drawn manor faces west to the hamlet below it, another south down its hill to the town, and the third is tilted so that its front wall runs parallel to the diagonal road it fronts, its gate opening onto the roadbed. The fallback when nothing is said is south - the formal orientation, laid down for a Chinese local government office by regulation and by ritual propriety: the seat sits to the north and faces south, civil on the left and military on the right, the court in front and the sleeping quarters behind.

Tilting the compound is cheap to draw and expensive downstream. A tilt changes the manor's footprint and therefore the ground it blocks, which reshuffles the seeded blocks of housing around it and can push a caste's count outside the range set for it. The fix used is to re-roll the map's seed until the counts land back in range; each of the dense towns and the drawn city carries a hand-picked seed for exactly this reason. Hand-correcting the individual block counts instead is whack-a-mole: each correction perturbs the next block, and the map stops being the output of its own seed.

The rule the map follows: on the Japanese model the manor stands at the edge of the town, its gate set deliberately to face either the dwellings it administers or the road it fronts, and the whole compound is turned where it fronts a road at an angle so that its front wall lies parallel to the roadbed; on the Chinese model it stands inside the wall on the main avenue. Which way the gate faces is a calibration against the drawn maps, not a finding.
