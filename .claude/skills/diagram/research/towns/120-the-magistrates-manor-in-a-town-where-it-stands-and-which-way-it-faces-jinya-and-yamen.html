The magistrate's manor in a town: where it stands and which way it faces (jin'ya and yamen)

A town's magistrate worked and lived in one enclosed compound. In Japan the shogunate's intendant governed from a jin'ya, which was at once their residence, their office, their court and the store for the year's tax. The lord of a small domain governed from a jin'ya too, a seat ranking next to a castle. In China a county magistrate governed from the county yamen. What stood inside the walls, court by court, is at Magistrates' compounds (jin'ya and yamen).

The two stood differently in their towns. A Japanese seat had no one siting, and was often built beside a settlement that was already there, while the Chinese county yamen we found stood inside its town, in the middle, on the main street. Under the Qing, a local government office faced south, by regulation.

  • An intendant's jin'ya kept the year's tax inside its own grounds.
    The intendant's jin'ya was where the annual tax was stored, and the intendancy at Takayama kept its numbered earth-walled storehouses inside its grounds; eight of them survive, numbered one to four and nine to twelve.
  • Jin'ya differed in what they held and in how strongly they were enclosed.
    A small domain's jin'ya could ring itself with earthworks or moats but no tall stone walls, and hold a residence, offices, retainers' houses, earth-walled storehouses and a drill ground. Jin'ya varied in how far retainers' quarters and townhouses gathered around them, in what they held inside, and in how defensible their perimeter was.
  • Did a jin'ya stand at the edge of its town?
    Often, it seems. Some seats grew a small town of their own around them, but the seats of outlying holdings and the like were often built beside a village or town already there. One surviving domain seat, at Kashiwara in Tamba, stands at the highest point of its old town, on a gentle slope backed by the hill behind it, though barely above the rest of the town. That a seat beside its settlement stood at the town's edge rather than in its middle is this project's reading of these two.
  • No page we read says which way a jin'ya's gate faced.
    The pages on the jin'ya and on the Kashiwara seat say where a seat stood and what it held, not which way its gate opened.

In the Chinese towns we found, the magistrate sat in the middle instead, and regulation fixed which way the office faced.

  • Did a Chinese county yamen stand in the middle of its town, on the main street?
    Both the yamen we found stood on a main street inside their towns. Neixiang's yamen stands on East Main Street at the center of its county town. The yamen of Huozhou department stands inside its city on the north side of its East Main Street.
  • Under the Qing dynasty, regulation set a local government office facing south.
    The yamen at Neixiang, a Qing county yamen, follows the Qing regulations for local offices: it sits to the north facing south, civil on the left and military on the right, the court in front and the sleeping quarters behind, the jail to the south. The facing is the formal orientation, set by regulation and the ritual code rather than by luck, and it describes Chinese offices, not Japanese ones.
