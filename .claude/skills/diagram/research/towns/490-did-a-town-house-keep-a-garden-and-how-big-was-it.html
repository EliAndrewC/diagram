Did a town house keep a garden, and how big was it?

Sources: niwa-jawiki (the small garden's size, and why it was made), kyomachiya-jawiki (the Kyoto town house's back garden and its courts), townhouse-jawiki (where the small garden falls in the house's plan), siheyuan-zhwiki (the Chinese courtyard's planting)

A merchant's house: a small court, and often a garden behind. The tsubo-niwa is a small garden of about one to two tsubo, seen mainly in Kyoto's town houses: houses built close together had trouble with light and air, so a back garden was made as if a piece of the house had been cut out, against the neighbor; it arose with the town house early in the early-modern period. One to two tsubo is about 6 ft square to 6 by 12 ft. In the plan it comes near the back, after the washroom, privy and bath and before the storehouse. Many Kyoto town houses also had a back garden, the zashiki garden, its planting called senzai; in the grander form with a separate shop block at the street, an open entrance court and a tsubo-niwa lay on either side of the block joining the shop to the house, and a fireproof storehouse stood at the back.

A farming household: a vegetable garden. Where a town's households also farmed, the back of the lot was often a vegetable garden, under which way a town house's door faces. A tenement unit was a single room with its earth floor, and by this record's reading of that plan it had no garden; the plan is under which way a city house's door faces.

A Chinese courtyard house: the courtyard planted. Flowering and fruit trees were planted in a courtyard house's courtyard for enjoyment - commonly jujube and persimmon, with peony, magnolia, lilac, crabapple, wisteria and pomegranate among the flowering kinds.

The pages read speak of Kyoto; none says how common the tsubo-niwa was in a provincial market town. The decision: the garden follows the house, not a roll per town. A merchant's house keeps a small open court inside its footprint toward the back, and its plot behind is a planted garden rather than vegetables; a farming household's plot is its vegetable garden; a tenement has none.

The rule the map follows: a merchant's town house shows a small open court inside its footprint near the back, about 6 by 6 to 6 by 12 ft, and the plot behind it is drawn as a planted garden - shrubs and a tree or two - rather than as vegetable beds - both carried over from Kyoto's town houses, and a guess for a provincial town; a farming household's plot stays a vegetable garden, and a tenement row draws none.
