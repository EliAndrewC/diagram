Does a town's hayfield hold hay bales and a fence, or haystacks?

Sources: baler-enwiki (when the machine that makes a bale came about)

A bale is a machine's. A rectangular block of hay, pressed and tied, is what a baling machine makes, and the machine is late: mechanical cutting devices were developed in the 1860s, and from them came mowers and balers; a reaper whose knotter bound hay into bundles was invented in 1872 and sold from 1874. The article rests those dates on one twine maker's page and gives no date for the first hay press, so they bound the machine rather than fix it; either way it is a nineteenth-century Western machine. That is after the period these maps draw, in Japan as in China. No readable page shows hay pressed into blocks before the machine, in Japan or in China.

No fence either. Why a town's fodder meadow is open grass, cut and carried to the stable, with no fence, pen or grazing animals, is What hayfield and grazing ground surrounds a town for its horses? A common where fodder was cut and carried to the stable.

What stood there instead. Dried fodder kept in bulk was kept in stacks in the open, as What are the barns in a town's hayfield, and how big are they? sets out from a sixth-century Chinese farm book and a Ming novel's army fodder depot. The Japanese record read, on the other hand, names dried grass as a fodder but shows it cut on shared ground and carried to the stable, and no page read shows it stacked in the field; the same question and the one on the town's fodder common set that out. Both forms are attested, so which a town shows is a knob rolled per town.

What it means for a map: the maps do not draw hay bales or a fence line around the hayfield; the rectangular bale is a modern form and the fence rests on nothing. A town's hayfield is open cut grass, and on a town that rolls the stacked form it carries a few round haystacks in the open. How many stacks, and how big they are drawn, is a guess. The caption says hayfield or fodder meadow, not grazing.

The rule the map follows: a town's hayfield has no fenced or dashed outline and no rectangular bales; its edge follows the lie of the land. Per town, rolled from the seed, it either carries no stored hay or carries a few round haystacks standing in the open grass, a GUESS at their number and size, drawn to read as stacks rather than as buildings.
