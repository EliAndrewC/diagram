How big is a town's paddy plot?

Sources: kato-1999-ittanbu-kukaku (most paddies of the domain era irregular plots of every size, and the one-tan plot of an 1869 replanning); jsidre-kochi-seiri (the narrow, irregular plots that land consolidation replaced, and western Japan's older regular ones); the plot-size finding on the fields page and its in-field features finding

A town's paddies are drawn on a fine comb, and the grain is set by what the town's own canals can feed rather than by the look of the quilt. The comb is set to plots about 58 ft across on rows 52 to 72 ft apart , which is a bunded paddy of roughly 0.08 acre. A coarser grain was tried and declined: at about 66 ft across, the comb's own rule that an offtake must stand at least two plot-widths along its canal put the minimum offtake spacing above the length of the short canals a town has room for, and the delivery network starved.

What a pre-modern paddy measured on average is on no page read. What is attested is its shape and spread: in the domain era, when most paddies were opened, plots as orderly as those of the ancient jori grid were few, and irregular plots of every size made up the great majority; it was those narrow, irregular plots, come down from the early-modern period, that land consolidation later reorganized into standard ones - though western Japan already held regular plots from the ancient grid. An early standard plot, laid out for horse plowing in an 1869 replanning, was one tan, 10 by 30 ken, about a quarter acre. A town plot of 0.08 acre is a third of that, at the small end of what these maps otherwise draw and inside the 0.02-0.25 acre band the fields page takes as its own figure; that it is small and irregular is accurate, and its size is a calibration.

The small in-field flourishes that a hamlet's paddies carry - a pocket pond, a rock, a grave island - are switched off at town scale and above. That is the GM's reading of the sheet: at a foot to the pixel the glyphs stop reading as flourishes and start reading as a pond and an ossuary standing in the middle of the rice. It is a map drawing convention, not a claim that town paddies had none.

The rule the map follows: a town's paddy comb is drawn at about 58 ft across the plot with rows 52 to 72 ft apart, about 0.08 acre to the plot, and the in-field flourishes are off at this tier and above. The plot's size is a calibration against what the town's canals can feed.
