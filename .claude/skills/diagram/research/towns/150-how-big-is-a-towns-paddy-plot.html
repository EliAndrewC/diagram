How big is a town's paddy plot?

Sources: kato-1999-ittanbu-kukaku (most paddies of the domain era irregular plots of every size, the one-tan plot of an 1869 replanning, and its forerunners of 1849 and the jori grid); jsidre-kochi-seiri (the narrow, irregular plots that land consolidation replaced, and western Japan's older regular ones); ndl-crd-hitsu (a survey register's entry was one lot, whatever its size); iwamuro-kenchicho (the paddy lots of a 1602 village survey register); shokukaku-suiden-jawiki (the tiny excavated paddies of the Yayoi and Kofun periods); the plot-size finding on the fields page and its in-field features finding

A town's paddies are drawn on a fine comb, and the grain is set by what the town's own canals can feed rather than by the look of the quilt. The comb is set to plots about 58 ft across on rows 52 to 72 ft apart , which is a bunded paddy of roughly 0.08 acre. A coarser grain was tried and declined: at about 66 ft across, the comb's own rule that an offtake must stand at least two plot-widths along its canal put the minimum offtake spacing above the length of the short canals a town has room for, and the delivery network starved.

What a pre-modern paddy measured is attested only in pieces. Its shape and spread are clear: in the domain era, when most paddies were opened, plots as orderly as those of the ancient jori grid were few, and irregular plots of every size made up the great majority; it was those narrow, irregular plots, come down from the early modern period, that land consolidation later reorganized into standard ones - though in western Japan regular plots, the ancient grid's among them, were already widespread.

Its size survives in the land-survey registers, which set down each lot of paddy, dry field or house land one place at a time, whatever its size. On the page transcribed from a 1602 register of Iwamuro village in Omi, the paddy lots in cultivation run from 3 se 22 bu to 1 tan 8 se 1 bu, about 370 to 1,800 square meters (0.09 to 0.44 acre), and five of the eight are over a tan. A register lot is a parcel, though, and whether it was one diked paddy or several, no page read says. A single diked paddy is measured only much earlier: the excavated paddies of the Yayoi and Kofun periods are plots of under a hundred square meters, the smallest about five. No page read gives an average for either. The quarter-acre tan plot, 10 by 30 ken, laid out for horse plowing, first appears as a replanning plot in the Meiji Restoration years, in a replanning of 1869; but it was not new. Twenty years earlier, in 1849, a replanning of about 8 cho in the same district laid out plots of 8 by 39 ken, 1 tan 12 bu, about the same area and also meant for horse plowing, and the ancient jori grid's half-fold plot was a tan of 12 by 30 ken. A planned plot of about a quarter acre is therefore premodern too, but as the plot of a planned grid or of one late-Edo replanning, not of the irregular fields most paddies were.

A town plot of 0.08 acre, about 330 square meters, sits just under the smallest paddy lot in cultivation on the 1602 page and far above an ancient diked plot; read as one diked paddy inside a lot, it is inside what these sources span, and it lies inside the 0.02-0.25 acre band the fields page takes as its own figure. That it is small and irregular is accurate; its exact size is a calibration against the canals, not a measured pre-modern average.

The small in-field flourishes that a hamlet's paddies carry - a pocket pond, a rock, a grave island - are switched off at town scale and above. That is the GM's reading of the sheet: at a foot to the pixel the glyphs stop reading as flourishes and start reading as a pond and an ossuary standing in the middle of the rice. It is a map drawing convention, not a claim that town paddies had none.

The rule the map follows: a town's paddy comb is drawn at about 58 ft across the plot with rows 52 to 72 ft apart, about 0.08 acre to the plot, and the in-field flourishes are off at this tier and above. The plot's size is a calibration against what the town's canals can feed, set just under the smallest paddy lot of the 1602 survey register; the planned quarter-acre plot (the jori half-fold, the 1849 replanning, the 1869 and later standard) is not its anchor.
