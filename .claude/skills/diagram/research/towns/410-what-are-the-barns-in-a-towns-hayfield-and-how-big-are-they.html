What are the barns in a town's hayfield, and how big are they?

Sources: umaya-kotobank (the stable standing apart, what was built on with it and above it, and fodder thrown into the stall), townnews-zama-magusaba (the ground where fodder was cut), xuanquanzhi-neac (one Chinese post station's stable area, measured, and its horses)

No hay barn on any page read. The record found where a horse's fodder came from and where the horse lived, but not a building in between. The fodder was cut on shared ground: the villages' common land where roofing material and fodder for the animals were cut. It went into the stable. In some regions the stable floor was dug more than 60 cm deep and wild grass thrown in load after load, part of it eaten and the rest trodden with the dung into manure. A farmhouse stable could stand apart from the house, and then it usually had a privy and a shed built on with it; a stable usually had a ceiling, with a servants' room or a storeroom above it. Neither article says what the shed or the storeroom held. No readable page, Japanese or Chinese, describes a separate building for storing hay or fodder, and so the barns in a town's hayfield are a guess.

The nearest measured thing. A Chinese post station of the Han, Xuanquanzhi near Dunhuang, was a walled compound 50 m square with a stable area 30 m square (about 100 ft) projecting at one corner, and the stations of its commandery kept about forty horses each. That is a stable yard for a large relay, two thousand years before the setting's period and on a desert frontier, not a hay barn. The stables themselves are in How big is a stable, and what is its yard like?, and whose horses a road town kept in What did a post station keep for its relay horses and porters, and does a city keep the same?.

The setting. The setting's own notes give one clan's lands farmland set aside by law for hay and stockpiles of it held against war, so hay stored in bulk is the setting's, not something the maps invent; how it was housed is not in the notes.

What it means for a map: the barns a road town draws in its hayfield are a guess, and so is their size - the 84 by 56 ft footprint is a calibration, not a measurement. The forms the record does attest are the stable standing apart with a shed built on, and the fodder cut on open ground and carried to the stable; a town that wants its hayfield's buildings to rest on the record draws them as outer stables with their sheds, near the town, rather than as barns out in the grass.
