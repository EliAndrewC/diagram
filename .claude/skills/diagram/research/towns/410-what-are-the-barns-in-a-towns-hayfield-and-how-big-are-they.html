What are the barns in a town's hayfield, and how big are they?

Sources: magusa-jawiki (dried grass named and collected as fodder in ancient Japan), qimin-yaoshu-yangyu (hay stacked inside round fences), qimin-yaoshu-enwiki (the treatise's date), shuihu-caoliaochang (a fodder depot of stacks and thatched storehouses), water-margin-enwiki (the novel's date), umaya-kotobank (the stable standing apart, what was built on with it and above it, and fodder thrown into the stall), townnews-zama-magusaba (the ground where fodder was cut), xuanquanzhi-neac (one Chinese post station's stable area, measured, and its horses)

Hay was kept, and kept in stacks. Dried grass was a fodder in Japan long before the setting's period: the Wamyo Ruijusho gives one of the two old characters for fodder the reading karakusa, "dried grass", and the Engishiki had it collected from the home provinces of the Kinai for the court's horse bureaus. The Chinese record shows how such hay was kept. The Qimin Yaoshu, finished in the sixth century, has a flock's hay cut in the eighth and ninth months and stacked on high, dry ground inside two round fences of mulberry and thorn, each about five or six paces, where a stack a zhang high does no harm and the sheep pull their feed through the fence all winter. The Water Margin, a novel of the Ming set in the Northern Song, describes an army fodder depot: an earth wall around it with a two-leaved gate, seven or eight thatched buildings inside used as storehouses, the horse fodder in stacks all round, and two thatched halls in the middle, where the keeper handing it over counted the stacks. In both, the hay itself stands in the open as stacks; the depot's buildings are storehouses beside the stacks, inside a wall, and the novel does not say what they held.

No hay barn on any page read. The Japanese record found where a horse's fodder came from and where the horse lived, but not a building in between. The fodder was cut on shared ground: the villages' common land where roofing material and fodder for the animals were cut. In some regions the stable floor was dug 60 cm or more deep and wild grass thrown in load after load, part of it eaten and the rest trodden with the dung into manure. A farmhouse stable could stand apart from the house, and then it usually had a privy and a shed built on with it; a stable usually had a ceiling, with a servants' room or a storeroom above it. Neither article says what the shed or the storeroom held. No readable page, Japanese or Chinese, premodern or later, describes a large building standing by itself in a field to store hay.

The nearest measured thing. A Chinese post station of the Han, Xuanquanzhi near Dunhuang, was a walled compound 50 m square with a stable area 30 m square (about 100 ft) projecting at one corner, and the stations of its commandery kept about forty horses each. That is a stable yard for a large relay, two thousand years before the setting's period and on a desert frontier, not a hay barn. The stables themselves, and whose horses a road town kept, are in Relay stations: the Imperial waystation, the relay office and their stables (umaya, toiyaba). What stands on the hayfield itself, and why it is no bale, is Does a town's hayfield hold hay bales and a fence, or haystacks?.

The setting. In the setting's own notes, one clan's lands have farmland set aside by law for hay, and stockpiles of it are held against war, so hay stored in bulk is the setting's, not something the maps invent; how it was housed is not in the notes.

What it means for a map: the maps do not draw barns in a town's hayfield - the large free-standing barn, and its 84 by 56 ft footprint, rest on nothing in the record. Hay kept in bulk is drawn as the record keeps it: as stacks standing in the open, and, where a store is a big one, as stacks and thatched storehouses together inside a wall. The buildings a town's horses do need are outer stables with their sheds, near the town, rather than barns out in the grass. How big a stack is drawn is a guess: the one premodern measure read, a zhang high inside a fence five or six paces around or across, is the sixth century's and is not converted here.
