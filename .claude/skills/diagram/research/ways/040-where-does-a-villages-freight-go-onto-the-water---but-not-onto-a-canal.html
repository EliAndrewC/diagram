Where does a village's freight go? Onto the water - but not onto a canal

Sources: chinaknowledge-caoyun (the tribute-grain transport and the canal that carried it); the Japanese half rests on general reading and is not cited; the GM's setting notes, which are canon for this setting rather than evidence about history, daba-jawiki, chuma-jawiki

In Ming China bulk goods - above all the tribute rice - moved by canal and river boat, and the Grand Canal was the spine of it: eight provinces were ordered in 1472 to send four million shi of rice a year up it to the terminal outside Beijing, and sea carriage was given up in its favor.11 Japan's heavy freight likewise went by water.20 Land carriage was for what a barrow or a packhorse could take.22

The setting departs from the Chinese model here, deliberately and by the GM's own canon. Artificial transport canals are not an Empire-wide feature: they belong to the Lion, who embrace the Chinese precedent, while the other clans rely on natural water or on land and simply have no need of them. Crane lands are coastal and never far from the ocean; the Dragon have the Drowned Merchant River; the Unicorn have the Firefly River valley and are in any case horse-focused; Scorpion holdings are smaller. So for a settlement in this setting the rule inverts: where there is navigable natural water, that water is the main freight route and the settlement gets a boat landing as its primary link to the world - but an artificial transport canal is never the default.

One more piece of canon shapes what a road on these maps means. The Imperial trunk network is unusually well kept: tens of thousands of miles of Imperial road with thousands of staffed waystations, maintained by corvee - the peasantry owes ten to twenty days a year, called for most often to repair the roads after the spring thaws and the autumn storms - and road tolls were outlawed by an Emperor. That is the trunk network, though, and none of it reaches down to a settlement's own lanes: those, and the local connector, stay humble tracks. An Imperial road, where a map shows one, may read as a maintained road - earth or gravel, and wider - plainly distinct from the village track.

The rule the map follows: a settlement standing on navigable natural water - a river or the coast - gets a boat landing or jetty as its main freight link. A transport canal is drawn only for a map in Lion lands or when the GM asks for one; it is never added because a settlement needs a way to move goods. A settlement with no navigable water beside it moves its freight by its land track, and that track is legitimately its main connector.
