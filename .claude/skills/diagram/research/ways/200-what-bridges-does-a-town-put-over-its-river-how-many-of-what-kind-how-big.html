What bridges does a town put over its river - how many, of what kind, how big?

Sources: dobashi-jawiki (what most Japanese river bridges were), hlj-traditional-bridges (what the castle towns built, and where stone arches were the exception), nihonbashi-bridge-jawiki (the size of the shogun's capital's chief timber bridge), zhouzhuang-zhwiki (a Chinese market town's stone arch bridges)

Of what kind. In Japan until the Edo period the overwhelming majority of river bridges were earth-decked, dobashi - logs laid side by side with earth trodden over them - and quite long bridges were built that way; bridges decked with planks were limited to an important few, and stone bridges were very few indeed. A bridge engineer's account agrees: before the early modern period almost all Japanese bridges, except very short stone ones, were timber, most of them timber girder bridges, and when the main castle towns built their bridges they were nearly all timber posts and girders; the stone arches of parts of Kyushu are the exception. China's wet south built in stone: Zhouzhuang, a canal market town near Suzhou, keeps its old stone arch bridges. How the earth-decked bridge is built, and how it differs from the footplank over a farm ditch, is this page's plank-bridge question.

How big, and how many. The upper bound is the capital's: an early seventeenth-century account gives the deck of Nihonbashi, the bridge in Edo from which the highways were measured, as 37 ken 4 shaku 5 sun (about 69 m) long and 4 ken 2 shaku 5 sun (about 8 m) wide. A town's bridge is smaller, but its own length and width, and how many bridges a town kept over its river and canals, are given on no page that was read. The maps take both from the ways the bridge carries: a bridge stands where a street or road meets the water, at the crossing itself; its deck is as wide as the way it carries; and it runs from bank to bank with a landing past the water at each end.

So a town's road bridge is one of two attested forms, rolled per settlement: an earth-decked timber bridge, the Japanese majority, or a stone arch, the Chinese water town's. Its width and length follow from its way and its water, and the count from the streets that meet the river; none of the three is a figure from a record.

The rule the map follows: a town rolls one bridge form for its road bridges: an earth-decked timber bridge, drawn as a flat deck on posts, or a stone arch. One bridge stands wherever a street or road meets the river or a canal, its deck as wide as that way and running past the water at each bank; no town bridge is drawn wider than about 26 ft or longer than about 225 ft, the capital's chief bridge. The count is a guess.
