What does a village on a highway put on the road? Teahouses at the ends of its street, and no inn

Sources: shuson-jawiki (the row village and the street village), mlit-tokaido-tateba (how many villages kept a rest stop, and where its teahouses stood), tateba-jawiki (what a rest stop was and where it was set), ainoshuku-jawiki (the in-between station and its ban on lodging), mlit-tokaido-hatago (the teahouses between post towns, and how a teahouse was built)

How the village lies along the road. A village strung along a road takes one of two forms. A row village (roson) lines both sides of the road with dwellings, with little trade, its houses a little spaced and not living from the road; a street village (gaison) lives from the road, its houses packed tighter, with shops and lodging. The two are so alike in plan that a map can hardly tell them apart.

The rest stop. Between post towns the highway had its rest stops (tateba): set partway along where the next post town was far, or at a difficult place such as a pass, with teahouses and stalls, where horses and palanquins were sometimes changed; some were set up by a domain, others grew up at the hands of the people living by the road. Not every village kept one. On the Tokaido through Kanagawa there were 56 villages between its nine post towns, and 18 rest stops - about one village in three. Between the post towns there were many teahouses.

Where the teahouses stood. A rest stop's teahouses were generally concentrated at the corners at both ends of the street's row of houses; they were also set where the view was wide at the top of a slope past a post town's end, at a fork where another road left the highway, and in the villages beside the great unbridged rivers. The same ministry page counts teahouses standing eave to eave partway up a slope within Kanagawa post town. A teahouse was built open: an earth floor, and rooms divided not by walls but only by sliding fittings, kept distinct from an inn because it was a place to rest, not to lodge.

No inn. Lodging was the post town's. A rest stop that grew into a street of shops became an in-between station (ai-no-shuku), officially a village, where lodging travelers was in principle forbidden, though rice dealers, sake dealers and other shops lined it as in a post town. The article on the rest stop says inns were set up in such a grown one, which the article on the in-between station contradicts; the map follows the ban. A post town's own inns are on the towns page, and the ribbon of shops a city lays along the Imperial road is a question on the city fabric page. The milestone mounds a traveler passed on the way are on the vegetation page.

What this means for the map. A village the Imperial road runs through lays its houses along both sides of the road, and whether it is a quiet row village or a close-packed street village with shops is a knob rolled per village. About one such village in three, calibrated on the Kanagawa count, keeps a rest stop, whose teahouses stand at one or both ends of its street; how many teahouses a rest stop kept no page read says, so their number is a GUESS. No village draws an inn.

The rule the map follows: a village on the Imperial road fronts it on both sides. The knob, rolled per village: a row village, its houses spaced along the road with yards between them; or a street village, its houses closer and a few of them shops facing the road. About one village in three also carries a rest stop: two to four teahouses (the count a GUESS), open-fronted buildings facing the road, at one or both ends of the village's row of houses. No inn is drawn in a village. A town on the road carries the same cluster of teahouses at one or both of its ends.
