What was a village lane surfaced with, and does it show? Bare trodden earth

Sources: jarimichi-jawiki (trodden earth the Japanese norm, gravel only on main roads), ishidatami-jawiki (the bare-earth road and its mud; stone paving on the highways' difficult stretches), gokaido-jawiki (gravel and sand on the highways, the Hakone round stones, the Kyoto cart-stones, the villages' share of the upkeep), mlit-kinsei-michi-gokaido (the dressing that could not be kept up, and the 1612 order), mlit-tokaido-michi (the same order in a second wording, and the field road's width)

What a village lane carried and how wide it is drawn is the question on the vehicle and the width; this one is what the traveler walked on.

The village lane. In Japan, until modern times, roads trodden hard by people and horses were the norm, and gravel roads were limited to main roads. A road in its simplest state is a strip worn bare by feet - bare earth, which rain turns to mud. So a village lane is packed earth: dust in dry weather, mud in wet. A traditional classification of roads, said to be Ieyasu's own testament, gives the field road (sakuba-michi) a width of 3 shaku, about 3 ft.

The highway. The main roads were dressed. The early Edo road works hardened the highway's surface by spreading gravel and sand, but the dressing could not be kept up everywhere, and when it rained travelers walked the mud in high clogs. An order of 1612 told those responsible to spread sand and stone where puddles and mud formed; the two government pages that summarize it word its clause on the road's sides differently - letting water run off at the side of the road, or dampening the sides and hardening them. The work fell on the villages: the road magistrate assigned the highway's upkeep to the post towns and villages along it, and where traffic was heavy to villages off the road as well, set as the towns and villages within about 20 km of the highway.

Stone. Stone paving was the exception, laid where earth failed. The shogunate ordered the old highways' difficult stretches paved with stone, and on the old Tokaido some of it survives at Hakone and Kikugawa; a record says the Hakone mountain road was repaired and paved with round stones in 1863. The one road paved for wheels was the heavy haulage road into Kyoto, where thick granite cart-stones were laid in two wheel tracks and gravel was spread on the ox path beside them - the ox-cart stone route the question on the vehicle and the width takes as a ceiling.

Steps on a slope. No readable page says a village lane climbed a slope by stone steps. Steps on a village lane are a GUESS and are not drawn.

What this means for the map. The surface does not vary among the forms the maps draw, so it is not a knob: a village lane is bare trodden earth. An Imperial road's sand-and-gravel dressing is earth-colored at a settlement map's scale, so it is drawn in the same earth tone as the lanes - a drawing convention, the real surface being sand and gravel over earth. Stone paving is drawn only on the stretch of an Imperial road that climbs a steep pass.

The rule the map follows: a village lane and a town's back lane are drawn as bare earth, with no paving, no gravel and no steps. An Imperial road is drawn in the same earth tone (a drawing convention). Where an Imperial road climbs a steep pass on the sheet, that stretch alone is drawn as stone paving; nowhere else is a way paved.
