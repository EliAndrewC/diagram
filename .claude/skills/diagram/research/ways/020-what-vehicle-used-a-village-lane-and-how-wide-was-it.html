What vehicle used a village lane, and how wide was it?

Sources: lowtech-chinese-wheelbarrow (what the Chinese rural network was built for, and who used it), daihachiguruma-jawiki (the Edo hand cart and the three places it was allowed), kotobank-daihachiguruma (how far the hand cart spread in practice), stone-routes-enwiki (the width of a purpose-built ox-cart route), toyama-1988-road-undevelopment (why the Japanese countryside had no carts, and what the highway prohibition was for), ctie-michi-nazenaze (the width of the Five Highways, and the wider figure near Edo), nippon-com-gokaido (a second, wider account of that width); the GM's ruling of 2026-08-27; also cited, kotobank-suiun, danzhang-zhwiki, packhorse-enwiki (heavy freight by water, the shoulder-pole porter, and the packhorse where wheels cannot go). None of these gives a width for an ordinary village lane.

China leads here, as it does everywhere in this setting's geography, with Japan corroborating. The Chinese countryside was served by ways for the one-wheeled barrow rather than for the cart: its rural network was "narrow paths, scarcely wide enough for foot passengers and wheelbarrows", with rough unpaved cart tracks predominating only on the eastern plains, which is not the rice-growing south our settlements are modeled on. Those paths suited the traffic that used them - pedestrians, porters with carrying poles, pushers of wheelbarrows, and bearers carrying passengers in seats slung from poles. So the vehicle to picture on a village lane is the wheelbarrow and the shoulder-pole porter, and the track to picture, on this page's reading, is one barrow or one packhorse wide.

Japan says the same thing from the other side. Up to the end of the Edo period the horse-drawn carriage did not exist there at all: inhabitants went on foot or on horseback, and goods moved by human power or on a horse's back. Hand carts were confined to the wide level streets inside the cities, and on the highways their use was forbidden to the very end of the shogunate - to keep the post-horse system alive, the fear being that more vehicles would mean fewer horses. Road wear is NOT the reason: the slow light carts a person could push damaged the surface little, and the causes the paper names for Japan's having taken up riding but never the carriage are its terrain and climate, the kind of horse, and attitudes toward the horse, among others. Heavy freight went by boat. The Edo hand cart (大八車, daihachiguruma) was confined by law to Edo, Owari (Nagoya) and Sunpu (Shizuoka), though an 1815 report already had it in use in Kyoto, Osaka and the castle towns of other provinces, and in the late Edo period it spread beyond the castle towns as well - where to, the entry does not say, and the highway ban above stood to the end. The one wheeled way that was built for carts sets a ceiling rather than a norm: the purpose-built ox-cart stone route was typically 2.7 m wide - 9 shaku, about 9 ft - with a 90 cm track laid for the beast itself. That is Edo Japan; this setting differs, putting carts and wagons on the roads between its towns ("Could a cart use the roads here, and where would it go?"). The trunk highways were wider again: about 5.4 to 7.2 m (3 to 4 ken) as the ordinary width, with mountain roads expressly left out of that figure, and 9 m (5 ken) where the road ran close to Edo. A second account puts the width at from 4 ken (7.2 m) up to, in places, 7 or 8 ken (12.6 to 14.4 m), so the two readable accounts bracket the width rather than agree on it. What a mountain road narrowed to, and the 2 ken often given for the Hakone barrier, are on no page that was read. Paving followed the same split: China had many narrow stone-slab causeways, especially in the wet rice south where a paved way keeps porters and barrows above the mud, while Japan paved little beyond steep highway passes and the one ox-cart stone route above. In neither country was an ordinary village lane a wide two-lane road.

What the record does not give is a number. A search pass of 2026-08-27, made at the GM's request - "I just want to make sure that we did not set it arbitrarily" - read twenty-three pages and found no numeric width for an ordinary village lane or farm path in any language. A figure that circulates as "a rural way is one ken wide" traces to a page that does not say it, and neither the Japanese article on rural ways nor a prefectural authority's own list of questions about them gives a width. So the widths on these maps - 3 ft for a footpath, 5 ft for the cluster's spine and its field spur, 6 ft for the track out to the wider world, at the hamlet's scale of one foot to the pixel - are a map drawing convention, chosen inside the bounds the record does give (wider than nothing, narrower than the 9 ft cart road) and neither corroborated nor contradicted by a measured lane. A survey of a surviving hamlet lane would settle it.

Where a lane may run - off the row crops, off the wet toe - is the next question.

The rule the map follows: a village lane is a narrow single track of packed earth with a soft worn shoulder and no marking down its middle, never a wide two-lane road. At the hamlet's scale the map draws a footpath 3 ft wide, the spine and the field spur 5 ft, and the connecting track 6 ft; those three widths are a map drawing convention, not a measured figure. A lane is a corridor nothing is built on - farmsteads front it, and no corner of a house stands on its tread. An important inter-village or pilgrimage path may be a narrow stone causeway; a modest settlement's own lanes are earth.
