Village boundaries and their markers (murazakai)

A village had two edges. The one its villagers felt as the village's edge, 村境 (murazakai, "village boundary"), where they held their rites against evil, was where a road from outside reached the houses - so a village had one for every road leading out. The boundary god, the dosojin, usually stood there, a rope or a straw figure might be set across or beside the road, and some villages planted an enoki tree beside it. A post town on a main highway marked its two ends in earth and timber instead, each with a pair of banks, a bend in the road and a tall post.

The other edge was the line where a village's land met the next village's, far out in the hills and fields. It was fixed on the survey at the start of the Edo period, but villagers seldom held their boundary rites there or felt it as the village's edge.

  • A village had as many entrances as it had roads leading out.
    A Japanese encyclopedia of folk custom puts the boundary villagers treated as theirs at the edge between the settlement and its fields, far inside the line with the next village, at the point where a road from outside is about to enter the settlement, "as many village boundaries as there are roads leading out".
  • The entrance was also a gathering place.
    Because villagers gathered there, a Japanese encyclopedia of folk custom says, the spot also served as a plaza, a center for the village.
  • The boundary god usually stood at the entrance.
    The dosojin is "usually enshrined" at the village boundary, as it was at a village's other thresholds - its entrances and exits, passes, crossroads and the feet of bridges.
  • Villages "cut the road" against evil at the entrance.
    Beside the boundary god a village might cut the road, michikiri, to keep evil out: a sacred rope or a straw serpent stretched across the way, a rope shaped like a centipede, a snake or a dragon fixed to a tree at the boundary, or straw sandals offered.
  • The ropes and straw figures set against evil at village entrances varied by region.
    A thick rope strung across at New Year, the kanjo-tsuri, is common in the Kinki region, huge straw figures are often seen in Tohoku, and big straw serpents are laid at village boundaries across the country. Straw ropes, figures and sandals are set at village boundaries in farming areas throughout Japan, and around Kinki they cluster in Wakasa, Omi, Iga, the eastern mountains of Nara and southern Yamashiro.
  • Did villages plant a great tree beside the boundary god at the entrance?
    Some planted an enoki, by the one account found: a guide to the great old trees of Chiba prefecture, east of Tokyo, says that in former times a boundary god or a small shrine was kept near a village's entrance and an enoki planted there - it is thought because the enoki had long been believed to ward off evil spirits, because it spreads its branches wide and gives deep shade, and because its fruit can be eaten.
  • No page we read says how many villages kept an entrance tree.
    The pages that place the enoki at the entrance give no count or share.
  • Beyond its entrance, no page we read puts a village's great tree at its crossroads or its well.
    The searches for a great tree at either found none.

A town on a main highway marked its ends more solidly than a village did.

  • Each end of a post town was marked by a pair of earth banks, the mitsuke.
    At each entrance of a post town stood a mitsuke, one at the end toward Edo and one at the end toward Kyoto, and the stretch between them was the post town: a pair of banks, one each side of the road and at right angles to it, fenced along the top.
  • At Kanagawa post town, each of the mitsuke's banks was about 8 ft high and 14 ft thick at its base.
    At Kanagawa's Edo-side mitsuke each bank was about 14 ft thick at its base and 2 ft across the top, about 8 ft high, with a fence about 2.5 ft high on it.
  • Did a post town's mitsuke banks still matter by the late Edo period?
    Perhaps not much. The road ministry's page on them suggests they began as simple defenses that also showed where the town began, and, since a survey of the Tokaido's post towns has no entry for them, wonders whether by the late Edo period they mattered much.
  • At both ends of a post town the road was bent into a dogleg.
    The highway was bent into a dogleg, the masugata, at both ends of a post town.
  • Gates stood at both ends of a post town, near the banks.
    Gates, the kido, stood at a post town's ends near the mitsuke banks.
  • A tall wooden boundary post stood at both ends of most post towns on the Tokaido through today's Kanagawa prefecture.
    Seven of the nine post towns there, all but Odawara and Hakone, had one at each end.
  • No page we read says how a market town off the highways marked its ends.
    Every page read describes the ends of a post town on a main highway.

The second edge, the line between one village's land and the next, lay well beyond the houses and fields a traveler passed.

  • The line between villages ran through the hills and wild land.
    The territorial boundary lies where a village's hills and wild land, and sometimes its fields, meet another village's.
  • The line between villages was fixed by the land surveys at the start of the Edo period.
    It grew gradually clearer as the self-governing medieval village took shape, and the land surveys and the partition of villages, murakiri, at the start of the Edo period made it possible to show on a map which fields belonged to which village.
  • The line between villages was drawn with an eye to irrigation water and rights in the common hills and fields.
    The partition of villages, murakiri, drew the lines with an eye to irrigation water and to each village's rights in the common hills and fields.
  • No page we read says whether the line between villages usually followed a ridge or a stream.
    None of the pages on the boundary or the partition says what natural line, if any, it usually followed.
  • Villagers seldom held their boundary rites at the line between villages, and it was little felt as the village's edge.
    A boundary god is sometimes enshrined, or the road cut, at this outer line too, but such cases are few, and in general it was not strongly felt as the village's boundary.
  • Where a highway crossed the line between villages, was it marked?
    On the one highway whose record we read, mostly yes: on the Tokaido through today's Kanagawa prefecture, posts marking the village boundary can be confirmed for most of the villages between the post towns, quite tall wooden posts lettered, it seems, with the boundary and the distances.
  • Boundary posts were in use long before the Edo period.
    The boundaries of temple precincts and medieval estates had been marked by posts of wood or stone, or now and then by a rock or a standing tree.

The other great roadside tree a traveler met on the way to a village stood on the highway's milestone mounds.

  • A milestone mound, the ichirizuka, stood beside the main highways at every ri.
    Tokugawa Ieyasu ordered them in 1604, at the start of the Edo period. The mounds were raised at every ri, about 2.4 miles, counted from Nihonbashi in Edo.
  • Each milestone mound was about 30 ft square and 10 ft high, with a tree on top.
    A mound was 5 ken (about 30 ft) square and 1 jo high, about 10 ft, with an enoki or another tree planted on top so that travelers could rest in its shade. A jo is ten shaku; the article's own conversion of it to 1.7 m (~6 ft) is an error.
  • The milestone mounds stood in pairs, one each side of the road.
    In its original form a milestone mound was a pair, one each side of the highway, built facing each other across the road.
  • Most of the milestone mounds' trees were enoki.
    On the five main highways their trees were 55% enoki, 27% pine and 8% cedar, and the standard large dictionary of Japanese gives the enoki as the rule.
