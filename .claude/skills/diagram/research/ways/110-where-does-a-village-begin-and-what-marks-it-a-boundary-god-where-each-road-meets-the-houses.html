Where does a village begin, and what marks it? A boundary god where each road meets the houses

Sources: kotobank-murazakai (where the village boundary is felt to lie, how many there are, and what stands there), dosojin-jawiki (the boundary god at a village's entrances and exits), michikiri-jawiki (the forms of cutting the road), kanjonawa-jawiki (the sacred rope and where it is found), mlit-tokaido-mitsuke (the post town's gate-mounds and their size), shukuba-jawiki (the crank and the gates at a post town's ends), mlit-tokaido-bojikui (the boundary posts at a post town's ends)

The line between one village's land and the next is the question after this one. This one is the place a traveler would say the village begins.

Where. The boundary villagers themselves treated as the village's edge, and where they held their rites, is the edge between the settlement and its fields, far inside the line with the next village; it is the point where a road from outside is about to enter the settlement, so a village has as many such boundaries as it has roads leading out. Because people gathered there, the spot also served as a plaza, a center for the village.

What marks it. The boundary god, the dosojin, is usually enshrined there, as it is at a village's other thresholds - its entrances and exits, passes, crossroads and the feet of bridges. Beside it the village may cut the road (michikiri) against evil: a sacred rope or a straw serpent stretched across the way, a rope fixed to a tree at the boundary, straw sandals offered. The forms vary by region. A thick rope strung across at New Year, the kanjo-nawa, is commonest in the Kinki region, huge straw figures are often seen in Tohoku, and big straw serpents are laid at village boundaries across the country; the ropes, figures and sandals of straw are concentrated around Kinki, from Wakasa to southern Yamashiro. A great enoki often stood by the boundary god; that tree is a question on the vegetation page.

A post town. A town on a main highway marked its two ends in earth and timber. At each entrance of a post town stood a mitsuke, one at the end toward Edo and one at the end toward Kyoto, and the stretch between them was the post town: a pair of banks, one each side of the road and at right angles to it, fenced along the top. At Kanagawa each bank was about 14 ft thick at its base and 2 ft across the top, about 8 ft high, with a fence about 2.5 ft high on it. The page describing them suggests they began as simple defenses that also showed where the town began, and wonders whether by the late Edo period they mattered much. At both ends the highway was bent into a crank (masugata), and gates (kido) stood near the mounds. A tall wooden boundary post stood at both ends of seven of the nine post towns on the Tokaido through Kanagawa. How a market town that was not a post town marked its ends, no page read says.

What this means for the map. A village's entrance is not one point but one per road out, each where the road leaves the last houses for the fields, and each carries its boundary god. What stands beside the god is a knob rolled per village among the attested forms: nothing more; a rope across the road; a straw figure or serpent at the roadside. The share of villages that keep each is a GUESS. A town on the Imperial road carries its gate-mounds, its crank and a boundary post at both ends of the road through it.

The rule the map follows: every lane or road that leaves a village for the fields or the next settlement carries, where it passes the last house, a boundary god's stone at the roadside, with a small trodden open patch beside it (its size a GUESS). The knob, rolled per village: in about half, nothing more; in about a quarter, a rope stretched across the road there; in about a quarter, a straw figure or serpent beside the stone (the shares a GUESS). A town on the Imperial road draws at each end of its street a pair of earth banks facing each other across the road, each about 14 ft thick at the base, 8 ft high and fenced on top, a crank in the road just inside them, and one tall boundary post beside the road.
