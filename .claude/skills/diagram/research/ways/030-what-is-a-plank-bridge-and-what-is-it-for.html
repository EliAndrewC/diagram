What is a plank bridge, and what is it for?

Sources: itabashi-kotobank (what an itabashi is, and the word's age), guo-shan-nongjia-gushiwen (a plank bridge beside a farmhouse in Tang China), dobashi-jawiki (how an earthen deck is built), aze-jawiki (the bund as a path); the GM's rulings of 2026-07-22; this project's census of the planks on the shipped maps

The small crossing drawn over a farm ditch is an itabashi (板橋, ita plank + hashi bridge): a bridge made of boards, and the glyph draws it as a planked deck with the seams running across it. It is not a dobashi (土橋), an earthen deck - logs laid side by side with no gaps, then earth thrown over them and trodden down until it fills the hollows and the surface is level - and the two are different objects rather than two names for one. The name matters because the glyph has to keep its meaning when a reader clicks it.

What such a plank is FOR is the whole of where it may be drawn. The bunds between paddies also serve as the paths field-workers walk, and the plank is taken to be the board they lay where a bund path meets an irrigation ditch - a GUESS, since no readable page describes it - so no path leads to one, because the way to it is the bund, and the map draws it square across its ditch rather than obliquely. The plank bridge itself is an old farm crossing: a Tang poem of the eighth century opens on a traveler crossing a plank bridge over a spring stream beside a mountain farmer's house, and the Japanese word itabashi is on record by about 1310. Neither puts one on a paddy bund, and no page read, from any period, does. It is laid only over water one cannot stride across; the width at which a household bothers to build one, and the GM's ruling behind that width, are on the water page. And because it exists so that someone can cross TO something, both of its banks must land on ground worth crossing to - cultivated ground, wet or dry, the settlement itself, or a walked dike - and never on reed marsh, on scrub commons, or off the edge of the map.

That last rule rests on a census of every plank on every map rather than on one bad crossing: the drainage toes and the diagonal edge-drains along a field's outer boundary are where a plank has nothing on its far side, so the rule takes the plank off them, while every field-to-field, dry-to-wet and settlement-to-field crossing stands. A ditch running along a field's outer margin with nothing beyond it carries no plank at all: nothing to cross to needs no crossing.

Its size is a ruling, not a finding. The GM set both the width and the length on 2026-07-22: a footplank is a single-file crossing about 4 ft wide over a ditch of roughly 8 ft, with a short abutment at each end, and a board any wider or longer reads as a jetty. It is kept both narrower and shorter than a cart lane on purpose, because the deck that carries a way is as wide as the way it carries, and a footplank that reached that width would read as a road bridge.

The rule the map follows: a standalone plank crosses its ditch square, at about the middle of a long stretch, and a very long stretch takes further planks at intervals along it; both of its ends land on cultivated ground, on the settlement, or on a walked dike, and a plank that can find no such spot is not drawn. No path leads to it. A ditch stretch whose far bank is marsh or scrub for its whole length carries none.

The rule the map follows: a plank never sits on a farmhouse. A bridge is allowed to lie on the water it spans - that is what it is for - but its deck is tested against every house on the map, and a plank near the settlement's edge slides along its own ditch, staying over the water, until it clears them.
