Where does one village end and the next begin? A line on the survey, seldom marked on the ground

Sources: kotobank-murazakai (the territorial boundary, when it became clear, and how seldom it was marked), aric-sato (what the village partition weighed in drawing the line), mlit-tokaido-bojikui (the boundary posts where a highway crossed the line), boji-jawiki (the older boundary markers of estates and precincts)

A village has two edges. The one a traveler meets first, where the road reaches the houses, is the question before this one. This one is the outer line, where the village's land meets the next village's.

Where the line runs. The territorial boundary lies where a village's hills and wild land, and sometimes its fields, meet another village's. It grew gradually clearer as the self-governing medieval village took shape, and the land surveys and the partition of villages (murakiri) at the start of the Edo period then made it possible to show on a map which fields belonged to which village, fixing the line. The partition drew village boundaries with an eye to irrigation water and to rights in the common hills and fields. Whether the line was usually set on a ridge or a stream, no page read says.

Whether it was marked. Mostly it was not. A boundary god is sometimes enshrined or the road cut at this outer line too, but such cases are few, and in general it was not strongly felt as the village's boundary. Where a highway crossed it, it was posted: on the Tokaido through Kanagawa posts marking the village boundary can be confirmed for most of the villages between the post towns, quite tall wooden posts lettered, it seems, with the boundary and the distances. The practice is older than the Edo village: the boundaries of temple precincts and medieval estates had been marked by posts of wood or stone, or now and then by a rock or a standing tree.

What this means for the map. The outer line was a line on the survey and not a thing on the ground, so a village sheet draws no boundary line - a finding, and a drawing convention too, since these maps draw no administrative lines. The one mark the line leaves on the ground is the boundary post beside a highway.

The rule the map follows: no boundary line between villages is drawn. Where an Imperial road runs across a village sheet, one tall wooden boundary post may stand beside it where the road leaves the village's own fields for the next village's; where that falls, and whether it falls inside the sheet, is a GUESS rolled per map. No post stands beside a lane.
