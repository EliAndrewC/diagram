How far past the bank does a bridge land?

Sources: ritter-timber-bridges (what a timber beam's bearing seat measures, and what an abutment holds), nrcs-ts14q-abutments (scour under a small bridge's abutment), saruhashi-jawiki (the Edo-period cantilever bridge set into its banks), aimoto-kintaikyo-sekaiisan (the Aimoto Bridge of 1663), kong-qingpu-guqiao-mingcheng (the old Chinese names for an abutment's walls); the GM's ruling of 2026-08-09; this project's measurement of the drawn decks. None gives a distance from the water's edge to the abutment.

A bridge does not stop at the water's edge. Its girder bears on an abutment whose footing the current undercutting the bank must never reach: a small bridge stands on a shallow strip footing, too shallow to be sunk below the scour, so the bank is armored against it instead. Behind the sill the abutment is doing its other work, holding the roadway embankment in place. What does NOT account for the landing is the seat the girder bears on: a timber beam's bearing length is measured in inches - 10 to 24 of them under highway loading, one of them on a glulam beam 95-1/2 ft long out to out - so a foot or two of timber is the whole of it, and the seat cannot be what carries a deck feet past the water. The maps draw 10 ft of deck past the water on each side. That distance is a GUESS: no page a reader can open gives a landing distance for a bridge of this size, and what the record can offer for it is the scour setback and the embankment behind the sill, not the seat.

The engineering manuals above are modern, but a bridge carried back into its banks is not a modern idea. Edo-period Japan had a bridge with no pier at all, the hanebashi (刎橋, "cantilever bridge"): holes were cut in the bank rock and timbers set into them at a slant to project over the water, each layer reaching a little further than the one beneath it. The Aimoto Bridge over the Kurobe River, built, by the page read, in 1663 where floods carried off any pier within a year, was made of great timbers thrust out from both banks, and the Saruhashi, 30.9 m long and 3.3 m wide, has been a bridge of that form since at least 1756. In China an abutment's walls had names of their own in old books: the front wall that bears the load was the jingang qiang (金刚墙), and the splayed walls either side of it the shunshui jingang qiang (顺水金刚墙). So the maps keep their landings. What none of these gives is a length: no page read, premodern or modern, says how far past the water a small bridge's deck ran, so the 10 ft is a guess with no period figure to calibrate it against.

The GM ruled on 2026-08-09 that every corner of a deck stands on dry ground, and the reason is obliquity. Where a way crosses a watercourse at an angle, the water under the deck is wider than the channel - the channel's width divided by the sine of that angle, and the deck's own width pushes its outer corners further out again before they clear the bank. The crossing is solved for that angle exactly, and the test is applied to the deck's four real corners rather than to the ends of its centerline, because a corner is what runs out of bank first.

The rule the map follows: every corner of a carried deck stands on dry ground, with about 10 ft of deck past the water on each bank. A standalone footplank is the exception and keeps a deliberately short landing: its ends simply rest on the bank, and giving it a bridge's landing makes it read as a jetty. Both figures are real feet at every tier, converted to the sheet's own scale when drawn.
