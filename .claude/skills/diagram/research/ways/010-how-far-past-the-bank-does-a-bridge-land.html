How far past the bank does a bridge land?

Sources: ritter-timber-bridges (what a timber beam's bearing seat measures, and what an abutment holds), nrcs-ts14q-abutments (scour under a small bridge's abutment); the GM's ruling of 2026-08-09; this project's measurement of the drawn decks. Neither gives a distance from the water's edge to the abutment

A bridge does not stop at the water's edge. Its girder bears on an abutment sill set back from the channel edge, because the current undercutting the bank must never reach the bearing: a small bridge stands on a shallow strip footing, too shallow to be sunk below the scour, so the bank is armored and the footing kept clear of it instead.12 Behind the sill the abutment is doing its other work, holding the roadway embankment in place.13 What does NOT account for the landing is the seat the girder bears on: a timber beam's bearing length is measured in inches - 10 to 24 of them under highway loading, on spans up to 94 ft1 - so a foot or two of timber is the whole of it, and the seat cannot be what carries a deck feet past the water. The maps draw 10 ft of deck past the water on each side. That distance is a GUESS: no page a reader can open gives a landing distance for a bridge of this size,14 and what the record can offer for it is the scour setback and the embankment behind the sill, not the seat.

The GM ruled on 2026-08-09 that every corner of a deck stands on dry ground, and the reason is obliquity. Where a way crosses a watercourse at an angle, the water under the deck is wider than the channel by the sine of that angle, and the deck's own width pushes its outer corners further out again before they clear the bank. The crossing is solved for that angle exactly, and the test is applied to the deck's four real corners rather than to the ends of its centerline, because a corner is what runs out of bank first.

The rule the map follows: every corner of a carried deck stands on dry ground, with about 10 ft of deck past the water on each bank. A standalone footplank is the exception and keeps a deliberately short landing: its ends simply rest on the bank, and giving it a bridge's landing turns it back into a jetty. Both figures are real feet at every tier, converted to the sheet's own scale when drawn.
