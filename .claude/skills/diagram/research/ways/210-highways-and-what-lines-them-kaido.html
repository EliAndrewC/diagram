Highways and what lines them (kaido)

Japan's great highways (kaido) were set by the shogunate's order of 1605, early in the Edo period, to a standard width of about 30 ft, narrower in the mountains, with a mound raised at every ri to mark the distance and trees planted along the road. Avenues of pine and cedar lined the Five Highways and the other main roads, and China had lined its official roads with trees since the Zhou dynasty.

The highways carried official travel: in each post town on them a transport office relayed official men and horses, and lords on alternate attendance lodged near its middle, with the merchants and craftsmen farther out. Between the post towns it passed through villages strung along the road, and on the Tokaido through Kanagawa a little under one in three of them kept a rest stop. In this setting the highway is the Imperial road, a through-route meant to wire the domains together into one trade web.

  • How wide were Japan's great highways?
    About 18 to 30 ft, narrower in the mountains. The roads ministry gives the standard set by the order of 1605 as about 9 m (~30 ft), cut to about 4 to 7 m (~13-23 ft) in the mountains. Another account gives the Five Highways as generally 3 to 4 ken, about 18 to 24 ft, leaving out the mountain roads, and 5 ken, about 30 ft, near Edo.
  • Through a castle town a highway could be narrower: about 15 ft at Hiroshima.
    The Saigoku road through Hiroshima's castle town was roughly 2.5 ken wide, about 15 ft, and its milestone mounds and roadside pines were set up by the Asano, the lords of the domain. Where a highway becomes a town's street, its width is at How wide is a town's street, its side lane and its back alley?
  • No page we read gives the width of the road before an official's gate.
    Nor of a samurai residence's gate, or of a lane to a side gate.
  • The shogunate's highway order of 1605, early in the Edo period, had a mound raised at every ri and trees planted along the road.
    The roads ministry's page sets both in the same order. A ri is about 2.4 miles. The mounds' size, their pairing and their trees are at Village boundaries and their markers (murazakai).
  • A tree was planted on each mound to shade travelers.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on the distance mound says trees such as the enoki were planted on top, so that travelers could rest in the shade.
  • The shogunate kept avenues of pine and cedar along its main highways.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on the Nikko cedar avenue says the Tokugawa shogunate set out avenues of pine, cedar and other trees along the main highways, the Five Highways first among them.
  • No page we read says where an avenue gave way to a town's houses.
    Neither the Japanese pages on the highways and their avenues nor the essay on China's roadside trees says where one ended.

China lined its official roads far earlier, and marked their distances too.

  • In the Zhou dynasty, the trees along an official road were called guanshu, "official trees".
    A modern essay on China's roadside trees says the trees planted along both sides of a road built with official funds bore that name, and paraphrases the Zhouli as requiring trees beside the roads of the suburbs and the countryside, "lining trees to mark the road" and to shade travelers.
  • Under the Western Wei, an earth mound beside the road marked every li, and one governor replaced his with pagoda trees.
    The same essay says that because rain washed the mounds away, the governor of Yongzhou had pagoda trees planted where they had stood in his jurisdiction.
  • The Yuan dynasty ordered elm, willow and pagoda trees planted along the roads.
    The same essay names the Yuan edict "Planting elm, willow and pagoda trees along roads".

Back in Japan, where a highway ran through a post town, the road became the town's street, and the town lined it.

  • A post town was laid out along the highway from its middle out.
    The transport office and the lodgings for lords, the honjin and waki-honjin, stood near the middle; before and behind them came the inns, teahouses and cookshops for ordinary travelers, with the porters' and packhorse offices; and farther out stood the merchants and craftsmen.
  • A post town also kept a place for its notice boards (kosatsuba).
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on post towns says inns, cheap lodgings, teahouses and shops stood in rows along its road, and that a place for the notice boards was set up too. Where the notice board stood is at Notice boards (kosatsuba).
  • In 1803, late in the Edo period, Oiso, a post town on the Tokaido, had three honjin and 85 inns.
    The inns were of every size, large, middling and small.
  • In Kanagawa post town, many teahouses stood eave to eave partway up a slope.
    The caption of a road map on the roads ministry's Tokaido pages sets them partway up the Daimachi slope, overlooking the sea.
  • How fully a post town's road was built up followed its traffic.
    On the busy highways, such as the Tokaido and the Nakasendo, each lot's main house was built across its whole frontage on the road; on byways and at mountain stations the inns often kept a front garden toward the road and gaps from their neighbors.

Between the post towns the highway ran through farming villages, and some of them lived from it.

  • Geographers class a village strung along a road as a road village or a street village.
    A road village (roson) lines both sides of the road with dwellings, with little trade, its houses a little spaced and living little from the road; a street village (gaison) lives from the road, its houses packed tighter, with shops and lodging. The two are so alike in plan that a topographic map can hardly tell them apart.
  • Between post towns the highway had rest stops (tateba), with teahouses and stalls.
    They were set partway along where the next post town was far, or at a difficult place such as a pass, and horses and palanquins were sometimes changed there. Some were set up by a domain, and others grew up at the hands of the inhabitants along the road. A roads ministry page on the Tokaido adds that there were many teahouses between the post towns.
  • How many villages kept a rest stop?
    A little under one in three, on the one stretch counted. On the Tokaido through Kanagawa there were 56 villages between its nine post towns, and 18 rest stops, two of them within post towns - so 16 of the 56 villages kept one.
  • A rest stop's teahouses gathered at the ends of the village's street.
    They were generally concentrated at the corners at both ends of the street's row of houses; they were also set where the view was wide at the top of a slope past a post town's end, at a fork where another road left the highway, and in the villages beside the great unbridged rivers.
  • No page we read says how many teahouses a rest stop kept.
    The pages on the rest stop and on Kanagawa's rest stops name its teahouses but give no count for one rest stop.
  • A teahouse was built open, as a place to rest, not to lodge.
    It had an earth floor, and its rooms were divided not by walls but only by doors and screens, kept distinct from an inn because it was a place to rest, not to stay the night.
  • Could an in-between station lodge travelers?
    Officially no, and accounts differ on whether it did. An in-between station (ai-no-shuku) was a stop larger than a rest stop but not a recognized post town: officially it was a village or town, and lodging travelers was in principle forbidden, though rice dealers, sake dealers and other shops lined it as in a post town. The Japanese Wikipedia article on the rest stop says instead that an in-between station was a rest stop grown into a large settlement, with inns set up in it too.
