How many cross streets does a town have, and how does its road come in?

Sources: shukubamachi-kotobank (the strip lots along a post town's highway, and the barrier banks at its ends), oiso-shukuba-mlit (how much of one post town stood on its highway), shukuba-jawiki (the crank at both ends, and the gates), nakatsugawa-masugata (what the crank was for), jokamachi-jawiki (the castle town's bent roads and dead ends), ginza-machiwari-column (the planned block of an Edo commoner district), zhouzhuang-zhwiki (a Chinese market town's two main streets in a T)

One street, or two in a T. A Japanese post town was one street. Its lots were laid out along both sides of the highway in planned narrow strips, of nearly constant depth and differing frontage. In 1803 Oiso on the Tokaido had 605 houses, and 269 of them stood on strip lots eave to eave along both sides of the highway; where the rest stood, and by what lanes they were reached, the page does not say. A Chinese market town could have two: by the early Qing, Zhouzhuang near Suzhou had grown around its Fu'an bridge into a busy town of two main streets, its north-south market street and its middle market street, meeting in a T. A county seat's cross or T of gate-to-gate streets is the city fabric page's question. A planned grid belongs to a larger place: the commoner land of Edo was laid out in blocks 60 ken (about 120 m) square, with an open lot in the middle and town lots 20 ken deep along the streets, which is a city's grid, not a town's. How many side lanes a town ran off its street is counted on no page that was read.

The ends. The road did not run straight in. At both ends of a post town the highway was bent into a crank, the masugata; a post town was built to serve in part as a fortress, and its masugata bent the road twice at right angles so that an enemy would find it hard to get in. On the Five Highways the town's ends were also marked by a mitsuke of stone walls or earth banks, and gates stood at the ends near the mitsuke, the stretch between them counting as the post town. A castle town did the same on a larger scale: past its edge the main road was lined with houses leaving no gaps, so the castle was hard to see, and bent into a key shape or ended in dead ends, lengthening the way to the castle.

So a town's plan is a knob, rolled per settlement between two attested forms: one street, the road itself, with lots down both sides; or two main streets meeting in a T. Either way the road comes in through a crank at each end of the built street, which is accurate - and is the opposite of a city's, where the Imperial road runs straight from gate to gate as a deliberate deviation. The count of side lanes is a guess, set by the houses that need one, as a street's reason for being requires.

The rule the map follows: a town rolls one of two plans: a single main street along the road, built up on both sides; or two main streets meeting in a T. At each end of the built street the road bends twice at right angles before running on, and a low bank or wall may mark the end. Side lanes are laid only where houses need them, their number a guess.
