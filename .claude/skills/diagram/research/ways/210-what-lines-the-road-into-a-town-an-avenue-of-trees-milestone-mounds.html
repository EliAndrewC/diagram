What lines the road into a town - an avenue of trees, milestone mounds?

Sources: mlit-kinsei-michi-gokaido (the highway's standard width, its mounds and its trees), nikko-suginamiki-jawiki (the pine and cedar avenues of the main highways), thepaper-road-trees (China's tree-lined official roads, their species, and the roadside distance mound), ichirizuka-jawiki (the tree planted on a Japanese milestone mound)

How wide. The road into a town on a trunk route is the highway itself, and in Japan its standard from 1605 was about 9 m, cut to about 4 to 7 m in the mountains. The maps draw the Imperial road 30 ft wide on every sheet, as the capital page records; where it becomes the town's street it is this page's street-width question, and a lesser road in is a village lane.

What lines it. The same order of 1605 had a mound built at every ri and roadside trees planted; a ri is about 2.4 miles. The shogunate kept avenues of pine, cedar and the like along the Five Highways and the other main roads. China did the same far earlier. In the Zhou dynasty, the trees planted along both sides of an official road were called guanshu, "official trees", and the Zhouli required trees beside the roads of the suburbs and the countryside, "lining trees to mark the road" and to shade travelers. Under the Western Wei distance was marked by an earth mound heaped beside the road at every li, and because rain washed the mounds away a governor had pagoda trees planted in their place; the Yuan issued an edict to plant elm, willow and pagoda trees along the roads. Where along the road an avenue gives way to the town's own houses is on no page that was read.

So the Imperial road into a town is lined with trees, which is accurate, and their kind is a knob rolled per settlement: pine or cedar, as on the Japanese highways, or elm, willow or pagoda tree, as on the Chinese. A distance mound stands beside the road at its interval where one falls on the sheet. What stands at the town's gate end of the road, the gate market, is the towns page's question, and the strip outside a city gate is the city hinterland page's.

The rule the map follows: the Imperial road approaching a town, 30 ft wide, is lined on both sides by a row of trees of one kind, rolled per settlement from pine, cedar, elm, willow and pagoda tree, running from the edge of the sheet to where the gate market or the town's first houses begin; that stopping point is a guess. A low earth distance mound, planted with a tree, stands beside the road where the road's measured interval falls inside the sheet, and nowhere else.
