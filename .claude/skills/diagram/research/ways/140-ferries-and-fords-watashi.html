Ferries and fords (watashi)

In Edo Japan (1603-1868) the shogunate restricted bridges over the great rivers, so a traveler on a highway crossed them by boat at a ferry, or on foot through the shallows at a ford; the smaller rivers were bridged. A ferry crossed only at landings the shogunate designated, and a highway ferry was a large concern: the Rokugo ferry on the Tokaido kept 14 boats and 24 boatmen, with a river office, a notice board and the boatmen's huts at its landing.

  • The shogunate restricted bridges over the great rivers, and why is debated.
    The reasons given are several: that a river served like a barrier station, that the shogunate meant to keep the post towns on both banks and the river porters in work, or that bridge building was not equal to it. Either way, a traveler crossed by boat or on foot.
  • Ferries ran where a bridge was hard to build or not allowed.
    Under the Edo regime ferries ran mainly where bridging was difficult, or where, as near a barrier station, bridges were not permitted for military reasons. On the Naka and Edo rivers no bridge was permitted, which made the ferries that joined the roads important.
  • Only the great rivers went unbridged.
    On the Tokaido through Kanagawa, the Rokugo and Banyu rivers were crossed by boat and the Sakawa on foot, while the Tsurumi, Katabira, Hikichi and other rivers were bridged.
  • The setting's Imperial road counts ferry stations among its engineered works.
    The campaign notes list ferry stations beside the bridges over major rivers in the Imperial road's budget for specialized engineering.

A river with no bridge could be crossed three ways: by ferry, by ford, or on a bridge of boats.

  • A ferry's boats set out from a landing.
    The ferry landing is the place where ferry boats depart and arrive.
  • At a ford, travelers were carried through the shallows, and the crossing closed when the water rose to about 1.4 m (~5 ft).
    Where no bridge was built, travelers were taken through the ford on foot, on a litter or on horseback, and at a depth of 4 shaku 5 sun the river was closed.
  • On the Tokaido, the Abe, Oi and Sakawa were forded and the Fuji and Tenryu ferried.
    A Japanese encyclopedia's entry on the crossing names them.
  • A bridge of boards over a line of boats was the third way, and the rarest.
    The Kiso rivers were generally crossed by ferry, and bridged with boats only when the shogun or the Korean embassies crossed. At Toyama a bridge of 64 boats tied together and chained to posts on both banks crossed a river up to 430 m (~1,411 ft) wide.

The two Tokaido ferries through Kanagawa, at Rokugo and Banyu, are described landing by landing.

  • A ferry crossed only at landings the shogunate designated.
    Crossing anywhere else was strictly restricted.
  • The Rokugo ferry's landing held a river office, a notice board and the boatmen's huts.
    They stood on the Kawasaki bank, and the village of Hachimanzuka on the far bank had a notice board too. Twenty-four boatmen worked the ferry, with 14 boats: 8 for horses and 6 for travelers on foot.
  • The Banyu ferry kept its river office on one bank only.
    Its designated landings were at two villages, Yanagishima and Banyu, with the river office and the notice board at Banyu, and 16 boatmen were on duty day and night.
  • A highway ferry's boats were about 9 to 13 m (~30-43 ft) long.
    The Banyu ferry's small boat for travelers was about 9 m (~30 ft) long and carried about 20, and its large flat-bottomed boats were about 13 m (~43 ft) long and 2.7 m (~9 ft) wide.
  • A ferry was kept by whoever the authorities contracted.
    The Rokugo ferry was run first by Edo townsmen or by the village on the far bank, and from 1709 by Kawasaki post town; the Yagiri ferry on the Edo river, designated by the shogunate, was run by farmers.
  • How many settlements kept a ferry landing? On the Tokaido through Kanagawa, four of 65.
    The road there passed nine post towns and 56 villages, and only the four on the banks at Rokugo and Banyu - a post town and three villages - held a landing. The count is ours, from the two ferries' accounts.
  • How many boats did a ferry kept by one village hold?
    No page we read says: the boat counts found are those of highway ferries.
