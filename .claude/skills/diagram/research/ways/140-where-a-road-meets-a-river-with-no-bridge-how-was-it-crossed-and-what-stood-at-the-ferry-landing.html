When a river was not bridged. In Edo Japan the shogunate restricted bridges over the great rivers - whether because a river served as a barrier, to keep the post towns on both banks and the river porters in work, or because bridge building was not equal to it, is debated - so a traveler crossed either by boat or on foot. Ferries ran where a bridge was hard to build or, as at a barrier, not allowed; on the Naka and Edo rivers no bridge was permitted, which made the ferries joining the roads important. Only the great rivers were left open: on the Tokaido through Kanagawa the Rokugo and Banyu were crossed by boat and the Sakawa on foot, while the Tsurumi, Katabira, Hikichi and other rivers were bridged. In the setting, the campaign notes count ferry stations with the bridges over major rivers among the Imperial road's engineered structures.

Three ways across. A ferry carried people and horses in boats from a landing (watashiba), the place the boats set out and came in. At a ford, where no bridge was built, travelers were taken through the shallows on foot, on a litter or on horseback, and the crossing stopped when the water reached about 4.5 ft. On the Tokaido the Abe, Oi and Sakawa were forded and the Fuji and Tenryu ferried. The third way, a pontoon bridge of boards laid over a line of boats, was rare: the Kiso rivers were generally crossed by ferry and bridged with boats only for the shogun and the Korean embassies, while at Toyama a permanent pontoon bridge joined 64 boats across a river up to 430 m wide.

What stood at the landing. A ferry crossed at designated landings set by the shogunate, and crossing anywhere else was strictly restricted. At the Rokugo ferry the landing on the Kawasaki bank had a river office, a notice-board place (the opposite village had one too) and the boatmen's huts; 24 boatmen worked it, with 14 boats, 8 for horses and 6 for people on foot. The Banyu ferry had a landing in the village on each bank, with the river office and the notice board on one side only; 16 boatmen were on duty day and night; its small boat for travelers was about 30 ft long and carried about 20, and its large flat-bottomed boats were about 43 ft long and 9 ft wide.

Who kept it, and how many did. A ferry was kept by whoever the authorities contracted: the Rokugo ferry was run first by Edo townsmen or by the village on the far bank, and from 1709 by Kawasaki post town, and the Yagiri ferry on the Edo river, designated by the shogunate, was run by farmers. Few settlements kept one: of the nine post towns and 56 villages on the Tokaido through Kanagawa, only the four on the banks at Rokugo and Banyu - a post town and three villages - held a ferry landing.

What this means for the map. A ferry belongs only where a road meets a river too wide to bridge; a stream is bridged. Where a village's, town's or city's road meets such a river, the crossing is a knob rolled per map: a ferry, with a landing on each bank; or a ford, the road running down into the shallows on both sides. An Imperial road never fords: the canon names bridges and ferry stations for it, and this project reads that as the whole of its crossings. How many boats a village-kept ferry held, no page read says.

The rule the map follows: a road that meets a river too wide to bridge ends at a ferry landing on each bank, or, on a road that is not the Imperial road, runs down into a ford (the choice rolled per map). A landing is a cleared stretch of bank where the road reaches the water, with boats drawn up at it; on one bank only stand a river office, a notice board and a boatmen's hut. A highway ferry's boats are up to about 14, the foot boats about 30 ft long and the cargo boats about 43 by 9 ft; a village-kept ferry has two or three boats (a GUESS).
