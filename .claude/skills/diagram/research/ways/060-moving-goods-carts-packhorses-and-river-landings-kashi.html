Moving goods: carts, packhorses and river landings (kashi)

The tax rice of China and Japan went by water. In Ming China the tax rice went up the Grand Canal to the capital, and in Edo Japan it went to Edo and Osaka from river landings (kashi) built to carry it. Overland a load went on a packhorse's back, about 135 kg (~298 lb) to a horse, and Japan kept its carts to the towns and off its highways (Village lanes).

This setting differs. Its wagons and carts travel the roads between towns, and its canals are dug only by a domain with an inland river worth canalizing.

  • In Edo, goods moved by hand cart, horse and ox cart.
    A Japanese government reference panel on Edo's streets, which cites no sources, names those three.
  • Osaka used a cart of its own, not the Edo hand cart.
    A Japanese encyclopedia says the Edo hand cart, the daihachiguruma, was not used in Osaka, which used the beka-guruma, with disc wheels and a plank bed.
  • The Edo hand cart's bed was about 8 by 2.5 ft.
    It was 8 shaku long and 2 shaku 5 sun wide, a sun being a tenth of a shaku.
  • A packhorse's load was thirty-six kan, about 135 kg (~298 lb).
    That was the Edo-period rule for the da, the unit of what one horse carried on its back.
  • A hauler led three or four horses at once.
    In the chuma hauling trade one man ordinarily led three or four horses and carried around 100 kan, about 375 kg (~827 lb), at a time.
  • Ming China's tribute rice went by the Grand Canal.
    The grain was a tax levied in the lower Yangtze provinces and shipped along the canal to the capital. In 1472 eight provinces were ordered to send four million shi of rice a year to Tongzhou, the canal's terminal near Beijing, and as Beijing's demand grew, carriage by sea was given up for the canal.
  • Japan's tax rice went by river, from landings built for it.
    When the Edo period began, the shogunate and the domains improved the rivers on a large scale, and new landings were set up under those lords to carry the tax rice and other goods from the provinces to Edo and Osaka.
  • A town's river landing grew into a commercial settlement of its own.
    In the Edo period wholesale merchants and their storehouses gathered at the landings. What a town's landing held is at Wharves and landings: piers, quays and stepped landings (kashi, gangi).

The setting departs from both countries on the road and on the canal.

  • In this setting, wagons and carts use the roads between towns.
    The setting's notes have a caravan's wagons of tea crossing a bridge, a charcoal merchant robbed of a cartload on the road, and hay hauled in wagons behind teams of oxen. Carts on the highways are the setting's own departure from Edo Japan.
  • In this setting, a canal is a domain's work, never the Empire's.
    A domain has canals only where it has a sizable inland river worth canalizing, as the Lion's canal from Toshi Ranbo to the Drowned Merchant River, while a coastal clan such as the Crane moves its goods by sea and carts them only the last few miles. Unlike Ming China, the setting has no canal the empire keeps for its tax rice.
