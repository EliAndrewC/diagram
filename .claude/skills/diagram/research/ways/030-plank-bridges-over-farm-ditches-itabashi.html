Plank bridges over farm ditches (itabashi)

A plank bridge, 板橋 (itabashi, "plank bridge"), is a bridge made of boards, and the Japanese word is on record from the early fourteenth century. Over a brook or a narrow stream, a crossing could be made in three ways: a single log or board laid across, logs laid side by side with earth trodden over them, or a planked deck. Over Japan's rivers, up to the Edo period (1603-1868), the earthen bridge was by far the commoner and the planked deck the rarer. Chinese pages, which describe the same objects, are read beside the Japanese ones.

The bunds between rice paddies also served as the paths field-workers walked, and a plank over a farm ditch is taken to be the board laid where such a path meets an irrigation ditch, so that field-workers walking the bunds could cross from paddy to paddy. That is a GUESS: no page we read, from any period, puts a plank on a paddy bund.

  • Plank bridges are centuries old in China and in Japan.
    A poem by Gu Kuang, of China's Tang dynasty (618-907), opens on someone crossing a plank bridge over a spring stream beside a mountain farmer's house. The Japanese word itabashi is first recorded in a poem by Fujiwara no Tameie, in an anthology of about 1310.
  • A single log or board laid across is the simplest crossing.
    A Japanese dictionary defines the log bridge as "a bridge that is just a single log laid across", and the one-log bridge as one laid "across a brook or mountain stream". The Chinese single-log bridge is the same object, made "of a single tree trunk or board" and used "to let walkers or livestock cross relatively narrow rivers and gorges", so one board laid across counts with one log rather than as a different thing.
  • An earthen bridge is a different object from a plank bridge.
    An earthen bridge, 土橋 (dobashi, "earth bridge"), is built of logs laid side by side with no gaps, then earth thrown over them and trodden down until it fills the hollows and the surface is level. The two are different things, not two names for one.
  • Was the earthen bridge the commonest kind of bridge in old Japan?
    For bridges over rivers, the one statement of proportion we read says so: "until the Edo period the overwhelming majority of bridges over Japan's rivers were earthen bridges", while "plank-decked bridges were limited to an important few". The same article says of a waterway spanned by many small bridges in a Hiroshige print that "small bridges of this kind were mostly built this way". But the statement is about river bridges, it says nothing of the single log, and the article cites no sources of its own.
  • Who laid a crossing over a farm ditch?
    The only account we read of a household bridging a farm channel is modern: a memoir of a Chinese rice village, criss-crossed by ditches, in which the grandfather "found four log sections and laid them across the canal as a simple timber bridge", a thing the writer calls "a common practice" there. The logs were slippery in the rain. That canal was 2-3 m (~7-10 ft) wide, several times a delivery ditch, so the memoir shows the habit of a farm household laying its own crossing, not what a village before the modern era laid over a narrow paddy ditch.
  • No page we read says what crossed a paddy ditch before the modern era.
    Nothing we read says, for a Japanese or a Chinese village, whether it was a plank, a log, or earth over logs, nor how often a crossing stood along a ditch.
  • This project takes an old farm ditch to have been about 2 shaku (~2 ft) wide.
    The old farm water channel may have been about 2 shaku, some 60 cm (~2 ft) wide, running beside farm paths of 3 shaku, the sanshaku-michi - but both widths are a GUESS of our own: no page we read gives either. No page we read gives the width of the smallest modern field ditch, or of its crossing, either.
  • No page we read says at what width a farm ditch was bridged rather than stepped across.
    A plank bridge is a thing a household builds and keeps up, and this project takes it that nobody builds one over water they can step across - a GUESS, since no page we read says so. Nothing we read says whether a farmer of the Edo period laid a board over a 2-shaku ditch or stepped across it.
