What vehicle used a village lane, and where could the lane run?

Sources: lowtech-chinese-wheelbarrow (what the Chinese rural network was built for, and who used it), daihachiguruma-jawiki (the Edo hand cart and the three places it was allowed), stone-routes-enwiki (the width of a purpose-built ox-cart route), toyama-1988-road-undevelopment (why the Japanese countryside had no carts, and what the highway prohibition was for), ctie-michi-nazenaze (the width of the Five Highways, and the wider figure near Edo), nippon-com-gokaido (a second, wider account of that width); the GM's rulings of 2026-07-21, 2026-08-12 and 2026-08-27. China leads here, as it does everywhere in this setting's geography, with Japan corroborating. The Chinese countryside was deliberately built for the one-wheeled barrow rather than for the cart: its rural network was "narrow paths, scarcely wide enough for foot passengers and wheelbarrows", with rough unpaved cart tracks predominating only on the eastern plains, which is not the rice-growing south our settlements are modeled on.2 Those paths were made for the traffic that used them - pedestrians, porters with carrying poles, pushers of wheelbarrows, and men carrying litters.3 So the vehicle to picture on a village lane is the wheelbarrow and the shoulder-pole porter, and the track is one barrow or one packhorse wide.26

Japan says the same thing from the other side. Up to the end of the Edo period the horse-drawn carriage did not exist there at all: inhabitants went on foot or on horseback, and goods moved by human power or on a horse's back.4 Carts were confined to the wide level streets inside the cities, and on the highways their use was forbidden to the very end of the shogunate - to keep the post-horse system alive, the fear being that more vehicles would mean fewer horses.15 Road wear is NOT the reason: the slow light carts a person could push damaged the surface little, and what is named instead for a country that never built a carriage road is its terrain and climate and the kind of horse it kept.16 Heavy freight went by boat.25 The one wheeled way that was built for carts sets a ceiling rather than a norm: the Edo hand cart (大八車, daihachiguruma) was confined by law to Edo, Owari and Sunpu,5 and the purpose-built ox-cart stone route was 2.7 m wide - 9 shaku, about 9 ft - with a 90 cm track laid for the beast itself.6 The trunk highways were wider again: about 5.4 to 7.2 m (3 to 4 ken) as the ordinary width, with mountain roads expressly left out of that figure, and 9 m (5 ken) where the road ran close to Edo.7 A second account puts the ordinary width at 4 ken (7.2 m) rising in places to 7 or 8 ken (12.6 to 14.4 m), so the two readable accounts bracket the width rather than agree on it.17 What a mountain road narrowed to, and the 2 ken often given for the Hakone barrier, are on no page that was read.18 Paving followed the same split: China had many narrow stone-slab causeways, especially in the wet rice south where a paved way keeps porters and barrows above the mud, while Japan paved only its highways and only on steep passes.8 In neither country was an ordinary village lane a wide two-lane road.

What the record does not give is a number. A search pass of 2026-08-27, made at the GM's request - "I just want to make sure that we did not set it arbitrarily" - read twenty-three pages and found no numeric width for an ordinary village lane or farm path in any language. A figure that circulates as "a rural way is one ken wide" traces to a page that does not say it, and neither the Japanese article on rural ways nor a prefectural authority's own list of questions about them gives a width. So the widths on these maps - 3 ft for a footpath, 5 ft for the cluster's spine and its field spur, 6 ft for the track out to the wider world, at the hamlet's scale of one foot to the pixel - are a map drawing convention, chosen inside the bounds the record does give (wider than nothing, narrower than the 9 ft cart road) and neither corroborated nor contradicted by a measured lane. A survey of a surviving hamlet lane would settle it.

Where a lane may run was ruled on the map. A trodden path never crosses row crops: historically it runs on the baulk between plots or along the edge of the hem, and the GM ruled so on 2026-07-21 after looking at a village's west field spur.23 It may touch a plot's boundary, since paths hug field margins by design. A limitation was accepted with it: where a staircase of dry plots blocks the way to the paddy and no clear baulk exists, the field track simply stops at the hem's edge, because past it farmers walk the plot baulks.24 The alternative - routing a detour around the foot of the staircase - was drawn and measured, and it disturbed the farmstead layout on every seed of the map it was tried on, while the hem-edge stop left that layout untouched.

The other ruling concerns wet ground. The GM, on 2026-08-12: "there's supposed to be a rule that paths don't pass through marshland". None of these maps draws a causeway, so a track does not cross the reed toe - but it does not have to turn away over the settlement's back either, because the wet toe is only as wide as the ground the fan waters (see the water page), leaving dry footslope at both of its lateral ends.

Two further questions about lanes are answered on the homesteads page: whether every farmhouse is reached by a lane, and in what form, and how a village lane bends.

The rule the map follows: a village lane is a narrow single track of packed earth with a soft worn shoulder and no marking down its middle, never a wide two-lane road. At the hamlet's scale the map draws a footpath 3 ft wide, the spine and the field spur 5 ft, and the connecting track 6 ft; those three widths are a map drawing convention, not a measured figure. A lane is a corridor nothing is built on - farmsteads front it, and no corner of a house stands on its tread. It runs on the baulk between plots or along the hem's edge and never across row crops, and it stops at the flooded paddy, since from there the way into the fields is on foot along the bunds. An important inter-village or pilgrimage path may be a narrow stone causeway; a modest settlement's own lanes are earth.

The rule the map follows: the track that links the settlement to the wider world continues out of the cluster and runs off the edge of the sheet. It never stops in mid-landscape, which reads as a path to nowhere, and it carries no label, because only an Imperial road is labeled. It keeps off wet ground: it leaves downslope past the end of the wet toe, on the flank the cluster already sits on, and turns along the contour only where the fan reaches the frame and leaves no dry ground to pass by. The field-ward spur and the road-ward connector are the two ends of one through-route. The settlement's own inner lanes are exempt from the off-the-edge rule: they legitimately end in the cluster or at the paddy.
