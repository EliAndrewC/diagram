What is a town street made of, and does it carry a drain?

Sources: mlit-gokaido-seibi (Edo's streets in the rain), yidao-zhwiki (a stone-slab official road through a Chinese post station), l7r-budgets (the GM's setting notes: the stonework of the Imperial roads' upkeep), shukubamachi-kotobank (the watercourse down a post town's street), mizu-edo-gesui (the eaves ditch along the front street, and the ditch before each house), roji-jawiki (the ditch under the back alley's boards), yichang-drains-hubei (a Chinese walled city's covered drains)

The surface. A Japanese town's street was earth. A writer of about 1630 complained that Edo's streets, after a little rain, were deep in mud and hard to travel, and people went about in high clogs. In China an official road could be paved where it ran through a post station: the 1.7 km that survives at Yunnanyi in Yunnan is a road of bluestone slabs, built to about one zhang wide, enough for two horses to pass. In the setting the Imperial roads are kept to a heavy standard, with stone-cutters among the paid labor. Whether an ordinary Chinese town's own streets were paved is on no page that was read.

The drain. A town street did carry water, in one of three forms. In a post town a watercourse was laid down the middle of the road or along its edge, for fire protection and to water men and horses. In Edo the ditch that took the rain off the eaves ran along the front streets, and the ditch before a house was 7 to 8 sun (about 9 in) wide, one stride over; a back tenement's kitchen water ran into the ditch outside, and that ditch on into the front street's drain. The back alley carried its own, a dobu 6 to 7 sun wide under boards laid down its middle. A Chinese walled city could put its drains underground: Yichang in the 1860s had five main drains and a number of small ones, every one covered, built of brick or stone with stone lids that carts, horses and people crossed. How wide a post town's channel ran is given on no page that was read.

So the map draws a town's streets as earth, which is accurate. The Imperial road's stretch through a town is either earth or stone slab, rolled per settlement, since both are attested for an official road and the setting pays for stonework on its roads; the town's own streets stay earth. The street's drain is one of three distinct forms, rolled per settlement: an open channel down the middle of the street, an open ditch along its edge under the eaves, or a covered drain under stone lids. A city's street surface and its alleys are the city fabric page's question.

The rule the map follows: a town street is drawn as earth. The Imperial road through a town is drawn as earth or as stone slab, rolled per settlement. Each town rolls one drain form for its main street: an open channel down its middle, an open ditch about 9 in wide along each edge at the house fronts, or no visible channel at all where the drain runs covered under stone lids; a back alley's ditch is not drawn. The width of a middle channel is a guess.
