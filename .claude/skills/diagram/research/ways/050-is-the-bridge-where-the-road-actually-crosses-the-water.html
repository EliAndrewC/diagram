Is the bridge where the road actually crosses the water?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-27; this project's measurement of every deck on the two drawn provincial cities; pingjiang-tu (the bridges on the 1229 plan of Suzhou); higashiyokobori-jawiki (the bridges over one Osaka canal); wumenqiao-zhwiki (the bridge at Suzhou's Pan Gate)

Yes. The GM, looking at a city map on 2026-07-27: "the bridge by the cargo basin is NOT aligned with the road, which results in not only the bridge being crooked but the road just running over the water". A deck that stands near its crossing but at the wrong angle is precisely what a reader sees as a road running through the water past a crooked plank, so nearness alone is not enough: the deck has to lie on the crossing and run along the way.

A deck typed at design coordinates drifts away from its crossing as soon as the way it belongs to is re-derived - by tens of feet and tens of degrees (about 50 ft and 39 degrees on the worst deck measured), which is what a reader sees as a road running through the water - so a crossing is solved from the water and the way rather than placed by hand.

The tolerance comes from measuring the difference. Every deck the engine solves lands within about a yard and a degree of its crossing - rounding, and nothing else - so asking for roughly 24 ft and 8 degrees separates a solved deck from an eyeballed one by two orders of magnitude, with no honest deck anywhere near the line.

That a town's streets cross its canals on bridges, and on many of them, is how canal cities were built. The 1229 stone plan of Suzhou marks 359 bridges, and a single Osaka canal about 3 km long, dug in the late sixteenth century, is crossed by 14 bridges on today's list. A road meeting a river too wide to bridge, away from a town, is another case: it was often ferried or forded, and Where a road meets a river with no bridge answers it.

One adjacency that looks like a drawing error is not. Where a cargo canal enters a walled city through a water gate, the ring road necessarily crosses it a few paces inside the arch, because the ring runs at a fixed inset from the rampart, so the deck and the water gate share ground. A bridge standing hard by a city's canal gate is attested: at Suzhou's Pan Gate the Wumen Bridge, first built in the Northern Song and rebuilt in stone in 1872, carries the main way in and out of the gate over the Grand Canal, beside the gate tower. That bridge stands outside the wall, though; that a road ran along the inside of a Chinese city's wall and bridged each canal just inside its water gate is found on no page read, so the inside crossing is this project's geometry, a GUESS as history. The overlap is deliberate, and shortening the deck does not separate the two.

The rule the map follows: wherever a way - a road, a ring road, a street or a lane - crosses a watercourse, a railed timber deck carries it over, drawn above both the water and the roadbed and oriented along the way. Every such crossing is bridged: a way does not simply run through open water. The one exception is a road meeting a river too wide to bridge, which ends at a ferry landing or runs into a ford by the rule of Where a road meets a river with no bridge. A way that merely runs alongside water needs no bridge, and a buried conduit recorded only for the water's topology is not a crossing at all, since there is no seam on the ground. Each deck lies on its crossing, within a few paces of it, and runs along its way within 8 degrees; a deck with no crossing beneath it does not belong on the map. Standalone footplanks are outside this rule - no way carries them, and they cross their ditch square by construction.
