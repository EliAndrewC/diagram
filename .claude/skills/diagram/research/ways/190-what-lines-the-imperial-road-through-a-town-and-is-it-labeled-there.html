What lines the Imperial road through a town, and is it labeled there?

Sources: shukubamachi-kotobank (the order of a post town's buildings from its middle outward, and how fully they fronted the road), shukuba-jawiki (the inns, lodgings, teahouses and shops, and the notice board), oiso-shukuba-mlit (one post town's count of lodgings for lords and inns), l7r-budgets (the GM's setting notes: who keeps a county's minor roads)

Where the highway ran through a post town, the town was built along it in order from the middle out. The transport office and the lodgings for lords, the honjin and waki-honjin, stood near the middle; before and behind them came the inns, teahouses and cookshops for ordinary travelers, with the porters' and packhorse offices; and farther out stood the merchants and craftsmen. Inns, cheap lodgings, teahouses and shops stood in rows, and a place for the notice boards was set up in the town. In 1803 Oiso on the Tokaido had three honjin and eighty-five inns of every size. How fully the road was built up followed its traffic: on the busy highways each lot's main house was built across its whole frontage on the road, while on byways and at mountain stations the inns often kept a front garden toward the road and gaps from their neighbors.

The Imperial road is the setting's busiest road, so where it runs through a town the town lines it as the busy highways were lined: both sides built up without gaps, the lodgings for officials and the inns toward the middle, shops and trades toward the ends. That is accurate. Where the notice board stands is the urban features page's question, and what a city puts along the same road is the city fabric page's.

The label "Imperial Road" goes beyond the town's built edge, never on its street. In the setting a county's minor road and bridge repairs are the county's own business, and this record reads the stretch of the road inside a town as the town's street, as it reads the stretch inside a city's walls. A city's label is placed by the same reasoning, which is the city fabric page's question.

The rule the map follows: where the Imperial road runs through a town it is built up on both sides with no gaps, the lodgings for officials and the larger inns near the middle of the built stretch, the smaller inns, teahouses and shops around them, and the trades toward the ends. The label "Imperial Road" is placed on the road beyond the town's built edge.
