Water: the research behind the flow, channel and wetland rules

The findings behind the water on these maps - the flow, the channels, the moats and the wetlands - the decisions they drove, and - where no generator draws the feature yet - the rules a map follows.

Anchors: headwater streams cluster near ~0.32 m; Himeji's moats average ~20 m (max 34.5 m); Osaka's outer moat is ~70-90 m (unsourced); tameike reservoirs run ~3-26 ha (~150-600 m across) (unsourced). A field ditch is ~1/300 of the 1-cho (~100 m) paddy it feeds2 - at true scale a hairline, essentially invisible.

The stroke convention, and what it is not (the GM's ruling of 2026-07-21). Watercourses on these maps are LINEWORK, and the ladder above spans about 250 to 1 in true width - about 1,700 to 1 once a reservoir is counted. That cannot be drawn on one sheet without the finest ditch disappearing: a 1 ft ditch at provincial-city scale is a third of a pixel. So the thin end is drawn true-width-or-floored - the field ditch parks at a minimum-visibility floor rather than at its true hairline - while the honesty anchors on the LARGER features, which draw true: a city moat is drawn at its real 66 ft, the low end of the provincial-seat band in the table above. That floor is a sanctioned minimum visibility and never a size license. What always survives it is the ORDERING and the coarse steps: a hairline ditch at the floor (about 2.5 ft on a hamlet sheet), a pond feeder at roughly twice that, a village creek at roughly three and a half times it, a stream feeding a moat drawn as wide as the moat itself - the water that enters has to be carried - and the city moat heaviest of everything on the sheet. A pond is areal: it is drawn as a polygon at the scale of the paddies beside it and never sits on the line-width ladder at all; what sits on the ladder is its outlet. A map drawing convention, disclosed - and what is held is the ordering and the size of the gaps between tiers rather than any exact width, so the ladder can never collapse back into one undifferentiated blue line.

A channel taper is a SQUARE ROOT, not a straight line

Sources: lacey-regime,tabayashi-1987, nougyoudoboku-suikou

What prompted it (GM 2026-08-17). The GM asked whether the supply ditches were right to thin as they feed the paddies (they are - that is Tabayashi's rule, already encoded),35 and then whether it is correct for one to "reach what looks like the current minimum width and then just stop, or if they should continue getting thinner for some time" - and whether the widths were researched at all or invented. The taper RULE is Tabayashi's; the taper's SHAPE is what this entry examines.

What the research found. Two relations, and between them they fix the profile completely.

  • Width goes as the square root of discharge. Lacey's regime equation for an unlined alluvial canal - which is exactly what these are - gives wetted perimeter P = 4.75 * sqrt(Q), and the regime power laws put width at Q^0.5 against depth Q^0.33 and velocity Q^0.1736.
  • Discharge falls linearly along the run. A delivery ditch sheds water through one mizuguchi per plot37 into a row of near-equal paddies, so it loses about the same flow per unit length; the collector gathers tail-water the same way in reverse. Uniform shedding over a run is Q linear in the fraction traveled.

The decision it drove. The width SQUARED interpolates linearly along the run, because the width squared is what tracks the discharge.

Why not a straight line. Interpolating the WIDTH linearly quietly asserts Q proportional to w, which is a different law and not one anything in the hierarchy obeys. It also looks wrong in exactly the way the GM caught: a straight line spreads the whole narrowing evenly along the run, so the stroke thins imperceptibly for its entire length and then stops dead at a still-substantial width. Under the true law the ditch keeps most of its working width while it still has most of its water to deliver, and then dwindles hard over the last stretch - which is what "the water is leaving it" ought to look like. At the widths these maps now draw, that shape is data rather than something the eye can follow. Measured on Inashiro's delivery ditches (8.0 -> 3.0 ft):

fraction along 0 0.25 0.5 0.75 0.9 1.0
linear 8.00 6.75 5.50 4.25 3.50 3.00
square root 8.00 7.09 6.04 4.77 3.81 3.00

The same law runs the collector in reverse (3.0 -> 12.0 ft: 8.75 rather than 7.50 at mid-run), so a drain now visibly gains body in its first stretch, which is where a collector picks up the largest share of its catchment. It barely moves a SHALLOW taper - canal A's 12.4 -> 10.1 ft pieces shift by under 0.1 ft - which is the right behavior: the law only bites where a stroke really is shedding most of what it carries.

What this entry settles, and what it leaves to others. This fixes the taper's SHAPE and the tier ORDERING; the absolute widths are the subject of The comb net is drawn at TRUE SIZE below, and a delivery drawn wider than the canal feeding it is resolved in A delivery is never drawn wider than the canal feeding it.

Where the drawn net STOPS - the tier below the last ditch we can draw

Sources: nougyoudoboku-matsutan, tabayashi-1987

The question (GM 2026-08-17). Should a delivery ditch keep thinning past its drawn minimum - taper away to a point - rather than ending at a visible width?

What the research found: no, and the reason is that there is a whole further TIER, not a vanishing. Both reference traditions terminate the fixed net at a channel with a real working cross-section, and then hand over to something that is not a channel at all.

  • The tier ladder is explicit and finite. China names five fixed grades - 干渠 / 支渠 / 斗渠 / 农渠 / 毛渠 (main / branch / lateral / farm / field), the maoqu being "the last grade of fixed channel, the small ditch that carries water into each individual plot".38 Japan's design vocabulary is the same shape with39 three: 幹線用水路 -> 支線用水路 -> 末端用水路 (matsutan yosuiro, the terminal channel), mirrored on the drainage side as 末端 -> 支線 -> 幹線排水路 (terminal -> branch -> trunk drainage canal) (unsourced). A terminal channel is not a taper's vanishing point; it is a designed object with a section sized to peak demand,40 its bed set -5 to +10 cm against the paddy surface3 so a farmer can both run water in and get a foot in it to clean it (unsourced).
  • The finest tier is far below our visibility floor. The ladder above puts a field ditch at ~0.3 m - about 1 ft, i.e. one pixel at hamlet scale and a third of a pixel at city scale. The tier we actually stop at, ~1 m / ~3 ft, is the distribution lateral, and drawing one grade finer would put the stroke below the sanctioned minimum-visibility floor - about 2.5 ft at hamlet scale - and collapse the hierarchy the water-width ladder exists to keep legible.
  • And in a PRE-MODERN system the last tier is frequently not a channel at all. Before land consolidation, Japanese paddy was watered 田越し灌漑 (tagoshi kangai) - plot-to-plot: water enters the head paddy of a string and cascades down through the aze from one plot to the next, so the interior plots have no ditch of their own to draw (unsourced). Tabayashi records the same relationship from the tenure side: the ditches that bring water to the plot inlets "are often considered parts of the paddy fields they serve".41 The maps already draw this: a column of plots cascades several to ~10 rows past where its ditch stops - so the drawn ditch ending mid-field IS the handoff to the cascade, not a line that was left unfinished.

The decision it drove. Keep the floor: the finest DRAWN channel ends at the lateral tier (~3 ft) with a blunt end, because that is a real terminal channel handing its flow to a mizuguchi and a cascade, and both of those are below what this sheet can render. The taper law above is what makes that ending read correctly - the stroke arrives at the floor by dwindling.

The bund runs along the channel bank, and the mizuguchi is too small to draw

Sources: nougyoudoboku-suikou, aze-standard

The question (GM 2026-08-17). We draw the earthen bunds running along the banks of the irrigation channels. Is that correct - and is the water reaching the paddies through gaps too small to render?

What the research found: yes to both, and the second is precisely quantified.

  • A continuous bund along the channel is right. The paddy's own aze IS the channel's bank - a basin has to be watertight on every side, and the side that faces the ditch is no exception, or the plot would simply drain into it. Japanese design puts the terminal channel's bed at -5 to +10 cm relative to the field surface with the bund between them,42 which only works if the bund is unbroken except where an opening is deliberately made. The attested pre-modern case says the same: at Toro the canal and the bunds were revetted together with the same yaita sheet boards, i.e. a bank and a bund built as one structure (unsourced).
  • The opening is the mizuguchi (水口), and it is sub-pixel. Standard practice is one intake per plot (a second only when they would stand more than 50 m apart),43 of width within 50 cm4, its sill 0-10 cm above the field surface so the inflow does not scour the bed,44 passing water at about 0.4 m/s.5 The plot's outlet - the shirimito (水尻 / 落水口), an overflow set 5-10 cm below the field surface45 - is the same size (unsourced). At the hamlet scale of 1 ft per pixel a 50 cm notch is 0.5 px wide; at provincial-city scale (3 ft/px) it is 0.17 px.
  • For calibration, the bund itself is standard too. The outer aze is a trapezoid of 30 cm top width and 30 cm height at 1:1 side slopes - so ~1 ft across the crest and ~3 ft at the base46 (colder regions go to ~50 cm x 40 cm for deep-water irrigation and frost heave; unsourced). Our drawn bund width of ~1.5 real ft sits between the crest and the base, which is the honest way to draw a trapezoid as one line.

The decision it drove (a deliberate NON-render). The mizuguchi and shirimito are never drawn on any settlement map. A 0.5 px notch cannot be rendered, and a legible one would be a ~6x size inflation on the most numerous feature on the map - one per paddy, on a map with hundreds. The bund is therefore drawn CONTINUOUS along every channel bank, and the reader is expected to understand that water crosses it through openings below the resolution of the sheet, exactly as they understand that a drawn farmhouse has a door. The unbroken bund is not a claim that the paddies are unwatered.

The comb net is drawn at TRUE SIZE

Sources: lacey-regime,fao-paddy-duty

What prompted it. Having sourced the taper law, the same sources let the ABSOLUTE widths be priced. The answer was that the net was about 5-6x oversize: Inashiro's 20.9-acre (~8.5 ha) fan carries roughly 0.025 m3/s at the puddling peak,47 for which Lacey gives a wetted perimeter near 0.76 m6 and Manning a ~0.6 m top width on a hand-dug 1:1 earthen section - call it 2.5 to 5 ft depending on how much freeboard and bank you count as "the channel". The GM's ruling (2026-08-17), given the measurement: "we can narrow the widths to be more realistic... update the net to be actual size (at least for hamlets)... it sounds like we want to keep things absolute scale."

How each figure was set. The attested tier ladder is primary and the hydraulic calculation only corroborates it, deliberately: the tiers are measurements of real channels, while a Manning figure rests on four assumed parameters (roughness, grade, section aspect, freeboard) and moves by a factor of two if you nudge them. So each stroke is placed on the attested ladder by the COMMAND AREA it serves - a field ditch watering one paddy ~0.3 m, a distribution lateral ~1 m (unsourced), a district main (yosui) ~5 m48 - and a hamlet fan of 8.5 ha lands its head-race between the lateral and small-main tiers. The result, in true feet:

stroke true width tier it sits on
head-race (whole fan, above the fork) 6.0 small main
canal A (high margin) 4.5 -> 1.5 main dwindling to lateral
canal B (far margin) 4.0 -> 1.5 branch dwindling to lateral
delivery ditch 2.5 -> 1.2 lateral dwindling to field ditch
drain / akusui 1.2 -> 5.5 field ditch gathering to a branch drain

Where the head-race sits on that ladder, and why it is 6.0 ft rather than 5.0. A fan of this size lands its trunk between the lateral and the small-main tiers, which is a band rather than a point, and the first figure taken from it was 5.0 ft. At 5.0 the top THREE tiers sat within a foot of each other, so the one junction where the hierarchy most wants to read - the division point where the trunk forks into its two arms - was where it read least. The trunk carries both arms, and under the width-goes-as- the-square-root-of-discharge law this record already adopts, a trunk carrying a 4.5 ft arm and a 4.0 ft arm is the root of the sum of their squares: 6.0 ft. That is TIER SELECTION within the attested band, using the law as a sanity check on where the trunk sits - not conservation at junctions, which is separately ruled against (drawn width is RANK, not discharge: never chain drawn widths down from the source). It also has a real referent: a sluice-fed head-race genuinely is wider and slower than the water feeding it, ponding above the weir.

A collector is sized for drawdown plus storm runoff rather than for the irrigation duty, so it may carry appreciably more water than the supply ever delivered - a reading of the hydrology, not a sourced figure. The maps do not draw it that way: the drain's tail is 5.5 ft against a 6.0 ft head race, so the collector is the wider stroke only against the ditches it collects from, not against the race at the head. Raising the tail above the race was tried and MEASURED on the reference hamlet: the wider band takes the margin the settlement stands on, and the cluster came to rest 102 ft from the field it works, against a 60 ft bar. The figure is left where it is and the relationship is stated as drawn.

The decision, and the one place SCALE enters. Widths are stored in FEET and converted at draw time so the same real channel is the same real size on every sheet - which is what "to-scale" has always meant for every other feature on these maps. The single scale-dependent figure is the minimum channel width, 1.5 px: true widths are honest at hamlet and village resolution and vanish above it (at a provincial city's 3 ft/px the terminal tier is 0.4 px, which is not a line), so a stroke draws at its true width or the floor, whichever is larger. The coarser the sheet, the more of the ladder collapses onto that floor. This is the honest form of the minimum-visibility convention the water-width ladder already sanctions - a floor on the finest tier.

Measured on Inashiro, which draws at a foot to the pixel. Head-race 6.0 ft; canal A 4.5; delivery ditches 2.5 at the head and 1.5 at the tail; drain outfall 5.5. The paddy fabric dominates the sheet and the water reads as a fine engineered net over it, rather than as blue ropes laid across the crop. The hierarchy survives the narrowing (6.0 > 4.5 > 4.0 > 2.5 > 1.5 ft, a 4:1 span) because the floor sits below the finest tier rather than on top of it.

The departure we are taking knowingly. The floor is a real, if small, inflation of the finest tier at hamlet scale: a 1.2 ft delivery tail draws at 1.5 px rather than 1.2. The GM sanctioned ~2 px at the narrowest point; 1.5 was chosen instead precisely so the finest tier still SHOWS a taper rather than arriving pre-flattened at the floor - at 2.0 a delivery ditch would run 2.5 -> 2.0 px and the taper work above would be invisible on the very tier it matters most for. Re-inflating the FEET to make a coarse-scale map read better would lie about every scale to fix one.

A delivery is never drawn wider than the canal feeding it

Sources: none - this is a legibility rule following from the width-as-rank convention, not a finding about real channels

Why it matters more than it looks. Drawn width is RANK, not discharge - that ruling says the in-and-out widths at a junction are not meant to add up, because width conveys position in the hierarchy. A child drawn wider than its parent breaks the ONE thing the convention is for, which is why this is a defect where an unbalanced junction is not.

The decision. A delivery's head is capped at 0.8 of its parent's LOCAL width at the takeoff, and a mid-block sub-ditch at 0.75 of the delivery it branches off. The cap only bites low in the tree, where the parent has already dwindled - at the first offtake the delivery keeps its full tier.

What was deliberately NOT done. Deriving the delivery's width from the flow the parent sheds at that offtake is the physically exact version and was rejected: it is conservation at junctions by another name, which the 2026-08-16 ruling forbids, and it makes a delivery's width depend on how steeply its parent happens to taper there. The cap is the weaker and sufficient guarantee. Measured on Inashiro: zero inversions, every delivery head (1.8-2.5 ft) below its parent's local width (2.2-4.5 ft).

One asymmetry, and it was measured rather than chosen. A delivery is capped against its canal's LOCAL width; a mid-block SUB-ditch is capped against its parent delivery's HEAD. Sizing the sub against the local width too is worse: at true scale a delivery is ~2.2 ft a third of the way down, so 0.75 of that is 1.64 ft, which against the visibility floor - 1.5 ft at hamlet scale - leaves a sub-ditch 0.14 ft of room to taper in. The taper rule (a tail under 0.85 of the head) rejected it on 22 of 24 test maps, and rightly - a ditch that cannot taper should not be drawn claiming to.

What drawing at TRUE SIZE left open

Sources: none - these are consequences of the true-size decision

Narrowing the net by 5-6x changed what several neighboring rules were tuned against. Each of these is measured.

  • The dry-hem stand-off is DERIVED from the canal's bank. It is the stroke's LOCAL half-width at each hem boundary point plus a 5.0 ft berm: a ~1 m embankment top, which is GB50288's (China's national irrigation design standard) minimum for a lateral/farm canal,49 plus room to stand for the annual dredging. Measured: bare berm 4.89 ft median, held unbroken over a 1,592 ft chain, with the residue at the fork recorded below.

What remains, measured and ACCEPTED. Three hem corners at the bunsuiguchi keep less than the full berm - 2.00 ft, 2.19 and 2.90 - against a 4.89 ft median held over the whole 1,592 ft chain. Geometric rather than a bug: the stand-off runs along ONE canal's normal and cannot clear a stroke crossing at an angle, and a fork is where three strokes meet. Invisible at fit zoom, and a review judged it shippable.

The taper is SUB-PERCEPTUAL at true scale - RULED ON, not merely noticed. The GM looked at the finished map and asked the right question: "Is that getting narrower as it goes down into the fields, or along the edge at the top, though? It doesn't visually look like it is." It is not. What matters here is the GRADIENT, not the ratio, and the two had been quietly conflated:

stroke run width ft lost per 100 ft
canal A (the top edge) 1,504 ft 4.5 -> 1.5 0.20
delivery ditch (down into the fields) ~500 ft 2.5 -> 1.5 0.20
canal B (short west arm) 343 ft 4.0 -> 1.5 0.73
a delivery drawn ~3x oversize 540 ft 8.0 -> 3.0 0.93

So a delivery sheds ONE FOOT of width over five hundred, and canal A three over fifteen hundred. The ink is monotone and the head-to-tail ratio is honest, so the two ENDS of a canal differ plainly side by side - but there is no gradient to follow along a stroke, and canal B is the only one that reads, because it is short. Canal A's gradient is identical to a delivery's; only its total drop is larger.

Why this is the honest outcome rather than a defect. A real ditch narrowing from ~1.4 m to ~0.45 m over 460 m is not visually dramatic from above either. A taper the eye can follow comes from inflating the net, not from the taper law - so true absolute size and a visible taper are in genuine tension and no amount of engineering dissolves it.

The decision, with the alternatives priced. Both were put to the GM against keeping true size: x1.5 (delivery 3.75 -> 1.8, gradient 0.39) and x2 (5.0 -> 2.4, gradient 0.52), each a single documented multiplier preserving every relative size. The GM chose true size. Recorded here so a future reader meets a deliberate trade rather than an apparent bug.

A plank is laid only over water you cannot stride across. A plank bridge is a real object a household builds and maintains, and nobody builds one over something they step over.

The line is judged on the ditch's WIDEST point (a run wide at its head and a thread at its tail is worth a crossing somewhere, and the plank is seated where the water is wide enough for one).

RULED 2026-08-26 (GM): the line is 2.0 ft. The GM asked of the reference hamlet: "I think there should probably be plank bridges over the irrigated channels in a few places. Right? Or are these too narrow for that? They don't look too narrow. Is it intentional that they're missing or an oversight?" The research pass (web, Japanese and English, six queries): the traditional farm-level water channel is 2 shaku, ~60 cm (farm paths 3 shaku, the sanshaku-michi), which is precisely the width of our delivery ditches (2.5 -> 1.2 ft); modern ほ場整備 standard drawings size the smallest field ditches at 30-60 cm and carry a crossing at every plot entrance, but that is machinery-era practice; and NOTHING found says whether an Edo farmer laid a board over a 2-shaku ditch or stepped it. The GM had already ruled on the map. So the line is 2.0 ft: the standard farm ditch earns a board, the ~1.2-1.5 ft tails do not. On Inashiro: 10 planks - one per delivery branch (2.0 / 2.0 / 1.95 ft of water, seated just below the branch heads because the head itself is a confluence) and two more down the main (2.8 / 2.4 ft); the two 1.8 ft branches stay unplanked. The three branch planks sit at the TAIL of each branch's qualifying run, 160-250 ft below the head, each on a bund crossing mid-field - accepted, since a head seat would stand ~120 ft from the canal plank at the junction. The parallel pair at x~2225/2240 has one branch planked and its twin 12 ft away not, the price of two deliveries running 5-25 ft apart for 300 ft. And at 2.7 ft of drawn width against 3.0-3.7, the unplanked 1.8 ft branches are indistinguishable from the planked ones on the page: the map shows some branches with a crossing and some without.

ACCEPTED (not fixed) 2026-08-17: the visibility floor lands exactly on the paddy bund's stroke width. The channel floor is 1.5 ft at hamlet grain and the bund is also 1.5, so the finest water tier is drawn at precisely the width of the field boundaries it runs among, separated only by hue. A floor of 1.2 ft was tried and rejected: the floor is not cosmetic - it also sets the clearance the bunds keep from a supply channel, so lowering it moves every bund that hems a supply stroke and re-welds the plot seams. A floor of 1.2 ft broke the paddy seams of two of twenty-four test maps that hold at 1.5. A cosmetic ambiguity does not buy two broken maps.

The bund width is 1.5 ft by its own research (a plain aze ran ~1-2 ft),50 so there is room to take the BUND to ~1.3 and leave the water alone - which changes a stroke width and touches no clearance at all.

Which way does the water run, and how does a map say so?

Sources: the GM's rulings of 2026-07-24 and 2026-07-25; this project's measurement of the drawn pool

Not from the picture. These maps carry no topographic markings at all, deliberately (the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25): they already hold a great deal of visual detail, and contour lines would add clutter faster than they add understanding. So the terrain exists nowhere on the sheet. It exists only in two figures declared for every map - the LAND'S FALL, the direction the ground slopes, and the DRAINAGE BEARING, the direction the water leaves - plus a direction carried by each watercourse. That is exactly why those declarations are mandatory rather than optional: they are the only place the terrain exists at all, and a reader cannot catch by eye what the data does not say. The omission is a map drawing convention, disclosed here because a reader may reasonably wonder where the hills are.

What the declarations are FOR: verisimilitude, not uniformity (the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25, and the standard every later map is held to). The goal is that a map reads like a real place of the kind that actually existed - not that its water all points one way. A map is never flattened into a single flow direction, because real landscapes are not, for two separate reasons. Local topography rises and falls at small scale, so neighboring fields legitimately drain different ways: Tango's fans span 210 degrees between them, and the farms round its moat differ by their own ground - some drain INTO the moat, others have paddies FED BY it. And most of these works are artificial: a ditch dug ALONG a slope rather than down it is precisely what is built to intercept downhill water and keep it out of somewhere, which is why the drainage standard quoted in The wet toe is as wide as the FAN puts a field-toe interceptor drain nearly along the contours. A contour-hugging channel is engineering with a purpose, not an error.

The one hard rule, and the anti-pattern it exists to kill. Water never gains elevation. The GM's named objection to the published maps of the game this setting is built on is a river drawn running parallel to a mountain range along its base - it looks superb and it is impossible. Water at the foot of a slope runs ALONG the valley that slope drains into, descending as it goes; a course that hugs a contour without descending is a canal, and a canal needs an engineered gradient and a reason to exist. So a watercourse running across the fall rather than down it is perfectly allowed - but the reason has to be sayable, and it is recorded: a collector gathering runoff along the low margin, an interception ditch protecting something, a navigation cut held at level. A course with no descent and no purpose is the mistake.

Two figures, not one, because slope and flow genuinely differ. The fall is the LAND'S; the drainage bearing is where the WATER leaves, and a valley floor runs across the fall of the valley sides it lies between, so the two diverge by design. Hoshizora's ground slopes south-southwest at 115 degrees while its valley stream runs 145; Hikari no Sato has a drain 88 degrees off its map's fall. Deriving the flow from the slope would force every stream straight down the fall line, which is wrong for exactly the valley-floor and contour-drain cases the pool already contains.

How far the two may diverge: up to, but never reaching, a right angle. A dug contour canal is laid nearly along the contours, deviating only enough to keep a working gradient - so a divergence approaching 90 degrees is the defining property of a dug channel rather than a defect. What is impossible is 90 degrees or more, which would give the water a net UPHILL component. The bound is therefore a strict "under 90" and not a tighter tolerance. A tighter one was drafted and declined: an earlier version of this rule proposed plus or minus 60 degrees, which would have condemned legitimate contour works. The widest real divergences in the drawn pool are 60 degrees (a map's bearing against its fall) and 69 (one course against its map's bearing), so nothing drawn comes near the bound.

The LIMIT of both angle rules, measured and ACCEPTED. Each of them compares a local fact - one map's fall, one course's heading - against a SINGLE map-level bearing, and confluence geometry can legitimately break that. A bank settlement whose land falls perpendicular into a trunk river that runs along it has a fall and a drainage bearing 90 degrees apart or more with nothing wrong anywhere, and Nagahara is exactly that shape. The rules are kept anyway, because the defect they actually catch is a REVERSED direction - a 180-degree error, which no legitimate geometry produces - and because no drawn map comes near the bound. What that costs: a future bank-sited map can trip one of them while being entirely correct. The alternative declined: loosening or dropping the bound, which would give up the reversed-direction catch that is the whole of their value. If a bank map ever does trip one, the bearing model is revisited; the map is not "fixed".

A known gap, ACCEPTED rather than closed: the two provincial cities declare no land fall. They declare a drainage bearing like every other map, and no fall, and that is deliberate - so every rule about drainage slope, which is conditioned on a declared fall, has never run on either city. Declaring a bearing turns all of them on and both cities fail. A sweep of every bearing at 10-degree steps found NO satisfying value for Tango and EXACTLY ONE for Nagahara - 350 degrees, the land falling east into the river - and that one is curve-fitting rather than terrain: two of Nagahara's own three comb fields declare falls of 100 and 95 degrees, southward, so a map-level east bearing contradicts the map's own geometry. What was accepted: the two largest maps stay unjudged on slope rather than carry a fictitious single bearing. What it costs: three rules that run on every village never run on either city. The alternative declined: adopting the 350 to turn them green.

The one watercourse with no downstream direction to declare. A cargo canal is cut at the level of the water it joins, which is what lets a barge be poled BOTH ways along it: its gradient is nil, a water gate holds it at river level, and a dead-end dock basin has no through-flow at all. Giving such a cut a downstream bearing would be a fiction (this rests on general reading; no source is cited)70.

The rule the map follows: Town and city tier, where no generator draws the settlement yet. Every map declares its land fall and its drainage bearing, and every watercourse on it declares which way its water runs; a moat declares an inlet and an outlet instead (below). A navigable canal declares that it is held at a LEVEL, and the drainage-bearing rule then skips it - Nagahara's canal runs 187 degrees against that map's 90-degree bearing and is correct, because it is a navigation cut and not a drain. No map carries contour lines or any other topographic marking.

Where does a field's drain let its water go?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25; this project's measurements of the drawn pool

Off the map, into a brook, or into a drainage pond - and WHICH END of the collector it leaves by is the question, because that is what says whether the water is running the right way. Each field carries its own fall and each drain is judged against its own field; but reading the discharge end off the geometry kept giving the wrong answer, and the four ways it did are recorded here.

The decision (the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25): every discharge channel NAMES the field it drains. A collector then matches its own discharge channels exactly, and nothing is inferred from position. The four positional shortcuts that were tried first, each failed by a real map: matching by PROXIMITY - Hirameki carries seven such channels and several of them sit on top of a DIFFERENT field's drain; keying on the channel's first point - misread six of Tango's ten drains; keying on its last point - broke Hirameki, because which end of a discharge channel touches the drain varies from map to map; and taking a fixed tail length off the drain itself - on a two-point drain that overshoots the drain's whole span and hands back the entire drain.

Read the SINK end, then pool all the evidence and take the LOWEST. A discharge may leave from a point partway along a collector - one map's west drain runs off the map edge from the middle of its run - so the informative end of a discharge channel is its sink: whichever of its two ends lies farther from the drain, falling back to the last point when both lie on it. And a collector legitimately has SEVERAL sinks: that same west drain also tees a relief culvert back to the stream at its far end, and another runs off the bottom of the sheet at BOTH ends. So every end carrying evidence of a discharge - a named channel, a brook alongside, the frame edge - is gathered, and the LOWEST of them is taken as the outfall. The rule then has teeth only on the case that matters: when EVERY piece of evidence puts the discharge at the drain's HIGH end, the water is running backwards.

An outfall at the sheet's edge must be at the DOWNHILL edge. Nearness to an edge is evidence of a discharge only when that edge is downhill. One city fan has its HEAD about 84 ft from the top of the frame - inside the 96 ft tolerance, and on the UPSLOPE side - so nearness alone would call that high end an outfall and report the water as running backwards. Nearness to a STREAM is evidence on its own, since a brook is a real sink wherever it lies.

A mirrored fan stamps the fall of the DRAWN geometry. Where a fan is laid out by negating the land's fall, building the comb and mirroring the points back, the two negations cancel and the fall that was actually drawn is the original value. Stamping the negated working figure instead put one city fan 90 degrees out.

And a TRIMMED in-wall drain is not a contour collector. A drainage ditch inside a city wall is cut off short of the patrol ring and dropped through a gate into an underground culvert, so what survives of it on the page is only the last leg to that outfall: one such drain is two points and about 400 ft long where every untrimmed drain on the same map runs 480 to 690 ft. Judging that stub's bearing against "a collector runs along the contour" is a category error, so a drain that was trimmed records the fact and is exempt from that rule.

Does a moat have a current?

Sources: the GM's catch of 2026-07-25; the junction-angle finding at Canal junction angles; this project's measurements of the two drawn provincial cities

Yes - but not one current. A ring has no upstream end: water enters at one point and leaves at another, and it travels BOTH ways round the circuit to get there. So a moat records an INLET and an OUTLET rather than a bearing, and "downstream of a moated city" is reasoned from those two points. An open, river-cut moat derives them for itself, because its two junction feet ARE the inlet and the outlet; a closed ring declares them, because the ring is dug before its feeder and its outfall exist.

Where a channel meets the ring, the junction is SWEPT with the current (the GM caught it by eye, 2026-07-25). An offtake leaves the moat pointing downstream and a tributary joins it pointing downstream: its heading AT the junction must carry a downstream component. A square tap sheds sediment into its own mouth, and on the page it says nothing at all about which way the water runs. The angle finding behind it is Canal junction angles.

Four things making it work taught, each measured, and each a trap worth keeping.

  • Measure the LOCAL segment, not the channel's net vector. The net vector is near arbitrary for an offtake that leaves the ring roughly perpendicular - which is why the coarse cardinal test below stays a separate and deliberately different question.
  • Use the AVERAGED vertex tangent. The tangent at a vertex of a ring is the chord between its two NEIGHBORS. Taking the forward segment alone tilts every reading by about half the vertex's turn angle - 8 to 9 degrees on these rings, consistently in one direction - which was enough to make every square tap look upstream-facing and sent a whole diagnosis down the wrong path.
  • Move only the MOAT-SIDE end. The attachment walks along the rim - upstream for an offtake, downstream for an arriving drain - until the throat is swept enough; the sluice does not move, so the field it feeds does not shift. Walk by ARC LENGTH and not by vertex: one vertex step is about 420 ft against a throat of about 90 ft, which overshoots into a channel running nearly parallel to the rim.
  • Cap the arriving walk at about 270 ft. Wander further and a culvert's far end lands nearer the drain's OTHER endpoint, flipping the outfall attribution that the drain's discharge end depends on - which is exactly what one city's north fan did.

What it caught. All five of Tango's offtakes and all four of Nagahara's met the ring square, at 86 to 93 degrees, because the tee had been built purely radially; and beyond those, one drain culvert doubling back to enter at 146 degrees and two more arriving at 124 and 98. After the regrade every offtake sits at 47 to 48 degrees and every drain between 14 and 60. The two junctions that were already correct moved 15 ft.

ONE direction model (the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25). A moat records an inlet and an outlet, and every rule about its current reads those two points - never a direction worked out again from the geometry alongside them, because two sources of truth for one fact is how drift starts.

Why the current is deliberately SNAPPED TO A CARDINAL, and not measured precisely. Modeling the ring exactly - taking the ring's tangent at each tap, in the direction of travel along that tap's own arc - was built, and it is the physically correct picture of the water. It was rejected because it makes the question meaningless. An irrigation offtake leaves the ring roughly PERPENDICULAR, so its component along a precisely measured tangent is near arbitrary: the exact version flagged one of Tango's taps and two of Nagahara's while sparing their neighbors on nothing more than which side of the tap the field happened to sit. The coarse hemisphere is what makes the test mean "the field is not back upstream". So the coarse form is kept on purpose; the fine version measures the wrong quantity. A map drawing convention in the honest sense - a simplification chosen because the precise measurement answers a different question.

The rule the map follows: Town and city tier, where no generator draws the settlement yet. A ring moat records an inlet and an outlet, never a single direction; an open, river-cut moat takes them from its two junctions with the river. Where a channel meets the moat, the channel's heading AT the junction carries a downstream component - an offtake leaving, a drain arriving - and a tap that meets the ring square is regraded by walking its MOAT-SIDE attachment along the rim by arc length until the throat is swept; the sluice and the field it feeds do not move. Separately and coarsely, an irrigation offtake must not leave the ring from a point back upstream of the ground it waters, judged against the current snapped to a cardinal.

A fed closed moat must drain - the physics and the precedent

Sources: fao-pond-water (evaporation and seepage as a reservoir's losses); the balance equation itself is textbook hydrology stated from principle, not quoted

  • The physics (the load-bearing "why"). Water balance at a stable level: outflow = inflow + rain - evaporation - seepage. With NO outlet, outflow = 0 forces inflow = evaporation + seepage; a real perennial stream delivers far more than a moat's surface can evaporate or its bed can seep,7 so storage climbs and the moat overtops its banks8 - "the level just keeps rising" is not a steady state, it is flooding. (A stream's upstream profile is set by its own bed slope, not the pond it enters - raising the moat only backs water up a short distance at the mouth, it cannot lift the whole feeder, so you cannot absorb continuous inflow by "raising the stream too.") The terminal-pond regime is real but belongs to a spring/rain-fed moat in an arid climate where small diffuse input is balanced by evaporation; a wet rice climate (modest evaporation; a high water table and puddled paddies that seal seepage) cannot absorb a live stream, so a stream-fed moat there MUST shed the surplus through a channel.

  • What the research found (China first: Beijing's gated water-passes + the Forbidden City moat; Japan agreeing: Edo/Matsue jokamachi moats).9 Flow-through was the standard, engineered deliberately. Beijing admitted water through gated wall passes, branched it into through-city rivers, and discharged SE to the Tonghui/Grand Canal; the Forbidden City moat is fed from the Jade Spring and "flows in from the NORTHWEST and discharges to the SE" - inlet one corner, outfall the diagonal. Flushing mattered for sanitation (a stagnant moat stank and bred summer fevers; the remedy was flow-through + annual dredging), for storm drainage (a city moat doubled as the flood sluice - a drain with no outfall is not a drain), for a fire-water reserve, and for irrigation offtake. The pond-like look is legitimately achieved by an overflow weir/sluice that holds a set level and sheds only the surplus (Beijing's gated behavior) - placid surface, real outlet underneath. A closed moat with a live feeder and no outlet is historically wrong.

The rule the map follows: City tier, where no generator draws the settlement yet. A closed ring moat that is FED must also drain. A second off-map stream leaves the ring on the face opposite the feeder, so the current flushes the circuit corner to corner - the Forbidden City arrangement described above - and it is drawn as wide as the feeder, because the water that comes in has to go out. The outfall leaves by the LOW, downstream rim. Irrigation taps to the farms around the city are a legitimate seasonal SECONDARY offtake and never a substitute for it: a 21 ft summer tap cannot balance a 66 ft year-round feeder. A moat with no feeder at all - spring- or rain-filled - needs no outfall, having no inflow to shed; and a river-cut open moat already has one, since it taps the river upstream and returns to it downstream.

The diverted-stream moat is a historical type

Sources: sotobori-jawiki (the Hirakawa relocated to make the moat; the entry's own word for the shape is 「the character の (the kana "no")」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「「の」の字」)69); the Forbidden City's NW-in / SE-out flush: no readable page supports the direction - a GUESS

  • The diverted-stream moat is itself a historical type (GM asked whether Tango's arrangement - a stream seemingly turned into the moat, then resuming its course - was real, 2026-07-23). It is, and commonly so: a dry-site Chinese seat dug its hucheng he by turning a nearby stream through the ring (the Forbidden City's NW-in / SE-out flush is the model Tango already encodes), and Japanese castle towns fed moats by the same river diversions (Edo turned the Hirakawa into its moat spiral).10 Two honesty notes, both deliberate: (1) no relic of the stream's pre-diversion bed is drawn through the city - after centuries of urban buildup the abandoned course is built over, so its absence is realistic, not an omission; (2) the ring splits the feeder's flow into two live arcs (west-about and east-about) that rejoin at the outfall, which is why irrigation taps on BOTH flanks can legitimately draw "with the current".

One name per river - and why that is anti-historical

Sources: chang-jiang-jawiki, shinano-gawa-jawiki, sumida-gawa-jawiki (Ryogoku-gawa, 両国川, is not on the page)

What the research found: one-river-many-names was the NORM in the pre-modern world, in both reference cultures. China: the Yangtze never had a single pre-modern name - its course ran Tuotuo He / Tongtian He / Jinsha Jiang,11 then regionally Chuan Jiang (Sichuan) / Jing Jiang (Hubei)12 / Xunyang Jiang (unsourced) / Yangzi Jiang (originally only the Nanjing-Shanghai reach; modern standardization settled on Chang Jiang).13 Japan: per-stretch renaming was standard practice - the Chikuma-gawa becomes the Shinano-gawa at the Shinano/Echigo provincial border,14 the Seta/Uji/Yodo system renames at each confluence, and within Edo itself one river carried different names neighborhood by neighborhood (Asakusa-gawa / Ryogoku-gawa / Okawa / Sumida-gawa, all the same water).15 The mechanism: pre-modern river names were LOCAL - people named the reach they lived on and used, and nobody needed a whole-course name because nobody administered or traveled the whole course; names broke at both political borders and confluences. One-name-per-river is a modern bureaucratic artifact (national mapping agencies, Japan's 1896/1964 River Laws, the PRC settling on Chang Jiang) (unsourced).

Marsh - wet rice is reclaimed FROM wetland

This is grounded in the fact that wet rice is reclaimed FROM marsh: the lower-Yangzi / Tai-Lake paddies are embanked polders (圩田/围田) diked out into marsh and lake,16 and where reclamation stops (or the ground is too wet to manage) it stays reed wetland (abandoned paddy reverts to marsh).17

The wet toe is as wide as the FAN, not as wide as the valley

Sources: senjochi-jawiki (the fan's three zones and the spring line at its toe), mlit-senjochi-kurashi (paddy and settlement at the toe, mulberry and orchard at mid-fan), woods-westbrook-2006 (slope wetlands at fan perimeters), kohai-shicchi-jawiki, shizen-teibo-jawiki (backswamp formation and land use), maff-drain-shape

What prompted it (GM 2026-08-12): a map whose reed toe ran from one edge of the sheet to the other, so that a settlement whose fall pointed at its own frame had no dry exit anywhere - every road out had to turn away over the settlement's back, which contradicts the water-mouth doctrine. The GM asked where that wall-to-wall wetness had been established, and the honest answer was: nowhere - the only stated rule was that the marsh lies downhill of the field. The GM's instinct was to derive the width from what the fan waters, and asked for it to be checked against real farmland first.

What the research found: real wet toes are FEATURE-bounded, not valley-bounded, and two separate landforms say so.

  • The alluvial fan (扇状地), which is the landform our comb fans actually depict. Its three zones are canonical in Japanese physical geography: at the 扇頂 (apex) the gradient is steep;51 across the 扇央 (mid-fan) the river water sinks into coarse gravels, so streams run dry and the ground is poor for paddy - historically woodland, then mulberry (桑畑), now orchards;5259 and at the 扇端 (toe) that water re-emerges in a spring line, 湧水帯, which is why settlements and paddy have clustered there since antiquity.53 The decisive point for us is WHERE that line runs: it emerges where the permeable fan deposits meet the impermeable floor beneath (unsourced), so it follows the fan's own geometry rather than the width of the valley the fan sits in. The wetland literature says the same from the other direction - slope wetlands form at breaks in slope where groundwater discharges, and are typically found on the perimeter of permeable alluvial fans.54
  • And the mechanism is fed by the command area itself. In fan-country paddy districts, river infiltration and seepage from the rice fields are the significant sources of groundwater recharge55 - the water that emerges below is largely the water the fan put in. Canal and field seepage causing waterlogging below an irrigated command is the same relationship in the modern engineering literature. So the wet ground below a fan is downstream of what the fan waters in a causal sense, not merely a coincidence of elevation.
  • The one landform that could have contradicted it does not. On a floodplain the wet ground is the 後背湿地 (backswamp) - but it is bounded by the 自然堤防 (natural levees) that make it,5619 which is again a feature boundary rather than the valley's width, and in Japan most backswamp was itself converted to paddy rather than left as reed waste.57 Neither landform gives us wetness from valley wall to valley wall.

The decision: the toe band takes its cross-slope extent from the CULTIVATED extent (the fan's plots plus the dry hem) with a 90 ft shoulder each side for the seepage spreading a little past the watered ground, clamped to the canvas. The shoulder is the same 90 ft the band already used for its uphill overlap.

The departure we are taking knowingly: a real spring line is an ARC following the fan's toe, and ours is a straight contour band with square lateral ends. Curving it would be more faithful; the band is a ground-cover region whose reeds are scattered and feathered to nothing at the margin, so the square end is not visible as an edge.

The GM, on Inashiro (2026-08-26): "I would have expected that the marshland would run parallel to the drainage ditch, but what we actually have is a drainage ditch which runs at an angle ... and then marshland where the demarcation ... appears to run exactly east to west parallel to the edge of the map. that makes it look like a mistake rather than a feature of the geography." The research pass (label: accurate): MAFF's field-drainage design standard places the field-toe interceptor drain (承水路) "nearly parallel to the contours, with a gentle gradient", and the collectors (集水路/排水路) perpendicular to them at 200-300 m spacing58 (MAFF 排水路の形状・構造と適用条件); the fan-toe spring line is an arc along the fan's foot (above). So in reality the toe drain and the wet edge run TOGETHER, and our collector's drawn grade is a legibility exaggeration of a fall of ~0.1% (convention - a map drawing convention, recorded: the drain is drawn steep so its flow reads; the marsh must not inherit that fiction). The decision on the wet edge: the toe band's inner edge is sampled across the slope - at each station the shoulder above the LOCAL lowest crop point, smoothed over three stations - so the reeds begin at the fan's foot everywhere along the collector.

Two ways the wet edge is set, and the rule for choosing between them. Some maps in the pool draw their toe by hand, with literal coordinates, and deliberately claim a generous region that OVERLAPS the crop - the reeds then fill up to the field's true low edge whatever its shape, because the scatter skips any point standing on a paddy. Kikuta's hand-drawn marsh reaches well above its entire collector, so it claims ground at rice height as wet, and that is invisible only because of the skip. A computed toe takes its inner edge from the cultivated extent instead. Neither is a rule to copy by its method: decide by what the ground IS, not by which path drew it.

What it means on the maps: dry ground appears at both lateral ends of every toe, and with it every settlement keeps a downslope exit: Ikegami's and Akagahara's roads run south over ground that is dry for their whole length, while Moritono's, whose fall is west and whose straight track would run down the wet side, keeps its diverted course.

What ground is too wet to build on?

Sources: the GM's rulings of 2026-07; the map review of 2026-08-12; this project's measurements of the drawn hamlets; the wetland finding at Marsh - wet rice is reclaimed FROM wetland

The bog, and the water itself. A marsh on these maps is unbuildable ground, and the first thing that means is that nothing SACRED or funerary stands on it. The GM, 2026-07: "no one would ever put a graveyard - or a shrine - in a marsh". You do not raise a hall or dig graves in a bog. So a drawn marsh registers its own footprint as ground nothing may be built on - collision-placed features, houses, wells and byres alike, keep off it - and a hand-sited shrine or churchyard goes on dry ground, the spur or the high ground. A pond's reed FRINGE is exempt: it is a water margin wherever the pond lies, not a valley bog. The case that produced the rule: a split village's shrine and its graveyard first landed on the broad wet toe below the fields, and were moved to the dry ground beside the western block.

And no wellhead is sunk in the bog. A map review on 2026-08-12 found a hamlet's south-eastern well standing among the drawn reeds, about 50 ft from the drainage pond. You do not dig a draw-well in standing surface water: a well wants a water table beneath dry ground you can stand a well rim and a windlass on (this rests on general reading; no source is cited)71. The cause was DRAW ORDER rather than siting - wells are placed early and the reed toe is drawn late, so the reeds simply appeared around a well sited before they existed - and the fix was to make the wet band answerable in advance, so a well can be asked about it at the moment it is placed.

Two traps in measuring it, both paid for and recorded. First, the test is against ground below the crop's LOWEST point, not against the drawn marsh polygon. The wet band deliberately begins about 90 ft ABOVE the crop's low edge so the reeds tuck under the field, so its uphill lip overlaps farmed and built ground that carries no reeds at all - and testing the polygon would refuse every wellhead standing on that lip. Second, the rule is scoped to hamlet and village, because towns and cities draw no toe marsh at all: shipped unscoped, it cost the provincial city six farmhouse wells by reserving dry city ground as imaginary bog.

Dwellings keep off the wet low toe below the drain, for the same reason. The GM, 2026-07: "would there be farmhouses below the drainage ditch if south of it is marshland?" A field drains to its lowest edge, and the ground downslope of that collector - reed marsh, or low reclaimed paddy, or the drainage pond itself - is the wettest in the valley. It is not always marsh; but whatever it is, it is wet, so no farmhouse sits there.

"Below the drain" is not the same as "low ground", and one story that sounded right was refuted by measurement. The terrain is modeled as a tilted plane, so height depends only on position along the fall: the wet toe is a band across the slope and its upper boundary is a CONTOUR, not the ditch. And the drawn collector is not a contour - it descends along its length to reach the pond it empties into, a descent that is itself a legibility exaggeration of a very gentle real fall (see The wet toe is as wide as the FAN). The refuted story is that a rectangular field implies a drain running across the slope: measured on the drawn maps, a collector's height spread is 269 ft on Akagahara, 340 ft on Kikuta and 352 ft on Hoshigaoka. It is not a cross-slope line. So the rule flags a dwelling only within the drain's OWN cross-slope span, projected onto the declared fall.

And it is scoped to DISPERSED settlements, which was measured rather than assumed. Each strewn farm is sited individually and must individually stand on dry ground; a NUCLEATED cluster is placed as one unit and is governed by its own rule. A tight cluster beside a DIAGONAL drain reads as "downslope of the drain line" without standing in any wet toe at all, which is what the unscoped rule fired on at Ueda, whose west-margin cluster sits south-west of a north-west to south-east drain. A farm off to the SIDE, past the drain's ends, is a legitimate flank homestead and is not flagged; only the central toe below the drain is.

The rule the map follows: Hamlet tier. No sacred or funerary ground stands on marsh: no shrine, temple hall, cemetery or mausoleum, and not by any part of its footprint - a clipped corner is as wrong as a center. A pond's reed fringe does not count as marsh for this. A shrine or churchyard that has nowhere dry inside the settlement goes onto the spur or the high ground outside it.

The rule the map follows: Hamlet tier. No dwelling stands on the wet toe below a field's drain. The ground judged is what lies downslope of the collector along the declared fall and within the collector's own cross-slope span; a farmstead out past the drain's ends, on a dry flank, is not on the toe and stands there legitimately. The rule applies where farmsteads are strewn individually across the ground, not to a nucleated cluster placed as one block, and it needs both a declared fall and a drawn drain to mean anything.

The rule the map follows: Town and city tier, where no generator draws the settlement yet. A channel is a no-build corridor: nothing is built within about 33 ft of its centerline, which is the width calibrated to keep a farmhouse's whole FOOTPRINT off the water rather than merely its center - a hamlet farmhouse's half-diagonal is about 26 ft - and at a coarser tier the corridor is whatever keeps the largest neighboring footprint clear. The test is on the footprint: a corner clipping a channel is a failure, which is how one village once shipped farmhouses standing on its field-to-field channels.

No toe marsh at town/city scale - the drainage-investment gradient

Sources: yato-jawiki, shitsuden-kotobank, hibiya-irie-jawiki, fukada-kotobank (深田 defined; reed cutting after harvest)

What the research found: the real pattern is a gradient of drainage investment and land value. VILLAGES had neither the capital nor the incentive to eliminate every wet margin: residual wet ground at the low edge of village land is very well attested22 (Japanese yatsuda valley-bottom paddies were essentially managed marsh;20 reed beds were harvested village commons under iriai tenure - thatch, screens, annual burning (unsourced); poorly drained fukada "deep fields" persisted until Meiji-and-later drainage projects68),21 and crucially it was USED wet ground - reed bed, wet meadow, more paddy at the reclamation frontier - which is exactly what our sparse harvestable-reed rendering reads as. TOWNS/CITIES commanded corvee-scale hydraulic engineering and premium peri-urban land: ditch discharge went into an engineered moat/canal/river network (Suzhou's canal grid; Edo's canals and the immediate infill of the Hibiya inlet after 1590),23 and the outskirts were intensively worked (suburban vegetable belts fed by urban night soil; Edo-period reclamation waves around Osaka and Edo). Where wetness DID persist near a city it was bounded and purposed - a moat, a lotus/fish pond, a flood basin, a deliberate defensive inundation - never a diffuse seepage marsh below a ditch.

Defensive marshland - the engineered wet belt

Sources: bitchu-takamatsu-jawiki; the Song Hebei tanglao (塘泺) pond-and-marsh belt (He Chengju, 988-989) is unsourced here

What the research found: the Northern Song built an artificial marsh-and-pond belt across the northern Hebei frontier (from 989, He Chengju's lake chain) specifically as anti-cavalry terrain; Japanese numajiro "marsh castles" (Bitchu Takamatsu, besieged by water 1582) used surrounding marsh as their primary defense;24 and flooded paddies around castle towns functioned as a de facto glacis. The constant: peri-urban wetness that survives is PURPOSED - so a defensive belt is military ground, not waste.

The rule the map follows: Town and city tier, where no generator draws the settlement yet; no drawn map carries one. A defensive wet belt is hand-placed OUTSIDE the wall circuit, hugging the perimeter: it begins at the moat's outer bank or the foot of the wall and stays within about 180 ft of the wall or moat line, because a bog detached from the fortification defends nothing, and it may be drawn only on a map that HAS a wall or a moat. It does not follow the valley toe - it follows the circuit wherever the wall runs - so the rule that puts marsh on low ground does not apply to it. An approach road crossing it is a CAUSEWAY and keeps a bare tread, since few and constricted approaches are the belt's whole military purpose. It is unbuildable ground exactly like the valley-toe marsh, sacred and funerary siting included.

Canal junction angles - an offtake leaves pointing downstream

Canal practice: an offtake leaves its parent at an ACUTE angle pointing downstream - studied optimum 15-45 deg, explicitly "30 or 45 instead of 9025"

Where two watercourses meet, how is the junction drawn?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-08-28

As ONE body of water, with no seam. Water on these maps is drawn semi-transparent, so two strokes laid down independently double up where they cross and leave a darker patch exactly at the junction - and a pond's dark shore rim drew a hard border straight across the mouth of every channel feeding it. The GM, 2026-08-28: "when a stream meets a irrigated channel or where an irrigated channel or a ditch meets a pond ... it clearly looks like one is rendered on top of the other ... water just flows", and the pond's rim "should not be present at the place of their intersection".

The decision. Every watercourse on a map composites in ONE block, at the latest position any of them needs. Bottom to top: every pond RIM first, then every BED in a single shared transparency group with the pond's own fill last among them, then every SHEEN. So overlapping beds read as one continuous body of water - a crossing, or a feeder running into a moat, reads as a real confluence rather than as two lines laid over each other; a pond's rim lies under every bed that reaches it, so the shore opens exactly at each mouth; and because the pond's fill is painted last among the beds, it covers the small overshoot a feeder's rounded end makes inside the rim.

The label. This is a map drawing convention and nothing more. It changes how the ink is composited and never where any water lies, and it applies to every map at every tier. The village lanes took the same route on the same day, drawing through one shared ground block so that a junction reads as a single tread instead of one lane's shoulder crossing the other's.

Why does every ditch turn on a curve?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25; meander-enwiki (how far a natural channel's bend swings against its own width, and what a turn does to the two banks)

Because water turns on a SWEPT BEND and never on a mitered corner. A hand-dug earthen ditch that changes direction does it on a sweep: a sharp corner scours on the outside and silts on the inside until the water has rounded it anyway,72 and the crew that maintains the ditch re-digs the bend rather than the corner (this rests on general reading; no source is cited)73. Sharp corners belong to MASONRY - a stone-lined aqueduct, a sluice box, a revetted canal - and nothing at these scales is lined, so nothing on these maps shows one. Revisit only if a literal aqueduct is ever drawn.

How wide the sweep is. The radius is about 2.5 channel widths, capped at 35% of either adjacent leg. A meandering channel turns on a radius of two to three times its own width,74 which is the geometry the figure is taken from, and a dug farm ditch is no tighter - which is this record's own step from a river's bend to a dug one, not the article's - so the bend SCALES with the ditch instead of being a fixed drawing quirk. A fat head-race sweeps wide and a thin lateral turns tight, both at the same real ratio, at every map scale. The cap stops two neighboring bends from eating the leg between them.

What is deliberately NOT rounded. An OFFTAKE, where a branch leaves a main: a notch cut in the bank is a junction, not a bend, and the main is not turning there. And a turn under about 8 degrees, which has no visible elbow and would only gain points from being rounded.

Irrigation topology - one pond outlet that branches

Sources: kagawa-tameike-structure (one intake facility, 斜樋 into 底樋; "occasionally two" not addressed there)

A tameike reservoir has a single intake facility (the inclined shahi into the bottom sokohi)26 - occasionally two - so all its irrigation leaves by one main channel (yosui) that then FORKS downstream to the fields, dividing at junctions;27 it is NOT several independent pipes drilled into the pond.

Drawn width is RANK, not discharge - junctions do not conserve it

Sources: a rendering convention ruled by the GM; the one physical clause (a sluice-fed head-race is wider and slower than its feeder) is reasoning

The question. At the head junction the brook arrives at 7 px, becomes a 14 px head-race below the sluice, and forks into arms starting 12.4 + 11.2 px - drawn capacity appears to GROW downstream at every junction, and the fork makes that junction the focal water feature of every comb map.

The ruling (GM 2026-08-16, options weighed: keep / intake stilling pool / conserve-at-fork). Keep the convention. A stroke's drawn width depicts its rank and local importance in the irrigation hierarchy (the taper rule: widest at its head, dwindling as it sheds water to its offtakes), sized per stroke with no cross-junction coordination - because width-as-rank is what keeps the hierarchy legible at fit zoom, and conservation would chain every drawn width down from the brook's 7 px and thin the far arms to hairlines. The intake jump also has a real reading: a sluice-fed head-race genuinely IS wider and slower than the stream that feeds it (engineered cross-section, ponding above the weir), so width-not-discharge is not merely a stylization there. The arms each drawn narrower than their trunk is honest; only their SUM exceeding it is convention. So the in/out widths at a junction are not meant to add up; what the map holds is each stroke's own taper (head wider than tail) and the hierarchy read (trunk > branch > delivery), never the conservation arithmetic.

The head-race forks - supply commands both flanks

Sources: saitama-minuma-tsusenbori, jsidre-minumadai (Isawa corroboration in prose below)

What the research found (GM asked 2026-08-16, after noticing Inashiro's channel turns along one margin but never splits the way other maps' channels do). Gravity irrigation has one governing constraint: a canal commands only the ground BELOW it. Chinese canal doctrine states it as placement law - "干渠主要布置在灌区较高的地带，以便自流控制较大的灌溉面积"28 (main canals sit on the district's high ground so gravity commands the largest area) - and repeats it at every tier of the hierarchy ("布置在各自控制范围内较高地带":29 each canal on the high ground of its OWN command area), mains and branch canals along contours and ridge lines, field channels across them.30 The Kishu-school exemplar the comb layout already cites goes further: Minuma-dai (1728) deliberately DIVIDES its head channel into TWO canals - the east-edge and west-edge canals (東縁/西縁) - running along the two elevated margins of the reclaimed lowland31 so water can enter the paddies from both sides, with the Shiba River down the central lowland as the drain ("見沼代用水東縁・西縁は台地の縁に沿って流れ、芝川は低地の中央を流れる").32 At fan scale the same shape appears as radial canals: the Isawa fan (one of Japan's largest, ~15,000 ha) is watered by weir canals leaving the fan head and spreading over the fan (MLIT Isawa pages, consulted via search 2026-08-16).

The decision it drove. A comb fan is planted on BOTH sides of its bunsuiguchi - the layout already assumes so - so the DRAWN net must show both arms of the fork. Every hamlet gives canal B an offtake, which inks the second arm partway down its margin, tapering to a thread per the Tabayashi taper rule.33 Measured, every live hamlet's short flank carries ~170-200 ft of drawn supply against ~250-260 ft of paddy. Two terminations are honest, and the pool shows both: where the dug arm runs past its last offtake, the tail tapers to a thread and dies at the crop (Inashiro, Kashikawa, Mizuguchi); where the dug arm ENDS at its offtake, the canal hands its whole flow to the delivery and stops at the junction (Sawada) - what is dishonest is only a working-width canal chopped mid-margin, which the drawing prevents. The rule: on every flank with more than ~150 ft of paddy (cross-slope from the fork), drawn main or branch supply reaches at least 80 ft or 30% of that flank's extent.

When a single arm IS honest. The command principle cuts both ways: a tract lying wholly on ONE side of its supply needs no second arm - a ribbon strip along a contour canal, a terrace flight, a polder ring. None of those record a fork, so the rule does not touch them; and a fan whose far flank is a sliver (under the 150 ft demand line) legitimately runs one arm.

Where does the brook stop being a brook and become the ditch? At an intake on its bank - and the brook runs on

Sources: japanriver-koborebanashi-21 (the works in order; the weir since the Yayoi period), jawiki-toshuko (the parts of the head works), fao-irrigation-manual-m7 (the physics: offtake, weir, the lower flow returned to the river, the angle), maff-toshuko-history (the premodern forms and materials; the surplus returned; the bend), jawiki-seki (the overflow weir), fao-water-sources-u5835e (water flowing over the weir), zhwiki-dujiangyan and zhwiki-lingqu (the river divided in stated proportions), tabayashi-1987 (small streams dammed; the canal divided below the intake; Toro), icid-diversion-weirs and maff-nogyoyosui-suiden (whole diversion as a possibility), jsidre-miwa-2023 (natural intake; the oblique weir from the intake mouth), shimane-toshuko-lecture (one-bank intake; where the current touches the bank; the intake court), wangzhen-nongshu-18 (the village brook's timber palisade), zhwiki-zhengguoqu (crib weir or bare bend intake, both readings of one ancient head), maff-toshuko-sand-basin (the settling basin at the canal's start)

The question. The GM asked whether there is any logic to where a stream becomes a ditch, and whether the stream should not stay a stream right up to the point where it branches in two.

What the record found: the transition is a set of works, in a fixed order, and the stream goes on below them. Both traditions name the same sequence. In Japan the head works were originally the whole series - the weir, the intake mouth, the settling basin, the division works109 - and the modern term still lists the intake weir, the intake mouth and the settling basin as its parts.110 A general irrigation manual gives the same three: an offtake at the side of the river, a regulating structure across the river or part of it, and a sediment flushing arrangement.111 The weir on a small mountain stream for taking water into paddy is old - its prototype is placed in the Yayoi period, more than two thousand years ago112 - and in the mountains "small streams are dammed and the water is conducted through small canals usually for short distances and on gentle slopes".113 The canal, once it has left the stream, is what divides: water taken by a diversion dam "enters a main canal and is delivered into secondary and lesser canals one after another by gravity", the secondary canals tapping "diversion works on the main canals".82 The fork is on the dug canal, below the intake, and no source read puts one on the stream itself.115

And the stream is not swallowed. In old Japan the closures were mostly half-river closures75; a weir may be of the kind that lets water over its crest at all times, and Japanese has a name for it76; the Ishi-ibi works of 1615 took water at the sluice and returned the surplus to the main river between the island and the sluice77; a properly designed intake "should divert only the relatively clean upper part of the water flow into the canal and dispose of the lower part down the river"78, and when the river runs high "water will flow over the weir".79 The two great Chinese diversions divide their rivers in stated proportions rather than taking them: Dujiangyan sends six parts to the irrigation channel and four to the flood channel in spring and the reverse in flood80, and Lingqu returns seven parts to the Xiang and passes three to the canal.81 So the GM's reading is refuted in its letter and confirmed in its spirit: the stream does not become the ditch at the fork - the fork is the dug canal's own division point - but the stream does stay a stream below the intake, and the ditch begins where it leaves the bank.

Whole capture: possible by definition, shown nowhere. A diversion weir is defined as "a low dam or wall across a stream for the purpose of diverting part or all the water from a stream into a canal"83, and in an Edo drought "all the water flowing out of the mountain catchment" was taken into agricultural use, with each district's return flow used in full by the district below.84 No source read shows a single brook extinguished by a village's ditch.116 The maps therefore draw the attested form - the brook continues - and the whole-capture reading is recorded here as declined rather than as a second form: the maps roll between two forms only where the record shows a thing done two ways, and this one is shown done one way.

Two intake forms, both attested - so a knob. The first is the bare bank intake: "in old times, neither technique nor materials were worth much, so in many cases no intake weir was built at all - water was taken naturally, a place where natural intake was possible was sought for the intake mouth, and a conveyance canal was cut; where raising the water level ... was necessary, there were places that set up a temporary weir useful only for the intake season".114 Natural intake sets the intake mouth on the bank the current runs along, and a weir is added when the river falls too low to feed it.85 The same holds in the general manual: where the base flow keeps the level high enough, "a simple offtake structure to control the water diversion is sufficient".86 The second is the built weir. Before modern materials it was ox-frames, gabions and brushwood, singly or combined and covered with earth and gravel; the Tatai weir of 1183 was timber frames packed with stone, an oblique weir closing the whole river, whose upper frame at the deep channel was removed after each season and "installed anew every year in the irrigation season"87; up to the end of the Sengoku period intake works were "mostly small-scale, based on timber frames packed with stone".88 Many old weirs were oblique, "extended long in the diagonally upstream direction from the intake mouth", damming the shallow riffle and standing clear of the flood's fastest water.89 China has the village case in Wang Zhen's treatise of 1313: "if the banks of the brook are somewhat deep and the fields lie high, so that the water cannot reach them, then upstream on the brook one makes a palisade to check the water, causing it to issue out at the side and run down to irrigate, so reaching the place of the fields"90 - and the head of the Zhengguo canal is read by scholars as either stone-filled crib baskets or a low weir, or as no dam at all and a diversion off the river bend.91 The record gives no proportion between the two forms, so each hamlet rolls one or the other with an even chance, and that proportion is a guess.

Which bank, and at what angle. The intake is on one bank, the side of the land it serves - the rule on torrent and steep rivers, and the principle on gentle ones92 - at a point where the current's deep line touches that bank, beside the sand sluice.93 The offtake should stand in a straight reach of the river; failing that, on the outside of a bend, where silt does not settle; it may leave the river at an angle when the water is clean, and at a right angle when it is silt-laden - the same page noting that the outside of a bend erodes and may need its bank protected.94 A Japanese torrent-river intake sited just downstream of the middle of a bend's concave bank took in little sediment.95 A mountain brook feeding a hamlet fan is drawn with the clean-water offtake: the head race leaves the bank at an acute angle pointing downstream, the angle the record already gives for an offtake leaving its parent (below, where that angle is itself a guess: no source read gives a figure for it). A 1902 treatise placed the head works "where the river issues from the mountains"99, which is where a hamlet's brook meets the head of its fan.

How far from the intake to the fork. No source read gives a distance.117 In modern practice what stands in that first stretch is a settling basin, "immediately following the intake mouth, at the starting point of the canal"96, reached through an intake court that narrows the flow smoothly from the mouth into the canal.97 - whether a village dug one is not recorded118, and the maps draw none. Tabayashi says only that the small canals run "for short distances"; the third-century Toro fields, 7.5 hectares, were watered by a canal "more than 370 meters long".98 So the length of the head race is not a number the record holds. On the maps it follows from the geometry: the head race runs from the intake on the brook's bank to the division point at the fan's head, and its length is whatever that distance is, the intake's lead upslope of the fork rolled per map within the "short distances" the record allows - a guess as to the number, derived as to the shape.

What the map draws. The field is cut AWAY from the brook - the supply canal on the brook's flank stops short, so the fan leaves the stream its margin rather than the stream bending round the fan. The brook comes down off the high ground, passes the head of the fan on one side, and runs on down that flank of the field to leave the map or to receive the field's drain (the next question). At the fan's head, on the fan's bank, is the intake, and the head race leaves it at an acute angle pointing downstream and runs to the division point. On a weir hamlet a bar of stone-packed timber crib crosses the brook there, running diagonally upstream from the intake mouth as the old oblique weirs did - the old form as the modern engineering histories describe it, since no period drawing of a village weir was read.119 It is drawn in stone gray with the crib's baulks ticked across it and a lip along its upstream face, so that it reads as a thing that holds water back rather than as one of the timber planks that carry a lane over a ditch. Note: we have drawn that weir as a full closure of the brook, in order to make it visible on the map at this scale, although half-river closures were the common old form75 - at a brook seven feet wide a half-bar is a pixel or two - and its thickness is drawn at five feet, a guess, because no source read gives the thickness of a village weir. On a bare-intake hamlet nothing is built across the brook: the head race simply leaves the bank.

Where does the water go once it has watered the paddies? Back to the river - by the next field, a shared channel or a drain that reaches it

Sources: maff-nogyoyosui-suiden (the three layouts; separation as the consolidation standard; the reuse of return flow), tabayashi-1987 (one network supplying and receiving; separation dated to the postwar projects), jawiki-yosuiro (the supply channel set apart from a channel for drainage alone), asaza-yatsuda (the used water flowing on to the river)

The question. The ditches that feed the paddies and the ditch that collects what runs out of them are both dug channels. Are they one kind of thing, and where does the collected water go?

What the record found. Japanese planning names three layouts of a paddy's water: field-to-field irrigation, in which water passes over the bunds from one plot to the next; the combined type, in which one channel both supplies and receives; and the separated type, with a supply channel above and a drainage channel below.100 The older network did both jobs at once - "the canal network extending widely over the area of paddy fields supplied irrigation water to paddies and received discharge from them"103 - and combined channels were sometimes an old river course, so that reuse of the water happened by itself; where it did not, works were built to return drainage to the supply channel or use it directly, until "within all paddy-irrigation districts a thoroughgoing irrigation-drainage reuse system was completed that lets out no wasted water at all".101 Separating the supply channel from the drainage channel, giving every plot its own inlet and outlet and cutting the drain about a meter below the field, is the standard of field consolidation102, which came with the postwar projects: "irrigation and drainage canals were separated and arranged along farm roads", in blocks laid out from 1963 "with a road, an irrigation channel, and a drainage channel adjacent to each block".104 Separating them raises the water a district needs, because "residual water is drained directly into the drainage canal and the opportunity for repeated use decreases".107 The language keeps the two apart all the same: a supply channel is a channel made to draw water in, and a channel whose purpose is drainage alone is another thing, not counted among them.105 And the water that leaves the paddies goes on: in a valley paddy "the water, after being used carefully, then flows into the river and eventually into the lake".106

What it means on the map. The two kinds are two things a reader can hover: the irrigation ditch - the head race, the supply canals and the delivery ditches that bring water to the paddies - and the drainage ditch - the collector along the low line and its run onward. A hamlet's drain is a dug channel that reaches a watercourse; the maps never give one a natural brook of its own, which is the map's rule rather than the record's: where the passing brook comes near the collector's outfall the drain joins it, as a district's drainage returned to the river to be taken up below108; where it does not, the drain runs on as a ditch to the edge of the map or to the pond at the field's foot, which is the map's earlier decision about where that pond stands (the fields record). The ANGLE at which the drain meets the brook is whatever the ground gives it, and nothing here rules on it: the record's canal-junction rule is about an offtake leaving its parent, and what angle a made outfall arriving at a watercourse was cut to was not asked in this pass. The separated layout the map draws - a supply net above, one collector below - is the form the record dates to modern consolidation; the older field-to-field and combined forms are attested and are not drawn, because a hamlet drawn in the field-to-field form would have no ditch between its plots for a reader to hover, and that trade is recorded here as a deliberate one.

Does a hamlet stand on one bank of its stream, or around it? It depends on the size of the water

Sources: imazato-1999 (one hamlet on a river terrace, and what its inhabitants call the far bank), harie-syozu and jawiki-harie (a settlement on one bank of its river with its own stream through the middle), mizu-no-bunka-60 (the channel that runs alongside the house), jawiki-senjo-shuraku (the both-sides that is a street, not a watercourse), jawiki-shijin-soo (the water on one side of a site), hu-shangantang and hu-shaanxi-villages (streams that flow around and past a village), hirasawa-murazakai (villages named by their side of a river)

The question. The maps keep a hamlet's houses on one bank of the brook that passes it. That is a rule about how a place was built, so it owes the record an answer.

Against a river, one bank is what the record shows. Harie in Shiga "lies on the left bank of the alluvial fan at the lower reaches of the Ado River, the largest river in the Kosei region".120 A settlement-geography study of Hagikura, a hamlet of 124 households on a river terrace in Nagano, carries the arrangement in the inhabitants' own words: the hill behind the settlement is the Urayama, the "back mountain", while the hill across the river is the Mukoyama, the "facing mountain" - the far bank is hillside, and it is named as the far side.121 And an Edo-period dispute over the Tenryu names its parties by their side of the water, "the villages east of the river" against "Shimada village, west of the river", with the boundary lying wherever the main current ran.122

Against the settlement's OWN small channel, the record shows the opposite. The same page that puts Harie on one bank of the Ado says the Harie Okawa "flows through roughly the center of the district".123 Its waterways are "the waterways within the settlement", feeding a named stream down its middle124, and an account of the kabata water-huts describes "the channel that flows alongside the house".125 Harie with its neighboring Shimofuri is a nationally designated Important Cultural Landscape, so this is a recognized form rather than an oddity.

So the two are not rival answers. They are answers about watercourses of two different sizes: a settlement stands beside a river and around its own channel. A brook seven feet wide, tapped by the hamlet it passes, is the second kind.

What the siting doctrine adds, and its limits. The four-gods formulation wants "flowing water on the left" of a site, with hills behind and a pond in front126; a Chinese village-landscape study describes Shanggantang as "mountains on three sides, water on one side"127; and a survey of Shaanxi villages describes streams that "flow around and past the village" on a named side.128 None of it contemplates a site divided by its water. The limit to carry is that this is capital- and residence-siting doctrine and village-landscape scholarship, not a rule anyone states for a farming hamlet's brook. One trap is worth naming: a LINEAR settlement is defined as buildings "lined up on both sides of a single street"129, and the standard definition of the farming linear village allows houses "on one side of it or on both"130 - but the thing with two sides there is a road, and neither says a settlement straddles a stream.

What the maps do, and why it is a guess. A hamlet is seated on one bank: a field margin the brook divides is struck out, and a homestead may not be seated across the water from the houses already standing. That is NOT the attested form for a brook at this size - the record shows the opposite at this scale - so it is labeled a guess. It stands for an engine reason rather than a historical one, and the reason is worth a reader knowing: nothing in these maps can draw a way across a stream. A watercourse is registered only as ground to keep out of, so no lane is ever routed over one and a deck is only ever drawn where a way already crosses water. A hamlet seated astride its brook would therefore be a hamlet with houses cut off from their own wells and neighbors. The day a lane can cross a brook, which bank a hamlet builds on becomes a knob to roll rather than a rule to keep, and that is recorded as the next step.

A reservoir's shore is reeded, and its EMBANKMENT is mown - the two are different ground

The question: is a reservoir's shore reeded, or is its bank dry ground? The intuitive answer is the counter-hypothesis, and it had to be tested first: a working reservoir is maintained - the bank repaired, the silt dredged, the water drawn down each season - so perhaps a managed tameike's margin is kept clear, and reeds there would mean neglect.

The record contradicts that outright, and the contradiction is the finding. Mineta 2007 in the Journal of the JSIDRE, reporting a Kagawa Prefecture study of tameike management against aquatic flora: "抽水植物や浮葉植物の出現種数に対して泥上げや池内の藻刈りとの間に有意な正の相関が認められた"60 - a statistically significant POSITIVE correlation between the number of emergent- and floating-leaf plant species and the practice of dredging silt and cutting algae in the pond - and it is the small ponds where water use has stopped and mowing has declined that are LOSING plant diversity.61 Active management is what sustains the reed fringe, not what clears it. So the reeds stay, and they stand on wet ground.

But the record also draws a line we were already drawing, which is the pleasing part. The EMBANKMENT itself is a different piece of ground and is kept clear: 「the embankment is managed to keep its strength - cultivation on it is forbidden, and mowing and burning-off are carried out to hold back the growth of grass and trees」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「堤は耕作を禁じたり、草刈りや野焼き を行ったりして草木の生育を阻む等、強度を維持するための管理が行われる」) - cultivation is forbidden on it and it is mown and burned to stop woody growth, to keep the bank strong - and the plants named as growing there are dry-grassland herbs (ワレモコウ, キキョウ, リンドウ, オミナエシ - great burnet, balloon flower, gentian, golden lace - several of the seven autumn grasses),62 not wetland ones. The dam's water-contact slope is armored outright against wave erosion - "捨石、石張り、コンクリートブロック張"34. This is an INDEPENDENT confirmation of the rule the GM's own complaint produced on 2026-08-28 ("the hazy blue that denotes the marsh is clearly overlaid on top of the greenery of the earthen mounds"): reeds keep off a raised, maintained, planted bank and root in the shallow water outside it. We drew that rule from the GM's eye; the record says the same thing, and gives the reason - the bank is mown to keep it strong.

What the shallow shore carries, positively. MAFF's own technical material draws the zonation from land to open water as riparian forest -> wet-meadow plants -> emergent plants -> floating-leaf -> submerged, and names the emergent belt as ヨシ・ガマ・マコモ63 (reed, cattail, wild rice); Mineta puts 「The aquatic plants growing in a tameike can be divided, according to water depth and slope, into large emergent plants such as yoshi (common reed) and gama (cattail); small emergent plants such as tachimo (whorled water milfoil) and harii (spikerush), which become submerged plants depending on the growing season and the setting; submerged plants such as itomo (Potamogeton pondweed) and subuta (Blyxa); floating-leaf plants such as hishi (water chestnut) and asaza (fringed water lily); and free-floating plants such as ukikusa (duckweed) and sanshoumo (water fern) (Hamashima 2001).」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「ため池に生育する水生植物は水深や傾斜によって,ヨシやガマなどの大型抽水植物,生育時期や環境によっては沈水植物となるタチモやハリイなどの小型抽水植物,イトモやスブタなどの沈水植物,ヒシやアサザなどの浮葉植物,そしてウキクサやサンショウモなどの浮遊植物に分けることができる(浜島2001)。」) in the shallow, gently-sloped part of the pond by depth and slope.64 A tameike museum in Hyogo says the same65 of lowland ponds specifically: 「Tameike in the plains are dish ponds (sara-ike) ringed about by embankments; the water is shallow and eutrophication of the water quality has advanced. Around such ponds, large emergent plants such as yoshi (common reed), makomo (Manchurian wild rice) and himegama (lesser cattail) form colonies, and the water surface is covered by floating-leaf plants such as hishi (water chestnut), onibishi (large water chestnut) and onibasu (prickly waterlily).」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「平野部にあるため池は、まわりを堤防に囲まれた皿池で、水深は浅く水質は富栄養化が進んでいます。このような池の周囲にはヨシ、マコモ、ヒメガマなどの大型抽水植物が群落を形成し、水面はヒシ、オニビシ、オニバスなどの浮葉植物がおおっています。」). The low-diversity end of that spectrum is the concrete-armored urban pond, not the farmed one.66

The rules this produces:

  1. A pond fringe is REEDED and reads WET. The wet-tint haze belongs there as much as it does in a waterside bed.
  2. Reeds and haze keep OFF the embankment and off a pond's raised bank - because it is mown and burned to keep it strong, which is also why nothing is cultivated on it.
  3. Neither of the above is a map drawing convention. Both are what the record describes.

Deliberately NOT concluded. That the reed at a tameike margin was itself cut as a thatch or screen crop. The reed-harvest economy (yoshizu screens, thatch, an uncut bed going to ruin (荒れる)) is well attested - for Lake Biwa and river reed beds, by an organization named for Lake Biwa.67 No source, primary or scholarly, was found putting that economy on tameike margins, and extending it there is an inference the sources do not make.
