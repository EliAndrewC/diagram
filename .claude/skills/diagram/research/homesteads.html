Homesteads: the research behind the farmhouse, yard, garden and grove rules

The research behind the homestead rules.

Homestead groves (yashikirin) - the real scale and prevalence

Sources: kashima-kainyo-1987 (the 1987 survey's figures - 1,542 trees over 46 households, 48% sugi1), yashikirin-jawiki (the west-and-south sentence), isa-auf-fukugi-2011 (Okinawa's north-and-east groves, the cross-check that makes the side a knob), visit-toyama-sankyoson (what the Tonami plain is)

  • Historical scale - the real numbers. A homestead grove is a substantial STAND, not a few trees. The best hard data is a 1987 survey of Kashima in the Tonami plain2 (the dispersed-farmstead country whose homestead woodlands are the kainyo,3 46 households): ~33 trees of trunk diameter >= 10 cm per homestead (1,542 trunks over the 46 households - arithmetic on the two footnoted figures, not a number the page states), of which cedar (sugi) was ~48% (~16 cedars per house4), the rest spread over ~83 other species;5 ~6 species per homestead (range 1-14);6 a large homestead is said to run 200+ trees across 31 species7 - no readable page has been found to support it; kept as a GUESS at the top of the band. That count is trunks >= 10 cm ONLY - it EXCLUDES the bamboo stand (one stood on every homestead, and a count of trunks 10 cm and over does not reach a culm),8 saplings, and the shrub layers below the canopy (the page sorts the stand into four layers by height and counts 44 / 76 / 80 / 153 species from the tallest down)9 - so the honest figure for a typical grove is ~30-40 mature trees + a bamboo grove + understory, and a big one 100-200+. The grove canopy footprint is therefore the LARGEST homestead appurtenance - bigger than the farmhouse, and far bigger than the garden or threshing yard - wrapping the windward faces as a belt. How DEEP the belt is has no firm number in the record - the "2-3 rows of sugi" below is itself unsourced - so the drawn arm depth (13 ft, one to two crowns) is a GUESS. WHICH faces is the per-map windward knob, because the record gives more than one answer: on the Tonami plain the tall sugi stand from the south face round to the west, with hackberry, alder and a little bamboo from the west round to the north, and the general account of the mainland yashikirin puts the tall trees on the west and south;10 the maps put the belt on the northwest by default; a map with a Tonami-like wind takes the southwest arms. The map draws EVERY canopy tree, one crown at its real size on real spacing (Forest density and crown size: a mean crown radius of 8.5 ft on ~13 ft centers, no crown seated under another), on a footprint that is itself to scale - exactly as the farmhouse, the threshing yard and the garden are drawn at their true size. The grove's dominance is therefore not a glyph calibration; it falls out of drawing the real thing: clearly the dominant homestead feature, a dense stand of dozens of trees, never a garden-sized clump of 5-10. (Cross-check on the windward rule: Okinawa's homestead groves sit on the E/N sides, because there the damaging winds come from the north (the monsoon) and the east (typhoons),11 not the mainland's west and south - same logic, different geography, which is exactly why the windward side is a per-map knob.)

How big was the work yard, and how did the sizes spread?

Sources: kitamoto-mushiro-niwa, kamikanai-1771-houses, kikoba-kenchi, santome-shinden-allotment, yonekura-mushiro, tobunken-mushiro, irri-drying-floor, ndl-kokumori

The GM, on the map's own modal: "I see the threshing yard size is listed as a rendering convention. How large WERE these yards?" A figure of 100-300 tsubo (330-992 sq m) that is easy to mistake for the yard is the homestead LOT (Akishima city history: farms need a wide front yard, so lots run 100-300 tsubo) - a real number that belongs to a different thing.

The size, as households themselves stated it. Kitamoto (Saitama) city history, folk volume: 「At harvest time straw mats (mushiro) were spread over the whole of the yard, and the size of the yard was often expressed by the number of these mats. It was related to the area under barley cultivation; ordinarily forty to sixty mats, and among them there were households that exceeded a hundred. Incidentally, two mats correspond to one tsubo.」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「収穫期には庭一面にムシロが敷かれ、しばしば庭の広さはこのムシロの枚数で表現された。麦の耕作面積と関係 していて、普通四〇〜六〇枚、中には百枚を越す家もあった。ちなみに、ムシロ二枚が一坪にあたる。」) - the yard was measured in straw mats, 40-60 usually and past 100 for a few, two mats to the tsubo: 20-30 tsubo (66-99 sq m) ordinarily, past 50 tsubo (165 sq m) at the top12, and explicitly sized by the household's cropped acreage.13 Two independent lines agree: an Okayama museum records ~50 mats per farm,14 and the measured mat is 90 x 180 cm,15 so ~80 sq m; a directly measured yard at Kodaira is 70 tsubo (231 sq m) - a large Musashino holding,16 the upper end. No survey found tabulates yards as areas; these are the readable figures.

But Kitamoto is a BARLEY district, and this generator draws wet rice. Its yard is sized by the mugi crop, which the household spreads whole. Rice is field-dried on hazakake racks for 10-14 days (unsourced) before it reaches the yard and is threshed in batches over days, so a paddy household needs less standing floor. Deriving it from the crop instead - 1.3 koku/tan (中田 石盛, chuden kokumori, the middle-paddy standard yield)17 -> 247 kg momi at a 79% hulling yield -> mats at the International Rice Research Institute (IRRI)'s 2.5 cm spread,18 batched - gives 55-100 sq m for a full cho and 35-65 for five tan. The generator therefore centers a rice hamlet's yards at 18 tsubo (59.5 sq m) and keeps Kitamoto's 25 tsubo as the dry-field figure. The GM ruled between the two readings on 2026-08-28: "I agree with option two" - the sourced SHAPE everywhere, the sourced SCALE appropriate to what the household dries.

The spread is lognormal, and it is right-skewed. No survey gives a histogram of yards, so the shape comes from what the cadastres do tabulate, and every one is right-skewed: Kamikanai (1771) gives a complete main-house histogram whose19 31 commoner houses fit a lognormal of median 22.5 tsubo and sigma 0.46 in log space, with the headman detached at 3.1x; Kikoba's Genroku 検地帳 (kenchicho, the land-survey register) puts homestead lots at 20-100 bu with two peaks, at 30 bu and 50 bu (the page: 「Homestead lot areas are distributed between 20 bu and 100 bu」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「屋敷反歩は20歩から100歩の間に分布している」)「with 30 bu and 50 bu showing the peaks」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「30歩と50歩がピークを示し」); no comparable figure for great holders was found - the page's only "2倍" (twofold) concerns house COUNT, not lot area)20. Kitamoto's own band-and-tail implies sigma 0.35-0.45 - that convergence is what the drawn sigma of 0.40 rests on. A floor exists but is not zero: early registers carry a no-homestead class (無屋敷登録人, muyashiki torokunin), but by Genroku 「all peasant households, including those holding no fields, came to have a house plot」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「田畑を保有しない百姓も含め全百姓が屋敷を持つようになり」) (unsourced) - the landless simply occupy the small end, so the roll is floored at 8 tsubo rather than allowed to vanish.

The yard follows the household, but not proportionally. The proportional coupling is ADDITIVE - 「if the holding increases by about 5 koku, the tsubo count increases by about 10 tsubo」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「持高が5石ほど増加すれば坪数が10坪ほど増加する」)21 - so a 20x holder does not get a 20x yard (the sentence is from the same paper's paragraph on Kyoho-era Iwahara village; of Kamikanai itself the paper says the relation to holding size "is not marked", and holdings inside the village span landless to about 30 koku)22. The generator gives the yard the household's own deviation in log space, amplified and then perturbed by an independent positional draw: a large household is overwhelmingly likely to have a large yard, a mismatch is possible and rare - the GM's own reading of what the record implies.

One attested form is NOT skewed, and is a knob. A planned shinden colony issued every settler an identical homestead - Santome 1696, "屋敷の規模はまったくの均等配分"23 - so an "allotted" yard setting draws uniform yards. Two attested forms become a per-settlement knob.

What this means on the map. The yard is rolled per household from the distribution above, so every hamlet that dries rice has to find room for it beside the house.

The threshing yard's sun, and how far a farmhouse shades

Sources: derived - solar geometry at 38N for the 10th month, and the minka's 46 x 28 ft footprint; kayabuki-jawiki (the steep pitch as a material requirement; the 45-degree figure and the 6-7 m ridge of surviving farmhouses are not on the page, and are unsourced)

What prompted it (GM 2026-08-13): "Would threshing fields be directly to the north of another house? Or would the shadow from the farmhouse directly to the south block too much light?"

The house's height, DERIVED rather than assumed. A thatched (kayabuki) roof has to be pitched 45 degrees or steeper - that is what the material demands,24 and it is why a minka carries such a large dark loft. Our minka is 46 x 28 ft, so with the ridge along the long axis the roof rises about 14 ft from eaves to ridge; on a ~7 ft eaves wall that puts the ridge at roughly 20-22 ft, which agrees with surviving farmhouses (~6-7 m).

The shadow, computed for the season that matters. Threshing and drying are 9th-10th month work. At 38N (mid-empire on the weather skill's east-coast analog) in the 10th month, a 20 ft ridge casts:

solar time sun elevation shadow
noon 43 deg 21 ft
10:00 / 14:00 35 deg 28 ft
9:00 / 15:00 27 deg 39 ft
8:00 / 16:00 17 deg 65 ft

The decision: a yard needs 39 ft of clear ground to its south - the 9-to-3 window, which is the drying day that matters. Inside ~21 ft the yard is shaded even at noon. The rule is a keep-out corridor south of every threshing yard, and a neighboring FARMHOUSE may not stand in it.

The same rule holds for groves. A strip south of each yard is kept clear of a neighbor's grove for the same reason - a grove there would shade it - and a farmhouse is taller than a grove clump and shades further.

The row pitch has the room. Rows of homesteads are pitched 92 ft apart, while house depth (28) + yard depth (~26) + 39 ft of sun comes to ~93, so the spacing reserves the corridor; the corridor rule is what keeps a neighbor out of it.

The departure we take knowingly: the corridor is measured on the yard's own cross-slope span, as a rectangle, not as a true solar wedge that swings through the day; and real yashiki lots also resolved this by STAGGERING east-west rather than by spacing rows, which the map is free to do, since the rule forbids the shadow, not the neighbor.

The garden's sun, and how far the windbreak shades

Sources: derived from the threshing-yard entry above; the bamboo strip's side is a GUESS - no readable page supports it, and ja.wikipedia 屋敷林 puts Tonami's bamboo on the south25

What prompted it (GM 2026-08-25): "there is not enough space for sunlight to hit the gardens and thrashing yards ... The Windbreak Forest ... is so close to the gardens ... that I do not believe that those gardens would get sufficient sunlight."

The garden takes the yard's corridor - one number, not two. A dooryard garden's binding season is the shoulder month too: autumn greens and daikon are in the ground under the same 28 deg 9am sun that dries the rice, so a minka's ~20 ft ridge shades a bed 39 ft off exactly as it shades a yard. The growing-season figure is shorter (a 60-70 deg noon sun in the 4th-7th months throws 7-12 ft), so the shoulder binds, as it does for the yard. Rule: 39 ft of clear ground south of every bed, both directions at placement. The bed is side-dependent (SE/SW/E/W), so a bed the SE would put in a shadow moves to the other flank.

The belt's height, from the record. Searched: igune (Sendai/Osaki), kainyo (Tonami), tsuijimatsu (Izumo), Musashino yashikirin, Okinawa fukugi, Chinese fengshui woods. Found: the Sendai igune planting list classes sugi/keyaki/kuromatsu/hinoki as "tall trees" at >= 15 m, sub-canopy ~10 m, shrubs ~3 m;26 Tohoku sources put a mature skeleton at 20 m+; the one MEASURED igune (Osaki, drone survey, 2022) is "about 10 m", tall sugi on the west with a lower layer to 8 m (unsourced);27 Tonami's kainyo "over 10 m", sugi 2-3 rows thick on the S and W (unsourced);28 Izumo's tsuijimatsu pines are clipped to 8-12 m every 4-5 years (unsourced), the one attested topping practice;29 Okinawa fukugi belts average 7-10 m.30 Kainyo/igune are limb-pruned (edauchi), never height-capped. So the attested band is ~10 m for a working belt, 15-25 m for an untended mature stand.

Where the plots stood, from the record. The Tonami model homestead (Research Institute, 1996; no publicly readable source, searched 2026-09-06): house faces E, away from the SW wind; the front (E) yard is the work yard, "securing adequate open space" with only fruit trees and a persimmon in the yard center; S and W carry 2-3 rows of sugi; the N/W bamboo strip is "shady ... always damp" and given to the kitchen drain and service sheds. (A GUESS - no readable page carries this sentence, and ja.wikipedia "屋敷林" puts Tonami's bamboo on the SOUTH side with the storehouses - see the vegetation research.) Tohoku: the S-facing open ground in front is the drying yard. So the record's answer is that the HOUSE BODY was the spacer - plots on the sunlit lee side, the belt behind - and it holds no measured plot-to-treeline distance. For a nucleated village's communal belt the record is silent altogether. Hence the number is DERIVED, as the 39 ft was.

The shadow, computed for the season that matters. At 38N in the shoulder month the 3pm sun stands 28 deg high at azimuth ~232, so a belt throws 1.9 x its height to the NORTHEAST - a 10 m (33 ft) belt ~63 ft, of which ~50 ft reaches eastward; a 15 m belt ~95 ft / ~75 ft. In the 7th month the same hour gives ~35 ft / ~50 ft. The shoulder binds.

The decision: 50 ft of clear ground west and southwest of every yard and bed (the 3pm shadow starts from the southwest, so the keep-out runs from a plot's north edge to 50 ft below its south edge), enforced when the belt is planted (a clump that would shade a plot is refused and re-seated deeper, and the belt's near face is moved out by the lane where the wind puts the belt west). Windbreak-role groves only: the copse is the dooryard's own persimmon and bamboo, which the record puts IN the yard.

75 ft was tried first and declined - a ruling, not a taste. 75 is the same geometry at 15 m, the class floor of an untended stand. At 75 the belt must stand so far off the west rank that it falls outside the frame the hard features set, and the frame does not open for the belt (GM 2026-07-20: the communal windbreak clips at the view edge). Measured on Inashiro: at 75 the belt fell from 131 clumps to 38 and was no longer continuous. At 50 it stands whole (81 clumps, continuous) inside the frame. What is accepted: a belt kept at working height. Two rulings conflicted - a belt that stands off the plots (2026-08-25) and a frame that does not open for the belt (2026-07-20) - and the GM reconciled them the next day: "treat the innermost edge of the windbreak forest as being something that is preserved". The belt's inner FACE sets the frame with the standard 48 px margin; the belt's depth still clips. Measured: the front row shows whole (48 px at the face), no clump is drawn wholly off-page, per-band visible canopy 5-48 px (the thin bands are where the connector crosses the belt's line, a form matter, not the crop). What was priced and declined: letting the windbreak set the frame (reverses the 2026-07-20 ruling - the GM's to reopen, not a session's); a lane scaled to 15 m without moving the frame (measured: the belt is dropped). Who chose: this was settled on the measurement, and the GM on 2026-08-26 chose to open the frame at the face rather than shrink or raise the belt - 10 m stays as the record's measured working height.

Departures taken knowingly: the lane is a square, not a solar wedge that swings through the afternoon (the yard's south corridor takes the same shortcut); the 12 px stand-off of the belt's face is an average plot overhang, and the clump filter is the guarantee behind it.

May a byre stand beside a wellhead?

Sources: magariya-jawiki (the stable projecting on the south face, joined to the house and warmed from its hearth); the well inside the doma or a rear projection is not on the page, and is unsourced

Answer: yes, and the vernacular puts them far closer than our maps do.

On Kashikawa a shared byre stands 38 ft from a communal wellhead.

Japan (the closest analogue, and the strongest signal). In the magariya L-plan farmhouse the draft animal lives under the house's own roof: the stable wing (umaya) meets the dwelling (omoya) at right angles and extends off its SOUTH face (the Tono basin's arrangement - the article records both handed forms, and the magariya as the house of the better-off),31 deliberately taking the best sunlight, "indicating how valuable horses were" (unsourced). And the well (ido), where a house had one, sat "in the rear corner of the earthen-floored doma or in a rear projection room" - i.e. inside the same building. A household's horse and a household's well were therefore separated by the width of a farmhouse, on the order of 20-40 ft, as a matter of course. This is also why these maps draw no European multi-stall barn.

China (the guiding star). Vernacular villages co-located the animal facilities with the latrine - cattle sheds, chicken houses and pigsties grouped with the privy - because both ends of that group feed the same manure economy that kept the soil fertile for four thousand years ("treasure nightsoil as if it were gold"). That is a positive siting rule about the muck cluster, and it is worth knowing; what it is NOT is a rule holding livestock away from drinking water. No separation doctrine turned up in the geomantic or vernacular-layout material.

Elsewhere, corroborating. The public watering trough at a village or town water point - "a trough, preferably with a well and a pump" - is a widespread and well-documented arrangement. Where communities invested in water infrastructure at all, watering the beasts AT it was the norm rather than a transgression.

What is genuinely true and worth not over-reading: livestock near a shallow, unsealed well IS a real contamination vector, and modern rural-water studies in China measure exactly that. But that is a public-health finding about the consequences, not evidence that the builders sited to avoid it. A generator that separated byre from well would be drawing a modern sanitary intuition, not a Rokugani village.

The decision, therefore: the beasts are watered at the well, and a byre near one is correct. A byre never overlaps the wellhead itself.

Is every farmhouse reached by a lane, and in what FORM?

Answer: access is decisive (implement it); its FORM has two supportable shapes (make it a knob).

Decisive: a house in a nucleated cluster IS reached by a way. The Chinese material is explicit - "the organization of the village plan as a gridiron of narrow lanes is functionally the most efficient form of compact settlement", and "every house in the nucleated village is accessible via the interconnected system of narrow lanes and alleys". This is not a planner's ideal imposed after the fact; it is what compactness is FOR. The lanes are also socially live rather than purely circulatory - the narrow lateral ones are "colonised as semi-private space by the adjoining house", which is why they are narrow, irregular, and sometimes barely more than the gap between two walls.

Two supportable forms for delivering that access. Both are attested, and neither dominates:

  1. Alleys off the spine. Narrow lateral lanes branch from the through-lane between house plots and run back to serve the rank behind. This is the Chinese gridiron-of-lanes form, and the one whose laterals get colonised as semi-private space by the houses they pass.
  2. A back lane. A way parallel to the main street, behind the plots, serving their rear. This is documented as a planned-village device - "back lanes on each side of the main street which, together with the main street itself, provides a rectangular framework" - and it typically "divided the village from the main agricultural area", i.e. it doubles as the field-ward edge. Rear-access ground behind the housing lots is separately documented in traditional Manchu villages in northeast China,32 so this is not a purely European shape.

Note what distinguishes them: a back lane implies PLANNING (someone laid the framework out at once, and the plots are regular), while alleys off a spine imply ACCRETION (each household cut its own way to the road, and the result is irregular). A Rokugani hamlet can plausibly be either, so this is exactly the axis the project wants varied between maps rather than settled once.

Therefore: the generator must guarantee every farmhouse is served by a way, and chooses between the two forms per settlement.

Sources: the lane-gridiron and semi-private-lateral findings are from the nucleated-village morphology literature; the back-lane framework from planned-village morphology (see SOURCES.html); rear-access in Manchu villages from Ushijima, "Spatial composition and premise arrangement of traditional Manchu village in Northeast China", Japan Architectural Review (2020) (a GUESS - no publicly readable source, searched 2026-09-06).

The GM asked the question the other way round (2026-08-27). Looking at Inashiro: "a bunch of random scattered lanes strewn about without much rhyme or reason ... a short section of lane, between three farmhouses. It does not really connect to anything on either end ... I would have expected something like a lane leading to the reference hamlet and then probably just not even anything between the farmhouses. Is that right? Is that wrong? What does our research show?" The record above already answers both halves. (1) A lane to the hamlet and NOTHING between the farmhouses is a real form - it is the DISPERSED hamlet of the next section (散村 sankyoson, each steading reached from the field paths, no village street), and it is a knob value, not a correction. (2) For a NUCLEATED cluster, which is what Inashiro rolled, the record is decisive the other way: "every house in the nucleated village is accessible via the INTERCONNECTED system of narrow lanes and alleys" - the lanes are the point of compactness, and they are one network. So the research says the FORM on Inashiro is right: the lanes are one network, and a junction is where two treads MEET. Label: the interconnected web is ACCURATE for a nucleated cluster; a lane that runs along a garden fence to reach its junction is ACCURATE too (the plot fronts the lane; the 7 ft gap the map leaves between a lane and a garden fence is a drawing convenience, not a finding).

How does a village lane bend?

Sources: desire-path-enwiki, ninety-nine-pi-desire

Answer: like a line feet wear - as few turns as the plots allow, none of them sharp, and never back on itself. Decisive on the principle; the thresholds are drawing conventions.

The GM, on Inashiro: "there are at least 2 places on the map where the zig-zagging looks unnatural. There's a place where it looks like a loop-de-loop, which isn't how a lane would look. And then there's another place where it zig-zags just below the loop de loop for no apparent reason." Two lines of evidence, one from how paths form and one from how villages are laid out:

  • Desire-line research. Wikipedia "Desire path" and 99% Invisible: "as few as 15 passages over a site can be enough to create a distinct trail, the existence of which then attracts further use";33 Wikipedia cites Hampton and Cole 1988 for the figure;37 the path "usually represents the shortest or the most easily navigated route";34 and desire paths are not necessarily straight - they follow least resistance, sidestepping slopes and obstacles.35 A GUESS - no readable page supports it: that walkers "consciously or unconsciously minimize the number and severity of turns".36 What the read sources DO support is enough for the rule: a worn path is the shortest or easiest route between two points, so a switchback within a few paces - which is neither - is a path nobody walks, and a path nobody walks is never worn.
  • Village morphology. The lanes of a nucleated Japanese or Chinese village are the gaps left between household plots - the "gridiron of narrow lanes" of the lane-access entry above - so a lane bends at a PLOT CORNER and runs straight between corners. A bend has a reason on the ground (a fence, a bed, a yard); a bend with nothing at its elbow is a drawing artifact.

The rule: pull every lane taut like a string (a straight chord within the lane network's own margins, or right up to the buildings' footprints when that only removes jogs within 6 ft of the old line); cut a hairpin's arm when it is under 40 ft; join ends within 25 ft of one another at ONE point; cut a tail that runs on past a crossing for under 40 ft; and make each junction once. Labels: the principle ACCURATE (a worn path minimizes its turns; a bend sits at a plot corner); 140 degrees / 50 degrees within 40 ft / 25 ft / 6 ft are DRAWING thresholds chosen at the scale of a dozen paces, not findings.

How close does a farmhouse stand to the paddy? Up against it - but never on the bund

Sources: pmc7538448-levee, paddy-field-enwiki; the eave-gap figure is unsourced

The GM: "One of the farmhouses in the reference hamlet appears to actually be touching the edge of the rice paddy fields. Is this realistic? ... I do imagine that they would be pretty much right up against the edge, but actually touching looks wrong to me."

What the record gives, and what it does not. No source states a setback in feet - the question is not one anyone measured - so the floor is DERIVED from three attested parts:

  • The bund (aze) is a thing, not a line. PMC 7538448: rice paddy levees "are constructed and maintained to retain water in the paddies and to allow the passage of people and transportation of tools",38 and "farmers generally maintain levee grasslands by periodic mowing".39 Wikipedia "Paddy field": plots "separated by bunds approximately 10 cm in height" (a Korean example).40 A GUESS - no readable page supports it: the IRRI Rice Knowledge Bank's "How to construct bunds" says "bunds should be constructed no wider and taller than 50 cm x 30 cm41 ... high enough (at least 20 cm) to avoid overflowing". Pulling the other way: FAO's Fish Culture in Rice Fields, ch. 7 (fao.org/4/ac180e/AC180E07.htm) gives a permanent "dry bund" a 25 cm minimum height (60 recommended) and a 180 cm base tapering to 60 cm42 - a different, heavier structure than a field partition. So "about 1.5 ft" is one convention among several, and the 6 ft floor below leans on it lightly.
  • The house's roof reaches past its wall. A thatched farmhouse's eaves overhang the wall by about 3 ft (unsourced); its drip line is where the wall's rain lands.
  • The plot fronts the paddy. The nucleated cluster stands on the field margin with its face to the water (the 背山面水 seat - beishan mianshui, "back to the hill, facing the water" - research above), so "right up against the edge" is the norm the GM expects.

Therefore a wall nearer than bund + path + eave stands with its roof over the levee path and its drip line in the rice. 1.5 + ~1.5 + 3 ft is 6 ft, the floor a wall may never cross - a MINIMUM below the 10-13 ft the map draws, so nothing else on the map moves.

Labels: the levee's footpath role ACCURATE (read); the bund's width a GUESS (no readable page supports it); the 6 ft floor a DERIVED threshold - and a soft one, since two of its three parts are not read; "up against the edge" ACCURATE.

What stood on a farmstead - the inventory, with numbers

Sources: sugiura-1973-fuzoku (the one quantified source)

The GM: "What things would exist on a noticeable percentage of farmhouses that we are not currently representing on our maps ... What, if anything, are we missing?"

The one quantified source. Does a hamlet have to be NUCLEATED at all?

Answer: no. Three forms are supportable, so the form becomes a seeded knob, and the access rule above is true of exactly one of them.

The entry above settled that a house in a nucleated cluster is reached by a way. It did not ask whether a hamlet must be nucleated. The GM challenged the assumption from the other end on 2026-08-23, asking whether lanes should be laid before houses at all when in life they are trodden by the households already living there. That question turns out to be the same question: a settlement whose lanes are pre-laid can only be one whose houses front lanes.

DISPERSED (散村 sankyoson) - decisive, and our terrain is its terrain

The Tonami Plain in Toyama is the canonical Japanese case: over 7,000 farmhouses scattered across roughly 220 km² on an alluvial fan built by the Shogawa and Oyabe rivers, a pattern more than 500 years old.53

The mechanism is irrigation, and it is the mechanism our own maps model. Farmers built their houses in the middle of their own cultivated fields in order to manage water for those fields directly.54 An alluvial fan drains well - which is a PROBLEM for wet paddy, not a benefit - so water control is per-holding and unremitting, and living on your holding is how it is done. The farmhouses scattered naturally rather than by any plan. Edo-period land grants to those who had reclaimed the land then entrenched the pattern (unsourced).

Each house sits in its own grove. The regional term for the homestead woodland is kainyo; it shelters the house from winter seasonal winds and snowstorms, and from summer sun.55 Note this alongside the yashikirin entry above - both terms are real, kainyo is the Tonami one for exactly this feature, and the two describe the same thing at different regional scales.

Why this matters here: the field pattern these maps draw is an alluvial fan's, and a nucleated settlement shelters behind ONE grove rather than per-house belts - so the dispersed form's most visible signature is the road not taken.

Consequence for the access rule: a dispersed hamlet has no interconnected lane network to be reached by. The rule in the entry above is a rule about nucleated settlements.

LINEAR (路村 / row village) - supportable, but the weakest of the three

Settlements strung along a linear feature - a road, a riverbank, a valley floor - are a standard morphological category. The German Reihendorf is the best-documented type: one or two rows of farmsteads either side of a village street, each holding's farmland adjacent to its dwelling, which saves travel and transport effort. That functional argument transfers directly to a rice hamlet strung along a track between its paddies.

Recorded limitation: the English-language record for the specific term 路村 (roson, row village) was thin, and the strongest documentation of the row form is European. The functional logic is not culturally specific, and elongated road- and river-following forms ARE attested in the Chinese village-morphology literature (settlements in mountainous southwest Zhejiang "tend to expand in the direction of rivers and roads"). But this is weaker ground than the dispersed case, so the linear form is drawn less often than the dispersed one.

Consequence: linear is the one form in which the road genuinely comes first, and therefore the one form whose houses legitimately front a pre-existing way - the connector, which is exogenous.

What this changed in the generator

The connector and the field spur predate the settlement; the lanes inside it are the households' own.

Does a DISPERSED hamlet's outlying farm have its own well? Yes - and the question was never the GM's

Sources: visit-toyama-sankyoson, mdpi-sho-fan-groundwater

The finding. On the Tonami Plain in Toyama - the canonical Japanese dispersed settlement, ~7,000 farmhouses over ~220 km2 - farmers "used to build their houses in the middle of their cultivated rice fields so that they could easily manage the water for their own rice fields"56. The dispersal is not incidental to water; water is the REASON for it. Each house also carries its own kainyo homestead grove, so the farmstead is a self-contained unit by construction. The plain is an alluvial fan, where shallow groundwater is a mixture of river water and precipitation - a high, easily-reached water table (the record's inference - the paper gives no water-table depth; a GUESS),57 which is what makes a per-farmstead well cheap.

The answer. A shared well with a reach radius is a NUCLEATED settlement's arrangement: it presupposes a center to be near. A dispersed farmstead has no center to share with - that is what dispersed MEANS - so it carries its own water. The 500 ft reach rule is not "unenforced at hamlet scale"; it is the wrong rule for this form.

The consequence: a nucleated hamlet shares wells within reach; a dispersed one gives each farmstead its own. That is the same shape as the lane-access rule, which is conditional on the settlement's form rather than waived.

The outhouse faces the SUN, not away from the wind - and 72.7% of them do

The question, and the hypothesis it killed. A map review found every privy on Sawada standing UPWIND of its own house (11 of 12 north-east, 1 east) and proposed seating them downwind. That is the intuitive rule, and it is wrong. The record says the opposite consideration governs.

What the record says. Wang & Ochiai surveyed farmhouses in Arakawa village, Shiga - a windstorm-prone settlement, so a place where wind-conscious siting would show if it existed anywhere: "toilets, as important sources of fertilizer for the paddy fields in the past ... tended to be located in southeast and south directions, with a total percentage at 72.7%, as a relatively warm temperature helped quick fermentation of excrements."85 Night soil was a crop input, not a nuisance to be blown away; the sun on that side sped the composting.

And the wind finding in the same paper is about something else. It covers "storage buildings and retirement houses in the southwest and west directions ... thus forming wind fences to protect the open space in front of the house entrance"86 - and even there the paper frames the placement as sunlight-driven (the front yard wants sun) with the wind-fence effect as a consequence rather than the cause. The words leeward, downwind, windward, odor and hygiene do not appear in the article at all.87 No source, in English or Japanese, was found stating any general wind rule for koedame or benjo siting.

The rule this produces. A privy's share on the sun side is 0.727 - the GM's ruling (2026-08-29) was to use the figure literally rather than round it. The three attested seats (back door, 戸口便所 at the gate, beside the naya) keep their own weights as the tiebreak WITHIN each group, so a homestead that cannot put a privy to the southeast still seats it where the record says privies go.

How the seat is found. The privy is put on the sun side wherever there is clear ground, as near the house as it can be. The GM asked whether the yard leaves room: the real farmsteads this 72.7% comes from had threshing yards too, so why can ours not do what they did? Measured in answer, on Sawada: every one of the 19 houses has free sun-side ground - 49 to 151 clear spots each, the nearest 24-32 ft out, the same radius the privy uses on its north-east side. The yard blocks a slice of a 90-degree arc, not the side.

How far out, and why the realized share is NOT tuned to the target. The source gives a DIRECTION and no distance, and the three attested seats are all against the house - so the radius is ours to choose, and choosing it to hit 72.7% would be fitting the map to the statistic rather than to the place. Past it the privy walks out beyond its own work yard and, in a cluster where the next farmhouse is 50 ft away, out of its own homestead: at 72 ft an acceptance review measured 15 of 86 privies and manure pits nearer ANOTHER house than the one they serve, against 0 of 52 before the sun-side search - a legibility defect. A reader attributes a fixture to the nearest house whatever the record says.

So the realized share is 46% against a 72.7% rule, and the gap is the work yard: the near sun side is the threshing floor, and a privy may not stand on it. Closing it honestly would mean putting the privy on the sun side before the yard and gardens are placed, not widening the radius until the number comes out right.

The muck heap that reads as the neighbor's

The researched rule is that the heap lies BEYOND the privy (see "The farmstead's fixtures"). Once the privy moved to the sun side88, that rule sometimes puts the heap nearer a neighbor's farmhouse than the one it serves. Measured over the 13-map pool, 6 of 66 privies and heaps stand nearer a neighbor's drawn FOOTPRINT than their own - the number that matters, because a reader compares against the drawn rectangle rather than against a recorded center point. Ownership therefore stays a TIE-BREAK, promoting an unambiguous seat whenever the ground offers one.
