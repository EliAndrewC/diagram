Homesteads: the research behind the farmhouse, yard, garden and grove rules

The findings behind the farmhouses, work yards, gardens, groves and village forms on these maps, the decisions they drove, and - where no generator draws the feature yet - the rules a map follows.

Homestead groves (yashikirin) - the real scale and prevalence

Sources: kashima-kainyo-1987 (the 1987 survey's figures - 1,542 trees over 46 households, 48% sugi1), yashikirin-jawiki (the west-and-south sentence), isa-auf-fukugi-2011 (Okinawa's north-and-east groves, the cross-check that makes the side a knob), visit-toyama-sankyoson (what the Tonami plain is)

  • Historical scale - the real numbers. A homestead grove is a substantial STAND, not a few trees. The best hard data is a 1987 survey of Kashima in the Tonami plain2 (the dispersed-farmstead country whose homestead woodlands are the kainyo,3 46 households): ~33 trees of trunk diameter >= 10 cm per homestead (1,542 trunks over the 46 households - arithmetic on the two footnoted figures, not a number the page states), of which cedar (sugi) was ~48% (~16 cedars per house4), the rest spread over ~83 other species;5 ~6 species per homestead (range 1-14);6 for a large homestead, 200+ trees across 31 species is a GUESS at the top of the band, resting on no source7 - the Tonami archive page behind the figures above was read in full and carries nothing like it - so it is a GUESS at the top of the band. That count is trunks >= 10 cm ONLY - it EXCLUDES the bamboo stand (one stood on every homestead, and a count of trunks 10 cm and over does not reach a culm),8 saplings, and the shrub layers below the canopy (the page sorts the stand into four layers by height and counts 44 / 76 / 80 / 153 species from the tallest down)9 - so the honest figure for a typical grove is ~30-40 mature trees + a bamboo grove + understory, and a big one 100-200+. The grove canopy footprint is therefore the LARGEST homestead appurtenance - bigger than the farmhouse, and far bigger than the garden or threshing yard - wrapping the windward faces as a belt. How DEEP the belt is has no firm number in the record - the "2-3 rows of sugi" below is itself unsourced - so the drawn arm depth (13 ft, one to two crowns) is a GUESS. Which faces the belt takes is rolled per map, because the record gives more than one answer: on the Tonami plain the tall sugi stand from the south face round to the west, with hackberry, alder and a little bamboo from the west round to the north, and the general account of the mainland yashikirin puts the tall trees on the west and south;10 the maps put the belt on the northwest by default; a map with a Tonami-like wind takes the southwest arms. The map draws EVERY canopy tree, one crown at its real size on real spacing (Forest density and crown size: a mean crown radius of 8.5 ft on ~13 ft centers, no crown seated under another), on a footprint that is itself to scale - exactly as the farmhouse, the threshing yard and the garden are drawn at their true size. The grove's dominance is therefore not a glyph calibration; it falls out of drawing the real thing: clearly the dominant homestead feature, a dense stand of dozens of trees, never a garden-sized clump of 5-10. (Cross-check on the windward rule: Okinawa's homestead groves sit on the E/N sides, because there the damaging winds come from the north (the monsoon) and the east (typhoons),11 not the mainland's west and south - same logic, different geography, which is exactly why the windward side is a per-map knob.)

How big was the work yard, and how did the sizes spread?

Sources: kitamoto-mushiro-niwa, kamikanai-1771-houses, kikoba-kenchi, santome-shinden-allotment, yonekura-mushiro, tobunken-mushiro, irri-drying-floor, ndl-kokumori

The GM, on the map's own modal: "I see the threshing yard size is listed as a rendering convention. How large WERE these yards?" A figure of 100-300 tsubo (330-992 sq m) that is easy to mistake for the yard is the homestead LOT (Akishima city history: farms need a wide front yard, so lots run 100-300 tsubo) - a real number that belongs to a different thing.

The size, as households themselves stated it. Kitamoto (Saitama) city history, folk volume: 「At harvest time straw mats (mushiro) were spread over the whole of the yard, and the size of the yard was often expressed by the number of these mats. It was related to the area under barley cultivation; ordinarily forty to sixty mats, and among them there were households that exceeded a hundred. Incidentally, two mats correspond to one tsubo.」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「収穫期には庭一面にムシロが敷かれ、しばしば庭の広さはこのムシロの枚数で表現された。麦の耕作面積と関係 していて、普通四〇〜六〇枚、中には百枚を越す家もあった。ちなみに、ムシロ二枚が一坪にあたる。」) - the yard was measured in straw mats, 40-60 usually and past 100 for a few, two mats to the tsubo: 20-30 tsubo (66-99 sq m) ordinarily, past 50 tsubo (165 sq m) at the top12, and explicitly sized by the household's cropped acreage.13 Two independent lines agree: an Okayama museum records ~50 mats per farm,14 and the measured mat is 90 x 180 cm,15 so ~80 sq m; a directly measured yard at Kodaira is 70 tsubo (231 sq m) - a large Musashino holding,16 the upper end. No survey found tabulates yards as areas; these are the readable figures.

But Kitamoto is a BARLEY district, and this generator draws wet rice. Its yard is sized by the mugi crop, which the household spreads whole. Rice is field-dried on hazakake racks for 10-14 days100 before it reaches the yard and is threshed in batches over days, so a paddy household needs less standing floor. Deriving it from the crop instead - 1.3 koku/tan (中田 石盛, chuden kokumori, the middle-paddy standard yield)17 -> 247 kg momi at a 79% hulling yield -> mats at the International Rice Research Institute (IRRI)'s 2.5 cm spread,18 batched - gives 55-100 sq m for a full cho and 35-65 for five tan. The generator therefore centers a rice hamlet's yards at 18 tsubo (59.5 sq m) and keeps Kitamoto's 25 tsubo as the dry-field figure. The GM ruled between the two readings on 2026-08-28: "I agree with option two" - the sourced SHAPE everywhere, the sourced SCALE appropriate to what the household dries.

The spread is lognormal, and it is right-skewed. No survey gives a histogram of yards, so the shape comes from what the cadastres do tabulate, and every one is right-skewed: Kamikanai (1771) gives a complete main-house histogram whose19 31 commoner houses fit a lognormal of median 22.5 tsubo and sigma 0.46 in log space, with the headman detached at 3.1x; Kikoba's Genroku 検地帳 (kenchicho, the land-survey register) puts homestead lots at 20-100 bu with two peaks, at 30 bu and 50 bu (the page: 「Homestead lot areas are distributed between 20 bu and 100 bu」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「屋敷反歩は20歩から100歩の間に分布している」)「with 30 bu and 50 bu showing the peaks」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「30歩と50歩がピークを示し」); no comparable figure for great holders was found - the page's only "2倍" (twofold) concerns house COUNT, not lot area).20 Kitamoto's own band-and-tail implies sigma 0.35-0.45 - that convergence is what the drawn sigma of 0.40 rests on. A floor exists but is not zero: early registers carry a no-homestead class (無屋敷登録人, muyashiki torokunin), but by Genroku 「all peasant households, including those holding no fields, came to have a house plot」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「田畑を保有しない百姓も含め全百姓が屋敷を持つようになり」)101 - the landless simply occupy the small end, so the roll is floored at 8 tsubo rather than allowed to vanish.

The yard follows the household, but not proportionally. The proportional coupling is ADDITIVE - 「if the holding increases by about 5 koku, the tsubo count increases by about 10 tsubo」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「持高が5石ほど増加すれば坪数が10坪ほど増加する」)21 - so a 20x holder does not get a 20x yard (the sentence is from the same paper's paragraph on Kyoho-era Iwahara village; of Kamikanai itself the paper says the relation to holding size "is not marked", and holdings inside the village span landless to about 30 koku).22 The generator gives the yard the household's own deviation in log space, amplified and then perturbed by an independent positional draw: a large household is overwhelmingly likely to have a large yard, a mismatch is possible and rare - the GM's own reading of what the record implies.

One attested form is NOT skewed, and is a knob. A planned shinden colony issued every settler an identical homestead - Santome 1696, "屋敷の規模はまったくの均等配分"23 - so an "allotted" yard setting draws uniform yards. Two attested forms become a per-settlement knob.

What this means on the map. The yard is rolled per household from the distribution above, so every hamlet that dries rice has to find room for it beside the house.

The threshing yard's sun, and how far a farmhouse shades

Sources: derived - solar geometry at 38N for the 10th month, and the minka's 46 x 28 ft footprint; kayabuki-jawiki (the steep pitch as a material requirement; the 45-degree figure and the 6-7 m ridge of surviving farmhouses are not on the page, and are unsourced)

What prompted it (GM 2026-08-13): "Would threshing fields be directly to the north of another house? Or would the shadow from the farmhouse directly to the south block too much light?"

The house's height, DERIVED rather than assumed. A thatched (kayabuki) roof has to be pitched steeply, and more steeply the coarser the stems - that is what the material demands,24 and it is why a minka carries such a large dark loft. Our minka is 46 x 28 ft, so with the ridge along the long axis the roof rises about 14 ft from eaves to ridge; on a ~7 ft eaves wall that puts the ridge at roughly 20-22 ft, which agrees with surviving farmhouses (~6-7 m).

The shadow, computed for the season that matters. Threshing and drying are 9th-10th month work. At 38N (the empire's temperate middle latitudes) in the 10th month, a 20 ft ridge casts:

solar time sun elevation shadow
noon 43 deg 21 ft
10:00 / 14:00 35 deg 28 ft
9:00 / 15:00 27 deg 39 ft
8:00 / 16:00 17 deg 65 ft

The decision: a yard needs 39 ft of clear ground to its south - the 9-to-3 window, which is the drying day that matters. Inside ~21 ft the yard is shaded even at noon. So a strip of ground south of every threshing yard is kept clear, and a neighboring FARMHOUSE may not stand in it.

The same rule holds for groves. A strip south of each yard is kept clear of a neighbor's grove for the same reason - a grove there would shade it - and a farmhouse is taller than a grove clump and shades further.

The row pitch has the room. Rows of homesteads are pitched 92 ft apart, while house depth (28) + yard depth (~26) + 39 ft of sun comes to ~93, so the spacing reserves the corridor; the corridor rule is what keeps a neighbor out of it.

The departure we take knowingly: the corridor is measured on the yard's own cross-slope span, as a rectangle, not as a true solar wedge that swings through the day; and real yashiki lots also resolved this by STAGGERING east-west rather than by spacing rows, which the map is free to do, since the rule forbids the shadow, not the neighbor.

The hand-drawn maps are deliberately exempt, and the exemption cannot rot. Every hand-authored clustered map on the sheet breaks this rule: 45 of 85 yards shaded at noon on Ueda, 31 of 70 on Hoshigaoka, 27 of 55 on Kikuta, 21 of 36 on Ubame, 19 of 44 on Hoshizora, and 3 to 10 on each of the hamlets, with a neighbor's wall commonly 2-8 ft off a yard's edge; the provincial cities are clear (0 or 1 each), because their farm belt is loose. Re-packing those maps by hand was judged the wrong trade by the GM on 2026-08-13 - it would move every feature on a finished exhibit to satisfy a rule written after it was drawn - so the rule binds a map the moment a generator draws it, and a hand-drawn map keeps its packing until it is converted to a generated one. The alternative that was priced and declined is a list of exempt maps, which somebody then has to prune; nothing is listed, and the rule simply arrives with the generator. The cost of accepting it is stated plainly: on those maps a yard that would be in shadow is drawn anyway.

The garden's sun, and how far the windbreak shades

Sources: derived from the threshing-yard entry above; the bamboo strip's side is a GUESS - no readable page supports it, and ja.wikipedia 屋敷林 puts Tonami's bamboo on the south25

What prompted it (GM 2026-08-25): "there is not enough space for sunlight to hit the gardens and thrashing yards ... The Windbreak Forest ... is so close to the gardens ... that I do not believe that those gardens would get sufficient sunlight."

The garden takes the yard's corridor - one number, not two. A dooryard garden's binding season is the shoulder month too: autumn greens and daikon are in the ground under the same 28 deg 9am sun that dries the rice, so a minka's ~20 ft ridge shades a bed 39 ft off exactly as it shades a yard. The growing-season figure is shorter (a 60-70 deg noon sun in the 4th-7th months throws 7-12 ft), so the shoulder binds, as it does for the yard. Rule: 39 ft of clear ground south of every bed, both directions at placement. The bed is side-dependent (SE/SW/E/W), so a bed the SE would put in a shadow moves to the other flank.

And the morning sun, which a neighbor's grove takes. The bed sits on a sheltered flank, and a neighbor's windward belt stands against that neighbor's west wall - which is to say just EAST of our bed, in the way of the rising sun. Morning light is what a kitchen bed lives on, and the fix is placement rather than felling a windbreak: the GM asked in 2026-07 to "move it a bit south". Two things keep the east open. The communal copse keeps a sun lane to its east as well as to its south, so no clump of it is ever seated directly east of a bed; and once every homestead grove is drawn, an east-shaded bed is nudged a little south into open ground, so the trees fall to its northeast instead. It is a best effort and not a slot - most farms keep the plain east bed, and a bed hemmed in to the south by paddy, lane or neighbor stays where it is - so the rule is broken only where a clear southward shift existed and was not taken. Two things the move has to get right: it clears EVERY arm to its east in one step, because a short shift can slip a bed out of one arm's shade and straight into a taller arm just south of it; and it is measured against the footprints actually drawn, so a bed may drift into the margin of a neighbor's reserved ground but may never touch a drawn house, yard, bed or grove.

The belt's height, from the record. Searched: igune (Sendai/Osaki), kainyo (Tonami), tsuijimatsu (Izumo), Musashino yashikirin, Okinawa fukugi, Chinese fengshui woods. Found: the Sendai igune planting list classes sugi/keyaki/kuromatsu/hinoki as "tall trees" at >= 15 m, sub-canopy ~10 m, shrubs ~3 m;26 Tohoku sources put a mature skeleton at 20 m+; the one MEASURED igune (Osaki, drone survey, 2022) is "about 10 m", tall sugi on the west with a lower layer to 8 m (unsourced);27 Tonami's kainyo "over 10 m", sugi 2-3 rows thick on the S and W (unsourced);28 Izumo's tsuijimatsu pines are clipped to 8-12 m every 4-5 years,102 the one attested topping practice;29 Okinawa fukugi belts average 7-10 m.30 Kainyo/igune are limb-pruned (edauchi), never height-capped. So the attested band is ~10 m for a working belt, 15-25 m for an untended mature stand.

Where the plots stood, from the record. The Tonami model homestead (Research Institute, 1996; no publicly readable source, searched 2026-09-06): house faces E, away from the SW wind; the front (E) yard is the work yard, "securing adequate open space" with only fruit trees and a persimmon in the yard center; S and W carry 2-3 rows of sugi; the N/W bamboo strip is "shady ... always damp" and given to the kitchen drain and service sheds. (A GUESS - no readable page carries this sentence, and ja.wikipedia "屋敷林" puts Tonami's bamboo on the SOUTH side with the storehouses - see the vegetation research.) Tohoku: the S-facing open ground in front is the drying yard. So the record's answer is that the HOUSE BODY was the spacer - plots on the sunlit lee side, the belt behind - and it holds no measured plot-to-treeline distance. For a nucleated village's communal belt the record is silent altogether. Hence the number is DERIVED, as the 39 ft was.

The shadow, computed for the season that matters. At 38N in the shoulder month the 3pm sun stands 28 deg high at azimuth ~232, so a belt throws 1.9 x its height to the NORTHEAST - a 10 m (33 ft) belt ~63 ft, of which ~50 ft reaches eastward; a 15 m belt ~95 ft / ~75 ft. In the 7th month the same hour gives ~35 ft / ~50 ft. The shoulder binds.

The decision: 50 ft of clear ground west and southwest of every yard and bed (the 3pm shadow starts from the southwest, so the keep-out runs from a plot's north edge to 50 ft below its south edge), enforced when the belt is planted (a clump that would shade a plot is refused and re-seated deeper, and the belt's near face is moved out by the lane where the wind puts the belt west). Windbreak-role groves only: the copse is the dooryard's own persimmon and bamboo, which the record puts IN the yard.

75 ft was tried first and declined - a ruling, not a taste. 75 is the same geometry at 15 m, the class floor of an untended stand. At 75 the belt must stand so far off the west rank that it falls outside the frame the hard features set, and the frame does not open for the belt (GM 2026-07-20: the communal windbreak clips at the view edge). Measured on Inashiro: at 75 the belt fell from 131 clumps to 38 and was no longer continuous. At 50 it stands whole (81 clumps, continuous) inside the frame. What is accepted: a belt kept at working height. Two rulings conflicted - a belt that stands off the plots (2026-08-25) and a frame that does not open for the belt (2026-07-20) - and the GM reconciled them the next day: "treat the innermost edge of the windbreak forest as being something that is preserved". The belt's inner FACE sets the frame, standing off the page edge by the same margin every other feature gets; the belt's depth still clips. Measured: the front row of clumps shows whole, its face clear of the edge by that full margin; no clump is drawn wholly off-page; and every band of the belt shows canopy, from a sliver to the margin's full width (the thin bands are where the connector crosses the belt's line, a form matter, not the crop). What was priced and declined: letting the windbreak set the frame (this reverses the GM's ruling of 2026-07-20); a lane scaled to 15 m without moving the frame (measured: the belt is dropped). Who chose: this was settled on the measurement, and the GM on 2026-08-26 chose to open the frame at the face rather than shrink or raise the belt - 10 m stays as the record's measured working height.

Departures taken knowingly: the lane is a square, not a solar wedge that swings through the afternoon (the yard's south corridor takes the same shortcut); the small stand-off the belt's face keeps from the plots is an average plot overhang, and the clump filter is the guarantee behind it.

How big was a dooryard garden?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-21 that everything on these maps is drawn at its true size; this project's measurement of the drawn hamlets; kateisaien-jawiki (the name of the bed a household grows its own food on), shakkanho-jawiki (the tsubo and the se in square meters); no work is cited for the area band

The saien is the small intensive bed by the kitchen that feeds a household its daily greens - daikon, onions, beans, herbs - and nothing beyond them; the ground a Japanese household cultivates for its own consumption has its own name, the yashikibatake, the homestead field.89 Bulk vegetables and dry crops grew out in the household's own hatake dry fields, not here, which is why the bed is a matter of a few tsubo rather than of tan. The band the map holds a household's beds to is a few tsubo up to about 1.4 se, which is roughly 10 to 140 sq m (one tsubo is about 3.31 sq m; one se is 30 tsubo, about 99 sq m).90 Below the band the bed reads as no garden at all, and above it as a field. The same reading holds on the Chinese side: a rice-south farmhouse's dooryard plot was the same small intensive kitchen bed, with the bulk growing out in the household's fields. (The band and the Chinese comparison are both GUESSES: the article that names the bed gives no area for it, and no readable page was found that gives one or that measures the Chinese plot.)91

The band is a real quantity rather than a drawing habit because the bed is drawn at TRUE size at every tier, exactly as the house and the work yard are - the GM's ruling of 2026-07-21 that these maps draw things at the size they were. Nothing on a homestead is enlarged against the fields to make it read.

The rule the map follows: a household's garden is drawn at true size, and its beds TOGETHER are held to 10-140 sq m - a split plot is still one household's garden, so the beds are summed. The drawn hamlets land at about 35-115 sq m per household, centered near 55 sq m (about half a se), comfortably inside the band.

May a byre stand beside a wellhead?

Sources: magariya-jawiki (the stable projecting on the south face, joined to the house and warmed from its hearth); the well inside the doma or a rear projection is not on the page, and is unsourced

Answer: yes, and the vernacular puts them far closer than our maps do.

On Kashikawa a shared byre stands 38 ft from a communal wellhead.

Japan (the closest analogue, and the strongest signal). In the magariya L-plan farmhouse the draft animal lives under the house's own roof: the stable wing (umaya) meets the dwelling (omoya) at right angles and extends off its SOUTH face (the Tono basin's arrangement - the article records both handed forms, and the magariya as the house of the better-off),31 deliberately taking the best sunlight, "indicating how valuable horses were".103 And the well (ido), where a house had one, sat "in the rear corner of the earthen-floored doma or in a rear projection room" - i.e. inside the same building. A household's horse and a household's well were therefore separated by the width of a farmhouse, on the order of 20-40 ft, as a matter of course. This is also why these maps draw no European multi-stall barn.

China (the setting's model). Vernacular villages co-located the animal facilities with the latrine - cattle sheds, chicken houses and pigsties grouped with the privy - because both ends of that group feed the same manure economy that kept the soil fertile for four thousand years ("treasure nightsoil as if it were gold"). That is a positive siting rule about the muck cluster, and it is worth knowing; what it is NOT is a rule holding livestock away from drinking water. No separation doctrine turned up in the geomantic or vernacular-layout material.

Elsewhere, corroborating. The public watering trough at a village or town water point - "a trough, preferably with a well and a pump" - is a widespread and well-documented arrangement. Where communities invested in water infrastructure at all, watering the beasts AT it was the norm rather than a transgression.

What is genuinely true and worth not over-reading: livestock near a shallow, unsealed well IS a real contamination vector, and modern rural-water studies in China measure exactly that. But that is a public-health finding about the consequences, not evidence that the builders sited to avoid it. A generator that separated byre from well would be drawing a modern sanitary intuition, not a Rokugani village.

The decision, therefore: the beasts are watered at the well, and a byre near one is correct. A byre never overlaps the wellhead itself.

And a byre does not always stand against its own farmhouse. Both arrangements are attested, and neither is the correction of the other. A household that OWNS its team houses it in its own homestead - the magariya's short arm IS the stable, and the north-China courtyard house puts its animals in the EAST wing, the one the summer afternoon sun and the winter northwesterly make unfit to live in, which takes the stable, the kitchen and the grain store together92 - while a team that is SHARED or hired stands where the borrowing household can walk to it, on the common ground among the homesteads.104 Two supportable forms are rolled per settlement rather than settled once, which is also what lets two maps differ honestly: on a courtyard map the byres follow the wealthiest households and each stays within its owner's own yard, and on a commons map, the commoner of the two, they stand out among the homesteads where a borrower can reach one. A flat claim that a byre always abuts its farmhouse is not supported by the record and is not what the map draws.

How big is a draft-animal byre, and what stands in it?

Sources: fao-farm-structures-cattle (shade and stall dimensions per animal) and tnau-cattle-housing (covered and open space per adult beast), both modern cattle standards; this project's drawing decision

Wet-rice plowing and puddling turn on an ox or a water buffalo,105 and the shed drawn for one is a single-bay open-fronted lean-to of about 16 by 11 ft, some 15 sq m. That is the room one or two draft animals need and no more. Nothing that can be read measures a premodern byre: the only figures available are modern housing standards, and what a standard states is a generous minimum - the room a keeper today is told to provide for a modern beast - rather than the size of anything anyone built. One such standard, a design manual for the tropics, reckons a shade structure at 2.5 to 3 sq m per animal93 and a single stall at 2.2 m by 1.2 m for a cow of 500 to 600 kg.94 Checked against a second - an Indian extension table allowing an adult beast 3.5 sq m under cover with 7 sq m of open ground beside it and 60 to 75 cm of manger space95 - it comes out on the same order of size, which says the figure is a common one rather than one page's idea of it; both are modern recommendations, so their agreement cannot stand in for a measurement neither of them made. A 15 sq m shed holds one or two beasts by either allowance, with fodder and a passage beside them, and it is sized by that allowance rather than by anything attested. The size is doing a second job as well. Anything larger starts to read as a European multi-stall barn, and there are none on these maps, because the draft animal of an East Asian wet-rice farm lives under the farmhouse roof (the question above). A byre on the sheet is a shelter for a beast or two, kept visibly well under the farmhouse's own 120 sq m.

The rule the map follows: a byre is drawn as a small plank-roofed shed of about 16 by 11 ft with a dark stall mouth, distinct from the solid gray storehouse and from the dwellings, and it is kept small on purpose.

Is every farmhouse reached by a lane, and in what FORM?

Answer: access is decisive (implement it); its FORM has two supportable shapes (make it a knob).

Decisive: a house in a nucleated cluster IS reached by a way. The Chinese material is explicit - "the organization of the village plan as a gridiron of narrow lanes is functionally the most efficient form of compact settlement", and "every house in the nucleated village is accessible via the interconnected system of narrow lanes and alleys". This is not a planner's ideal imposed after the fact; it is what compactness is FOR. The lanes are also socially live rather than purely circulatory - the narrow lateral ones are "colonised as semi-private space by the adjoining house", which is why they are narrow, irregular, and sometimes barely more than the gap between two walls.

Two supportable forms for delivering that access. Both are attested, and neither dominates:

  1. Alleys off the spine. Narrow lateral lanes branch from the through-lane between house plots and run back to serve the rank behind. This is the Chinese gridiron-of-lanes form, and the one whose laterals get colonised as semi-private space by the houses they pass.
  2. A back lane. A way parallel to the main street, behind the plots, serving their rear. This is documented as a planned-village device - "back lanes on each side of the main street which, together with the main street itself, provides a rectangular framework" - and it typically "divided the village from the main agricultural area", i.e. it doubles as the field-ward edge. A traditional Manchu village in northeast China is NOT evidence for the back lane: there the ground behind the lots was opened by a state resettlement in about 1968, and the older row is described as courtyard joined to courtyard along the street. What that village does show is a village of this kind having more ways than its main street - two or three running east and west, supplemental lanes crossing them north and south, and blind alleys of 2 to 4 m reaching the lots32 - so a lane web is not a purely European shape, even though a lane serving the REAR of a row is not attested there.

Note what distinguishes them: a back lane implies PLANNING (someone laid the framework out at once, and the plots are regular), while alleys off a spine imply ACCRETION (each household cut its own way to the road, and the result is irregular). A Rokugani hamlet can plausibly be either, so this is exactly the axis the project wants varied between maps rather than settled once.

Therefore: every farmhouse is served by a way, and which of the two forms a settlement takes is rolled per map.

Sources: the lane-gridiron and semi-private-lateral findings are from the nucleated-village morphology literature; the back-lane framework from planned-village morphology (the works are listed in the sources registry); the hierarchy of ways in a nucleated village - two or three streets running east and west, supplemental lanes crossing them, and blind alleys of 2 to 4 m reaching the lots - from ushijima-2020-manchu, the measured survey of a traditional Manchu village in northeast China, which describes the older row as courtyard joined to courtyard along the street and dates the opening of the ground BEHIND the lots to a resettlement of the late 1960s - so it carries the lane hierarchy and not a back lane.

It does not really connect to anything on either end ... I would have expected something like a lane leading to the reference hamlet and then probably just not even anything between the farmhouses. Is that right? Is that wrong? What does our research show?" The record above already answers both halves. (1) A lane to the hamlet and NOTHING between the farmhouses is a real form - it is the DISPERSED hamlet of the next section (散村 sankyoson, each steading reached from the field paths, no village street), and it is a knob value, not a correction. (2) For a NUCLEATED cluster, which is what Inashiro rolled, the record is decisive the other way: "every house in the nucleated village is accessible via the INTERCONNECTED system of narrow lanes and alleys" - the lanes are the point of compactness, and they are one network. So the research says the FORM on Inashiro is right: the lanes are one network, and a junction is where two treads MEET. A lane that runs along a garden fence to reach its junction belongs to that network too, because the plot fronts the lane; the 7 ft gap the map leaves between a lane and a garden fence is a drawing convenience, not a finding.

How does a village lane bend?

Sources: desire-path-enwiki, ninety-nine-pi-desire

Answer: like a line feet wear - as few turns as the plots allow, none of them sharp, and never back on itself. Decisive on the principle; the thresholds are drawing conventions.

The GM, on Inashiro: "there are at least 2 places on the map where the zig-zagging looks unnatural. There's a place where it looks like a loop-de-loop, which isn't how a lane would look. And then there's another place where it zig-zags just below the loop de loop for no apparent reason." Two lines of evidence, one from how paths form and one from how villages are laid out:

  • Desire-line research. Wikipedia "Desire path" and 99% Invisible: "as few as 15 passages over a site can be enough to create a distinct trail, the existence of which then attracts further use";33 Wikipedia cites Hampton and Cole 1988 for the figure;37 the path "usually represents the shortest or the most easily navigated route";34 and desire paths are not necessarily straight - they follow least resistance, sidestepping slopes and obstacles.35 Walkers also prefer the route that turns least: pedestrian movement across four areas of London tracks an angular measure of route choice, because walkers are taken to have "angular preferences of fewest turns or least angle changes".36 The read sources are enough for the rule: a worn path is the shortest or easiest route between two points, so a switchback within a few paces - which is neither - is a path nobody walks, and a path nobody walks is never worn.
  • Village morphology. The lanes of a nucleated Japanese or Chinese village are the gaps left between household plots - the "gridiron of narrow lanes" of the lane-access entry above - so a lane bends at a PLOT CORNER and runs straight between corners. A bend has a reason on the ground (a fence, a bed, a yard); a bend with nothing at its elbow is a drawing artifact.

The rule: pull every lane taut like a string (a straight chord within the lane network's own margins, or right up to the buildings' footprints when that only removes jogs within 6 ft of the old line); cut a hairpin's arm when it is under 40 ft; join ends within 25 ft of one another at ONE point; cut a tail that runs on past a crossing for under 40 ft; and make each junction once. The 140-degree and 50-degree turn limits, and the 40 ft / 25 ft / 6 ft distances, are drawing thresholds chosen at the scale of a dozen paces rather than measured findings.

How close does a farmhouse stand to the paddy? Up against it - but never on the bund

Sources: pmc7538448-levee, paddy-field-enwiki; the eave-gap figure is unsourced

The GM: "One of the farmhouses in the reference hamlet appears to actually be touching the edge of the rice paddy fields. Is this realistic? ... I do imagine that they would be pretty much right up against the edge, but actually touching looks wrong to me."

What the record gives, and what it does not. No source states a setback in feet - the question is not one anyone measured - so the floor is DERIVED from three attested parts:

  • The bund (aze) is a thing, not a line. PMC 7538448: rice paddy levees "are constructed and maintained to retain water in the paddies and to allow the passage of people and transportation of tools",38 and "farmers generally maintain levee grasslands by periodic mowing".39 Wikipedia "Paddy field": plots "separated by bunds approximately 10 cm in height" (a Korean example).40 The IRRI Rice Knowledge Bank's "How to construct bunds" tells a farmer to "Construct no wider and taller than 50 cm x 30 cm bunds, around the field".41 Pulling the other way: FAO's Fish Culture in Rice Fields, ch. 7 (fao.org/4/ac180e/AC180E07.htm) gives a permanent "dry bund" a 25 cm minimum height (60 recommended) and a 180 cm base tapering to 60 cm42 - a different, heavier structure than a field partition. So "about 1.5 ft" is one convention among several, and the 6 ft floor below leans on it lightly.
  • The house's roof reaches past its wall. A thatched farmhouse's eaves overhang the wall by about 3 ft;106 its drip line is where the wall's rain lands.
  • The plot fronts the paddy. The nucleated cluster stands on the field margin with its face to the water (the 背山面水 seat - beishan mianshui, "back to the hill, facing the water" - research above), so "right up against the edge" is the norm the GM expects.

Therefore a wall nearer than bund + path + eave stands with its roof over the levee path and its drip line in the rice. 1.5 + ~1.5 + 3 ft is 6 ft, the floor a wall may never cross - a MINIMUM below the 10-13 ft the map draws.

The bund's width is a GUESS, since no readable page supports it, so the 6 ft floor is a soft threshold: two of its three parts are not read.

What makes the headman's house different?

Sources: the GM's rulings of 2026-07-21; no work is cited for what the office kept in store

Its size, and almost nothing else. The GM ruled on 2026-07-21, on a map that had drawn the headman as a special case, that the headman's house is a FARMSTEAD and not an exception: the grandest house in the village gets its work yard and its dooryard garden like every other farm, and both are capped at an ordinary farm's sizes, because a big house does not mean a field-sized kitchen bed. On a scattered map the headman's homestead brings its grove too, where there is room for one. What makes the house read as the headman's is that it is plainly the largest - about 92 by 56 ft against a plain farmhouse's 46 by 28 - and not a different plan, a compound wall or a gate.

What the office does add is a storehouse. The village headman (shoya) is by definition among its most prosperous farmers, and the office needs fireproof storage in a way an ordinary farm does not: the village's tax ledgers, its land registers and the tax rice waiting for collection are exactly what a kura is for (this rests on general reading; no source is cited).96 So the headman always has one, where an ordinary farm carries one only sometimes (the question below). The GM ruled that on 2026-07-21; left on the ordinary roll, every headman on the sheet had come up bare, which is chance reading as doctrine.

A map drawing convention, and the reason for it: the headman's house is drawn as a plain farmhouse enlarged rather than with the large-house glyph, because that glyph carries a storeroom wing that stands outside the ground the homestead reserves and lands on a neighbor's work yard.

Which farmhouses have a storehouse?

Sources: the Miyagi survey quoted in the farmstead inventory below; this project's measurement of the drawn maps

A minority, and that is the whole point of it. A fireproof kura was expensive, so only well-off farmers and landlords had one, and it reads on the map as a wealth marker precisely because most farms lack one. A simpler naya outbuilding was commoner but still not on every plot: many households stored inside the farmhouse's own earth-floored doma, and the poorest households had little to store. The one counted figure the record here offers is a storehouse on 0.24 of pre-1944 households in the Miyagi survey below (see the farmstead inventory).

The share the map draws is about 30%, and that is a calibrated choice rather than a finding. It sits just above the counted 0.24 - one district in one decade - and it is chosen so that a storehouse stays uncommon enough to mean something at a glance. Measured over the drawn maps, the realized share is 28.4%, 343 of 1,208 farmhouses.

The roll is positional, and what that costs was accepted on 2026-08-17. Whether a given farm has a storehouse is decided from where its house stands rather than from the order the houses were drawn in, so the same map drawn twice gives the same answer. The cost is that a re-packing which moves a house re-rolls its storehouse: one re-pack took a hamlet from 25% to 15% in a single roll, which read like storehouses disappearing. The arithmetic behind the roll is honest - measured at 0.2993 over 200,000 positions - so what changed was which houses drew it. The declined alternative is keying the roll to a household number instead, which would hold across a re-packing; it was not taken.

The rule the map follows: about three farmhouses in ten carry a storehouse, drawn as an annex against the house wall rather than as a free-standing shed - on the WEST wall of a scattered farm, and on the shaded NORTH wall in a cluster, where the sunny walls belong to the garden and the north is the one side the garden never takes. The headman always carries one (the question above), and the storehouse's ground is reserved with the rest of the homestead, so a neighbor never lands on it.

Why is a farmhouse longer than it is deep?

Sources: no work is cited for the bay reasoning or the band; this project's drawing decision

No two farmsteads were copies of one another, so the houses on a map vary - but they vary along their LENGTH and hardly at all across their DEPTH. A minka grew by adding bays to its long axis: another bay under the same ridge, an aisle down one side. It got longer, not much wider, and it never became a square or a four-to-one shed. So a clustered map varies a house's length by roughly 0.85 to 1.35 of its base and its depth by only 0.90 to 1.10, and the proportions that come out stay inside the range a minka keeps, about 1.3 to 2.5 long to deep; past about 2.7 to 1 the house is drawn wrong rather than drawn differently. (This rests on general reading of minka plans; no source is cited for the band.)97

A scattered map varies the same thing from the other end. There the whole homestead takes a wealth standing - poorer, ordinary or better-off - which scales the house and its grove together, so a richer farm reads bigger across the board. That scaling reaches what is DRAWN and not what is reserved, so it never moves a neighbor and never changes how many households fit on the map.

The rule the map follows: farmhouses differ in size and in proportion within a modest range, and a farmhouse is never drawn squarer than about 1.3 to 1 or longer than about 2.7 to 1. The largest house on the map is the headman's (the question above).

Does a hamlet have to be NUCLEATED at all?

Answer: no. Three forms are supportable, so the form becomes a seeded knob, and the access rule above is true of exactly one of them.

The entry above settled that a house in a nucleated cluster is reached by a way. It did not ask whether a hamlet must be nucleated. The GM challenged the assumption from the other end on 2026-08-23, asking whether lanes should be laid before houses at all when in life they are trodden by the households already living there. That question turns out to be the same question: a settlement whose lanes are pre-laid can only be one whose houses front lanes.

DISPERSED (散村 sankyoson) - decisive, and our terrain is its terrain

The Tonami Plain in Toyama is the canonical Japanese case: over 7,000 farmhouses scattered across roughly 220 km² on an alluvial fan built by the Shogawa and Oyabe rivers, a pattern more than 500 years old.53

The mechanism is irrigation, and it is the mechanism our own maps model. Farmers built their houses in the middle of their own cultivated fields in order to manage water for those fields directly.54 An alluvial fan drains well - which is a PROBLEM for wet paddy, not a benefit - so water control is per-holding and unremitting, and living on your holding is how it is done. The farmhouses scattered naturally rather than by any plan. Edo-period land grants to those who had reclaimed the land then entrenched the pattern.107

Each house sits in its own grove. The regional term for the homestead woodland is kainyo; it shelters the house from winter seasonal winds and snowstorms, and from summer sun.55 Note this alongside the yashikirin entry above - both terms are real, kainyo is the Tonami one for exactly this feature, and the two describe the same thing at different regional scales.

Why this matters here: the field pattern these maps draw is an alluvial fan's, and a nucleated settlement shelters behind ONE grove rather than per-house belts - so the dispersed form's most visible signature is the road not taken.

Consequence for the access rule: a dispersed hamlet has no interconnected lane network to be reached by. The rule in the entry above is a rule about nucleated settlements.

A scattered farm is measured against the rice that is DRAWN, not against the field the map claims. Every farm in a scattered settlement fronts its own fields, so each house has to stand within reach of one - and a field has two extents that can disagree. The water net claims a smoothed envelope; the planted plots stop where the drainage stops them, whatever the map asked for. The gap between the two is a tail of empty ground, invisible on the sheet and fully real to any distance measured from the envelope, so a farmstead hugging that tail reads as field-adjacent while standing well past the last rice. The GM saw the result on a hand-drawn hamlet in 2026-07 - "a single line of farmhouses that stretches much further south than the rice paddies themselves do", "there are still 3 farmhouses south of the paddies" - and the cause was a claimed field about 180 ft longer than the comb could fill. The answer was to size the claim to what is actually planted, not to nudge the farms. Two hand-drawn villages carry tails of about 420 ft and keep them on purpose: their houses are seated as one cluster and never ride the envelope, so a tail there changes nothing, and reshaping two finished fields to satisfy a rule that protects nothing on them would be churn.

The rule the map follows: a scattered settlement needs roughly TWICE the margin room per farm that a clustered one does - each homestead carries its own grove and keeps its own spacing, and the whole line is hugged to the field edge in single file - so a settlement declared at a clustered village's household count will not fit that many scattered farms in the same margin. Draw a scattered settlement on a roomier field, or declare fewer households; do not compress the homesteads to make them fit. This is a rule about fitting the drawing, not a finding about how far apart scattered farms stood.

LINEAR (路村 / row village) - supportable, but the weakest of the three

Settlements strung along a linear feature - a road, a riverbank, a valley floor - are a standard morphological category. The German Reihendorf is the best-documented type: one or two rows of farmsteads either side of a village street, each holding's farmland adjacent to its dwelling, which saves travel and transport effort. That functional argument transfers directly to a rice hamlet strung along a track between its paddies.

Recorded limitation: the English-language record for the specific term 路村 (roson, row village) was thin, and the strongest documentation of the row form is European. The functional logic is not culturally specific, and elongated road- and river-following forms ARE attested in the Chinese village-morphology literature (settlements in mountainous southwest Zhejiang "tend to expand in the direction of rivers and roads"). But this is weaker ground than the dispersed case, so the linear form is drawn less often than the dispersed one.

Consequence: linear is the one form in which the road genuinely comes first, and therefore the one form whose houses legitimately front a pre-existing way - the connector - the road that reaches the settlement from outside, which was there before the houses.

What this changed in the generator

The connector and the field spur predate the settlement; the lanes inside it are the households' own.

Why does one village look nothing like the next?

Sources: the geomantic siting question below; this project's reading of the drawn villages; no work is cited for the field-system and market groundings

A village is built from a handful of choices rolled from the map's own seed, each carrying a rule about when that value is even possible, so that a combination comes out coherent instead of merely various. The choices are rolled independently rather than drawn as whole village "types": independent draws give the widest variety and make it natural to fix one thing by hand and roll the rest, and what keeps the result honest is the rule on each choice, not the bundle it came in.

What each choice rests on. WHERE the cluster sits: a village took whatever non-arable higher and drier ground lay beside its paddy, and "back to the hill, face the water" says which way it turns (the question below) - but which corner of a valley that is depends on the valley, so the high windward margin is one common case and not the only one. Its SHAPE followed the dry ground it had: a knoll gives a round blob, a levee a string, a field edge a crescent, two flanking patches a split village. Its LANES followed its site: a spine along a ridge, a T where a spur met the field track, a Y where two approaches merged, a cross at a small market node - the 草市 (caoshi, "grass market"), the informal periodic country market that grew up away from the walled towns - and a lane along the water in a stream village. Its WATER entered from wherever the catchment could deliver it above the fields, which is not always the same corner, and a small village often had a chain of little ponds rather than one reservoir. Its PLOTS took their size and their regularity from how the land had been divided: tiny hand-shaped plots in fields that grew up organically, rectilinear blocks where a survey had laid a grid over them (the 条里 jori system in Japan, the allotment remnants in China), long strips under strip tenancy. And the GRAIN of the paddy drifts off the fall line, because real valleys let it. (The field-system and market groundings rest on general reading; no source is cited for them.)98

The biggest difference between two villages is the settlement's FORM. When the drawn villages were audited for pairs that read as copies - pairs sharing a water direction, compared on eight coarse structural axes, which side and which kind rather than which pixel - the pairs that still looked like one place twice were the ones varying only the internal detail of a shared archetype. A pair could differ on four of the eight and still read the same, because a cross of lanes against a spine of lanes is a detail, while a ribbon against a blob is not. That is why the form of the settlement - clustered, strung along a line, scattered, or built on the water - was made an axis of its own and counts as the largest structural read there is. The GM's own eye confirmed the calibration: once one of the two villages was re-rolled as a ribbon along its field margin with a drifted field grain, against the other's clustered blob and its geomantic pond, they differed on five axes and read as two places.

The rule the map follows (village tier): a village rolls its cluster's position and its shape, its lane skeleton, the position of its water source, its plot size and regularity, and the drift of its field grain from its own seed, and a map may fix any one of them by hand. A rolled value its own rule forbids is never drawn: a lane along the water needs water running ALONG the cluster; a split village needs room for two hamlets to read apart, so a hamlet never splits; a gridded plot pattern needs a surveyed field; an entry edge needs a stream and a pond corner needs a pond; and a water source below the fields it feeds is refused outright, because water does not run uphill. A hamlet rolls none of these.

How tightly does a nucleated village pack?

Sources: this project's measurement of the drawn villages

A clustered village is a compact FABRIC and not a wide hollow arc, and one drawn village came out as the arc: a thin string of houses around an empty middle. The cause was not the shape the houses were seeded in - it was its WIDTH. The placer pulls every homestead in to hug the paddy edge and then packs it along that edge, so a wide seed cloud gets strung out by that pull while a narrow deep one packs into a blob. Narrowing the crescent's lateral reach and letting DEPTH carry the frontage fixed it: built coverage went from 0.20 to 0.31 and the cluster's span halved, from about 1,900 ft to about 1,070 ft.

The calibration, and an honest limit. Coverage here means how much of the ground inside the houses' own outline is actually BUILT - houses, gardens, work yards and homestead groves. The pathological arc measures 0.20; the rolled villages that look right measure 0.28 to 0.31; the tightly hand-placed villages measure about 0.40. A floor of 0.25 sits clear below the healthy band and above the pathology, which is exactly what it is for: it catches the pathology, and it does not demand hand-placed density. The rolled placer tops out near 0.31, because hugging and packing along an edge is looser than a person seating houses one at a time, and that is accepted - a few back-row houses still stand about 700 ft from the crops, which reads as the honest back of a compact village rather than as a stranded arc.

The rule the map follows (village tier): a clustered village of a dozen houses or more keeps at least a quarter of the ground inside its own outline built. A hamlet is legitimately loose and is not held to it, and neither is a cluster too small for the measure to mean anything.

What this measure cannot see, and must be re-calibrated for before the next village rolls. The measure counts homestead groves as built ground, and the grove's own footprint is the bounding box of the house cloud - so the numerator and the denominator move together, and a clump refused because a house stands there relocates to the next gap instead of dropping. A cluster that SPREADS therefore grows its grove into the gaps it opened, and the coverage figure can rise while the built fabric thins. Measured on the hamlets of 2026-09-13: one cluster's grove went from 22 clumps to 246 with no change in its household count, its coverage on this definition rose from 0.294 to 0.339, and its houses-gardens-yards fabric alone fell from 0.279 to 0.130 while its outline more than doubled. The 0.25 floor above was calibrated on villages whose groves had not relocated, so it should be re-measured - or stated with and without groves - before it is applied to a village again. No village ships today, and hamlets are exempt by the sentence above, so nothing currently drawn turns on it.

Why is there a crescent pond in front of some villages, and why is it labeled?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-21; no work is cited for the pond's own doctrine

What it is. The half-moon pond (半月塘, banyuetang) dug at the front of a village is GEOMANTIC and not religious: no deity, no rites, nobody worships at it. It is cosmological engineering of the same kind as facing a house south. A well-sited village wants the hill behind it and water in front of it - 背山面水, beishan mianshui - and where the back half is the high ground with its grove, the front half is water; where no river obliges, the village DIGS it. Still water is held to gather and keep a lineage's fortune where running water carries it away, and the HALF shape is deliberate: a complete thing can only wane, so the shape leaves the lineage room to grow. The grove arc behind and the water arc in front cradle the settlement as one system. In practical terms it is the rain-fed runoff basin, deliberately NOT connected to the irrigation, and the village's fire water, its fish and duck water and its washing water, with the flat bank serving as the open forecourt where threshing and ceremony happen. The shrine is where religion happens; the pond is how a well-sited village is SHAPED. (This rests on general reading of geomantic village siting; no source is cited for it.)99

Why it carries a label when almost nothing else does. These maps do not label what a reader can see for themselves - no "gate", no "north", no naming of an ordinary road - and this pond is the one deliberate exception, ruled by the GM on 2026-07-21: the half-moon pond is culturally specific and reads as nothing but a pond-shaped pond to a reader who has not met one. "Geomantic pond" is the GM's own wording for the label, chosen over a religious one.

The rule the map follows: where a village digs one, the pond is drawn as a half-disk at the front of the settlement with its flat bank toward the houses, that bank left open as the forecourt, and it labels itself "geomantic pond". No vegetation touches its water: the groves keep clear of the whole disk, and the scrub and reed scatter skips it as it skips any other water.

Does the village's name say where it stands?

Sources: the GM's general rule of 2026-07

A Rokugani place name usually says something about where the settlement sits relative to its fields, its water or its ground, and the GM made it a general rule in 2026-07 that the name is read BEFORE the settlement is seated: "the map should be able to be read back into the name". The worked example is Ueda (上田, "upper paddy"), whose paddies are the upslope ones - so its dwellings stand DOWNSLOPE of them, facing up-valley toward the fields. On that map's northeast-high ground that puts the cluster on the LOWER, southwest flank with the bulk of the paddy rising above it, which is the opposite of the default reading, a village on the high flank.

Other cues worth reading the same way: shimo- (下, lower) says the reverse of Ueda; kawa- or -gawa (川, river) puts the settlement hard by the watercourse; and yama- (山, mountain), saka (坂, slope), hara (原, plain or moor), tani or -dani (谷, valley floor), sawa (沢, a marshy stream), hama (浜, shore) and -guchi (口, a mouth or entrance or pass) each name the ground the place stands on. Where the name says nothing about the ground, the default holds.

The rule the map follows: a settlement whose name names its ground is seated to match the name, and the default - a clustered village on the dry high flank, hugging its paddy - applies only where the name is neutral. No generator reads a name; this is a rule for whoever specifies the map.

Does a DISPERSED hamlet's outlying farm have its own well? Yes - it carries its own water

Sources: visit-toyama-sankyoson, mdpi-sho-fan-groundwater

The finding. On the Tonami Plain in Toyama - the canonical Japanese dispersed settlement, ~7,000 farmhouses over ~220 km2 - farmers "used to build their houses in the middle of their cultivated rice fields so that they could easily manage the water for their own rice fields"56. The dispersal is not incidental to water; water is the REASON for it. Each house also carries its own kainyo homestead grove, so the farmstead is a self-contained unit by construction. The plain is an alluvial fan, where shallow groundwater is a mixture of river water and precipitation - a high, easily-reached water table (the record's inference - the paper gives no water-table depth; a GUESS),57 which is what makes a per-farmstead well cheap.

The answer. A shared well with a reach radius is a NUCLEATED settlement's arrangement: it presupposes a center to be near. A dispersed farmstead has no center to share with - that is what dispersed MEANS - so it carries its own water. A 500 ft reach from a shared well is the wrong rule for this form, not a rule that has been let slide.

The consequence: a nucleated hamlet shares wells within reach; a dispersed one gives each farmstead its own. That is the same shape as the lane-access rule, which is conditional on the settlement's form rather than waived.

The outhouse faces the SUN, not away from the wind - and 72.7% of them do

The question, and the hypothesis it killed. A map review found every privy on Sawada standing UPWIND of its own house (11 of 12 north-east, 1 east) and proposed seating them downwind. That is the intuitive rule, and it is wrong. The record says the opposite consideration governs.

What the record says. Wang & Ochiai surveyed farmhouses in Arakawa village, Shiga - a windstorm-prone settlement, so a place where wind-conscious siting would show if it existed anywhere: "toilets, as important sources of fertilizer for the paddy fields in the past ... tended to be located in southeast and south directions, with a total percentage at 72.7%, as a relatively warm temperature helped quick fermentation of excrements."85 Night soil was a crop input, not a nuisance to be blown away; the sun on that side sped the composting.

And the wind finding in the same paper is about something else. It covers "storage buildings and retirement houses in the southwest and west directions ... thus forming wind fences to protect the open space in front of the house entrance"86 - and even there the paper frames the placement as sunlight-driven (the front yard wants sun) with the wind-fence effect as a consequence rather than the cause. The words leeward, downwind, windward, odor and hygiene do not appear in the article at all.87 No source, in English or Japanese, was found stating any general wind rule for koedame or benjo siting.

The rule this produces. A privy's share on the sun side is 0.727 - the GM's ruling (2026-08-29) was to use the figure literally rather than round it. The three attested seats (back door, 戸口便所 at the gate, beside the naya) keep their own weights as the tiebreak WITHIN each group, so a homestead that cannot put a privy to the southeast still seats it where the record says privies go.

How the seat is found. The privy is put on the sun side wherever there is clear ground, as near the house as it can be. The GM asked whether the yard leaves room: the real farmsteads this 72.7% comes from had threshing yards too, so why can ours not do what they did? Measured in answer, on Sawada: every one of the 19 houses has free sun-side ground - 49 to 151 clear spots each, the nearest 24-32 ft out, the same radius the privy uses on its north-east side. The yard blocks a slice of a 90-degree arc, not the side.

How far out, and why the realized share is NOT tuned to the target. The source gives a DIRECTION and no distance, and the three attested seats are all against the house - so the radius is ours to choose, and choosing it to hit 72.7% would be fitting the map to the statistic rather than to the place. Measured, at three radii, over the five scripted maps:

Past it the privy walks out beyond its own work yard and, in a cluster where the next farmhouse is 50 ft away, out of its own homestead: at 72 ft an acceptance review measured 15 of 86 privies and manure pits nearer ANOTHER house than the one they serve, against 0 of 52 before the sun-side search - a legibility defect. A reader attributes a fixture to the nearest house whatever the record says.

So the realized share is 46% against a 72.7% rule, and the gap is the work yard: the near sun side is the threshing floor, and a privy may not stand on it.

The muck heap that reads as the neighbor's

The researched rule is that the heap lies BEYOND the privy (see "The farmstead's fixtures"). Once the privy moved to the sun side,88 that rule sometimes puts the heap nearer a neighbor's farmhouse than the one it serves. Measured over the 13-map pool, 6 of 66 privies and heaps stand nearer a neighbor's drawn FOOTPRINT than their own - the number that matters, because a reader compares against the drawn rectangle rather than against a recorded center point. (Measured center to center it is 4 of 66; the two figures disagree because a fixture can clear a distant house's center while standing against its wall. The worst case is Kashikawa's heap: 32.0 ft from its own farmhouse's wall and 8.4 ft from a neighbor's, which the center metric renders as a mild 46.7 against 33.0.) A reader attributes such a pit to the nearer house; the map's own record says otherwise, and only the interactive page resolves it on click.

What is left is the geometry: where a privy stands on the sun side and the neighbor is that way too, every seat beyond it belongs to that arc, exactly as it would in a real interlocked cluster. Ownership therefore stays a TIE-BREAK, promoting an unambiguous seat whenever the ground offers one.
