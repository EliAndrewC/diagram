Fields: the research behind the paddy, plot and crop rules

The findings behind the paddies, plots and crops on these maps, the decisions they drove, and - where no generator draws the feature yet - the rules a map follows.

In-field features - flat flooded paddy hosts obstacles least

Sources: none read for the per-archetype matrix (feng-shui grave siting, terrace outcrops); the grave-island liberty is the GM's ruling of 2026-07-20.

  • What the research found. Flat, flooded valley-bottom paddy is the archetype that hosts non-rice obstacles LEAST - it is the most valuable, most intensively worked, and wet. Grave mounds and feng-shui knolls are MARGIN/slope features in the rice south (feng-shui wants a backing hill + downslope water view, so the dead go on the slope, not the wet center); rock outcrops are a TERRACE feature (bedrock the risers wrap around) and are absent on alluvial valley/polder and delta dike-pond; small OPEN-WATER ponds (a low pocket / header tameike / half-moon by a hall) are the one thing that genuinely belongs in the wet middle. The maps follow a per-archetype table of which features each field type may host.

  • CALIBRATED LIBERTY (GM 2026-07-20, disclosed). The in-field grave ISLAND (the "graves among the paddy" look) is a north-China-dry-plain / Japanese-corroborated signature, NOT the rice-south default (which is margin/slope). The GM approved "both", so it is drawn rarely (~30% on valley/terraces/ribbon) as a deliberate departure, not an oversight.

Paddy plots - irregular patchwork, and why the grid is anachronistic

Sources: kochi-seiri-jawiki (the grid is Meiji consolidation), nougyoudoboku-keihan (the standard bund), aze-standard (the bund height), tabayashi-1987 (pre-modern fields widely scattered and irregularly shaped). No source was read for the azenuri timing or the 2-5 ft azemichi.

Pre-modern paddies were fitted to the land and water by piecemeal reclamation and inheritance, so plots are odd-sized and odd-shaped with bunds meeting at T-junctions72; the tidy rectangular grid is a modern (Meiji/Showa) land-consolidation (kochi seiri) artifact1 and reads as anachronistic.

What the research found: what separated real paddies was the aze (China: tiangeng) - a puddled-mud ridge roughly 1-2 ft wide and ~1 ft high,273 re-plastered each spring (azenuri) so each basin holds its shallow sheet of water3 (how deep, and the drained stages between, in 'How deep the water actually stands' below); the walking bunds (azemichi) ran ~2-5 ft.

Bunds are SHARED, and the fabric is continuous

What the research found. The aze is the wall BETWEEN two basins, and it is built once. Three things make a second, parallel ridge with a strip of ground between it and the first impossible in practice. It doubles the azenuri - the spring re-plastering,4 the single largest maintenance job the bund network carries (unsourced - no page read ranks bund maintenance jobs against each other) - for no gain. It holds no water: neither basin's rim is improved by a wall standing off in the middle of a strip. And the strip itself is idle land inside an irrigated command area, which is the most valuable ground on the map - the same land hunger that keeps field margins down to one scythe swath (the field-margin entry in vegetation) does not tolerate a few feet of bare mud between two paddies. What real paddy fabric looks like is therefore ONE connected bund network whose lines meet at T-junctions; a free-standing four-sided ring inside it is not a paddy at all. The odd, piecemeal parcels that fabric produces are the honest look - and note that the detached, individually-walled rectangle is exactly the kochi seiri read this section already flags as anachronistic, arrived at from the other direction.

The decision it drove (GM 2026-08-17, on Inashiro: "a tiny little standalone rectangle of earthen walls is just smack dab in the middle of where the field should be ... it should basically always be the case that two adjacent rice paddies share a single earthen wall rather than two different earthen walls").

Disclosed departures. (1) A shallow lap is left alone, in both halves: a plot drawn over part of its neighbor paints out the bund it covers, so the pair still reads as one shared wall. Only near-containment is a fault. (2) The rule's upper bound is 24 real ft of gap - wider than that the ground between two basins is bare floor. (3) A pocket too thin to bund is absorbed rather than planted, matching the fan toe's existing thickness rule - a needle basin cannot be leveled or bunded at any sane cost.

A bund runs on, or it turns for a reason - it does not step sideways and carry on

What the research found. Nothing new about the aze - this is the shared-bund finding read one step further, and it is worth writing down separately because the two rules pull in opposite directions and a reader who has only one of them will break the other. The section above says real paddy fabric is odd-shaped and piecemeal, and the section head says STRAIGHT rectangular plots are the modern land-consolidation artifact: the organic waver is period-correct and must not be cleaned up. What that permits is a bund that BENDS, a basin that is a trapezoid, and a wall that runs out at a T-junction. What it does not permit, and what nothing in the record produces, is a wall that runs, hops a few feet SIDEWAYS, and carries on parallel to itself.

Three reasons, all of them the aze's own economics. The bill for a bund is its LENGTH, since azenuri is re-plastering along it every spring; a jog buys the extra run between two corners for nothing. Corners are the part of a puddled ridge that slumps and so the part that is re-plastered hardest, and a jog buys two of them. And the ground the jog trades is worth nothing to either party: it sits at the same level, floods from the same offtake and is reachable from either basin, so no farmer pays a wall's maintenance to move a boundary a few feet sideways along its own line. Where a real parcel boundary DOES step there is a thing at the corner - a ditch, a path, a rock, an inherited holding - and at the scale these maps are drawn we draw those things, so a step with nothing at its corner is not a boundary at all.

The decision it drove (GM 2026-08-18, on Inashiro: "the earthen wall is kind of going in a southward direction, and then instead of just continuing on and meeting at the four way intersection between the north south earthen walls and the east west earthen walls, it just goes sharply to the left before going down, thus making these extremely irregular shapes. This really, really looks like a rendering error"). Measured, it was one. What the fabric carries now is single, small, isolated corners.

Disclosed departure. A long sideways offset is not judged a step at all: past a certain length the offset is a LIMB and the parcel is an L, which is exactly the honest odd shape this file describes.

A basin never tapers to a point - the fan toe truncates

GM ruling 2026-08-17, closing the fan-toe SUNBURST that the backlog had carried as an open question. The question was posed honestly - "a real cascade fan does narrow to its outfall, and the honest question is whether this narrows too tidily" - and the GM's standard was simply realism.

The shape is authentic; the angles were not. Two things had to be separated before anything could be fixed, because a rule that got them backwards would have destroyed something real:

  • Radial convergence at the outfall is real and is not a defect. An alluvial cascade fan genuinely narrows toward its collector, and the bunds of the basins on it converge with it. Any rule that flattened that would be making the map less faithful.
  • Narrowness is real too. The strips at Shiroyone Senmaida and in the Philippine Cordilleras are genuinely a few feet wide and worked by hand.48 So the rule is deliberately NOT a minimum plot width - that would have been the obvious rule and it would have been wrong.
  • What no real basin does is taper to ZERO. A paddy is a level, bunded, puddled unit holding standing water at an even depth. The arithmetic is the whole argument: at a 7.5 degree apex a plot is 5 ft wide 40 ft back from its point and 2.6 ft at 20 ft, while an aze is ~1.5 ft of puddled mud49 on each side. The last yards are therefore two bunds with no floor between them - it cannot be leveled, cannot hold a depth, and cannot be transplanted. At 25 degrees the same wedge is 17 ft wide 40 ft back, which is a workable bunded strip.

What a real fan toe does instead is what this file's shared-bund section already says a real fan did with any awkward ground: the point is truncated into a headland along the collector, or the odd corner is simply left unpaddied - "the odd unplantable scrap was simply taken into the basin beside it rather than walled off on its own." The fix is therefore not new doctrine; it is the existing doctrine applied to a case that was slipping through.

The deliberate residue: a strip that needles every basin it touches stays bare, and that is the honest answer rather than a failure - the fan's base floor draws under it, so it reads as the toe's own ground. This is the same treatment the slivers dropped for thinness have always had.

Minimum basin SIZE - there is no absolute floor, and the real floor is a ratio

GM question 2026-08-17, reading a scripted hamlet: "most of the rice paddy fields are rectangular, but then there are a few very small triangles. Is that realistic? It looks like it is just a mistake, like, basically, a rendering artifact rather than something that is from our historical research. Relatedly, should there be a minimum rice paddy size? I would expect that there would be."

Sources: senmaida, bench-terrace-riser

What the research found: no absolute minimum exists

Shiroyone Senmaida on the Noto peninsula works 1,004 basins on about 4 hectares5. The average paddy is quoted at ~18-20 m2;6 many run about 1 m2 (our reading - the page gives only the average and the smallest), and the smallest is roughly half a meter square - two rice stalks50. The local anecdote is the clearest statement of the scale: two paddies once reported missing turned up under a straw raincoat that had been laid on the ground.51 Obasute carries over 2,000 small paddies; Longsheng's largest terrace is 0.62 mu (~0.10 acre) and most are far smaller. So a floor stated in acres would condemn the most famous paddies in Japan, and our own smallest scripted-hamlet basin - 240 sq ft, ~22 m2 - is larger than a typical Senmaida paddy.

The absolute floor was therefore PRICED AND DECLINED. It is the obvious rule and it is wrong, in the same way and for the same reason that a minimum plot WIDTH is the wrong rule for a fan toe.

Why those micro-basins are a TERRACE phenomenon, which is the whole finding

The discriminator is what the wall is for, and it is a physical difference, not a matter of degree.

  • On a terrace the wall already exists. A bench terrace is a level platform cut into a slope and held by a riser - a near-vertical retaining face 0.8-1.5 m high, stone-faced where stone is to hand52 - and the riser is demanded by the SLOPE whether or not anyone subdivides. Water is held by a small 20-25 cm lip on top of it (the 10-15 cm the same page gives is its water depth, not the lip).74 The marginal cost of one more tiny bench is close to nothing, and the alternative to a 2 m2 bench is bare rock. Micro-paddies follow.
  • On a valley-floor cascade fan there is no riser. The aze IS the whole structure, built only to hold water: puddled mud, re-plastered every spring (azenuri, the largest single maintenance job the bund network carries - unsourced),7 standing on a strip of the most valuable land on the map. Its cost is charged entirely to the basin it creates, and the alternative to a scrap is never "no rice" - it is making the basin next door bigger, which costs no new wall at all.

That is this file's own shared-bund answer for an awkward scrap ("taken into the basin beside it rather than walled off on its own") arrived at from the size direction. So the floor is real, it is contextual, and it is a ratio to the fan's own design cell rather than an area.

Where the ratio sits - two independent derivations, and they agree

  1. Geometry. A quarter of the design cell's AREA is half its linear size in both directions. A parcel below that is not a cell that came out small; it is a fragment of one.
  2. Cost. With the bund at 1.5 ft and half charged to each side, the bund eats a share of the ground it encloses that climbs as the basin shrinks: 8.1% at a hamlet's 38.6 ft design cell, 16.2% at 19.2 ft. A quarter of the cell is exactly the square at which that overhead has doubled. The doubling point barely moves with scale - 0.248 of the cell at 38.6 ft, 0.256 at the village's 47 ft - so the number is not scale-tuned.

Both land on 0.25, which is why it is not a compromise. So the placer refuses a basin below a quarter of its fan's design cell, and a basin is judged wrong below a fifth of it.

The residue this leaves is also worth naming: the defect the GM saw lives in that narrow band, so it is small parcels rather than absurd ones - 240-370 sq ft against a 1,488 sq ft cell. The rule is a fabric correction, not a rescue.

The cost, measured before the number was chosen

Over the 2,829 basins of the four scripted hamlets, 1.63% sit under 0.25 of their cell and 0.46% under 0.20. Nothing is lost: each such scrap is taken into the basin it shares the most bund with, so planted acreage, the field outline and the household count are all unchanged (Inashiro stays at 20.5 / 19.5 acres and 15 of 15 households).

The arrowhead: a chevron is pointed AND notched, and neither half alone can see one

What separates it is the conjunction. A basin may taper honestly (the hem strips do). A basin may be concave honestly (one wrapped round a neighbor is). One that does BOTH has a point at one end and a bite out of its side, and that is an arrowhead. Over the 2,777 carved basins of the four scripted hamlets: apex < 40 deg alone is 71 (2.6%), solidity < 0.90 alone is 49 (1.8%), the conjunction is 13 (0.47%) - small enough to read one by one, which is how the thresholds were chosen rather than fitted. Both are round on purpose: 40 deg is "comes to a point", 0.90 is "has a real notch". Fitting tightly to the motivating ring (39 / 0.88) would have caught 11 and been overfitting to one example.

The other declined alternative: paddies are NOT restricted to four sides

The GM raised this himself and doubted it, correctly. A rule that every basin be a quadrilateral was considered and DECLINED outright: it would re-impose exactly the tidy rectangular grid that this file's own "irregular patchwork" section identifies as the kochi seiri land-consolidation artifact, and it would contradict the attested look - the tanada mosaic of odd, piecemeal parcels meeting at T-junctions. A triangular basin is legitimate; several survive on every map. What was wrong was never the number of sides but the SIZE, with triangularity as its symptom, because a clipped corner of the plot lattice at the fan's boundary is the shape a fragment naturally takes.

The bund's cross-section. The bund page gives a standard trapezoid of 30 cm height and 30 cm TOP width on 1:1 slopes,8 i.e. about 1 ft high and ~3 ft at the base - the entry's "1-2 ft wide, ~1 ft high" reads as a top-to-base span and is consistent; cold regions run ~50 cm top / ~40 cm high. Nothing read supports a bund a meter high.

Why the bund shows at all in high summer, and why it is drawn dark red-brown

Sources: the bund's own dimensions, from the cross-section above; the GM's color ruling of 2026-07-24, made on a rendered ladder of five candidates.

The bund is drawn at near-true width - about 1.5 ft of ridge at whatever scale the map uses, with a floor beneath which the line would thin away to nothing when the sheet is rendered. The stroke it is drawn in was chosen deliberately. An earlier pale tan depicted 4-5 ft - an azemichi, a walking bund, on EVERY boundary - and read as an orange lattice dominating the green. The GM picked a red-leaning dark mud brown from a five-color ladder rendered on one village, over a lighter chestnut that stays more nameably brown at hairline width: a line that dark blends toward black against the rice green at map view, and the GM preferred that weight. The pale tan survives as the exposed-earth AREA fill for true-width earthworks - perimeter dikes, dike-top house pads, dike-pond banks - where the ground really is bare earth several feet across. A terrace lip takes the dark color but keeps a wider stroke, because a riser face has real projected width where a flat bund does not.

The disclosed departure - a map drawing convention. That color is honest for SPRING, when the ridge is fresh wet azenuri plaster. By high summer, which is the growth stage the paddy surfaces depict, a real bund greens over with grass and bund beans and very nearly disappears into the field (this rests on general reading; no source is cited)80. So a visible bund network at that season is a stylization, kept because it is what carries the plot structure to the eye; the honest summer alternative is green on green, which erases the field's whole fabric. It is a rendering choice and is deliberately left unchecked, the plot geometry being checked instead.

How deep the water actually stands, and why a single number is the wrong shape

Sources: maff-suitou-mizu, zennoh-mizukanri

  • The depth is an inch, not four to six, and there is no single number. The maintained figures are 2-3 cm, about an inch - 活着後は水深2~3cmのやや浅水とし53 ("after rooting, a slightly shallow 2-3 cm"), and for the twenty days from heading, 2~3cm程度の湛水状態を保つことが重要です.9 And a single depth is the wrong SHAPE, which is the larger point: there is no season-long depth to state.
  • The season, as the sources give it. 3-4 cm at transplanting, held slightly deep against cold and wind until the plant roots;54 2-3 cm once rooted, deliberately shallow so the water warms and tillering starts early;55 then 中干し (nakaboshi), the mid-season drain (a modern extension calendar's stage; that a premodern paddy was drained mid-season is a GUESS until a period source carries it) - the field is taken down to nothing and dried until 田面に小さなヒビが入り、軽く足跡がつく程度56 ("small cracks appear in the field surface and a light footprint shows"); then intermittent wet-and-dry;10 standing water again at heading, 2-3 cm for twenty days;57 intermittent through ripening;11 and a final drain 5-7 days before harvest.58 A paddy is not under water all season, and for part of midsummer it is dry enough to crack.
  • Where a four-to-six-inch figure DOES appear, and why it misleads. 10 cm and 20 cm are real numbers in the record - but as a cold contingency,59 not a norm: MAFF (Japan's Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries) gives them under 気温が下がる恐れがある場合は ("when there is risk of falling temperature"), 10 cm at panicle formation and 20 cm at booting, to protect the young panicle. A figure read out of that context becomes a maintained depth that nobody maintains.
  • What the irrigation survey covers. The irrigation survey this record leans on elsewhere (tabayashi-1987) classifies irrigation systems by their water source and says nothing about water depth.60
  • What we could NOT get, and what that costs. No pre-modern depth figure, in Japanese or Chinese material. The searches returned Edo irrigation infrastructure and water-dispute histories and no number. So the figures above are modern extension guidance applied backward, and the map's explanation says so to the reader rather than presenting them as a historical finding. What is plausible about applying them backward - that the agronomy of a puddled basin has not changed, and that shallow warm water for tillering is a constraint of the plant rather than of the century - is reasoning, not evidence, and is not asserted.
  • The bund is NOT re-derived from this. The aze runs roughly 1-2 ft wide and about a foot high (sourced separately, above).61 A foot of ridge over an inch of water is not a contradiction to fix: the ridge has to hold the 10-20 cm cold-protection state, keep freeboard in rain, and be walked. That is inference rather than a finding, and is labeled one rather than presented as a sourced claim; nothing drawn depends on the depth - the drawn bund is a WIDTH.
  • The map shows one moment. The staging is a real seasonal axis: a map showing nakaboshi would draw cracked mud where this one draws water. The map depicts one moment.

Nitrogen - a flooded paddy makes its own

Sources: azolla-enwiki (Azolla on the flooded paddy releasing nitrogen, and its cyanobacterial symbiont). No readable source for the renge green manure (searched 2026-08-28: the IntechOpen chapter intechopen.com/chapters/69541 reads only a general "biological nitrogen fixation" clause; Springer 978-3-662-10385-2_22 paywalled; MDPI agriculture9020029 403).

  • Nitrogen - the paddy makes its OWN, so soy is food not fertilizer. A flooded paddy is near self-sustaining for nitrogen: the standing water hosts N-fixing cyanobacteria + azolla12 and the irrigation water carries in silt/nutrients from upstream - which is why paddies crop continuously for centuries13 where dry-field monoculture exhausts the soil. Legumes entered as winter green manure grown IN the drained paddy (renge / Chinese milk vetch, plowed under before spring flooding) + applied night soil / ash / fish-and-oilseed cake - NOT soy on the margins washing in. So the ~6% soy is a food crop (dry fields, and characteristically on the paddy bunds - aze-mame, "ridge beans"),14 NOT the paddy's nitrogen supply.

Why ruled rows waited for Meiji

Sources: seijoue-kotobank, seijoue-seika, kubota-transplanting (seijoue rare before Meiji, promoted nationally in the 1890s-1900s17 with the 田打車 weeder, ropes and rulers). The police standing over the planting and the 1903 order: a GUESS - no readable page supports it (only search syntheses of agri.hakase-jyuku.com "サーベル農政")

  • WHY rows waited for Meiji when row planting is ancient (GM 2026-07-23 - the idea was never the bottleneck, the economics were). Dry-crop rows are FREE: the seed goes into a plowed furrow, and the furrow IS the row. Wet rice is TRANSPLANTED into a puddled flooded sheet with no furrows and no guide lines, so rows must be PURCHASED - marked ropes or a rolled gridding frame,15 plus every planter aligning to them - and the bill lands in the year's tightest labor window (the whole village transplants in days, on a shared water schedule). And for centuries the purchase bought nothing: rows pay when a tool travels BETWEEN them (the ancient dry-field hoe/cultivator), but nothing could travel between rows in a flooded paddy - weeding was by hand and foot either way. What changed in Meiji was the arrival of the between-rows tool for mud: the hand-pushed ROTARY PADDY WEEDER, which only works on plants ruled in both directions - so seijoue + marking frame + rotary weeder spread as ONE package,16 pushed by state extension hard enough that police sometimes stood over farmers to enforce straight lines ("saber farming"), itself evidence the private payoff was marginal before the full package. Traditional transplanting was 乱雑植え, "disorderly planting", not evenly spaced76, and only became evenly spaced once row planting arrived - which is exactly what the sparse unruled shoot-mottle renders.

How dense that mottle is drawn - and a decision deliberately left unmade. Traditional transplanting set hills, clumps of two to six seedlings, at roughly 20-30 cm centers, which is about one hill per square foot (this rests on general reading; no source is cited)81. So even on the hamlet sheet, drawn at one foot to the pixel, one dot per plant would be one dot per pixel: a solid fill, not a texture. Any mottle that can be drawn at all is therefore a SAMPLE of the planting, and how coarse a sample is a drawing choice rather than a finding. The parcels of rice left between a polder's fish ponds, where the crop has to read distinctly against the water, took a denser one at the GM's choice: centers about 4.5 ft apart with alternate rows half-offset, about 1.5 ft of jitter on each and roughly a tenth of them dropped, so the shoots land irregularly with no ruled row or column anywhere. The comb maps keep the original sparse random scatter. Whether to bring the denser sample to the ordinary comb paddies is a decision deliberately LEFT UNMADE: nothing in the record decides between them, both are honest samples of the same irregular planting, and the change would move the surface of every comb map at once.

Why is every rice plot the same green?

Sources: the GM's decision that a color difference on a map must mean something; this project's reading of a shared irrigation turn and exchanged labor.

A village transplants TOGETHER - the water is released on one schedule and the labor is exchanged between households - so at any moment its basins are largely at a single growth stage, with only a minor spread from early and late varieties (this rests on general reading; no source is cited)82. The body of the paddy is therefore ONE green; the bund network and the footpath dots carry the field's structure instead.

What remains is that every color on cultivated ground MEANS something. A blue-green basin is one of the low plots sitting on the drain collector, pooling before the outfall (the entry on the wettest plots below); a gold one is early ripening, where a map uses it at all; and a plot on the upslope margin drawn in its own color with its own furrow direction is a dry crop. So a reader can trust the palette: uniform green is the one rice crop at the one stage, and a different color is a real difference in the ground rather than decoration.

Where does a field's water come from, and how is it shared out?

Sources: tabayashi-1987, kagawa-tameike, jsidre-minumadai, japanese-wiki-corpus, beitang-studies

  • Pond: a valley-head tameike behind an earthen dike, sitting ABOVE its fields ("located at a valley head and constructed by dividing off the valley mouth with an earthen dike...18 at elevations higher than the surface of the paddy fields they serve" - Tabayashi 1987 (the PDF's own header gives 1987)78, Geographical Review of Japan 60(1)). ONE outlet: an inclined intake (shahi) feeding a bottom conduit (sokohi) through the dam; the spillway is flood-safety, never distribution (Kagawa pref. tameike docs).19 Parent/child pond linkage (oyaike/koike, Kagawa)77; "melon-on-the-vine" in China is reported flavor for larger systems (unsourced).
  • Distribution: sluice -> head-race -> division point (bunsuiguchi) -> a branching TREE. "Main canals gradually decrease in size as they are tapped by branch canals" (Tabayashi)20 - hence the drawn taper. The smallest ditches "are often considered parts of the paddy fields they serve"21 - hence ditch-as-plot-boundary. SPARSE is correct: a village digs the minimum network; a ditch beside every paddy (yohaisui bunri) is a Meiji land-readjustment (1899/1905) anachronism.22
  • Layout modes (terrain-driven):
    • COMB (the default): supply canals along the HIGH margins, delivery ditches perpendicular down-slope, one drain along the low line. Grounding: the Edo Kishu-school layout (Minuma-dai 1728 - the LAYOUT is sourced, the NAME less so: 紀州流 is attested as Izawa Yasobei's river-channelization method rather than as a field layout;24 the layout is supply on the elevated margins, drainage channel on the lowest line, water reused downstream)23 AND codified Chinese canal doctrine (mains along contours/ridges on high ground, field channels perpendicular to contours).25 Chinese beitang pond systems - the direct tameike analogue - were THE dominant village-scale mode in rice China (8.3M ponds serving ~39% of irrigated area into the 1950s,26 ~71% in hilly regions (unsourced)); the GM chose the Chinese default deliberately (Rokugan demographics anchor to Song/Ming China).
    • FAN: gently-descending canals radiating from a valley-mouth apex - the Dujiangyan / Tedori-alluvial-fan geometry.27 Correct where the land fans out below the pond.
    • JORI GRID: from the 8th century (743 CE, the mid Nara period, is the first appearance of jori land notation; the page does not single out the plains - it says remains exist "throughout Japan except Hokaido and Okinawa") much of Japan's long-settled PLAINS carried an astronomically-oriented 109 m grid (jori-sei: 1-cho squares in 6x6-ri blocks, cut into ~12 x 109 m tan strips).28 A plains village in an ancient core province shows semi-regular GRIDDED paddies, not organic patchwork - Rokugan analog: ancient heartland provinces (e.g. Crane/Phoenix cores). The organic warp-thread patchwork is correct for terrain-following villages like Kikuta.

How wide is a drain where it starts - as narrow as the ditches that feed it

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25; the taper finding in 'Where does a field's water come from, and how is it shared out?' above, of which this is the mirror.

A gathering drain is the supply side run backwards. The delivery ditches taper to a thread as they give up their water to the basins, so the collector that receives them has to BEGIN as a thread and earn its full width downstream at the outfall, where it finally carries the whole fan's runoff. The GM ruled on 2026-07-25 that the head be drawn as narrow as a delivery tail - about 1.5 ft across - rather than as a ditch already several feet wide, which is what it had been.

Two independent arguments land on the same figure, which is why it is not a compromise between them. Hydraulically, the head of a collector drains the toe of ONE basin - a tenth of a hectare, a fraction of a liter a second even under a storm drawdown - so the flow buys it no width at all. Practically, a ditch that is cleaned out by hand every year needs a bottom a hoe fits into, call it a foot, and side slopes that stand up, which puts about 1.5 ft across the top; a channel narrower than that is not a maintained ditch but the seasonal furrow re-cut at each drawdown (this rests on general reading; no source is cited)83. So the floor is a real one, and it sits exactly where the supply side already ends.

Two channels are exempt on purpose, both of them because they do not gather along a run: a polder's ring canal holds one section the whole way round, because it is the trunk that rings the block and is fed from every side rather than accumulating along a run; and a supply canal's own head is set by the ground it commands.

Why does the drain run across the slope instead of down it?

Sources: this project's reading of how a cascade fan drains; the rules and the declined alternatives below are its own decisions.

It looks wrong at a glance and it is right. The drain (akusui) crosses the field's low margin roughly PERPENDICULAR to the fall, because it is a collector: it has to gather runoff from every cascade column, and a drain that ran straight downhill would follow one column and collect nothing. A contour drain meeting a valley stream at about a right angle is ordinary side drainage (this rests on general reading; no source is cited)84. On a map whose land falls diagonally the effect is stronger still: on a sheet high to the northwest the outfall sits toward the east, which is downhill, and also up the page, where an eye expects the high ground to be. The water is not running uphill; the page is.

The rule that was DECLINED. No rule forcing a drain to run within so many degrees of the slant was added, and that is a decision rather than an oversight: such a rule would ban the very collectors that are historically right. What is pinned instead are the three things that are actually constrained - the collector runs roughly across the fall rather than along it, more than about fifty degrees off it; the end that discharges is the lower-ground end, never uphill of the head; and the brook that takes the water away heads downhill. Together those say "a collector crosses the slope, and the water only ever moves downhill", which is the true constraint, while the angle rule would have said something false.

Two readability fixes priced and declined, and the one adopted. If the perception still misleads, the answer is to fix the READING and not the hydrology. Slope cues drawn as contour lines were declined as awkward on a map already dense with line work, and flow arrows inside the channels were declined because the channels are drawn too thin to carry one. What was adopted is cheaper: the brook CURVES out of the collector's exit heading over its first few steps, so the junction reads as the collector turning down the valley into the stream instead of meeting it at a hard corner. The turn is held under 65 degrees, each heading averaged over about 40 ft of its own line so that a short jittery segment cannot fool the measure.

Does the lowest bund of a paddy run with the drain or across it?

Sources: the GM's report of 2026-08-08 on a town map; the bund's construction, from 'Bunds are SHARED' above.

The GM, reading a town sheet on 2026-08-08: "the dark brown earthen bunds appear to overlap with the drainage ditch instead of aligning with it. I would expect the earthen bunds bordering the ditch to be at the same angle as it." That was a real construction error being read correctly, not a stray plot.

The physical fact the drawing has to honor. A paddy's lowest bund IS the collector's top-of-bank. The two abut and cannot interpenetrate: the bund is what holds the water in and the ditch is what takes it away, so a bund drawn across the collector is a basin with its wall standing in the drain (this rests on general reading; no source is cited)85. The field's drain-side edge is therefore a single line running WITH the ditch, with the column bunds meeting it end-on at the fall angle.

Two independent causes, both in the drawing. The hem pass laid its plots on the CONTOUR while the collector is fitted at up to about nineteen degrees to it, so across a 26 ft plot the bund drifted some 9 ft relative to the ditch: it began above the collector and ended below it, every time. On the map's west fan the hem covered 14 of the 31 plots along that edge, against 4 from the closing rank, so this WAS the field's edge, and it read as a sawtooth with brown ticks poking out into bare ground. The hem now samples the drain at both of its own bounds and runs both of its edges parallel to what the ditch does between them. Separately, the set-back from the drain was a flat figure blind to the collector WIDENING downstream, so even a correctly angled bund was drawn inside the ditch's stroke over the lower half of its run; the set-back is now half the ditch's drawn width at that point plus half a bund, so the two abut instead of overlapping.

And one residue accepted. The collector's line wanders on purpose - about 6 ft of node jitter at 120-170 ft spacing- so successive stretches differ by a few degrees. A paddy basin has STRAIGHT sides and cannot chase that, and a bund pinned only at its two ends cuts the corner at every bend and lands in the water. So a bund is lifted to clear the highest point of the bank across its own span, which is also what a farmer laying a straight bund against a crooked ditch would do. The visible consequence, accepted: a bund can sit a few degrees off its LOCAL stretch of drain - about nine on one pocket - while clearing it everywhere. It is also why the rule is written about POSITION rather than angle. A correct straight bund and a wrongly contour-laid one occupy the same band of angles on a gently fitted drain, so an angle threshold would have to be either toothless or wrong.

Plot sizes, pond sizing and acreage from population

Sources: kokudaka-jawiki (one koku a year; real stipends ~1.8), gokogomin-kotobank (40% then 50% - the "~45%" is the midpoint). No source was read for the 2,000-2,500 m3/ha tameike ratio (the Aomori 21nn_17ike.pdf could not be read), the 1.3 koku/tan yield, the 0.02-0.25 acre plots or the Tedori straightening.

  • Plots: pre-modern 0.02-0.25 acre, irregular, about 9 scattered plots per household (the ~9 is unsourced) (fragmented holdings were normal).29 STRAIGHT rectangular channels/plots are post-1900 consolidation (the Tedori fan's ditches were only straightened in the early 1900s)30 - the organic waver is period-correct.

    Which figure is which, because two different things get called a plot. A leveled, diked CELL is one basin that has to hold water at an even depth, and that is physically small: the maps target about 0.05 acre for it (roughly 200 m2, a 47 ft square), derived from real feet at whatever scale the sheet is drawn to, so a basin is the same real size on a hamlet map and on a city map. The older drawing, still used by the maps that have not been rebuilt on the water-first engine, subdivides a field recursively to a PARCEL grain instead - about 0.10 acre at the finer setting the villages use, about 0.19 acre at its default. All three figures sit inside the 0.02-0.25 acre band above, and neither grain is a legibility inflation: the coarser one is a parcel, which a holding really was, and the finer one is the cell the water-first engine derives. A parcel subdivides into several cells on any slope, which is why the cell sits below the surveyed-parcel figures.

  • Pond sizing (the rule): sole-storage tameike run ~2,000-2,500 m3 of storage per irrigated ha (typical depth 2-4 m); a STREAM-FED pond refilling 1-2x a season is comfortable at ~1,200-1,500 m3/ha. Hoshigaoka: 31.8 ha of paddy -> ~1.5 ha pond surface ~ 47,000 m3 at ~3 m ~ 1,470 m3/ha + feeder stream. Pond area is kept proportional to command area.

  • Acreage from population (the sizing rule): a person eats ~1 koku/yr;31 pre-modern yield ~1.3 koku/tan; coarse grain fills part of the diet while ~45% of rice goes to tax32 -> ~0.8-1.0 tan gross paddy per person -> 350 inhabitants ~ 280-350 tan = 69-86 acres (+ dry margins later). Kikuta lands at ~79 acres / ~600 plots.

Tract sizes - no settlement-class cap

Sources: mdpi-kunisaki

The PLOT question (one leveled cell, ~0.05 ac) is settled above; this entry is the layer above it - the TRACT: one contiguous field system (a comb fan, a terrace flight, a polder) and how much ground it commands. Asked by the GM 2026-08-02 after Hoshizora's west comb read as "extremely unusual": what range of rice paddy sizes might we see in a mixed-use settlement - partially urban, partially pastoral grazing, partially food-growing farms?

  • Per-household paddy (China first). Mid-Qing Jiangnan farms averaged ~10 mu per farmer - Li Bozhong's "ten mu per farmer" (unsourced - the review page does not state this figure) - at the Ming-Qing mu of ~614 m2, so ~1.5 acres of intensively worked wet rice per farm household; Buck's surveys corroborate that a holding was scattered over several parcels.33 Japan corroborates: the Edo average farm household held ~1 cho (~2.45 ac) TOTAL, paddy plus dry (unsourced - not stated on the ARIC page or its chapter PDF), putting its paddy share in the same ~1-1.5 ac. The working band: ~1-2.5 acres of paddy per farm household - the same number the diet-side acreage-from-population rule above reaches independently (~0.8-1.0 tan/person x ~4.5-person households).

  • The communal-system floor. A comb fan is communal waterworks - weir, head-race, canal fork, tapering deliveries, a drain collector. The smallest attested community systems are pond/tank-fed: small tameike systems run ~10 ha each (Kunisaki's Tsunai ward: 5 systems totaling 50 ha across 11 farmers),34 and traditional village tanks typically command tens of hectares, well under 200 (a GUESS - one case read, no range; note 35).35 Even a handful of cooperating households implies ~1.5 ac each, so the floor for a system that justifies drawn head-race-and-collector infrastructure is roughly 3-8 ha (~8-20 ac) - exactly the hamlet tier. Nobody builds a weir and a canal fork for 2 acres; ground that size is ONE household's holding, watered by a single ditch. The GM's framing is the right mental model: a hamlet IS a small paddy tract with farmhouses around it, and a hamlet-sized tract is the honest minimum for any fully-drawn fan, wherever it appears.

  • The town edge has NO tier of its own. A market town / county seat is the CENTER of a farmed hinterland (Skinner: the standard marketing community is ~18 villages over ~300-500 km2),36 and cultivation historically pressed against the built edge - Chinese county seats with farmland to the walls and farmers walking out from town; Japanese post towns strung along highways through continuous paddy. Tract size is set by water, terrain, and mouths fed - never by settlement class. A mixed-use edge (urban core + hay/grazing + farms, the Hoshizora premise) legitimately carries anything from a hamlet-grade fan (~8-20 ac) where irrigable ground is short, through village-grade tracts (~50-90 ac), up to open farmland bounded only by the frame; the attested LOW end is terrain-limited (the upland Kiso post towns), and even there the limit is the terrain, not the town-ness. Small is legal where the map shows the terrain reason (hay country, forest, slope); tiny-with-full-waterworks is not attested anywhere.

  • The rule (GM 2026-08-03). 8 acres is the hard floor for any ENCLOSED fan (one that does not run off the frame), reserved for mixed-use / terrain-limited maps that SHOW the reason there is no more irrigable ground (Hoshizora's relay hayfields + grazing + forest, and a thin near ring); ~20 acres is the ordinary small end for a non-specialized town - in practice drawn as a modest enclosed fan plus off-frame slices, since a real town sits amid continuous farmland anyway; villages stay population-sized at ~50-90 ac; cities keep the off-frame slice convention. Off-frame slices stay exempt from the floor ON-MAP (the truncation says "more beyond"); the floor bites exactly where an enclosed fan reads as a complete system. An enclosed fan's own ring reads as ~1-2.5 ac/household for the steadings living off it; the rest of a town's depicted farmers read as working the off-frame fields, which is why every town map must show a field running off its edge.

What is the farmland around a town or a city made of?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-23; the marketing-community and county-seat material in Tract sizes above; this project's measurements of its own drawn town and city maps.

What the reasoning found. A market town or county seat sits in the middle of its best land, and the ring worked hardest is the one closest in, the labor-limited fallow retreating to the far margins (this rests on general reading; no source is cited)86. The tempting alternative - a few paddy clusters in a sea of scrub right up to the gate - has that gradient exactly upside down. What a town map shows is a SAMPLE, though: the far countryside is implied off the sheet, and coverage of the near ring need not be total, since in the GM's words "the presumption is that there's more we don't see".

The decision it drove (GM 2026-07-23). The farmland around a town or a city is drawn EXCLUSIVELY with the same real irrigation works as a village's: a sluice tapped on a real source, a head-race forking into supply canals, flooded basins cascading downhill, a dry hem on the upslope margin, a drain, and farmhouses ringed alongside. The near ring is the settlement embedded in those fans, and the open ground between them reads as pasture and commons and as the head of paddy country carrying on past the frame.

Two fillers REJECTED, recorded so they are never reinvented. Before that ruling the near ring had twice been filled with something cheaper: once as a quilt of dry and garden tiles, once as gridded paddy basins with no waterworks of their own. Both drew the near ring as something other than comb fans, which is precisely what the ruling forbids, and an old, higher density floor was calibrated with the quilt in place and would have forced it back.

The rule the map follows: on a TOWN map at least one field runs OFF the map edge. A partial field at the margin is what says "more farmland beyond", and a town that shows only enclosed fans claims to show all of its hinterland, which no town sheet can. A field running off the edge is exempt from the rules that want a field ringed with farmhouses and tied to a water source, because its houses and its water are off the map too.

The rule the map follows: the near ring reads PACKED, not sampled away to bare scrub. Within about 800 ft of the rampart of a walled CITY at least 12% of the ground is cultivated, and on a TOWN at least 28%; the sample is taken in the map's own space rather than in whatever the frame happens to show, so widening or tightening the frame cannot change the answer. Both figures are calibrated against our own drawn maps - a bare city ring measured about 7% and a five-fan town about 31% - rather than derived from a source. The look they aim at is a dense wet-rice near-city plain, roughly 70-75% paddy with the rest bunds, ditches, lanes and farmsteads, at about 1.5-2 farming households per hectare of paddy (this rests on general reading; no source is cited)87.

The rule the map follows: on a CITY map the head of a fan is planted, not left bare - the ground along the supply canals and the triangle between the two arms at the head-race fork. That ground sits at or above its own canals, so gravity cannot command rice onto it, and it is exactly the well-drained high ground that dry crops take: barley, millet and buckwheat beside the head-race. No more than a fifth of the ground sampled along the main channels may read as bare parchment. The one-fifth ceiling is calibrated against the drawn maps, which ran about 25% before the heads were quilted and 13-16% after, not derived from a source.

Where do the samurai estates sit when the paddy has the near ring?

Sources: the GM's setting notes on land tenure and the samurai country estate; the GM's ruling on approach lanes.

The fans claim the near ring BEFORE any country estate is placed, and the gentry keep the pockets left over - most of their estates stand farther out in the rural district, off the sheet altogether. This rests on the GM's setting notes rather than on the historical record. It means a city map showing as few as ONE estate, even one whose grounds run off the frame, is the honest signal rather than an omission; a ring of estates crowding the wall would be the error.

The decision: estate DRIVEWAYS are not drawn. The GM ruled them out because the long worn approach lanes read on the sheet as fat roads laid across the countryside, and the drawing already held that an approach lane is not a thing drawn at this scale. The manors therefore stand as clean walled compounds with their approaches implied off the map.

The rule the map follows: a CITY map shows at least one samurai estate; one of them cut by the frame edge is legitimate and says its land continues beyond. Estates cluster on the side of the map that faces the capital, stand at least about 600 ft apart so they never read as a row, and vary in size. No estate is drawn with a driveway.

Which plots belong to the temple - the tax-free glebe

Sources: the GM's setting notes on village headmen and the allocation of the common fields; the drawing choice below is this project's, made on a rendered comparison.

A village's common fields are doled out by the headman in non-contiguous strips held in usufruct, so a household's holding is scattered rather than blocked - which is the same fragmentation the attested plot sizes above show from the other direction. The religious figure's allocation is tax-free and law-bounded to about two households' worth. This rests on the GM's setting notes rather than on the historical record.

The drawing choice, and the one declined. Where a map marks the tax-free plots they are tinted vermillion and outlined: high contrast against the greens, and the shrine's own sacred color. A thin gold outline was tried first and declined - it blended into the tan bunds and was hard to pick out at map view.

The rule the map follows: marking the tax-free plots is OPTIONAL. A village map that marks none is correct and complete. A map that marks them marks two or three, scattered non-contiguously across the common fields rather than blocked together, in vermillion - and if it marks any, it marks that many, because the allocation is bounded by law and a larger one would say something about the temple that the setting does not.

Where dry (hatake) crops go - the topographic catena

Sources: satoyama-enwiki (the mosaic and the foothill-to-flat border zone). The Takeuchi-school catena sentence ("large middle river terraces... large areas of crop fields and small areas of paddy") is a GUESS - no readable page supports it (the mekongwatch PDF could not be read).

WHERE dry crops go: wet-rice villages sort by a topographic CATENA - irrigated paddy holds the flat valley bottom / plain; DRY fields (hatake) take the HIGHER, well-drained ground37 the water cannot command (river terraces, natural levees / micro-highs threading the plain, alluvial-fan edges, lower slopes, AND the slightly-raised ground the homesteads sit on); coppice woodland (satoyama) crowns the hills above. So dry fields are NOT one neat strip - historically they sit in SEVERAL positions, above all AROUND the houses ("each family has some paddy and some hatake", the household's dry plots near its home).

Why are the dry plots beside a canal square to the canal?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07; Buck's surveys for the size of a dry parcel; this project's measurement of its own maps.

A dry field bordering a supply canal is surveyed TO the canal: one edge runs along it and the other stands perpendicular to it, running upslope, so the plots come out as clean rectangles (this rests on general reading; no source is cited)88. The drawing had built them in the paddy's own frame instead - columns down the fall, rows on the contour - and since a head-race commonly runs diagonally to the fall, every plot came out sheared into a parallelogram.

The first fix, REJECTED. A first attempt merely softened that lean while STILL orienting the plots to the paddy, and the GM turned it down correctly: the plots take their orientation from the CANAL, not from the rice. The plots are now tiled by arc length along the canal's own line and each is built as a rectangle offset perpendicular to the local tangent, so neighbors abut continuously along the canal, nothing is sheared or stepped, and only the outer upslope edge is ragged. That the strips therefore run near-horizontal along one map's head-race and near-vertical along another's is the point, not an inconsistency. They stay on the dry side throughout, both edges upslope of the canal behind a thin berm, and where a map carries more than one fan a hem plot that would lap onto a neighboring fan's rice is dropped: dry crops on flooded ground contradict the whole reason the hem is where it is.

The rule the map follows: a dry parcel is a smallholder's strip, and the map-wide MEAN dry parcel stays under 0.25 acre. The attested mean Chinese dry parcel is near one mu, about 0.17 acre, and the drawn village strips land at 0.16-0.17 with the hamlet's garden-scale strips at about 0.04. The tiling is in real feet at every scale - roughly 92 ft across a parcel and 72 ft down a row at the village grain - so a dry cell is never drawn dwarfing the rice basins beside it, which is what happens when a pixel figure is carried unscaled onto a coarser sheet.

Why do neighboring dry plots run their furrows different ways?

Sources: the fragmented-holdings finding in 'Plot sizes' above; the GM's catch of 2026-07.

Dry crops are ridge-and-furrow row-cultivated, unlike the flooded sheet of a paddy, so a dry plot shows rows and the direction of those rows is a visible choice. The first version ran EVERY plot's furrows along the contour, on the reasoning that a ridge laid along the contour dams the rain and checks runoff. That reasoning belongs to a STEEP slope: it is an erosion measure, not a general rule, and on a gentle valley margin it is not forced (this rests on general reading; no source is cited)89. The GM caught what it cost - every plot's rows came out identical, so adjacent family strips were indistinguishable from one another.

The accurate look on gentle ground is the patchwork quilt. Holdings were fragmented, each household working several small non-contiguous plots, so neighboring strips were plowed to their own orientations. Each dry plot now takes a furrow angle up to about 63 degrees either side of the contour, never straight downslope, chosen by dropping it into the WIDEST gap between the angles of the neighbors already placed - which guarantees a real separation from each of them rather than hoping for one; no two adjacent dry plots run within about six degrees of each other, an angle being compared as an undirected line. A genuinely steep or terraced village is drawn the other way, by declaring a narrow spread so the rows converge back onto the contour, where the erosion-control reading is the correct one.

The wettest plots are their own kind of ground - shitsuden, and why they read blue

Sources: kotobank-shitsuden, kotobank-kanden, kotobank-yatsuda, kotobank-fukada, fao-rice-water

  • A wet paddy is a NAMED category, not a wetter example of the same thing. Pre-modern Japanese agriculture split paddy land in two. 湿田 (shitsuden), "wet paddy": 水はけが悪く、水稲を栽培 していないときでも過湿な状態の水田。また、麦などの裏作のできない水田。 - "a paddy with poor drainage that stays waterlogged even when rice is not being grown; also, a paddy on which a winter crop such as wheat cannot be grown". Against it 乾田 (kanden), "dry paddy": 水はけのぐあいが よく、水を入れないときには乾いて畑の状態になる田。 - "a paddy with good drainage that, when water is not let in, dries out to a dryland-field state". The distinction is about the ground and its drainage, and it holds all year, which is why it is a KIND of plot rather than a moment in one plot's season.
  • It is worse ground, and the record is blunt about how. 過湿、滞水のために農作業が困難で、また麦 などの裏作ができない排水不良田 - work is hard and there is no winter crop; 地温が夏は乾田より低く 酸素不足の状態で - the soil runs colder than a dry paddy in summer and short of oxygen; 倒伏や病害、 生育遅延などで収量は不安定である - lodging, disease and delayed growth make the yield unreliable. Flatly: 湿田の生産性は概して低い38 - "shitsuden productivity is generally low". So a household holding one is holding the plot nobody wanted, and that is the fact the map is worth showing.
  • The scale of the problem, from the other end. From Meiji the state ran 湿田の乾田化62 - the conversion of wet paddy to dry - as a national undertaking, and 全国の水田の2/3以上は乾田となった といわれる, "it is said that more than two-thirds of the nation's paddies became kanden". A program that size is the measure of how much wet paddy there was to convert, which is the reason a pre-modern map should carry some.
  • The valley bottom is where it sits, and it has its own word. 谷津田 / 谷地田 (yatsuda / yachida) is the valley-bottom paddy - 台地が開析されてできた谷間の低地すなわち谷地に分布する水田63 ... 一般に湿地で古く開発された, "distributed in the valley lowland formed by the dissection of a plateau ... generally wetland, developed long ago". 深田 (fukada), "deep paddy", is attested separately as どろの深い田。沼田 - "a paddy with deep mud; a swamp paddy" - the opposite of 浅田 (asada,64 shallow paddy). It names a DEGREE of mud, not a position on a slope.
  • What is an INFERENCE, and is drawn anyway. That the plots lying on the drain collector are therefore the wettest ground in the field is not a finding anybody wrote down. The attested part is the cascade: in traditional terraced paddy "water flows from one plot to another and no distinction can be made between irrigation and drainage"65, and surface runoff "can be re-used, i.e., recycled within the system"66. Gravity does the rest, and the map sites the tint that way. It is a reasoned inference from an attested mechanism, and it is labeled one here rather than presented as a finding.
  • Two things we did NOT get, and do not assert. No source read gives a maintained GROWING-SEASON water depth of four to six inches as such (the FAO (Food and Agriculture Organization) terrace page gives 10-15 cm - four to six inches - as the depth "when flooded completely", a flooded-state figure rather than a maintained season-long one)75 - the International Rice Research Institute (IRRI)'s 5-10 cm figure could only be reached in a search summary (unsourced) and FAO's 5-20 cm is a bund-construction range, not a maintained depth (unsourced - no page read states a 5-20 cm figure). And nothing read says the water surface stays visible between the plants until the canopy closes, which is the sentence a reader might expect to justify drawing one plot bluer than another; the drawn difference rests on the ground being wetter, not on a sourced claim about what a camera would see.
  • The drawing convention, stated where it will be read. Which plots wear the tint is the picture, not the topography, and the two kinds of field differ: a COMB field tints a random 45% of the eligible rank, so Inashiro shows 2 blue plots over 24 low ones, while a POLDER or TERRACE field tints every low plot, so Enokida shows 22 over 22 and Tanada 40 over 40. On a comb map, therefore, blue is a SAMPLE of the wet ground and not the whole of it.

What a bund bean actually looks like - the soybean plant against the bead we draw

Sources: nabunken-azemame, wikipedia-soybean, cropfarming-soybeans

What the research found. The practice, from the Nara National Research Institute for Cultural Properties' Asuka pages: "『畦豆（あぜまめ）』とも呼ばれる大豆です ... 田植え後に畦に種がまかれ、稲刈りと同時に収穫されます"68 - a soybean also called azemame, sown on the bund after transplanting and harvested with the rice; once grown all over Japan, mostly gone with land consolidation and herbicide, still grown at Asuka;69 the photo caption has them 「azemame growing in a long unbroken row along the bund of a paddy where the rice cutting too is near」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「稲刈りも近い田んぼの畦にずらっと育っている畦豆」) - growing in a row along the bund.39 The plant: "fully mature soybean plants are generally between 50 and 125 cm (20 and 50 in) in height" (Wikipedia, Soybean, Description);70 "The plant is erect and bushy, with branches coming off a central stem" and "The leaflets are broad, pointed, and medium green" (cropfarming.org71 - a trade site, the one page read that states the leaf color; Wikipedia's Description says nothing about color).40 Not found: a figure for one plant's spread on the bund - the Iowa State extension page on row spacing discusses canopy-closure timing, not plant width - so the record does NOT say how wide a bund bean stands. Not used, because only search snippets carry them: azemame roots firming the bund, miso from azemame in Noto, and nitrogen to the paddy (the last contradicted by 'Nitrogen - a flooded paddy makes its own' above).

The convention, stated for the reader. We draw each plant as a round bead about 3 ft across, in a single row on the bund, in a deep pine green chosen so the beads read against the pale rice; real foliage is medium green, and the bead is drawn darker than the plant. The plant is a knee-to-waist-high bush (50-125 cm);41 the bead says only where it stands. The GM chose the color on 2026-08-15 from a rendered ladder of three candidates, because the old olive sat between the rice green and the bund brown and vanished at map zoom; the two darker candidates on the ladder were declined with it, a hunter green that grayed out and a forest green that read as black against the near-black bund stroke.
