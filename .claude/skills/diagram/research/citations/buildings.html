Citations: Historical grounding: the "why" behind the Mode A realism checks

The passages behind the footnotes of Historical grounding: the "why" behind the Mode A realism checks - first the works they quote and why each one counts, then the notes themselves. Hover a footnote number on that page and the note appears beside it; the numbers here are the same ones.

The works cited on this page

takayama-jinya-city

Takayama City, 高山陣屋跡 (https://www.city.takayama.lg.jp/kurashi/1000021/1000119/1000847/1000954/1000956.html)

What it is: The city of Takayama's official page for 高山陣屋跡 (the site of the Takayama Jin'ya), the one surviving Tokugawa provincial governor's office and compound in Japan, published by the municipality that manages the designated historic site. It lists the buildings and how they are built, notes which structures were moved in from the castle and when, and gives the extent of the protected ground.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the strongest source in the record for what a provincial office contained: an intact compound described by the body that keeps it - the examining court with its white-gravel yard, the storehouses moved from the castle, the office range of 1816 - and its designated area. Its limits are that this is the only survivor of more than fifty such offices, in a mountain province under direct shogunal rule; that parts of what is described are a 1996 reconstruction from an 1830 drawing; and that the area figure, 11,219 square meters, is an exact sum of ten surveyed parcels under a modern legal boundary accumulated across four designations between 1929 and 1989, not a measured Edo footprint - so the compound area our maps derive from it, the total less the front plaza, carries the hedge rather than the source.

takayama-jinya-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 高山陣屋 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/高山陣屋)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 高山陣屋 (Takayama Jinya), on the shogunal intendancy office of Hida province at Takayama, held under direct shogunal rule from 1692 until the Meiji Restoration - the only such office to survive - with two references, the prefecture's 1992 survey report and the national cultural-property register. It records the 1816 rebuild of the office block as one range, the numbered rows of storehouses, the historic-site designation of 1929, and the 1996 restoration from an 1830 drawing.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the shape of a provincial office compound as it survived - the office range, the storehouse rows, the front court - which is what our magistracy plans are laid out from; we use its shape and not its exact dimensions. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary; that this is one atypically preserved compound in a mountain province under direct shogunal rule; and that the buildings it lists describe the post-1996 state, in which the storehouse-keepers' rowhouse, the intendant's residence and the inner rooms are a reconstruction from a drawing rather than standing Edo fabric.

neixiang-yamen-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 内乡县衙 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/内乡县衙)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on the same Neixiang county yamen (内乡县衙), covering its history, its layout and its individual buildings, including the jail in the southwestern part of the compound and the shrine to Gao Yao, the god of prisons, kept inside it.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives, with references, the parts of a county yamen our plan draws - the office in front and the residence behind, the jail in the southwest, the jail-god shrine - each on the page in so many words. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary of one site; that the plan it describes is the Qing dynasty's regulation for a local office, embodied in an 1894 rebuild kept as a museum, so a setting modeled on imperial China at large is reading a late and restored example; that the shrine pair on the east line is not on this page and rests on the newspaper feature instead; and that a jail dimension it quotes is larger than the whole compound and is not to be used.

henan-neixiang

Henan provincial government, "China's best-preserved county-level magistrate office in Neixiang" (https://english.henan.gov.cn/2023/11-23/2853070.html)

What it is: A page in English from the Henan provincial government's own website, dated November 2023, describing the county magistrate's office (yamen) at Neixiang, the best-preserved county-level yamen in China. Its standing buildings date from 1894, late in the Qing dynasty, and the compound has been kept as a yamen museum since 1984. The page walks it building by building: the Main Court with its Ming-era kneeling stones for plaintiff and defendant, the guesthouse, the courtyard for the god of the land and the god of the government office, and the prison lying southwest of the Major Court and so "usually called South Prison".

Why it applies, and its limits: A magistracy is the one compound where a player can ask what each building is for, and this describes a real one that still stands, in the order a visitor meets it - which is where our plan's court, shrine and prison positions come from. It is a government page written to attract visitors rather than a measured architectural survey, so we take arrangement and function from it and no dimensions; what a visitor meets is a late-Qing plan as conserved and restored for display; it records one Chinese county seat, whose plan we use as the model for a setting that also draws on Japan; and its wording is the site's own English.

tenmacho-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 伝馬町牢屋敷 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/伝馬町牢屋敷)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 伝馬町牢屋敷 (the Tenmacho jail), on Edo's main prison near Nihonbashi, in use until the 1870s - its extent, the plastered wall and outer moat that enclosed it, its internal divisions, and the categories of prisoner it held.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives a measured anchor for a jail compound - about 2,677 tsubo, roughly 8,850 square meters, behind a solid wall and a moat - and it settles what such a place was for: people awaiting judgment, with life imprisonment and imprisonment as a penalty being the exceptions rather than the rule, which is why our lockups are small holding buildings and not penitentiaries. It is a tertiary summary whose figures we take only where its own references support them, and it describes the shogunal capital's central jail, so a provincial magistracy's lockup is scaled far down from it rather than copied.

chuo-royashiki

Chuo City, 伝馬町牢屋敷跡 (https://www.city.chuo.lg.jp/a0052/bunkakankou/rekishi/tokyobunkazai/royashiki.html)

What it is: A local history page from Chuo City, one of Tokyo's wards, on the site of the Denmacho jail compound (伝馬町牢屋敷跡), the shogunate's central jail in Edo. It gives the compound's area as 2,600 tsubo, about 8,595 square meters, or more, notes the moat and embankment enclosing it on three sides, and describes the numbers held and the execution ground within the walls.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the hard numbers our jail block needs, an area, an enclosure and a population, from the municipality that now owns the ground and maintains the marker. It is a local government heritage page written for residents and visitors rather than a scholarly study, and it describes the largest jail in the shogunal capital, so for a provincial town it is an upper bound to work down from rather than a figure to copy.

neixiang-yamen

Neixiang county yamen (内乡县衙), Henan - site documentation and plan descriptions (https://cn.chinadaily.com.cn/a/202103/12/WS604b0287a3101e7ce9743b4c.html)

What it is: A feature article by the Henan Daily reporter Zhang Dongyun, republished on China Daily's Chinese-language site in March 2021, on the Neixiang county yamen in Henan - a county government compound first built in 1304 and rebuilt by a late Qing magistrate, now a museum. It describes the plan in detail: more than 47,000 square meters, eighteen courtyards in succession and over 280 rooms, laid out by Ming and Qing regulation with the offices in front and the residence behind, and it names what stands on each side of the first two courtyards.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the plan of a real county yamen in the words of a reporter walking it - the central axis, the two front courtyards, the Six Chambers, the total area and room count, and what stood east and west of the first court - which is what our magistracy plan is laid out from. Its limits are that it is a newspaper feature rather than a survey or excavation report; that the buildings are a late-Qing rebuild conserved and restored as a museum, so the plan is a preserved one rather than a documented original; that its west line holds the guest hall, the dining hall and the jail, not a clerks' lodging, and it names no sheds against the wall - the record uses Neixiang as the counter-example on both, and the runners' duty courtyard it does name stands on the east; and that it is imperial China rather than Japan.

tedai-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 手代 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/手代)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 手代 (tedai), on the clerks of that name - both the shop clerks of merchant houses and the junior staff who served under a Tokugawa intendant in a rural district office - covering where they came from and what they did.

Why it applies, and its limits: It tells us who the clerks of an intendant's office were - mostly the sons of village and town officials, hired from peasants and townsmen versed in rural administration - which is what fills the clerks' quarters on our magistracy plans with the right kind of household; it also gives one establishment, Fukuyama domain's twelve clerks under three intendants, four apiece, with their stipends. Its limits are that it is a thin tertiary article whose only citation concerns modern commercial law, so its Edo material is uncited; that a recruitment pattern is not a measurement; and that the one staffing figure is a domain's, not a shogunal intendancy's, so if it is ever used it is used as one example.

hatchobori-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 八丁堀 (東京都中央区) (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/八丁堀_(東京都中央区))

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on Hatchobori, a district of what is now Chuo ward in Tokyo. Among its local history it records that the many temples standing there were ordered to move to Asakusa in 1635, and that the group residences (組屋敷, kumi-yashiki) of the yoriki and doshin serving under the Edo town magistrate were afterward established on the vacated temple sites - the origin of the constables of period drama calling themselves "Hatchobori".

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for one structural fact about how a magistracy was housed: the magistrate's enforcement staff lived together in their own quarter, at a remove from the office where they worked, rather than inside its walls. It is a tertiary summary of a single district in a single city, the article itself hedges the origin story as something that "is said", and Edo was far larger than any settlement we draw, so what we take is the arrangement and not its scale.

hei-jokaku-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 塀 (城郭) (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/塀_(城郭))

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the 塀 (hei) of a castle - the wall built along the top of a castle's stone ramparts and earthworks. It distinguishes framed from unframed earthen walls, notes that each carries a small roof and is pierced with loopholes, and gives thicknesses: a shaku (about 300 mm) or more, with medieval walls as thin as three to seven sun before firearms spread in the late Sengoku period and pushed them to seven sun (about 210 mm) and beyond.

Why it applies, and its limits: A wall's thickness decides how heavy a line we draw for it, and this is a straightforward statement of the castle-class figure together with the reason it grew. It is a tertiary summary whose numbers carry no citation of their own, and it is about castle walls only - a village fence, a temple wall or a townhouse's boundary is a different thing built to a different standard, so the figure is an upper bound for our fortified work and not a general one.

kojodan-dobei

攻城団, お城の基礎講座 40. 土塀の種類 (https://blog.kojodan.jp/entry/2020/08/26/180000)

What it is: A 2020 post by the writer Takamaru in "Takamaru's Castle Basics course", a long-running explainer series on Kojodan, a Japanese castle enthusiasts' site. It sets out the kinds of earthen wall used at castles - the framed and plastered wall, the rammed-earth tsuijibei built up in layers inside wooden forms, and the frameless neribei of old tile and stone bedded in clay - with how each was built and how thick it came out.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it to decide how thick to draw a compound wall: a rammed-earth tsuijibei about a meter thick and three high, a frameless neribei perhaps 30 cm. Its limits are that it is an enthusiast's explainer on a castle fans' site with no sources behind it, not an archaeological or conservation report; that the author hedges the 30 cm figure himself while stating the 1 m one flat; and that these are the walls of castles, a different scale of place and a different job for a wall, which we apply to a magistracy or manor compound as an upper bound -

mingguanci-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 名宦祠 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/名宦祠)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on the mingguanci (名宦祠), the shrine in which a locality offered sacrifice to former officials of the place who had governed it well.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for what the building is and where it belongs - a state institution from the early Ming onward, standing in the Confucian temple precinct east of the Dacheng gate rather than inside the magistrate's own compound - which is how we place and label it on a town plan. It is a tertiary summary whose statements we take only where its own references support them, and it describes the Chinese institution, which our setting adapts rather than copies.

sado-bugyosho-fires

Sado magistracy fire history - unsupported by any readable page: search snippets across Sado tourism pages state the 奉行所, built 1603, 「It repeated destruction by fire and rebuilding five times.」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「焼失と再建を5回繰り返しました」); the three pages fetched (ja.wikipedia 佐渡奉行, visitsado.com, city.sado.niigata.jp museum) describe only the 2000 reconstruction and say nothing about fires (https://www.asoview.com/spot/15224af2172090002/)

What it is: A listing for the Sado magistracy (佐渡奉行所) on asoview, a Japanese leisure and sightseeing booking site, carrying a short history of the building: put up in 1603 at the start of the Edo period, burned down and rebuilt five times thereafter, remodeled into offices and a school after the Meiji Restoration, named a national historic site in 1929, and destroyed entirely by fire in 1942.

Why it applies, and its limits: One plain point is taken from it - that a magistracy's halls were ordinary timber buildings that burned and were rebuilt again and again, which is why our administrative compounds are drawn as wooden and replaceable rather than monumental. It stands in because nothing closer could be read on a public page: the encyclopedia, museum and tourism pages about the same building describe only its modern reconstruction, so the count of five fires rests on this page alone, and the page is a commercial listing written for visitors, with no sources of its own and its Japanese rendered into English by this project.

tfd-hongou-fire-history

Tokyo Fire Department, 防火対策の歴史 (Hongo station; https://www.tfd.metro.tokyo.lg.jp/fs/hongou/page_00029.html)

What it is: A page on the history of fire precautions (防火対策の歴史) published by the Hongo station of the Tokyo Fire Department, a short public-education account of how the inhabitants and authorities of Edo guarded against fire, including the water kept ready in ordinary households.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives two things a town map draws - the fire-water kept at the doorway and on the roof of a commoner's house, and the permission for tile, plastered storehouses and plastered houses in the merchant quarter - from the city that invented the regime. Its limits are that it is a fire service's public-education history with no author and no bibliography; that the passage we quote gives the practice without dates or quantities, though the page carries dated ordinances elsewhere; and that it describes Edo, the densest wooden city in the country under a shogunal fire regime, so the habit travels further than the regulation does.

thepaper-taipinggang

澎湃新闻, 这口大缸能防火？！ (https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_23268207)

What it is: 这口大缸能防火？！ ("This great vat can fight fire?!"), an article in 澎湃新闻 (The Paper) about the bronze and iron water vats standing in the courtyards of the Forbidden City - how many there were, the grades they came in, and the rule that governed where each grade stood.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the water vats of the Forbidden City - 308 originally, 231 surviving, their type and number per courtyard fixed by Qing regulation, set where a timber hall stood far from water - which is the principle our compound yards borrow: standing water where the buildings are. Its limits are that it is a post by a government-affairs account on a news platform, popular writing rather than a study, carrying the statute's figures at one remove; and that the palace was the most heavily regulated and best funded compound in the empire, so the principle carries down to a magistracy yard and the count does not.

hanko-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 藩校 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/藩校)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 藩校 (hanko), on the domain schools of the Edo period. It describes their founding, their curriculum in the Chinese classics and their compulsory enrollment of retainers' sons, gives the count of 255 schools from a published reference work, and lists the schools domain by domain - a list in which some entries name a drill hall (演武場) among the school's buildings.

Why it applies, and its limits: It tells us that a domain seat of consequence had a school, which is what puts one on a capital's map. Its limits are that it is a tertiary encyclopedia summary; that the count of 255 is a late-Edo peak, so near-universal coverage is an end state rather than a constant; and, the one that bears on the drawing, that the article never says a drill hall was a standard part of a school - the drill halls are inferred by us from the names of a few particular schools in its list, so a drill hall drawn on every school compound is our generalization and not the source's.

edo-three-dojos-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 幕末江戸三大道場 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/幕末江戸三大道場)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 幕末江戸三大道場 (the three great dojos of bakumatsu Edo), on the best-known swordsmanship halls of the city in the last decades of the Tokugawa period, with the year each was founded and the district it was founded in: Nihonbashi Minami-Kayabacho, Nihonbashi Shinagawacho and the foot of Kudanzaka. The page also records that two of the three later moved - to Minami-Hatchobori and to Kanda Otamagaike - and that the grouping itself is a post-Meiji convention that spread from a late-Edo rating verse.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for where a martial hall stood in a large city - on central commercial ground among the townspeople rather than out among the samurai residences - which is a placement rule a map has to get right. Its limits are that an encyclopedia is a tertiary summary, and this one rests on a single kendo-magazine feature from 1977; that a "three great" list is a later convention rather than any census of the city's halls, so it cannot tell us how many a town had; that the addresses are founding addresses and two of the halls moved, so a placement rule reads two different moments; and that these are all late Edo, the very end of the era our setting draws on.

jinya-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 陣屋 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/陣屋)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on 陣屋 (jin'ya), the walled administrative seat of a lord too small to hold a castle, or of a shogunal deputy. It sets out what such a seat contained: the lord's residence, the offices, official houses for retainers, earth-walled storehouses and a drill ground.

Why it applies, and its limits: The list of buildings is exactly what we need to lay out a small domain's seat on a map, and it is the compact statement of a program that longer works assume rather than list. It is a tertiary summary, so it gives the program and not any one seat's measured plan, and the passage we lean on describes a small domain's own seat rather than a shogunal deputy's office, which was a different institution with a different program.

tajima-2007-night-soil

Kayo Tajima, "The Marketing of Urban Human Waste in the Early Modern Edo/Tokyo Metropolitan Area", Environnement Urbain / Urban Environment 1 (2007) (https://journals.openedition.org/eue/1039)

What it is: A peer-reviewed article by Kayo Tajima, drawn from her 2005 Tufts doctoral dissertation, in Environnement Urbain / Urban Environment (2007), on the trade in human waste between Edo and the farming villages ringing it. It reads the Tokugawa farming manuals - Nogyo Zensho of 1697, Hyakusho Denki of about 1682 and Koka Shunju of 1707 - alongside the Meiji government's first agricultural inventory of Tokyo prefecture in 1890.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is about the place and the centuries our towns are modeled on, and it describes the arrangements we draw: farmers or their agents came to the door and paid for what the privies held; a village half a day out took one household's whole year of waste under a standing contract and paid in rice; an Edo landlord rather than his tenant owned the tenants' waste and was paid in coin; and a well-placed farmer supplied a daimyo's estate with firewood, charcoal and seedlings in exchange for the right to clean its privies. Its period passages reach us through a modern historian's quotations of the manuals rather than from the manuals themselves, and its prices come from two different worlds: the straw and cash rates around Kanazawa are early eighteenth century, while its tables of quantity and value are from 1890 and 1914, after the land tax was made payable in coin, the roads were paved, iron-axled carts arrived and Western fertilizer chemistry reached Tokyo, so those show a trade already partly modernized. The trade is also a city one, thick within about twenty kilometers of Edo and three to five of an ordinary town; a village beyond that fertilized from its own households, its animals and its woodland instead.

guernica-night-soil

Guernica, "Raising a Stink" (https://www.guernicamag.com/raising-a-stink/; the Howell OSU paper it draws on could not be parsed)

What it is: "Raising a Stink", a November 2021 piece by Lina Zeldovich in Guernica, a nonprofit magazine of art and politics, adapted from her book on human waste and sanitation. It describes the night-soil economy of premodern and early modern Japan - human excrement treated as a valuable farm commodity rather than as refuse, with collection contracts, contested collection rights and disputes over price - and sets it against European practice of the same centuries.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives, in a readable English account, the arrangements a map needs to know existed - the annual contract for a household's night soil paid in rice, and farmers supplying a lord's estate with firewood and seedlings for the right to empty its privies - and it names the scholarship it draws on. Its limits are that it is a popular magazine adaptation of a book rather than a study, used for the existence and form of the arrangements and never for prices, volumes or rates; and that the scholar it relies on for the market, Kayo Tajima, published her study of the Edo night-soil trade in an openly readable journal, so this is a retelling standing in front of a source a reader could reach.

kotobank-benjo

日本大百科全書(ニッポニカ) 「privy (benjo), the toilet」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「便所」), via kotobank.jp/word/便所-131200 (https://kotobank.jp/word/便所-131200)

What it is: The entry for 便所 (benjo, the privy) from Shogakukan's encyclopedia Nipponica, reprinted on the reference site kotobank. It describes how privies were arranged in different kinds of Japanese household - paired and attached to the main buildings in samurai and court-noble residences, gathered into one small separate building for a compound's rowhouses, set behind the guest parlor in townhouses and farmhouses, and provided in farm households as a single building standing clear of the main house.

Why it applies, and its limits: It answers the placement question a map has to answer - whether the privy is inside the house or a separate outbuilding - and it says the detached farm privy holding both the urinal and the privy was the usual arrangement, which is what we draw. Being an encyclopedia entry it is a tertiary summary and it describes a custom rather than counting it: "usual" is not a percentage, so how often our farmsteads show one is our own choice resting on the form the entry gives.

sinyoken-madori

sinyoken.sakura.ne.jp, 「Changes in the position of the toilet seen from the floor plan」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「間取りからみたトイレの位置の変遷」) (camadori.htm) and cayomo016.htm (https://sinyoken.sakura.ne.jp/caffee/camadori.htm)

What it is: An unsigned essay on a private Japanese website about traditional architecture (sinyoken.sakura.ne.jp), undated, on the floor plans of Japanese houses from the warrior residence of the Kamakura period through the Edo townhouse and farmhouse and on into the urban housing of Meiji and after. It reads published plans and lists three works as references, and it relates the farmhouse privy's position to the barn and the back door.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives where the privy stood in a house of rank - an upper and a lower privy, a form with Kamakura roots - and relates the farm privy to the barn and the back door, which is what places the privy on our farmsteads. Its limits are that it is a self-published essay with no named author and no editorial control, the weakest kind of source; that much of its narrative is post-Meiji and modern, and the passage one of our footnotes quotes is explicitly about urban houses after Meiji, so the era has to be selected out of it rather than assumed; and that the upper-and-lower privy is drawn from the warrior residence, a different scale of place from the farmstead it is used to populate.

matsue-bukeyashiki

Matsue Buke Yashiki (Shiomi Nawate), museum building documentation (https://www.matsue-bukeyashiki.jp/about/equipment.html)

What it is: The building documentation published by the museum that operates the Matsue Buke Yashiki, a preserved middle-rank samurai residence standing on the Shiomi Nawate street in the castle town of Matsue, describing the house room by room and the gate on its street front.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a surviving building described by the people who keep it, which is why we use it for the nagayamon standing on the street and doubling as the quarters of the gatekeeper and the household servants, and for a room program that includes the servants' service rooms - both things our residence plans have to place. It is one house in one castle town, presented for visitors rather than as a study, and a restored and interpreted one, so it tells us what a middle-rank residence in Matsue is like and not what a typical residence anywhere was.

hatakata-men-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 畑方免 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/畑方免)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on 畑方免 (hatakata-men), the tax rate assessed on dry fields as opposed to wet paddy in Edo-period Japan. It explains that the dry-field levy was in many places paid in cash rather than in grain, names the Kanto 畑永法 (hata-ei system) as the type case, and notes that the rate was fixed rather than adjusted downward in bad years.

Why it applies, and its limits: It answers a question the map raises directly - why coin circulates in a farming village at all - by showing that the dry-field half of a peasant household's tax obligation was normally settled in money, which means a village growing dry crops needs somewhere to sell them. It is a short tertiary summary of a fiscal practice that varied by domain, and it is specific to Tokugawa Japan; we take the shape of the arrangement from it rather than any rate, and it says nothing about how widely the cash rule held outside the Kanto example it names.

shoinzukuri-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 書院造 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/書院造)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 書院造 (shoin-zukuri), on the formal residential style of the warrior and priestly household that began late in the medieval period and was completed early in the Edo period: the study with its alcove and shelves, the formal rooms in a row, the veranda and the garden they open onto.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the orientation rule our compounds follow - the garden on the south of the formal rooms, beyond the veranda - which fixes the show face of every compound we draw. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary describing an ideal type rather than a surveyed house; that the article carries its editors' own notice that it lacks adequate citations, worth saying when a rule that fixes every compound's face is taken from it; and that it is silent about the north service side, so the kitchen and the servants' quarters rest on other sources.
