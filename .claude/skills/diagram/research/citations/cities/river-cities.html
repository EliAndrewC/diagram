Citations: River cities: the research behind the river, moat-junction and wharf rules

The passages behind the footnotes of River cities: the research behind the river, moat-junction and wharf rules - first the works they quote and why each one counts, then the notes themselves. Hover a footnote number on that page and the note appears beside it; the numbers here are the same ones.

The works cited on this page

kaifeng-pmc7048742

Storozum et al., "Geoarchaeological evidence of the AD 1642 Yellow River flood that destroyed Kaifeng", Scientific Reports 2020 (PMC 7048742) (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-60169-1)

What it is: Storozum and colleagues, "Geoarchaeological evidence of the AD 1642 Yellow River flood that destroyed Kaifeng", a peer-reviewed open access paper in Scientific Reports, 2020. The authors read the flood deposits under the modern city and set the 1642 disaster in the river's long record, which brought roughly forty floods to Kaifeng over the past three thousand years.

Why it applies, and its limits: Primary fieldwork on exactly the question a river-city map asks - five excavations through the flood layers of a city that sat below its river - so it is the strongest kind of evidence the record has for the danger of a bank. Its limits are that the frequency we quote (about forty floods in three thousand years) is a background statement in the paper rather than a result of its excavations, which measure the 1642 deposit and what was built over it - still a peer-reviewed statement, which is why we prefer it to uncited counts; that it is one city on the most flood-prone river in the world, the extreme rather than the average; and that it is the Chinese half of a setting also modeled on Japan.

kaifeng-flood-1642-enwiki

en.wikipedia 1642 Yellow River flood (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1642_Yellow_River_flood)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on the 1642 Yellow River flood, in which the dikes above Kaifeng were cut during a siege and the city was destroyed. It gives the traditional casualty figure, some three hundred thousand of the city's three hundred and seventy eight thousand inhabitants lost to the flood and to the famine and disease that followed.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the scale of what a river can do to a walled city in one year - the dikes cut during a siege, and most of the population dead - which is the reason a river city on our maps keeps its distance from the bank. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary of a single catastrophic year, not the ordinary risk of the bank; that its death toll is cited to one modern book on an adjacent subject rather than to the chronicles, and the encyclopedia contradicts itself between this article and its Kaifeng article on the numbers, as the footnote records; that the page gives three accounts of who breached the dikes, so we say only that they were cut during the siege; and that it is imperial China.

okayama-castle

Okayama Castle site and visitor documentation (https://okayama-castle.jp/learn-history-en/)

What it is: The English history page of the official Okayama Castle site, the castle's own visitor documentation, recounting how Ukita Naoie and his son Hideie built the castle on the hill of Okayama in the late sixteenth century, how the branching channels of the Asahi River were arranged to protect its north and east sides and used to form its moats, and how the castle town was laid out long and narrow between them.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is an attested Japanese case of a castle taking a river branch as the moat on two of its flanks, which is the thing our river city and edge castle maps need a real example for. It is a site's own account written to introduce the castle to visitors rather than an archaeological or documentary study, so we use it for the fact and the shape of the river diversion and would not press it for dimensions, dates or anything finer than it plainly states.

pan-gate-enwiki

en.wikipedia Pan Gate (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_Gate)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on Pan Gate (盘门), the surviving gate at the southwest corner of Suzhou's old city wall, which describes it as two separate gates in one structure, a land gate for road traffic and a water gate for boats, connecting the city's inner canals and its moat to the Grand Canal.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives a real case of a city gate built as a pair - one opening for the road and one for the canal, the water gate joining the inner canals and moat to the Grand Canal - which is what a canal town's gates on our maps are drawn from. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary whose whole article rests on two references, a city page about a different gate and a travel guide; that it is one gate of one exceptionally canal-dense southern city; that its dating is loose, a Yuan rebuild on an ancient foundation; and that it shows a water gate existed and says nothing about how one was closed.

guanxiang-zdic

漢典 zdic.net, 关厢 (https://zdic.net/hans/%E5%85%B3%E5%8E%A2)

What it is: The entry for 关厢 (guanxiang) in Zdic (漢典), an online Chinese character and word dictionary. It defines the word as the streets immediately outside a city gate and the area adjoining them.

Why it applies, and its limits: A dictionary is the right authority for what a word means, and what we take is the meaning: that Chinese had a settled term for the built-up strip outside the wall, which tells us such strips were a normal and named part of a walled city. That is all it supports - it says nothing about what stood there, and the commonly repeated claim that trade and inns concentrated in the guanxiang is not on this page and could not be read on any public page we reached, so nothing on our maps about what fills that strip rests on this source.

kaifeng-enwiki

en.wikipedia Kaifeng (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaifeng) - "Five major floods devastated the city between 1375 and 1416, and flooding occurred more than 300 times from the Southern Song Dynasty to the late Qing dynasty"

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on Kaifeng, the Chinese city on the Yellow River that served as an imperial capital and has been repeatedly buried and rebuilt by floods. The passage we use counts the disasters: five major floods between 1375 and 1416, and more than three hundred flood events from the Southern Song to the late Qing.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the shape of Kaifeng's history under the Yellow River - the repeated floods that buried the city layer on layer - which is the extreme case a river-city map is measured against. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary, and the flood count we quote from it carries no citation on the page, so it is an uncited encyclopedia statement, with the peer-reviewed frequency in the peer-reviewed flood study cited beside it the better anchor; that Kaifeng under a perched, silt-choked river is the worst case of river-city danger and not the average; and that it is the Chinese half of a setting also modeled on Japan.

kashi-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 河岸 and 雁木 (港湾) (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/河岸)

What it is: Two Japanese Wikipedia articles: 河岸 (kashi), on the river landing that grew into a warehouse and merchant district from the medieval period to its Edo peak, and 雁木 (gangi), a short stub on the stair-shaped landing structure that lets people and cargo go up and down as the water level changes, in whatever material it was built.

Why it applies, and its limits: They give the form of a working river landing - the stepped bank where cargo is worked, the warehouses and merchants behind it - which is what our river towns' waterfronts are drawn from. Their limits are that both are tertiary and below the usual encyclopedia bar - one carries a notice that its sources are insufficient, the other is a stub - so neither has references of its own to carry a claim; and that the form is taken from them and no date or dimension.

watersa-junction-angle

Water SA, "Numerical study of junction-angle effects on flow pattern in a river confluence located in a river bend" (https://scielo.org.za/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S1816-79502016000100006)

What it is: A 2016 paper in Water SA, the journal of South Africa's Water Research Commission, reporting a three-dimensional numerical model of a river confluence that sits inside a river bend. It runs the model across junction angles from 30 to 115 degrees and reports what each angle does to the zone of separated flow at the mouth, to the streamwise velocity near the inner wall, to the strength of the secondary spiral flow, and to the bed shear stress inside the junction.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for one thing: the DIRECTION in which a junction's angle changes the way a side channel enters a larger one, which is what sets the two feet of a river-fed moat where it meets its river. Its limits are worth stating plainly - it models a confluence located in a bend, so its figures (a 59% rise in peak bed shear between 30 and 115 degrees) belong to that geometry rather than to a straight reach; it is a numerical model rather than a survey of a real waterway; it is modern hydraulics, so it describes what water does and says nothing about what a premodern builder intended or achieved; and the slack water behind the mouth and the attack on the far bank that our record reads from it are not among its findings. One limit deserves to be spelled out, because it bears on whether the direction we take from the paper carries at all: the model holds the tributary's width and its share of the discharge fixed, at the proportions of a laboratory flume, whereas the offtake that feeds a moat draws a share of its river's flow smaller by orders of magnitude - a trickle beside a current rather than one stream meeting another - so we cannot be sure even the direction of the effect survives the change of scale.
