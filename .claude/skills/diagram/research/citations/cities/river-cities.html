Citations: River cities: the research behind the river, moat-junction and wharf rules

The passages behind the footnotes of River cities: the research behind the river, moat-junction and wharf rules - first the works they quote and why each one counts, then the notes themselves. Hover a footnote number on that page and the note appears beside it; the numbers here are the same ones.

The works cited on this page

kaifeng-pmc7048742

Storozum et al., "Geoarchaeological evidence of the AD 1642 Yellow River flood that destroyed Kaifeng", Scientific Reports 2020 (PMC 7048742) (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-60169-1)

What it is: Storozum and colleagues, "Geoarchaeological evidence of the AD 1642 Yellow River flood that destroyed Kaifeng", a peer-reviewed open access paper in Scientific Reports, 2020. The authors read the flood deposits under the modern city and set the 1642 disaster in the river's long record, which brought roughly forty floods to Kaifeng over the past three thousand years.

Why it applies, and its limits: Primary fieldwork on exactly the question a river-city map asks - five excavations through the flood layers of a city that sat below its river - so it is the strongest kind of evidence the record has for the danger of a bank. Its limits are that the frequency we quote (about forty floods in three thousand years) is a background statement in the paper rather than a result of its excavations, which measure the 1642 deposit and what was built over it - still a peer-reviewed statement, which is why we prefer it to uncited counts; that it is one city on the most flood-prone river in the world, the extreme rather than the average; and that it is the Chinese half of a setting also modeled on Japan.

kaifeng-enwiki

en.wikipedia Kaifeng (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaifeng) - "Five major floods devastated the city between 1375 and 1416, and flooding occurred more than 300 times from the Southern Song Dynasty to the late Qing dynasty"

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on Kaifeng, the Chinese city on the Yellow River that served as an imperial capital and has been repeatedly buried and rebuilt by floods. The passage we use counts the disasters: five major floods between 1375 and 1416, and more than three hundred flood events from the Southern Song to the late Qing.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the shape of Kaifeng's history under the Yellow River - the repeated floods that buried the city layer on layer - which is the extreme case a river-city map is measured against. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary, and the flood count we quote from it carries no citation on the page, so it is an uncited encyclopedia statement, with the peer-reviewed frequency in the peer-reviewed flood study cited beside it the better anchor; that Kaifeng under a perched, silt-choked river is the worst case of river-city danger and not the average; and that it is the Chinese half of a setting also modeled on Japan.

kaifeng-flood-1642-enwiki

en.wikipedia 1642 Yellow River flood (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1642_Yellow_River_flood)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on the 1642 Yellow River flood, in which the dikes above Kaifeng were cut during a siege and the city was destroyed. It gives the traditional casualty figure, some three hundred thousand of the city's three hundred and seventy eight thousand inhabitants lost to the flood and to the famine and disease that followed.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the scale of what a river can do to a walled city in one year - the dikes cut during a siege, and most of the population dead - which is the reason a river city on our maps keeps its distance from the bank. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary of a single catastrophic year, not the ordinary risk of the bank; that its death toll is cited to one modern book on an adjacent subject rather than to the chronicles, and the encyclopedia contradicts itself between this article and its Kaifeng article on the numbers, as the footnote records; that the page gives three accounts of who breached the dikes, so we say only that they were cut during the siege; and that it is imperial China.

xiangyang-cheng-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 襄阳城 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/襄阳城)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on the walled city of Xiangyang, Hubei, on the Han River's south bank; an encyclopedia article.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the city's siting with water on three sides and the moat's 250 m at its widest point, which is the one-bank form this page draws; it does not say the moat was dug or fed from the river.

okayama-castle

Okayama Castle site and visitor documentation (https://okayama-castle.jp/learn-history-en/)

What it is: The English history page of the official Okayama Castle site, the castle's own visitor documentation, recounting how Ukita Naoie and his son Hideie built the castle on the hill of Okayama in the late sixteenth century, how the branching channels of the Asahi River were arranged to protect its north and east sides and used to form its moats, and how the castle town was laid out long and narrow between them.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is an attested Japanese case of a castle taking a river branch as the moat on two of its flanks, which is the thing our river city and edge castle maps need a real example for. It is a site's own account written to introduce the castle to visitors rather than an archaeological or documentary study, so we use it for the fact and the shape of the river diversion and would not press it for dimensions, dates or anything finer than it plainly states.

shanghai-xiancheng-chengqiang-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 上海县城墙 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E4%B8%8A%E6%B5%B7%E5%8E%BF%E5%9F%8E%E5%A2%99)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on the wall of Shanghai's county town: its building in 1553 against the wokou, its gates and three water gates, and its moat - some 1,500 zhang long and six zhang wide - which communicated with the water gates and with the Huangpu's tides and was dredged by the magistrate in 1753 so that boats could pass.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for a Chinese county seat's moat as a worked waterway: tied to its river for navigation, fresh water and drainage, and dredged when a magistrate had it done. It is a tertiary summary of one Yangtze-delta town whose moat was tidal rather than fed from upstream, so it speaks to a moat kept full by its river and to dredging as an official's act, not to the upstream-intake and downstream-return arrangement these maps draw; the 1753 dredging is a single dated event with no interval or cycle behind it, so it attests that dredging happened rather than a maintenance rhythm; and the article gives its moat two different lengths (1,620 zhang in its lead, some 1,500 in its moat section), so its own measurements are not consistent.

pingyao-enwiki

en.wikipedia, Pingyao (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pingyao)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on Pingyao, the walled county town in Shanxi: its position on the east bank of the Fen river in the Taiyuan basin, its Ming wall, and the 4 m moat outside the wall.

Why it applies, and its limits: It places the town on its river. A tertiary summary used for the siting and the moat, and its 4 m moat is a measurement of the restored World Heritage site as it stands today, with no period source given for it.

pan-gate-enwiki

en.wikipedia Pan Gate (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_Gate)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on Pan Gate (盘门), the surviving gate at the southwest corner of Suzhou's old city wall, which describes it as two separate gates in one structure, a land gate for road traffic and a water gate for boats, connecting the city's inner canals and its moat to the Grand Canal.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives a real case of a city gate built as a pair - one opening for the road and one for the canal, the water gate joining the inner canals and moat to the Grand Canal - which is what a canal town's gates on our maps are drawn from. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary whose whole article rests on two references, a city page about a different gate and a travel guide; that it is one gate of one exceptionally canal-dense southern city; that its dating is loose, a Yuan rebuild on an ancient foundation; and that it shows a water gate existed and says nothing about how one was closed.

panmen-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 盘门 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/盘门)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on Pan Gate at Suzhou, the paired land and water gate whose surviving fabric dates to 1351 under the Yuan, its barbican added in 1356; an encyclopedia article.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the water gate's two layers, its sluice and grating and its depth, which is the gated canal entry this page draws; it names no arch and no basin; one exceptional surviving gate, restored four times through 1986, so its depth and grating are the monument's as it stands.

guanxiang-zdic

漢典 zdic.net, 关厢 (https://zdic.net/hans/%E5%85%B3%E5%8E%A2)

What it is: The entry for 关厢 (guanxiang) in Zdic (漢典), an online Chinese character and word dictionary. It defines the word as the streets immediately outside a city gate and the area adjoining them.

Why it applies, and its limits: A dictionary is the right authority for what a word means, and what we take is the meaning: that Chinese had a settled term for the built-up strip outside the wall, which tells us such strips were a normal and named part of a walled city. That is all it supports - it says nothing about what stood there, and the commonly repeated claim that trade and inns concentrated in the guanxiang is not on this page and could not be read on any public page we reached, so nothing on our maps about what fills that strip rests on this source.

shiliupu-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 十六铺 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/十六铺)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on Shiliupu, the wharf district of old Shanghai between the county wall and the Huangpu; an encyclopedia article.

Why it applies, and its limits: It attests a working riverfront of moorings and wholesale trade outside the water-side gate, which is this page's wharf outside the wall; its counts are Republican-era; the district as named dates from the 1860s and much of the article describes the treaty-port and steam-era port, so only the pre-treaty riverfront is taken.

watersa-junction-angle

Water SA, "Numerical study of junction-angle effects on flow pattern in a river confluence located in a river bend" (https://scielo.org.za/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S1816-79502016000100006)

What it is: A 2016 paper in Water SA, the journal of South Africa's Water Research Commission, reporting a three-dimensional numerical model of a river confluence that sits inside a river bend. It runs the model across junction angles from 30 to 115 degrees and reports what each angle does to the zone of separated flow at the mouth, to the streamwise velocity near the inner wall, to the strength of the secondary spiral flow, and to the bed shear stress inside the junction.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for one thing: the DIRECTION in which a junction's angle changes the way a side channel enters a larger one, which is what sets the two feet of a river-fed moat where it meets its river. Its limits are worth stating plainly - it models a confluence located in a bend, so its figures (a 59% rise in peak bed shear between 30 and 115 degrees) belong to that geometry rather than to a straight reach; it is a numerical model rather than a survey of a real waterway; it is modern hydraulics, so it describes what water does and says nothing about what a premodern builder intended or achieved; and the slack water behind the mouth and the attack on the far bank that our record reads from it are not among its findings. One limit deserves to be spelled out, because it bears on whether the direction we take from the paper carries at all: the model holds the tributary's width and its share of the discharge fixed, at the proportions of a laboratory channel, whereas the offtake that feeds a moat draws a share of its river's flow smaller by orders of magnitude - a trickle beside a current rather than one stream meeting another - so we cannot be sure even the direction of the effect survives the change of scale.

ignou-silt-control

Indira Gandhi National Open University, "Unit 10: Silt Control", Irrigation Engineering course unit, eGyanKosh (https://www.egyankosh.ac.in/bitstream/123456789/32984/1/Unit-10.pdf)

What it is: A unit of the Irrigation Engineering course of India's national open university, on silt control at a canal headworks: how sediment travels in the lower layers of a flow, how a regulator is oriented against the parent flow and what that orientation does to the sediment the canal takes in, where an intake belongs on a bend, and the raised sill and skimmer wall at a mouth.

Why it applies, and its limits: It states the offtake angle our drawn junctions follow, and states it as a reason rather than a convention: a canal taken off at 90 degrees to the main flow is the most objectionable orientation of the regulator there is, and a diversion angle of 30 to 45 degrees against the river's flow is what is normally recommended, because that produces a suitable curvature of the flow lines through the regulator. Its limits: it is a modern teaching text for engineered headworks with mechanical gates rather than an account of how a premodern village tapped a stream, so what transfers is the hydraulic reason and not the works; its angles are for a canal leaving a RIVER, and the same unit prescribes 60 to 80 degrees for a distributary leaving a parent canal, so a drawn junction has to say which of the two it is; it carries no lower bound below 30 degrees, so none is attributed to it; that the raised sill with a skimmer wall it also describes is a twentieth-century silt-excluder assembly at a gated barrage, with no premodern earthen counterpart, so it explains why silt enters a mouth low and clean water high and is not a feature this record draws; and both its access and its text are imperfect - a certificate warning in front of it, and an OCR layer that damages the very degree signs the finding turns on.

kotobank-hori

Digital Daijisen (Shogakukan), s.v. 堀, on kotobank (https://kotobank.jp/word/堀)

What it is: Kotobank's reproduction of the dictionary entry on the hori, the moat or cut channel.

Why it applies, and its limits: It defines the water moat by the standing water it holds, which is the stage this page says a moat wants; its first sense is a channel that passes water, so the entry cannot say a moat never does.

xian-wall-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 西安城墙 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E8%A5%BF%E5%AE%89%E5%9F%8E%E5%A2%99)

What it is: The same Chinese Wikipedia article 西安城墙, here for its account of the rampart itself - 12 m high, 15 to 18 m thick at the base and 12 to 14 m across the top, with 98 projecting towers standing 12 m out from the outer face and rising level with the wall.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the anchor numbers for how a great wall and its towers are drawn - the circuit's length and the area it encloses, the rampart's height and its thickness at base and top, the enemy platforms' projection, width and spacing, set at the range of bows and firearms. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary; that this is among the largest walls ever built, a ceiling rather than a norm; and that the numbers describe the wall as it stands after the brick facing of 1568, the works of 1781 and modern conservation, not a wall measured when it was raised in 1374.

hori-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 堀 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%A0%80)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the moat: its types, the natural moat made of an existing river, the sogamae moat around a castle town, boats on river-fed moats, and the sectioning of a wet moat with earthen weirs into pools at different levels.

Why it applies, and its limits: It supplies the vocabulary and the practices our moats are drawn with - the natural moat, the sectioned moat, the town moat. A tertiary summary used for those definitions.

water-gate-enwiki

en.wikipedia "Water gate" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_gate)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on the water gate, the fortified gate opening from a wall onto water, with surviving examples; an encyclopedia article.

Why it applies, and its limits: It attests the form with premodern examples on a tidal river, which is the river-face gate this page allows; European examples; a stub with no East Asian example, giving the generic form only, while the setting's own form rests on the Suzhou Pan Gate pair (panmen-zhwiki, suzhou-panmen-gov).

pier-enwiki

en.wikipedia "Pier" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pier)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on the pier, working and pleasure, and why a pier is long; an encyclopedia article.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the working pier's purpose, the pleasure pier's nineteenth-century origin and reach to deep water as the reason for length, which are the wharf forms this page contrasts; its reach example is tidal, not a shelving river bank.

gangi-hiroshima-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 雁木 (広島市) (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/雁木_(広島市))

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the gangi of Hiroshima, the stone stepped landings and revetments of the delta city's rivers; an encyclopedia article.

Why it applies, and its limits: It attests the stepped landing as itself a revetment, in timber and then granite, built where boats berthed and cargo was landed, and the step against the floating pier for a moving water level; a delta port on a 4 m tide, not a river's annual stage.

matou-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 碼頭 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/碼頭)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on the matou, the wharf or landing, in its two forms; an encyclopedia article.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the projecting mole and the flight of steps as the two forms of a landing, which this page draws; it names no material and ranks neither form; a general modern definition that dates neither form, so the premodern attestation of the step rests on the Hiroshima gangi page.

gangi-kowan-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 雁木 (港湾) (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/雁木_(港湾))

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the gangi as a harbor structure, the stepped landing of pre-modern boat landings; a short encyclopedia article.

Why it applies, and its limits: It defines the stepped landing and gives its reason - the water surface rising and falling with tide and river flow against a moored hull, a gangplank laid to the step rather than to a quay wall - which is this page's hydrology; its examples are sea-facing.

kashi-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 河岸 and 雁木 (港湾) (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/河岸)

What it is: Two Japanese Wikipedia articles: 河岸 (kashi), on the river landing that grew into a warehouse and merchant district from the medieval period to its Edo peak, and 雁木 (gangi), a short stub on the stair-shaped landing structure that lets people and cargo go up and down as the water level changes, in whatever material it was built.

Why it applies, and its limits: They give the form of a working river landing - the stepped bank where cargo is worked, the warehouses and merchants behind it - which is what our river towns' waterfronts are drawn from. Their limits are that both are tertiary and below the usual encyclopedia bar - one carries a notice that its sources are insufficient, the other is a stub - so neither has references of its own to carry a claim; and that the form is taken from them and no date or dimension.

xunqi-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 汛期 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/汛期)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on the flood season, the yearly rise of a river's level; an encyclopedia article.

Why it applies, and its limits: It attests that a river's level rises seasonally and predictably, which is the hydrology behind this page's stepped quay; it gives no magnitude.

takasebune-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 高瀬舟 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/高瀬舟)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the takasebune, the flat-bottomed river cargo boat, with Heian and Edo records of its dimensions; an encyclopedia article.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the boat's recorded lengths and beam and its cargoes, which are the barge this page moors at its quay; the figures are records of particular boats, not a fleet mean; the two records are nine centuries apart (884 and 1817) and differ by more than double in length.

kotobank-takasebune

Nihon Dai-hyakka Zensho and Seisenban Nihon Kokugo Daijiten, s.v. 高瀬舟, on kotobank (https://kotobank.jp/word/高瀬舟)

What it is: Kotobank's reproduction of the encyclopedia and dictionary entries on the takasebune, the shallow-draft river cargo boat, from the fifteen-koku Kyoto canal boat to the two-hundred-koku Tone barge.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the type's range and the large Tone boat's length, which set the frontage this page moors barges along; reference entries with no drawings; the 27 m Tone boat is the extreme of the type, an order above the 15-koku canal boat on the same page.

floating-dock-enwiki

en.wikipedia "Floating dock (jetty)" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_dock_(jetty))

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on the floating dock, the pontoon platform that rides the water level; an encyclopedia article.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the modern marina's answer to a moving water level, which this page sets against the stepped landing; a modern structure.
