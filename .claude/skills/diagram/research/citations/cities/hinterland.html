Citations: Outside the walls: the research behind the estate and in-wall farmland rules

The passages behind the footnotes of Outside the walls: the research behind the estate and in-wall farmland rules - first the works they quote and why each one counts, then the notes themselves. Hover a footnote number on that page and the note appears beside it; the numbers here are the same ones.

The works cited on this page

cdlib-local-elites

Esherick & Rankin (eds.), Chinese Local Elites and Patterns of Dominance, introduction (UC Press e-book, https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/public/book/chinese-local-elites-and-patterns-of-dominance.html)

What it is: The editors' introduction to Chinese Local Elites and Patterns of Dominance, edited by Joseph W. Esherick and Mary Backus Rankin (University of California Press, 1990), a collection of eleven scholarly essays on how local elites held power in late imperial and Republican China. The introduction summarizes the volume's finding that by Qing times much of the gentry had moved into administrative centers and canal towns and drew their income from land they no longer lived on.

Why it applies, and its limits: It answers the question our hinterland has to settle, whether rich houses cluster along the road outside a city gate, and it answers it against the clustering: an urbanized, absentee landlord class collecting through agents leaves the countryside without a ring of grand suburban compounds. Its limits are real: the absentee-landlord pattern it describes is the Lower Yangzi's, which we read as a general Chinese one; the volume's weight is on the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, later than the Ming-Qing and Edo anchor our maps take, so the pattern is being read backward; it is about China rather than Japan; and it describes where elites lived and how they drew rent rather than what their houses looked like.

guanxiang-zdic

漢典 zdic.net, 关厢 (https://zdic.net/hans/%E5%85%B3%E5%8E%A2)

What it is: The entry for 关厢 (guanxiang) in Zdic (漢典), an online Chinese character and word dictionary. It defines the word as the streets immediately outside a city gate and the area adjoining them.

Why it applies, and its limits: A dictionary is the right authority for what a word means, and what we take is the meaning: that Chinese had a settled term for the built-up strip outside the wall, which tells us such strips were a normal and named part of a walled city. That is all it supports - it says nothing about what stood there, and the commonly repeated claim that trade and inns concentrated in the guanxiang is not on this page and could not be read on any public page we reached, so nothing on our maps about what fills that strip rests on this source.

madian-mosque-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 马甸清真寺 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%A9%AC%E7%94%B8%E6%B8%85%E7%9C%9F%E5%AF%BA)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on the Madian mosque in Beijing, whose history section describes the place rather than only the building: the trunk road running north out of Deshengmen, the tribute horse trains that sold their culled animals there, the merchants in horses, cattle, sheep and camels who lodged there, the market that formed of itself, and the later move of the horse market into the guanxiang outside the gate, where at its height there were 48 horse inns.

Why it applies, and its limits: The gate suburb is a shape our city maps draw at every main gate, and this is the only readable page found that describes one as a place with trades and a count rather than defining the word. Its limits are that it is tertiary; that this is the busiest gate of a capital and one trade's premises, so the count is a ceiling for a provincial seat rather than a measurement of one; and that the figure carries no date beyond "at its height".

shizen-teibo-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 自然堤防 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/自然堤防) - 「On alluvial lowland, long-established settlements are sited first of all on the natural levees, which are also used as dry fields.」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「沖積低地において、古くからの集落はまず自然堤防上に立地し、また畑として利用される」), against the 後背湿地 behind it

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 自然堤防 ("natural levee"), on the low ridge of coarser sediment a river builds along its own banks when it floods, and on the wetter backswamp (後背湿地) that lies behind it on an alluvial plain.

Why it applies, and its limits: This is the single most useful fact for laying out a lowland village: houses and dry fields go up on the levee, which is the only naturally drained ground for miles, and the paddy goes into the backswamp behind - a rule the article states directly for long-established Japanese settlements. It is an encyclopedia summary of settled physical geography rather than a survey, so we take the siting principle from it and not any measurement; and it describes Japanese alluvial lowland, so it applies to our river-plain settlements and says nothing about hill or fan country.

chang-morphology-walled-capitals

Sen-dou Chang, "The Morphology of Walled Capitals", in Skinner (ed.), The City in Late Imperial China (http://web.stanford.edu/~mel1000/sen.pdf)

What it is: Sen-dou Chang's scholarly chapter "The Morphology of Walled Capitals", in the geographer G. William Skinner's edited collection The City in Late Imperial China (Stanford University Press, 1977). It is a comparative study of the physical form of Chinese walled administrative cities at every rank, county capitals included: the models their street nets follow between the gates, the drum tower at the crossing, and the cultivation, ponds and lakes that occupied surprisingly large parts of the ground inside the walls. The copy available online is a page-image scan.

Why it applies, and its limits: This is the strongest source behind the way our county seats are laid out, a thin skeleton of trunk streets with lanes elsewhere, walls sized to a city's administrative rank rather than its population, and a real share of the enclosed ground left unbuilt as siege insurance; Chang treats county capitals by name, so for the county seat it is a direct source and not a stand-in. Its limits are stated in our own text: the only proportion Chang gives is the lake at Jinan taking about a fifth of the walled area, so the street share and the share of unbuilt ground our maps use are this record's synthesis rather than his measurement; the public copy has no text layer, so every passage quoted from it is our own reading of the scan; and for the hamlet and village tiers his cities are a larger and grander class of place than what we draw.

king-forty-centuries

F. H. King, Farmers of Forty Centuries; Or, Permanent Agriculture in China, Korea and Japan (1911), Project Gutenberg ebook 5350 (https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5350/pg5350-images.html)

What it is: The American soil scientist F. H. King's account of what he saw farming in China, Korea and Japan on a journey made in 1909, published in 1911 - field sizes, holdings, manuring, and the movement of night soil out of the cities, described at first hand and long out of copyright.

Why it applies, and its limits: It states plainly for Japan the two things our tract-size entry needed and could not get from Buck, whose own survey is in a lending-restricted scan on no page a reader can open: that the lands worked by one family are seldom contiguous, may be widely scattered and are very often rented - with the consolidation laws in force since 1900 named as what was passed to undo it, which is the strongest form the statement can take - and that the average worked by one family is nearer 2.5 acres, which sits beside our roughly 1 cho figure as an independent reading. Its limits: it is a traveler's eyewitness account rather than a survey, so its averages are ones he was told or read rather than ones he measured; it describes 1909, at the very end of the order our maps depict and after Meiji land reform had begun, so a figure from it is the last state of that world and not its typical one; and the book's admiration for what it describes is well known and shapes what it chose to record.

chinese-city-wall-enwiki

en.wikipedia Chinese city wall (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_city_wall)

What it is: The English Wikipedia survey article on Chinese city walls: their construction from a rammed-earth core faced in brick or stone, their surviving examples at Nanjing, Xi'an and Kaifeng, and their plan, rectangular for capitals and important northern cities but irregular in broken country, where the line followed the terrain.

Why it applies, and its limits: It supports the shape rule our town walls obey, that a wall is a costly thing pulled tight around what must actually be defended and bent by the ground, rather than a tidy circle drawn around empty space. It is a tertiary summary covering the whole country over two thousand years, it gives no costs and no dimensions tied to a settlement's rank, and the economic argument our text makes from the shape is our own reasoning rather than the article's.

kohai-shicchi-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 後背湿地 and 自然堤防 (with GSI notes) (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/後背湿地)

What it is: Japanese Wikipedia's article on 後背湿地 (kohai shicchi, the backswamp) read together with its companion article on 自然堤防 (the natural levee), explaining how a river builds raised banks along its own channel and leaves poorly drained, fine-grained lowland behind them, and how that lowland was farmed.

Why it applies, and its limits: The landform does not change with the century, so a modern description of it serves a premodern map directly, and we use it for what lies behind a river's bank - wet ground, traditionally worked as paddy, extended over time by draining marsh - which decides where our fields and our houses go on a floodplain. It is a tertiary summary, so we take the description of the landform and its traditional use and no number from it.

zhangjiakou-fort-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 张家口堡 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%BC%A0%E5%AE%B6%E5%8F%A3%E5%A0%A1)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on the Zhangjiakou fort, which records that its guanxiang was established in 1480 when the fort was enlarged, because the population inside had grown past what the walled ground could hold, and names the crossroads and streets that formed within the suburb.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the one readable case of a gate suburb that formed for the OPPOSITE reason to the general pattern this record quotes - the walled ground filled first and the overflow went outside - which is worth a reader knowing beside the pattern. Its limits are that it is tertiary, that one case is not a refutation of a general pattern, and that this is a frontier garrison on the Mongolian trade route rather than an ordinary county seat.

qimin-yaoshu-zhongkui

賈思勰 齊民要術 (Jia Sixie, Qimin Yaoshu, c. 540), 卷第三, 種葵第十七 (the chapter on planting mallow), Chinese Wikisource (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E9%BD%8A%E6%B0%91%E8%A6%81%E8%A1%93/%E5%8D%B7%E7%AC%AC%E4%B8%89)

What it is: Book 3 of the sixth-century Chinese agricultural treatise, in the chapter on planting mallow, which sets out how a garden bed is made and how large it is, with the author's own interlinear notes giving his reasons, and then describes the winter-crop method for a commercial market garden of thirty mu backing onto the wall of a town with a market, with ten wells sunk along its length.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a primary text that states a dimension AND the reason for it - the bed is kept small because water cannot otherwise be spread evenly over it by hand, and because nobody should have to step into it - which is the rarest and most useful shape of evidence a drawing rule can rest on, and it sizes the very thing this record was asking about, a garden serving a walled town. Its limits are that it is Northern Wei north China, far earlier than the Ming and Edo anchor; that it prescribes good practice rather than reporting what was usual; that the text is read from a crowd-transcribed wiki copy rather than a critical edition, so a disputed character would have to be settled against a scholarly one; and that its measures are in bu and mu, which this record has not converted from a metrology source - and a Northern Wei bu is not a Ming bu, so converting with a later metrology would be positively wrong rather than merely unsupported. Every figure in feet derived from it is our own arithmetic.
