Citations: Domain capitals: the research behind the castle-town tier

The passages behind the footnotes of Domain capitals: the research behind the castle-town tier - first the works they quote and why each one counts, then the notes themselves. Hover a footnote number on that page and the note appears beside it; the numbers here are the same ones.

The works cited on this page

jokamachi-wiki-corpus

Japanese Wiki Corpus, Jokamachi (translation of the ja.wikipedia article) (https://www.japanesewiki.com/history/Jokamachi.html)

What it is: The same translated castle-town article in the Japanese Wiki Corpus, of how a castle town was arranged: the castle at the center, rings of samurai and foot soldier quarters graded by rank, streets named for the trade that worked them, the temple district on the rim, main roads led past the castle's front to show off the lord's standing, and the total outer enclosure that a few great towns built.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the whole grammar of the castle town in English - the castle at the center, the rank-graded rings of samurai residence, the occupational merchant wards, the temple quarter on the rim, the main road led past the castle's front - which is the skeleton every capital map is laid out on. Its limits are that its examples are the largest Japanese castle towns, a scale that has to be reduced before it reaches a provincial seat; that it is a tertiary summary at one further remove, the English being a machine-translation research corpus (the bilingual corpus of Wikipedia's Kyoto-topic articles made by NICT, Japan's National Institute of Information and Communications Technology) and a frozen snapshot rather than the maintained article; and that two other entries in this registry point at the same article and at the Japanese original it translates, so the three cannot corroborate one another.

okayama-castle

Okayama Castle site and visitor documentation (https://okayama-castle.jp/learn-history-en/)

What it is: The English history page of the official Okayama Castle site, the castle's own visitor documentation, recounting how Ukita Naoie and his son Hideie built the castle on the hill of Okayama in the late sixteenth century, how the branching channels of the Asahi River were arranged to protect its north and east sides and used to form its moats, and how the castle town was laid out long and narrow between them.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is an attested Japanese case of a castle taking a river branch as the moat on two of its flanks, which is the thing our river city and edge castle maps need a real example for. It is a site's own account written to introduce the castle to visitors rather than an archaeological or documentary study, so we use it for the fact and the shape of the river diversion and would not press it for dimensions, dates or anything finer than it plainly states.

hirosaki-castle

Hirosaki Castle (Wikipedia; Hirosaki Park official site) (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hirosaki_Castle)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on Hirosaki Castle in Aomori prefecture, seat of the Tsugaru clan, a tozama daimyo house of 47,000 koku. The article gives the castle's plan in meters (612 east to west by 947 north to south) and the keep as a three-story tower 14.4 m high; it gives no area for the enceinte or the keep.

Why it applies, and its limits: A modest domain's castle is the right anchor for a typical capital, and it makes the point a reader most often gets wrong: the tower is a small part of the castle, not the castle itself. It is a tertiary summary, and what it measures is the surviving park as it stands today, so its dimensions approximate the historical footprint rather than survey it. The hectare figures our castle sizing rests on (about 50 ha with the moats, a keep under one) are not on this page: they came from the park's own site, which we have not been able to cite, so they stand as a guess checked against the 612 by 947 m outline the article does give.

himeji-castle

Himeji Castle (Wikipedia) (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himeji_Castle)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on Himeji Castle, the largest and best-preserved of Japan's surviving castles and a World Heritage site. It gives the complex's overall measurements - a circumference of 4,200 m and an area of 233 hectares (576 acres), 107 hectares inside the middle moat, roughly 950 to 1,600 m east to west and 900 to 1,700 m north to south - along with moat dimensions averaging 20 m wide, 34.5 m at the widest and about 2.7 m deep.

Why it applies, and its limits: These are the numbers that fix the grand end of our castle range, and the moat widths in particular set how broad a defensive water line should read on a map. It is a tertiary encyclopedia summary and we take its figures only where its own references support them; Himeji is exceptional rather than typical, so it is an upper bound and never a median, and its measurements are of the modern site as preserved and restored, including ground whose Edo-period use is no longer visible.

hikone-castle-town

Nakasendo Way, The Story of Hikone Castle Town (https://www.nakasendoway.com/the-story-of-hikone-castle-town/)

What it is: "The Story of Hikone Castle Town", a background article on Nakasendo Way, the route guide published by the guided-walking company Walk Japan; no author or date is given. It narrates Hikone from the Ii clan's founding of the town in 1603 through the Meiji dissolution of the samurai class, covering how rank and defense shaped the street plan and the separation of samurai from merchant quarters, and it cites a census of 1695 counting 15,371 townspeople living in 53 wards, noting that samurai and ashigaru were normally exempt from such counts.

Why it applies, and its limits: That census line is our population anchor for a domain capital - it shows what a substantial domain's town actually held in commoners, and, with the exemption noted, why the samurai population has to be estimated separately rather than read off. It is a tour company's history page with no named author and no references, so we use its census figure as a reported number rather than a verified one, and it describes a single town in a single domain, which we treat as one point on a range and not as a standard.

edo-machi-kido

Edopedia (edoflourishing), machi - towns and villages; Edo nagaya/roji coverage (https://edoflourishing.blogspot.com/2013/10/machi-towns-and-villages.html)

What it is: A page from "Edo - the EDOPEDIA", an encyclopedia blog on the historical city compiled by Gabi Greve as a project of the Daruma Museum, posted in 2013 and titled "machi - towns and villages". Its account of how the city was divided into blocks is drawn from the historian Oishi Manabu's book on Edo place names, and it records that each block was closed by a wooden gate, the kido, open from about four in the morning until about ten at night.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for the gate that shuts a block at night and its hours, which is what tells us that these gates belong on a town map at all and roughly where. It says nothing about who held the keys or about the smaller gates on the tenement lanes; those rest on other sources. Its limits are that it is an enthusiast's compilation rather than a peer-reviewed or closely referenced work, whose figures are another book's, so we use it for the shape of the practice rather than as an authority on any figure; that the hours are given flat, with no date inside a period of two and a half centuries; and that it describes Edo, by far the largest city in Japan, which we apply to smaller towns with care.

qing-zhalan

Qing Beijing street-fence coverage (Dashilan street history; Beijing city fortifications; imperial-curfew reporting) (https://www.atlantis-press.com/proceedings/ssha-23/125988752)

What it is: "Dashilan Street: The Fence Culture of Ancient Beijing", a conference paper by Fei Liang, Shaoshuai Liu and Xiaoxi Liu in the proceedings of the 2023 International Conference on Social Sciences and Humanities and Arts, published by Atlantis Press. Drawing on court paintings, old maps and archival photographs, it traces the ornamental street gates and palings (zhalan, 栅栏) that gave the Dashilan commercial district its name, and their role in the curfew and block policing of Ming and Qing Beijing. Reporting on the curfew hours and penalties themselves is gathered under the same key.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is directly about the feature we draw - a fence closing a street at night - and it supplies the reason such a fence existed, which is the curfew that a city's night watch enforced. Its limits are that it is a short conference paper rather than a full study, and that Beijing is an imperial capital and so the strictest case of urban policing in the empire: a provincial town would have had a looser version of the same arrangement, and the specific curfew hours and lash counts come from separate reporting rather than from this paper.

jokamachi-zoning

Jokamachi surveys (Nakasendo Way, Castle Towns) (https://www.nakasendoway.com/castle-towns-2/)

What it is: The "Castle Towns" essay in the Nakasendo Way series that Walk Japan, a company running guided walks along the old Nakasendo road, publishes alongside its tours. It explains how a castle town separated samurai from townspeople, and notes that the townspeople's wards typically formed narrow strips between different bodies of samurai, laid along the main thoroughfares.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for the shape of the castle town's zoning - merchant wards forming strips along the main roads that separated one group of samurai from another, and the precise spatial arrangement that kept samurai and townspeople apart - which is what the ring-and-strip layout of our capitals is drawn from. Its limits are that it is a tour operator's essay for travelers, unsigned and without references, usable for the shape of the arrangement and for nothing numeric; that the same zoning is better anchored in the Japanese encyclopedia article; and that it says nothing about the walls and gates of individual compounds or about the size of merchant plots, so those rest on other sources.

edo-josui

Tokyo Waterworks Historical Museum; Tokyo Metropolitan Waterworks, Tamagawa Josui pages; Tamagawa Aqueduct (Wikipedia); IHCSA Cafe, Tamagawa Josui: Edo's Precious Waterworks (https://ihcsacafe-en.ihcsa.or.jp/news/josui/)

What it is: A group of public pages on the Tamagawa Josui, the aqueduct that carried drinking water into Edo from 1653: the Tokyo Waterworks Historical Museum and the Tokyo Metropolitan Waterworks pages on the channel, the Wikipedia article, and an unsigned 2022 article, "Tamagawa Josui: Edo's Precious Waterworks", on IHCSA Cafe, the English culture site of the International Hospitality and Conference Service Association. The IHCSA page is the one we link and the one our English quotations come from; the Japanese passage on the channel's length and fall, the unlined excavation, the buried pipe length and the count of draw-wells come from the other three, which the entry names but does not link. Between them they describe roughly forty-three kilometers of open cut from the Hamura intake to the Yotsuya gate, falling only about twenty-one centimeters in every hundred meters, and then buried stone and wooden pipe inside the city feeding thousands of draw-wells.

Why it applies, and its limits: This is the best-documented premodern water supply in Japan and it answers the questions a city map asks - where an aqueduct is an open channel and where it goes underground, what gradient it needs, what the pipes were made of, who built them, and how the water reached an ordinary tenement's well; the era and the place are exactly ours, so nothing here is a stand-in. Its limits are that these are museum, utility and cultural-outreach pages written for a general reader rather than engineering history, mostly unsigned, so the lengths and gradients are round public figures; that one key bundles four works behind one link, so a reader who follows it cannot read everything the key is credited with; and that Edo was the largest city in the country, so its waterworks is an upper bound that a provincial town would never match.

kanda-kakehi

Tokyo Metropolitan Library, Ochanomizu Aqueduct, Kanda Service Water Supply Flume; Nippon.com on Suidobashi; Tokyo Waterworks Historical Museum (Kanda Aqueduct stone pipe) (https://www.library.metro.tokyo.lg.jp/portals/0/edo/tokyo_library/modal/index.html?d=5655)

What it is: A digitized Edo-period view with the Tokyo Metropolitan Library's own commentary, showing the Kanda Josui - Edo's water supply - crossing above the Kanda River in a kakehi (懸樋), an open wooden flume carried on a bridge at Ochanomizu, read alongside a Nippon.com piece on Suidobashi and the Tokyo Waterworks Historical Museum's material on the aqueduct's stone pipes.

Why it applies, and its limits: For the question we ask of it - whether a city's water main crossed a river in the open, visibly, where a map would have to draw it - this is about as direct as evidence gets: a contemporary picture, a surviving place name meaning "aqueduct bridge", and a public library's description of both. The limits are scale and singularity: this is the shogunal capital, the largest city in the country, and one celebrated crossing there does not establish that a provincial city of ours would have built the same thing, only that the form existed and was ordinary enough to be a famous sight.

osaka-kurayashiki

Dojima Rice Exchange (JPX/ODEX); Japanese Wiki Corpus, Daimyogashi; rice-broker surveys (https://www.jpx.co.jp/dojima/en/index.html)

What it is: The English page on the Dojima Rice Exchange published by the exchange itself (the Osaka Dojima Exchange, through Japan Exchange Group), which recounts how the domains sold their tax rice through kurayashiki, the combined warehouses and residences clustered around Nakanoshima in Osaka, and how the rice bills issued to successful bidders were then traded; the entry also gathers a Japanese Wiki Corpus article on daimyogashi (lending to domain lords against their rice) and surveys of the rice brokers.

Why it applies, and its limits: It tells us that the daimyo's warehouse-residences stood at the market, in the commercial capital where the rice was sold, rather than at the domain seat - which is why our capitals do not carry rows of them. Its limits are that it is an institution's history of itself, not scholarship; that it describes one exceptional city at the peak of one trade; that the key bundles two other works that are neither linked nor quoted, so nothing readable rests on them; and that the figure of more than a hundred such houses at the early-1800s peak is not in the passage we quote, so it stands as a guess until a page carries it.

asakusa-kuramae

Asakusa / Kuramae district histories; Rice broker (Wikipedia) (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice_broker)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article "Rice broker", on the fudasashi of Edo - the licensed brokers of Kuramae in Asakusa, "before the storehouses", who warehoused the stipend rice of the shogunate's retainers, converted it to coin and lent against it, and whose wealth fed the neighboring entertainment district.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the institution our capitals draw beside the government granaries - brokers who turned tax rice into money, clustered before the storehouses - which is what puts a brokers' street next to the granary on our maps. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary carrying the encyclopedia's own notice that it lacks inline citations, so nothing in it can be checked against a reference; that it does not say where the shogunate's storehouses stood - the riverside site on the Sumida our maps assume is not on this page and rests on other sources; and that it describes the capital's brokers, whose scale no provincial city approaches.

fukui-bushi-jutaku

Fukui Prefectural Archives exhibit, the housing of Fukui-domain samurai (Matsudaira Bunko) (https://www.library-archives.pref.fukui.lg.jp/bunsho/category/tenji/32442.html)

What it is: An online exhibit from the Fukui Prefectural Archives on where the samurai of the Fukui domain lived, built out of the domain's own surviving paperwork and house plans in the Matsudaira family collection. It walks through residence plots by the rank and stipend of the household that held them, and reproduces a dated 1839 plan of the Suganuma house near the Yanagi gate with its measurements written out.

Why it applies, and its limits: A public archive showing the actual Edo-period plans is about as direct as evidence of samurai housing gets, which is why we lean on it for the ladder of plot sizes by stipend and for the detail that the buildings presenting their walls to the street were the servants' long-house quarters. The limits are that this is one castle town, and a large one - Fukui's ladder is being asked to stand for castle-town samurai housing generally - and that an exhibit shows a curated handful of plans rather than a full survey, so the size bands are drawn from a few well-preserved examples rather than from a count of every plot in the town.

shibata-ashigaru-nagaya

Shibata ashigaru-nagaya (1842, Important Cultural Property), Hoppou Bunka museum documentation (https://online.bunka.go.jp/heritages/detail/144338)

What it is: The Japanese Agency for Cultural Affairs' heritage listing for the former Shibata domain's ashigaru nagaya in Niigata, a thatched, hipped-roof terrace of 1842 housing eight foot-soldier families under one roof, 43.6 m along the ridge by 7.3 m deep, designated an Important Cultural Property in 1969; the listing describes one household's share as an entrance, two six-mat rooms, a three-mat room and a kitchen.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a surviving, officially documented building of exactly the type we draw for low-retainer housing, so it fixes the default terraced form and supplies real numbers for it: eight households in one range, about 18 ft of frontage each, roughly nine tsubo of floor apiece. Its limits are that it is one late-Edo building from one domain and that practice varied, since Kaga and Hikone housed their foot soldiers in detached houses with gardens, so the terrace is our default rather than a universal rule, and the listing is a modern conservation record of the building as it survives today.

shaoxing-towpath

Shaoxing ancient towpath coverage (CGTN; Shanghai Daily); Grand Canal surveys (https://news.cgtn.com/news/3d3d674d7755444d33457a6333566d54/index.html)

What it is: A 2019 travel feature on CGTN, the Chinese state broadcaster's English site, credited to Shanghai Daily and unsigned, on the qiandao towpath at Shaoxing on the Eastern Zhejiang Canal - the stone path along which boats were hauled, in its two forms, one laid on the bank and one carried on piers about half a meter above the water - as it survives and is presented to visitors today. The page dates the canal itself to the fifth century BC and gives the best-preserved stretch as about eight kilometers.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the form of the towpath - a real riverside way, and the slab-on-piers kind standing just above the water - which is what a towpath on our canal maps is drawn from. Its limits are that it is a broadcaster's travel feature rather than a survey or an archaeological report; that it describes a preserved heritage structure as it stands now, whose fabric is later than the institution; that the date, the length and the Marco Polo detail are not on the page and rest on nothing readable, so they stand as guesses; and that a Chinese canal stands in for a setting whose surface is often Japanese.

beijing-imperial-city

Imperial City, Beijing (Wikipedia) (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_City,_Beijing)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article "Imperial City, Beijing", on the walled precinct that enclosed the Forbidden City and the imperial services around it. The passage we take describes the Corridor of a Thousand Steps south of Chengtianmen, with the offices of the government ministries lining either side of it.

Why it applies, and its limits: It answers a layout question for a capital map: the bureaucracy sits on the ceremonial avenue outside the palace wall rather than inside it, which decides where the ministry compounds are drawn. Two limits are worth stating plainly - it is a tertiary summary, and that particular sentence is flagged in the article as lacking a citation, so we treat the arrangement as a plausible shape to draw rather than as a documented fact; and Ming and Qing Beijing is a Chinese imperial capital standing in for our setting's own, which is modeled on both China and Japan.

nagoya-castle

Nagoya Castle, the English Wikipedia article (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagoya_Castle)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on Nagoya Castle, the great Tokugawa castle of Owari domain, describing its enclosures and buildings - the keep in the inner bailey, the Ninomaru palace that served as both the lord's residence and the domain's supreme government office, and the third bailey holding the auxiliary mansions of the lords and the houses of senior retainers.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the arrangement our castle plans follow: the palace at the core as both residence and the working seat of domain government, the keep a separate defensive tower, and the third bailey given over to the mansions of the senior retainers. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary, and that Nagoya was one of the largest castles in the country, so it stands for the arrangement of a castle's parts and never for their size at a provincial seat.

matsumoto-goten

Matsumoto Castle official site, Honmaru/Ninomaru Goten pages (https://www.matsumoto-castle.jp/eng/about/palace)

What it is: The pages of the official Matsumoto Castle site describing the Honmaru and Ninomaru palaces that stood inside the castle's enclosures.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives one Edo-period case of a castle's inner enclosure proving too small for its government: after the 1727 fire the functions moved to the second bailey, and because that was smaller the county office, the town office and the hall where the lord met the village headmen were moved out into the town - the warrant for putting some of a castle town's official buildings outside the walls on our maps. Its limits are that it is the castle's own visitor page, unsigned, undated and without citations; that it is a single case; and that its dates want care, since the page carries at least one internal error in them.

hanko-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 藩校 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/藩校)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 藩校 (hanko), on the domain schools of the Edo period. It describes their founding, their curriculum in the Chinese classics and their compulsory enrollment of retainers' sons, gives the count of 255 schools from a published reference work, and lists the schools domain by domain - a list in which some entries name a drill hall (演武場) among the school's buildings.

Why it applies, and its limits: It tells us that a domain seat of consequence had a school, which is what puts one on a capital's map. Its limits are that it is a tertiary encyclopedia summary; that the count of 255 is a late-Edo peak, so near-universal coverage is an end state rather than a constant; and, the one that bears on the drawing, that the article never says a drill hall was a standard part of a school - the drill halls are inferred by us from the names of a few particular schools in its list, so a drill hall drawn on every school compound is our generalization and not the source's.

nanjing-wall-enwiki

en.wikipedia City Wall of Nanjing (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_Wall_of_Nanjing)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on the City Wall of Nanjing, the Ming circuit built around the first Ming capital, describing how it was laid out in a winding, free form that followed the city's broken topography, used the river as its natural moat, and so departed from the square or rectangular walls of Beijing and Xi'an.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives one clear case of a capital wall that follows the ground rather than the square - bending around lakes, hills and the river that serves as its moat - which is the form that lets our maps bend a wall where the terrain demands. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary whose history and construction sections carry the encyclopedia's own requests for citations, a sharper discount than the usual one; that it is an imperial capital, so no dimension transfers to a provincial seat; and that it gives no total length for the circuit, so the 35 km figure often quoted for this wall is on no page we could read and our text says so.

shanghai-xiancheng-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 上海县城 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E4%B8%8A%E6%B5%B7%E8%80%81%E5%9F%8E%E5%8E%A2)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on 上海县城, the old walled county town of Shanghai, describing among other things the wall first thrown up around the town in 1553 against pirates coming up the river, and its roughly circular rather than square outline.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for two things about a walled town: that a Chinese county seat could go unwalled for a long time and then be walled in a hurry under a specific threat, and that such a wall could take a round outline rather than the textbook square. It is a tertiary summary taken where its references support it, about one town in the Yangtze delta, and while the record likes the idea of a wall raised very fast, the article does not put a season on it, so the speed we imply is ours rather than the source's.

hancho-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 藩庁 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E8%97%A9%E5%BA%81)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on 藩庁 (hancho), the administrative seat of an Edo-period domain. It explains that domains ranked as castle-holders and above kept their seat in the castle itself, while domains of the lower ranks - castle-holder-in-name and castle-less lords, who were not permitted to maintain a castle - built a mansion somewhere in their territory and made that the seat of government instead.

Why it applies, and its limits: This settles a question a map has to answer before anything else: whether a domain's government sits inside a fortress or in a walled mansion in a town, and the answer turns out to follow from the lord's rank rather than from the size of the domain. It is a tertiary summary of a legal distinction, so it tells us which building held the government and nothing about what that building looked like - no plan, no size, no arrangement of offices, all of which we take from elsewhere.

honmaru-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 本丸 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%9C%AC%E4%B8%B8)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the 本丸 (honmaru), the innermost bailey of a Japanese castle. For the early modern period it describes what stood there: the 奥御殿 (oku-goten), the inner palace where the castle lord and his family lived, and the 表御殿 (omote-goten), the outer palace that was the seat of government, with the keep and turrets built alongside as required.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the division our castle plans are built on - the lord's residence and the domain's government office are two halves of one compound, one private and one where business is done - which is what a player is really asking about when they click on the inner bailey. It is a tertiary summary generalizing across many castles and two centuries, so it describes a common arrangement rather than any particular castle's plan, and it supplies no dimensions.

kakegawa-castle-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 掛川城 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%8E%9B%E5%B7%9D%E5%9F%8E)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on Kakegawa Castle (掛川城), a castle in what is now Shizuoka prefecture that headed a domain of roughly 51,000 to 59,000 koku. It describes the castle's history and the buildings of its enclosures, including the ninomaru goten, the palace in the second enclosure.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives one small-to-middling domain's castle where the everyday business of government was transacted in the second-bailey palace rather than the keep, which is what puts the working offices of our capitals in the bailey. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary; that the building named is the rebuild of 1861, a very late Edo datum standing for the whole period; and that two castles are a thin base for a rule - the other, Kawagoe, is a different article this key does not link.

karo-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 家老 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%AE%B6%E8%80%81)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the karo (家老), the senior retainers who formed the standing council of a daimyo's house and ran its administration.

Why it applies, and its limits: Our castle towns have to place the great retainer mansions and to decide where government was actually done, and this article supplies both in general terms: the karo decided matters in joint council at a hall such as the hyojosho, and their mansions stood near the castle. Its limits are precision and kind - it is a tertiary summary, "near the castle" is not a distance, and it names no ring or block of the town, so the concentric arrangement our maps draw is our reading of a general statement rather than something this article says.

jinya-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 陣屋 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/陣屋)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on 陣屋 (jin'ya), the walled administrative seat of a lord too small to hold a castle, or of a shogunal deputy. It sets out what such a seat contained: the lord's residence, the offices, official houses for retainers, earth-walled storehouses and a drill ground.

Why it applies, and its limits: The list of buildings is exactly what we need to lay out a small domain's seat on a map, and it is the compact statement of a program that longer works assume rather than list. It is a tertiary summary, so it gives the program and not any one seat's measured plan, and the passage we lean on describes a small domain's own seat rather than a shogunal deputy's office, which was a different institution with a different program.

nisshinkan-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 日新館 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%97%A5%E6%96%B0%E9%A4%A8)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the Nisshinkan (日新館), the domain school of Aizu, which stood immediately west of Aizu-Wakamatsu castle on a ground of about 120 ken by 60 ken and was among the foremost such schools in the country, with a recitation hall for boys up to fifteen, a swimming ground and an observatory. The article also covers the modern reconstruction, which serves as museum and as archery and martial-arts hall.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the dimensions of a domain school's ground - about 120 by 60 ken for the original Aizu school - which sets the size band a school occupies on our capital maps, and it shows archery and martial halls standing on such a ground. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary carrying the encyclopedia's own notice that it lacks sufficient sources, which matters because we take a dimension from it; that Aizu's was one of the foremost domain schools in the country, so it bounds the top of the range; that the halls it names belong to the 1987 reconstruction rather than the original; and that it states no rule pairing a lecture hall with a drill hall.

suzaku-oji-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 朱雀大路 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%9C%B1%E9%9B%80%E5%A4%A7%E8%B7%AF)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 朱雀大路 (Suzaku Ōji), on the great central avenue that ran from the palace gate to the southern gate of Heian-kyō and of the earlier Japanese capitals, with the corresponding avenue of Chang'an in Tang China for comparison.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the width of the ceremonial avenue of an imperial capital - 82 m for Heian-kyo, 147 m for Tang Chang'an - which is the top of the scale our widest streets are measured against.
