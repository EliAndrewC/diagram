Citations: Urban fabric: the research behind the frontage and row-packing rules

The passages behind the footnotes of Urban fabric: the research behind the frontage and row-packing rules - first the works they quote and why each one counts, then the notes themselves. Hover a footnote number on that page and the note appears beside it; the numbers here are the same ones.

The works cited on this page

kyototuu-unagi

京都通百科事典, 鰻の寝床 (https://www.kyototuu.jp/Life/ProverbUnaginoNedoko.html)

What it is: A page from 京都通百科事典, a Japanese web encyclopedia about Kyoto, on the saying 鰻の寝床 ("an eel's bed") for the long narrow townhouse, giving typical dimensions of about two ken of frontage (roughly 3.6 m) and ten to twelve ken of depth (roughly 18 to 22 m).

Why it applies, and its limits: Those two numbers are what we use it for; they give a town lot a believable width and depth on the map. It is a regional reference site rather than scholarship, and it also repeats the frontage-tax explanation of the shape, which the page itself hedges as something "it is said" and which the Japanese Wikipedia article on the machiya disputes, so we take its measurements and not its causal story.

nagaya-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 長屋 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%95%B7%E5%B1%8B)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the nagaya (長屋), the row tenement of Japanese towns - one long building divided across its width by party walls into separate rented dwellings. It covers the form in general and the Edo-period back-alley version in particular: the small unit of nine shaku by two ken, the shared privy in the alley, the shared well, and the townsmen and craftsmen who rented there while the better merchants kept shops on the main street.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the plain definition of the building type we draw along a town's back lanes, and of who lived in one, which is exactly what we take from it. It is a tertiary summary rather than a study, so we use its figures only where its own references support them, and what it describes best is Edo, the largest city in the country, so a provincial town's tenements on our maps are that pattern scaled down.

kyomachiya-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 京町家 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E4%BA%AC%E7%94%BA%E5%AE%B6)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on 京町家, the Kyoto townhouse: the narrow-fronted, deep-lot merchant house, its plan and its history. It also takes up the popular story that the shape was a dodge against a tax on frontage width, and rebuts it, explaining that Edo-period Kyoto assessed a total on the block community, which the house-owning townsmen then divided per house.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for the shape of an urban lot in a dense premodern city and for the correction to the frontage-tax story that a more popular source repeats. It is a tertiary summary, and it describes one city's building tradition, so our towns take the proportions of the form rather than Kyoto's specific detail.

hiyokechi-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 火除地 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%81%AB%E9%99%A4%E5%9C%B0)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on 火除地 (hiyokechi), the cleared fire-prevention lots the Tokugawa government laid out in Edo after the Great Meireki Fire of 1657. It records that five burned-over sites were first given over to the purpose, that more were added over time - chiefly northwest of the castle, reaching some thirteen by the Kyoho era - and that in the broad sense the term also covers the widened streets (hirokoji) made for the same reason.

Why it applies, and its limits: It settles the scale at which a firebreak was actually cut: whole blocks and broad streets, opened after a disaster, rather than gaps left between individual houses - which is how our towns show fire precaution. It is a tertiary summary and it is entirely about Edo, a city of a size no settlement we draw approaches, so a smaller town takes the form and the reason from it and not the count.

chang-morphology-walled-capitals

Sen-dou Chang, "The Morphology of Walled Capitals", in Skinner (ed.), The City in Late Imperial China (http://web.stanford.edu/~mel1000/sen.pdf)

What it is: Sen-dou Chang's scholarly chapter "The Morphology of Walled Capitals", in the geographer G. William Skinner's edited collection The City in Late Imperial China (Stanford University Press, 1977). It is a comparative study of the physical form of Chinese walled administrative cities at every rank, county capitals included: the models their street nets follow between the gates, the drum tower at the crossing, and the cultivation, ponds and lakes that occupied surprisingly large parts of the ground inside the walls. The copy available online is a page-image scan.

Why it applies, and its limits: This is the strongest source behind the way our county seats are laid out, a thin skeleton of trunk streets with lanes elsewhere, walls sized to a city's administrative rank rather than its population, and a real share of the enclosed ground left unbuilt as siege insurance; Chang treats county capitals by name, so for the county seat it is a direct source and not a stand-in. Its limits are stated in our own text: the only proportion Chang gives is the lake at Jinan taking about a fifth of the walled area, so the street share and the share of unbuilt ground our maps use are this record's synthesis rather than his measurement; the public copy has no text layer, so every passage quoted from it is our own reading of the scan; and for the hamlet and village tiers his cities are a larger and grander class of place than what we draw.
