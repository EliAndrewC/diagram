Citations: Urban fabric: the research behind the frontage and row-packing rules

The passages behind the footnotes of Urban fabric: the research behind the frontage and row-packing rules - first the works they quote and why each one counts, then the notes themselves. Hover a footnote number on that page and the note appears beside it; the numbers here are the same ones.

The works cited on this page

kyototuu-unagi

京都通百科事典, 鰻の寝床 (https://www.kyototuu.jp/Life/ProverbUnaginoNedoko.html)

What it is: A page from 京都通百科事典, a Japanese web encyclopedia about Kyoto, on the saying 鰻の寝床 ("an eel's bed") for the long narrow townhouse, giving typical dimensions of about two ken of frontage (roughly 3.6 m) and ten to twelve ken of depth (roughly 18 to 22 m).

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the proportions of the eel's-nest townhouse lot - about two ken of frontage to ten or twelve of depth - which is what our merchant plots are drawn to, and the frontage-tax story as a foil. Its limits are that it is a popular regional web encyclopedia written for tourism and a local-knowledge examination, with no citations of any kind; that it is one city's building tradition; and that both the tax story and the dimensions themselves are given as hearsay - "it is said" - with no source behind either.

nagaya-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 長屋 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%95%B7%E5%B1%8B)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the nagaya (長屋), the row tenement of Japanese towns - one long building divided across its width by party walls into separate rented dwellings. It covers the form in general and the Edo-period back-alley version in particular: the small unit of nine shaku by two ken, the shared privy in the alley, the shared well, and the townsmen and craftsmen who rented there while the better merchants kept shops on the main street.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the plain definition of the building type we draw along a town's back lanes, and of who lived in one, which is exactly what we take from it. It is a tertiary summary rather than a study, so we use its figures only where its own references support them, and what it describes best is Edo, the largest city in the country, so a provincial town's tenements on our maps are that pattern scaled down.

kyomachiya-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 京町家 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E4%BA%AC%E7%94%BA%E5%AE%B6)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on 京町家, the Kyoto townhouse: the narrow-fronted, deep-lot merchant house, its plan and its history. It also takes up the popular story that the shape was a dodge against a tax on frontage width, and rebuts it, explaining that Edo-period Kyoto assessed a total on the block community, which the house-owning townsmen then divided per house.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for the shape of an urban lot in a dense premodern city and for the correction to the frontage-tax story that a more popular source repeats. It is a tertiary summary, and it describes one city's building tradition, so our towns take the proportions of the form rather than Kyoto's specific detail.

hiyokechi-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 火除地 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%81%AB%E9%99%A4%E5%9C%B0)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on 火除地 (hiyokechi), the cleared fire-prevention lots the Tokugawa government laid out in Edo after the Great Meireki Fire of 1657. It records that five burned-over sites were first given over to the purpose, that more were added over time - chiefly northwest of the castle, reaching some thirteen by the Kyoho era - and that in the broad sense the term also covers the widened streets (hirokoji) made for the same reason.

Why it applies, and its limits: It settles the scale at which a firebreak was actually cut: whole blocks and broad streets, opened after a disaster, rather than gaps left between individual houses - which is how our towns show fire precaution. It is a tertiary summary and it is entirely about Edo, a city of a size no settlement we draw approaches, so a smaller town takes the form and the reason from it and not the count.

chang-morphology-walled-capitals

Sen-dou Chang, "The Morphology of Walled Capitals", in Skinner (ed.), The City in Late Imperial China (http://web.stanford.edu/~mel1000/sen.pdf)

What it is: Sen-dou Chang's scholarly chapter "The Morphology of Walled Capitals", in the geographer G. William Skinner's edited collection The City in Late Imperial China (Stanford University Press, 1977). It is a comparative study of the physical form of Chinese walled administrative cities at every rank, county capitals included: the models their street nets follow between the gates, the drum tower at the crossing, and the cultivation, ponds and lakes that occupied surprisingly large parts of the ground inside the walls. The copy available online is a page-image scan.

Why it applies, and its limits: This is the strongest source behind the way our county seats are laid out, a thin skeleton of trunk streets with lanes elsewhere, walls sized to a city's administrative rank rather than its population, and a real share of the enclosed ground left unbuilt as siege insurance; Chang treats county capitals by name, so for the county seat it is a direct source and not a stand-in. Its limits are stated in our own text: the only proportion Chang gives is the lake at Jinan taking about a fifth of the walled area, so the street share and the share of unbuilt ground our maps use are this record's synthesis rather than his measurement; the public copy has no text layer, so every passage quoted from it is our own reading of the scan; and for the hamlet and village tiers his cities are a larger and grander class of place than what we draw.

fires-in-edo-enwiki

en.wikipedia Fires in Edo (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fires_in_Edo)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article "Fires in Edo", an encyclopedia summary, well referenced to Japanese scholarship, of the repeated great fires of the Tokugawa capital - how often they came, what set them off, when in the year they fell, how the city fought them, and the thick-walled plastered storehouses (dozo) that survived them.

Why it applies, and its limits: We take from it the seasonal pattern - that the serious fires clustered in the dry months under the north and northwest winds, from January to April with March the worst - which is a matter of climate and so applies to a town as much as to the capital; and the construction of the storehouse, an earthen-walled, tile-roofed building a map draws differently from a timber house. It is a tertiary summary, used only where its own references support the point, and it is about the largest city in Japan: the season and the storehouse transfer to a smaller settlement, the scale and frequency of its conflagrations do not.

hinomi-yagura-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 火の見櫓 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%81%AB%E3%81%AE%E8%A6%8B%E6%AB%93)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the 火の見櫓 (hinomi-yagura), the fire-watch tower of Japanese towns and villages: a raised lookout platform used to spot a fire early, summon the fire brigade and sound the alarm to the neighborhood, generally carrying a hansho bell at its top. It covers how the towers developed alongside the firefighting system from Edo-period Edo, how far across the country they had spread by the early Showa era, and the town-by-town code by which the bell was rung.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the plain definition of the structure our city maps draw and of what the watch on it was for, and it is the only source in the record that dates the institution and its spread, which is what settles whether a small unwalled seat would carry one. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary rather than a study; that it describes Japan alone; and that much of the article is about the surviving steel towers and the modern volunteer companies, so the premodern form has to be taken from its historical passages and not from what it shows standing today.

hansho-kotobank

kotobank 半鐘 (https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%8D%8A%E9%90%98-118280)

What it is: Kotobank's entry for 半鐘 (hansho), aggregating standard Japanese reference dictionaries: the small bronze hanging bell hung on a fire-watch tower, and the code by which the number of strokes told a hearer what kind of incident had happened - for a fire, a continuous peal for a near one, fewer strokes the further off it was.

Why it applies, and its limits: It supplies the one thing our fire-tower explanation needs beyond the tower itself: that the bell carried information by its cadence rather than simply making a noise, which is why a watchman on a tower is worth drawing. Its limits are that it is a short tertiary dictionary entry with no citations, that it runs the Edo-period and later usages together without dating them, and that it describes Japan only.

takayama-jinya-city

Takayama City, 高山陣屋跡 (https://www.city.takayama.lg.jp/kurashi/1000021/1000119/1000847/1000954/1000956.html)

What it is: The city of Takayama's official page for 高山陣屋跡 (the site of the Takayama Jin'ya), the one surviving Tokugawa provincial governor's office and compound in Japan, published by the municipality that manages the designated historic site. It lists the buildings and how they are built, notes which structures were moved in from the castle and when, and gives the extent of the protected ground.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the strongest source in the record for what a provincial office contained: an intact compound described by the body that keeps it - the examining court with its white-gravel yard, the storehouses moved from the castle, the office range of 1816 - and its designated area. Its limits are that this is the only survivor of more than fifty such offices, in a mountain province under direct shogunal rule; that parts of what is described are a 1996 reconstruction from an 1830 drawing; and that the area figure, 11,219 square meters, is an exact sum of ten surveyed parcels under a modern legal boundary accumulated across four designations between 1929 and 1989, not a measured Edo footprint - so the compound area our maps derive from it, the total less the front plaza, carries the hedge rather than the source.

dongjing-menghualu-wikisource

孟元老, 東京夢華錄 卷三 (Chinese Wikisource) (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%9D%B1%E4%BA%AC%E5%A4%A2%E8%8F%AF%E9%8C%84/%E5%8D%B7%E4%B8%89)

What it is: The third chapter of 東京夢華錄 (Dreams of Splendor of the Eastern Capital), Meng Yuanlao's recollection of daily life in Kaifeng, the Northern Song capital, written in the twelfth century after the city had fallen, given here in the original literary Chinese on Chinese Wikisource. The passage we use describes the military patrol posts set through the wards and lanes and the brick-built 望火樓 fire-watch tower, with its lookouts above and its quartered soldiers and firefighting gear below.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a primary account, by someone who lived in the city, of exactly the institution our city maps draw - a permanent fire-watch tower with a manned station under it - in a dense premodern East Asian capital, which is the strongest kind of evidence the record can have for it. Its limits are that it describes one imperial capital at the very top of the urban hierarchy and not a provincial seat; that it is a nostalgic memoir composed from memory after the city was lost, so its figures are recollection rather than record; and that it says nothing about smaller towns, so it cannot be read either way on what an ordinary market town kept.

machibikeshi-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 町火消 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%94%BA%E7%81%AB%E6%B6%88)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the 町火消 (machi-bikeshi), the townsman fire companies of Edo: the ordinance establishing them, issued in 1718 by the South Town Magistrate Ooka Tadasuke on the advice of the block headmen and of Ogyu Sorai; the 1720 revision into forty-seven iroha companies west of the Sumida and sixteen more for Honjo and Fukagawa, one company to about twenty blocks; and the arrangement by which the companies stood under the town magistrate's command while each block bore their cost.

Why it applies, and its limits: It tells us who actually manned, paid for and commanded an urban fire watch, which is the point our setting departs from deliberately, and it shows that the departure is narrower than it looks, since command sat with the magistrate historically as well. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary; that it is about Edo alone, the largest city in the country; and that it describes an institution of the long Tokugawa peace, so its scale and its politics do not transfer to a provincial city of the earlier, more martial period these maps are pitched in.
