Citations: The government quarter: the research behind the ministries and martial training

The passages behind the footnotes of The government quarter: the research behind the ministries and martial training - first the works they quote and why each one counts, then the notes themselves. Hover a footnote number on that page and the note appears beside it; the numbers here are the same ones.

The works cited on this page

hanko-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 藩校 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/藩校)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 藩校 (hanko), on the domain schools of the Edo period. It describes their founding, their curriculum in the Chinese classics and their compulsory enrollment of retainers' sons, gives the count of 255 schools from a published reference work, and lists the schools domain by domain - a list in which some entries name a drill hall (演武場) among the school's buildings.

Why it applies, and its limits: It tells us that a domain seat of consequence had a school, which is what puts one on a capital's map. Its limits are that it is a tertiary encyclopedia summary; that the count of 255 is a late-Edo peak, so near-universal coverage is an end state rather than a constant; and, the one that bears on the drawing, that the article never says a drill hall was a standard part of a school - the drill halls are inferred by us from the names of a few particular schools in its list, so a drill hall drawn on every school compound is our generalization and not the source's.

edo-three-dojos-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 幕末江戸三大道場 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/幕末江戸三大道場)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 幕末江戸三大道場 (the three great dojos of bakumatsu Edo), on the best-known swordsmanship halls of the city in the last decades of the Tokugawa period, with the year each was founded and the district it was founded in: Nihonbashi Minami-Kayabacho, Nihonbashi Shinagawacho and the foot of Kudanzaka. The page also records that two of the three later moved - to Minami-Hatchobori and to Kanda Otamagaike - and that the grouping itself is a post-Meiji convention that spread from a late-Edo rating verse.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for where a martial hall stood in a large city - on central commercial ground among the townspeople rather than out among the samurai residences - which is a placement rule a map has to get right. Its limits are that an encyclopedia is a tertiary summary, and this one rests on a single kendo-magazine feature from 1977; that a "three great" list is a later convention rather than any census of the city's halls, so it cannot tell us how many a town had; that the addresses are founding addresses and two of the halls moved, so a placement rule reads two different moments; and that these are all late Edo, the very end of the era our setting draws on.

genbukan-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 玄武館 (search syntheses split between ~3,000 from 清河八郎's roster and "over 6,000") (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%8E%84%E6%AD%A6%E9%A4%A8)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 玄武館 (Genbukan), on the swordsmanship hall founded in 1822 at Nihonbashi Shinagawacho in Edo by Chiba Shusaku, later moved to Kanda Otamagaike, which the article ranks among the three great dojos of late-Edo Edo. It records the school's founding, its moves and the Chiba family's service as instructors to the Mito domain.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for a metropolitan dojo with a date and an address, so that one can be put on a street, and for the point that a big city had large private martial schools; it is not used for a count of disciples. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary, and that this was one exceptional institution, so it is a ceiling and not a typical dojo. The page says nothing about fees or commercial operation - the Chiba family ran the hall while serving a domain as instructors - so whether such a hall was a paying business rests on nothing readable here.

buke-hokonin-wiki

Japanese Wikipedia, 武家奉公人 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%AD%A6%E5%AE%B6%E5%A5%89%E5%85%AC%E4%BA%BA)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on buke-hokonin (武家奉公人), the servants of a samurai household: the chugen, komono, genan and kobito who did the household's odd jobs and attended their master out of doors. It covers their live-in status, their annual contracts and how they were hired.

Why it applies, and its limits: Whether servants show up on a city map as their own little houses or as nothing at all turns on whether they lived in their master's compound or in their own, and this article answers that directly: they were live-in staff on term contracts, hired through labor brokers as a household needed them. That the brokers stood in the merchant quarter is our inference, not the page's statement. It is a tertiary summary about people and their conditions of employment, not about buildings, and it describes a specifically Edo-period arrangement, so the drawn form of the quarters they occupied comes from other sources.

matsue-bukeyashiki

Matsue Buke Yashiki (Shiomi Nawate), museum building documentation (https://www.matsue-bukeyashiki.jp/about/equipment.html)

What it is: The building documentation published by the museum that operates the Matsue Buke Yashiki, a preserved middle-rank samurai residence standing on the Shiomi Nawate street in the castle town of Matsue, describing the house room by room and the gate on its street front.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a surviving building described by the people who keep it, which is why we use it for the nagayamon standing on the street and doubling as the quarters of the gatekeeper and the household servants, and for a room program that includes the servants' service rooms - both things our residence plans have to place. It is one house in one castle town, presented for visitors rather than as a study, and a restored and interpreted one, so it tells us what a middle-rank residence in Matsue is like and not what a typical residence anywhere was.

fukui-bushi-jutaku

Fukui Prefectural Archives exhibit, the housing of Fukui-domain samurai (Matsudaira Bunko) (https://www.library-archives.pref.fukui.lg.jp/bunsho/category/tenji/32442.html)

What it is: An online exhibit from the Fukui Prefectural Archives on where the samurai of the Fukui domain lived, built out of the domain's own surviving paperwork and house plans in the Matsudaira family collection. It walks through residence plots by the rank and stipend of the household that held them, and reproduces a dated 1839 plan of the Suganuma house near the Yanagi gate with its measurements written out.

Why it applies, and its limits: A public archive showing the actual Edo-period plans is about as direct as evidence of samurai housing gets, which is why we lean on it for the ladder of plot sizes by stipend and for the detail that the buildings presenting their walls to the street were the servants' long-house quarters. The limits are that this is one castle town, and a large one - Fukui's ladder is being asked to stand for castle-town samurai housing generally - and that an exhibit shows a curated handful of plans rather than a full survey, so the size bands are drawn from a few well-preserved examples rather than from a count of every plot in the town.

jta-nagayamon

Japan Tourism Agency multilingual commentary database, Nagaya-mon (H30-00651) (https://www.mlit.go.jp/tagengo-db/en/H30-00651.html)

What it is: An entry in the Japan Tourism Agency's multilingual commentary database, written for foreign visitors by the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism, on the nagaya-mon - the long gatehouse range at the street front of a samurai residence - as restored at the Takada family house in Kanazawa, built in the early 1860s. It describes the range's servants' quarters, with a wood floor and a built-in hearth, and its stable with a rammed-earth floor, and records that in the Kaga domain a nagaya-mon was permitted only to samurai of middle rank and above.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the form of the gate range that fronts a samurai compound on our capital maps and the rank rule that governed who could build one, which is what makes the gate a marker of status a reader can read. Its limits are that it is visitor commentary with no sources cited rather than a survey; that it describes one domain's regulation and one surviving building from the very end of the period; and that it names only the servants' quarters and the stable as the range's contents - a gatekeeper's room, a storeroom and street-facing lookout windows are not on the page, so where our maps draw them they are a guess.

bukeyashiki-wiki

Japanese Wikipedia, 武家屋敷 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%AD%A6%E5%AE%B6%E5%B1%8B%E6%95%B7)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the buke-yashiki (武家屋敷), the samurai residence of an early modern castle town. It sets out the zoning of such a town: upper samurai in hedged and walled plots inside the middle moat near the castle, and the row houses and small plots of ashigaru and other low-ranking retainers arranged outside them, often massed at the town's entrances for defense.

Why it applies, and its limits: This is the source for the rule our samurai wards follow, that rank sets distance from the castle and that ranks of small uniform housing belong on the outer fringe rather than inside a great house's fence. It is a tertiary summary and gives no plot dimensions, setbacks or counts, so it decides the pattern while the measurements come from elsewhere, and it describes the Edo-period castle town specifically rather than samurai housing in every era.

jta-ashigaru-kaga

Japan Tourism Agency multilingual commentary database, Ashigaru Residences / Samurai Ranks in the Kaga Domain (H30-00660, H30-00647) (https://www.mlit.go.jp/tagengo-db/en/H30-00660.html)

What it is: Two entries from the Japan Tourism Agency's multilingual commentary database, the English texts written to explain historic sites to foreign visitors, covering the foot soldier districts and the ranking of samurai in the Kaga domain around Kanazawa. They give the rows of hedged plots the foot soldiers lived on, each about 165 or 230 square meters and divided between house and garden.

Why it applies, and its limits: Very few sources put a measured plot size on the humblest warrior housing, and these give us both a size and the way enclosure rose with rank from hedge to fence to wall, which is what sets the fringe of our castle towns. The commentary is interpretive writing for visitors rather than a survey, and it says plainly that Kaga's detached foot soldier houses were unusual for the period, most domains housing them in row tenements, so we treat these plots as one domain's practice that our maps may follow rather than a national norm.

hikone-ashigaru

Hikone ashigaru kumi-yashiki documentation (hikone-bunkaisan.net) (https://hikone-bunkaisan.net/ashigaru/)

What it is: A page from 彦根文化遺産 ("Hikone Cultural Heritage"), a heritage site published by the local Hikone Historical Charm Utilization Executive Committee, documenting the ashigaru (foot-soldier) quarters of the Hikone castle town. It covers the preserved street plan and plot sizes, the character of the houses, and the 組 (kumi) grouping by which the ashigaru were organized and along which they were settled - an organization whose descendants still run the neighborhood's fire-prevention and local-governance associations. No author is named and the page carries no date.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a local record of a surviving quarter that tells us the three things our layout needs: that the lowest-ranking retainers were housed on the outermost edge of the castle town as a defensive ring, the size of a single plot, and that men of one unit lived together along a numbered stretch of one street. It is a heritage-promotion page rather than a scholarly study, and it describes one domain - Hikone housed its ashigaru in detached houses with gardens where other domains used barrack rows, so the plot form is that domain's practice and not a national rule.

shibata-ashigaru-nagaya

Shibata ashigaru-nagaya (1842, Important Cultural Property), Hoppou Bunka museum documentation (https://online.bunka.go.jp/heritages/detail/144338)

What it is: The Japanese Agency for Cultural Affairs' heritage listing for the former Shibata domain's ashigaru nagaya in Niigata, a thatched, hipped-roof terrace of 1842 housing eight foot-soldier families under one roof, 43.6 m along the ridge by 7.3 m deep, designated an Important Cultural Property in 1969; the listing describes one household's share as an entrance, two six-mat rooms, a three-mat room and a kitchen.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a surviving, officially documented building of exactly the type we draw for low-retainer housing, so it fixes the default terraced form and supplies real numbers for it: eight households in one range, about 18 ft of frontage each, roughly nine tsubo of floor apiece. Its limits are that it is one late-Edo building from one domain and that practice varied, since Kaga and Hikone housed their foot soldiers in detached houses with gardens, so the terrace is our default rather than a universal rule, and the listing is a modern conservation record of the building as it survives today.

daozuofang

倒座房 / 北京四合院 (Chinese Wikipedia and vernacular-architecture summaries) (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%80%92%E5%BA%A7%E6%88%BF)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article 倒座房 (daozuofang, the "reversed" row), on the southernmost range of rooms in a Beijing courtyard house, whose doors and windows face north into the court so that its back is the blank wall along the street. The article sets out what those poorly lit rooms were used for: guest rooms, the servants' quarters, a family school at the east end and the privy at the west.

Why it applies, and its limits: It answers directly the question we ask of it - who lives in the row a walled compound turns toward the street, and why that face is blank - which governs how we draw the street frontage of a courtyard compound and where the servants go. Its limits are that it is a four-sentence encyclopedia stub whose only two references are popular web pages, so it describes the ideal type of the room and no survey or date stands behind it (the use it gives is uncontroversial and appears in the standard literature on the courtyard house, but that literature is not what this link reaches); and that it describes the Beijing siheyuan in particular, a northern Chinese townhouse form, which we apply to our compounds as a family resemblance rather than as a plan to copy.

jokamachi-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 城下町 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%9F%8E%E4%B8%8B%E7%94%BA)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on 城下町 (jokamachi, the castle town). It describes how such a town was zoned around its castle, with samurai quarters ranked by their distance from it, merchant and artisan quarters beyond them and a temple district on the outer edge, and how streets were deliberately bent into right-angle dog-legs and stopped with dead ends to lengthen an attacker's approach.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the source of the zoning and the street tricks that give our towns and cities their shape, and it states in one place what scattered town histories show one town at a time. It is a tertiary summary describing the castle town as a general type, so it gives us the pattern rather than any single town's plan, and Japanese castle towns were an Edo-period form, which is later than parts of the setting our maps also draw on.

jokamachi-zoning

Jokamachi surveys (Nakasendo Way, Castle Towns) (https://www.nakasendoway.com/castle-towns-2/)

What it is: The "Castle Towns" essay in the Nakasendo Way series that Walk Japan, a company running guided walks along the old Nakasendo road, publishes alongside its tours. It explains how a castle town separated samurai from townspeople, and notes that the townspeople's wards typically formed narrow strips between different bodies of samurai, laid along the main thoroughfares.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for the shape of the castle town's zoning - merchant wards forming strips along the main roads that separated one group of samurai from another, and the precise spatial arrangement that kept samurai and townspeople apart - which is what the ring-and-strip layout of our capitals is drawn from. Its limits are that it is a tour operator's essay for travelers, unsigned and without references, usable for the shape of the arrangement and for nothing numeric; that the same zoning is better anchored in the Japanese encyclopedia article; and that it says nothing about the walls and gates of individual compounds or about the size of merchant plots, so those rest on other sources.

neixiang-yamen

Neixiang county yamen (内乡县衙), Henan - site documentation and plan descriptions (https://cn.chinadaily.com.cn/a/202103/12/WS604b0287a3101e7ce9743b4c.html)

What it is: A feature article by the Henan Daily reporter Zhang Dongyun, republished on China Daily's Chinese-language site in March 2021, on the Neixiang county yamen in Henan - a county government compound first built in 1304 and rebuilt by a late Qing magistrate, now a museum. It describes the plan in detail: more than 47,000 square meters, eighteen courtyards in succession and over 280 rooms, laid out by Ming and Qing regulation with the offices in front and the residence behind, and it names what stands on each side of the first two courtyards.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the plan of a real county yamen in the words of a reporter walking it - the central axis, the two front courtyards, the Six Chambers, the total area and room count, and what stood east and west of the first court - which is what our magistracy plan is laid out from. Its limits are that it is a newspaper feature rather than a survey or excavation report; that the buildings are a late-Qing rebuild conserved and restored as a museum, so the plan is a preserved one rather than a documented original; that its west line holds the guest hall, the dining hall and the jail, not a clerks' lodging, and it names no sheds against the wall - the record uses Neixiang as the counter-example on both, and the runners' duty courtyard it does name stands on the east; and that it is imperial China rather than Japan.
