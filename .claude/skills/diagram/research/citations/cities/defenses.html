Citations: City defenses: the research behind the wall, gate and tower rules

The passages behind the footnotes of City defenses: the research behind the wall, gate and tower rules - first the works they quote and why each one counts, then the notes themselves. Hover a footnote number on that page and the note appears beside it; the numbers here are the same ones.

The works cited on this page

xian-wall-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 西安城墙 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E8%A5%BF%E5%AE%89%E5%9F%8E%E5%A2%99)

What it is: The same Chinese Wikipedia article 西安城墙, here for its account of the rampart itself - 12 m high, 15 to 18 m thick at the base and 12 to 14 m across the top, with 98 projecting towers standing 12 m out from the outer face and rising level with the wall.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the anchor numbers for how a great wall and its towers are drawn - the circuit's length and the area it encloses, the rampart's height and its thickness at base and top, the enemy platforms' projection, width and spacing, set at the range of bows and firearms. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary; that this is among the largest walls ever built, a ceiling rather than a norm; and that the numbers describe the wall as it stands after the brick facing of 1568, the works of 1781 and modern conservation, not a wall measured when it was raised in 1374.

wengcheng-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 瓮城 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%93%AE%E5%9F%8E)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article 瓮城 (wengcheng, "barbican"), a tertiary encyclopedia entry on the walled outwork built in front of a Chinese city gate, the half-round and square shapes it takes, and named surviving examples such as the Zhonghua Gate at Nanjing, whose barbicans sit inside the gate rather than before it.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it only for what a barbican is and which forms it takes at a gate, which is the kind of settled definition an encyclopedia carries dependably. It is a tertiary summary written by volunteers, so we take its figures only where its own references support them, and it describes Chinese city gates - the setting's towns take the form from the imperial-Chinese side of their ancestry rather than from Japan.

chaoguan-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 鈔關 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/鈔關)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on the chaoguan (鈔關), the Ming and Qing tax barriers that levied transit duty on goods, carts and boats. It records where they were placed, at points where goods gathered and at strategic junctions of the water and land routes, and that by the Wanli era, late in the Ming, only eight major stations remained, seven of them strung along the Grand Canal.

Why it applies, and its limits: We cite it for a negative that matters to the drawing: Chinese customs practice put the barrier on the trade route, not at an ordinary town's gates, so our gate-throat inspection post cannot claim Chinese precedent and rests instead on the Japanese sekisho. That is an argument from the article's silence - a page about barriers on canals says nothing about county-seat gates, and a reader who follows the link will find no sentence ruling them out - so it is labeled as our reading. It is a tertiary summary of a fiscal institution rather than a study of gate architecture, and it is used to rule a layout out rather than to justify one.

greatmingmilitary-fortification-gate

Great Ming Military, "Chinese fortification: an overview of parts and terminology - Part 2: Gate and moat" (https://greatmingmilitary.blogspot.com/2019/06/chinese-fortification-p2.html)

What it is: The second part of a specialist blog series on Chinese fortification, on the gate and the moat: the gate tunnel, the barbican and its neck, the wengcheng, and what each of them was for tactically, illustrated from Ming military manuals.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the only readable text found that draws the comparison our gate rule rests on - the European barbican's narrow neck preventing a sortie against the wengcheng as a protected staging ground roomy enough to pass a body of troops either way. It is self-published and not peer-reviewed, so it sits below the scholarly work this record prefers; it is careful and consistent with the manuals it illustrates, but a claim resting on it alone is one specialist's synthesis rather than an attested statement, and it describes China, not Japan.

karamete-kotobank

Kotobank, 搦手 - 精選版 日本国語大辞典 and デジタル大辞泉 (https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%90%A6%E6%89%8B)

What it is: The Kotobank page for karamete, carrying two published Japanese dictionaries: the Shogakukan national-language dictionary, which defines it as a castle's rear gate and the express antonym of the ote or main front and dates its earliest attestation to 1235, and the Digital Daijisen entry for the rear gate itself.

Why it applies, and its limits: A dictionary is the right authority for what a term means, and the meaning is all we take: the rear gate is DEFINED against the main approach, which is what our postern comparison needs for its first term. Its limits are that it defines a word and not a building, so it gives no size, no construction and no use; that it is Japanese, while the wall it is set beside on our maps is Chinese in form; and that it says nothing about the umon, the shuimen or the European sally port, so the comparison drawing the three together remains this project's own.

xian-fortifications-enwiki

en.wikipedia Fortifications of Xi'an (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortifications_of_Xi%27an)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on the Ming walls of Xi'an, covering the circuit, the four corner watch towers and the ditch outside the wall, whose width and depth it gives in meters and feet.

Why it applies, and its limits: It supplies the one measured moat width this record could read for a Chinese provincial-capital enceinte, which is what the drawn figure is checked against. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary whose own citation for the figure was not followed; that Xi'an is among the largest walled circuits ever built, so its ditch is a ceiling and not an average; and that the measurement is of the ditch as it stands today after conservation.

suzhou-panmen-gov

Suzhou Municipal People's Government, Municipal Bureau of Culture, Radio, Television and Tourism, 水陆盘门 (The Water-and-Land Panmen), 2021-10-29 (https://www.suzhou.gov.cn/szwgjyhsj/yhsjjbgk/202110/3ad7341d62c24548bd075701ca6bbeda.shtml)

What it is: A city government page for Panmen, the only Chinese city gate that preserves a land gate and a water gate side by side, describing both and giving measured dimensions: the barbican court between the land gate's two layers, and the water gate's two gate layers, their spacing and its depth.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives real numbers, from the body responsible for the monument, for the two features our city gates draw and had none for - a barbican court and a canal gate - and it settles the barbican's PLAN, which is roughly square rather than urn-shaped. Its limits are that it describes one exceptional surviving gate of a great Jiangnan city rather than a norm, that its figures are of the monument as it stands after restoration, and that it is a public-information page rather than an excavation report.

touken-world-umon

刀剣ワールド 城, castle-terms glossary, 埋門 (umon) (https://www.homemate-research-castle.com/useful/glossary/castle/2122201/)

What it is: A castle-terminology glossary entry for the umon, the gate cut through the lower part of a castle's stone wall or moat face: concealed so that an enemy does not read it as an entrance, built plainly and so that it can be filled in easily, kept impassable except when wanted, and intended among other things as the route by which the lord of the castle escapes. It names surviving examples at Takamatsu and Nagoya.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the closest thing the East Asian record offers to a sally port's FUNCTION - a small concealed opening kept shut and opened when needed - which is the term our postern comparison leans on hardest. Its limits are that it is a curated commercial glossary with no citations of its own, tertiary and written for enthusiasts; that it describes Japanese castles rather than a Chinese city wall; and that the better sources for it, a cultural-property survey or the city that holds the famous surviving example, were not reached.

zhonghua-gate-enwiki

en.wikipedia Zhonghua Gate, Nanjing (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhonghua_Gate,_Nanjing)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on the Zhonghua Gate at Nanjing, the largest surviving Chinese city gate, which gives the length, clear width and height of each of the four gate tunnels in line and the distance between them.

Why it applies, and its limits: Four measured clear widths at one Ming gate are exactly what a rule about the opening a road passes through needs, and they are the only such set this record could read. Its limits are that it is tertiary; that this is the grandest gate of a Ming capital rather than a provincial seat's, so it stands at the top of the band and not in the middle of it; and that the figures describe the monument as restored.

jah-song-military-cities

刘炜, 黄茜, 徐腾, "A study of the building of mid-Northern-Song military cities based on the Wujing zongyao" (title translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「基于《武经总要》的北宋中期军事城池营建研究」), 『建筑史学刊』 (Journal of Architectural History) 2026(2): 122-132 (https://www.jgcm.ac.cn/jah/cn/article/pdf/preview/10.12329/20969368.2026.02011.pdf)

What it is: A peer-reviewed architectural-history study of how mid-Northern-Song military cities were built, worked from the Wujing zongyao, the state military compendium of 1044: the wall's form, the spacing of the projecting mamian, the towers set on them, and the arc-shaped corner tower the compendium calls a dituan.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is scholarship reading the period's own manual, and it answers the question our tower rule turns on with the reason attached - the corner tower was curved deliberately, to increase the attacker's difficulty and reduce the surface exposed. Its limits are that it describes Song practice as a compendium prescribed it, three to five centuries before the Ming and Edo anchor these maps take, and that a manual's prescription is not proof that every city built to it; the paper itself notes the form is absent from Han and Tang texts, so it is dated as well as prescriptive.

sinoss-cao-city-defense

曹文明, "The defense works of the ancient Chinese city: the form of the rampart and its development" (title translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「中国古代的城防设施：城垣的形制与演变」), 中国社会科学报, on the Ministry of Education's social-science portal (https://www.sinoss.net/c/2010-05-04/526722.shtml)

What it is: A short article by a specialist on the defense works of the ancient Chinese city - the rampart's form and how it developed, the projecting mamian platforms built against the outer face, and the wengcheng thrown around a gate - published in the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences newspaper and carried on the Ministry of Education's social-science portal.

Why it applies, and its limits: It states in one sentence what the wall's projections are FOR - striking a besieger at the wall's foot from two sides - which is the reasoning behind every tower rule these city maps follow. Its limits are that it is a newspaper article rather than a research paper, with no apparatus and no figures, so it is good for the settled point it states and not for any number; and that it speaks of the Chinese city across its whole history rather than of one period.

qq-xian-southwest-corner

"Why is the southwest corner of the Xi'an city wall round?", a popular-history piece on the Tencent news portal, 2026 (https://news.qq.com/rain/a/20260206A06Z5M00)

What it is: A popular-history article on why the southwest corner of Xi'an's wall is round, reporting the explanation it says archaeology and history most accept - that the Ming rebuild of 1370 followed the old line of the Tang imperial city and reused a round base platform standing at that corner - and citing the Ming shilu for the date.

Why it applies, and its limits: The record says the century of that inherited corner is disputed, and this is the readable page carrying the side of the dispute the encyclopedia article does not: a Tang origin against a Yuan one. Its limits are severe and are why it is used only for that: it is journalism rather than scholarship, it reports what it says the field accepts without citing a study, and it concerns one corner of one wall.

lijin-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 厘金 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/厘金)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on the lijin, the Qing transit tax first levied in 1853 against the Taiping; an encyclopedia article.

Why it applies, and its limits: It dates the tax, which is what this page needs to keep it apart from the Ming customs barriers; it does not mention them.

ditai-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 敌台 (城墙) (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/敌台_(城墙))

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on the ditai, the bastion projecting from a city wall, with the Wubei Zhi's dictum on it; an encyclopedia article.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the bastion's purpose - flanking fire from both sides - and shows square, round and semicircular forms in its images, which bears on this page's shape rule; a tertiary summary that gives no spacing figure, and whose round and semicircular exemplars are European walls (Ávila, Krak des Chevaliers), its Chinese example rectangular.

mengxi-bitan-wikisource

Shen Kuo, Mengxi Bitan, juan 11, on Chinese Wikisource (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/夢溪筆談/卷11)

What it is: The eleventh-century Northern Song miscellany's passage on the horse-face bastions Shen Kuo paced out, in the Wikisource transcription; a primary text.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the bastion's purpose in the author's own words - arrows and stones from close-set spurs reaching one another so that an attacker at the wall's foot is struck from every side - and one measured spacing, which is the crossfire this page's towers are set for; one city, one century; the wall Shen Kuo paced is the fifth-century Xia fortress of Tongwan, not a Song-built city wall.

pingyao-chengqiang-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 平遥城墙 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/zh-hans/%E5%B9%B3%E9%81%A5%E5%9F%8E%E5%A2%99)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on the city wall of Pingyao, the best-preserved Ming county wall: its circuit, its gates and towers, the 4 m moat outside it, and the southern side built along the Zhongdu river.

Why it applies, and its limits: It records that even this model Chinese walled town put one side of its wall along a river. A tertiary summary used for the wall's course and its moat, whose dimensions describe the wall as it survives after Ming rebuilds and modern conservation, not as first raised.

tang-changan-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 唐长安城 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/唐长安城)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on Tang Chang'an, its wards and their gates; an encyclopedia article.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the ward enclosed on all four sides with guarded gates shut at curfew, which is the sealed ward this page's fence stands for; a capital of the Tang, and it does not describe where a ward wall met the city wall; the walled, curfewed ward itself lapsed at the Tang-Song transition, so for a Ming-modeled seat it is an earlier form rather than a contemporary one.

fortified-tower-enwiki

en.wikipedia "Fortified tower" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortified_tower)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on the fortified tower, square, round and D-shaped; an encyclopedia article.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the round tower's resistance to sappers and projectiles and the square tower's vulnerable corners, which is Europe's reason this page sets beside the Song one; a general survey.

castle-enwiki

en.wikipedia "Castle" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castle)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on the castle; an encyclopedia article, read for its sentence on round towers against cannon.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the European preference for round towers as deflecting shot, one half of the comparison this page makes; a general survey; the reason it gives is the cannon-era one, later than the Song comparison this page draws and a different mechanism from the sapper-and-projectile reason.

hakone-seki-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 箱根関 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%AE%B1%E6%A0%B9%E9%96%A2)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the Hakone barrier, the checkpoint on the Tokaido highway where the shogunate controlled traffic between Edo and the west. It lists the buildings that made up the station: a main guard house with its upper guard room, guards' duty room, rest room and bath house; an opposite guard house with its half-shift duty room, rest room and lockup; stables, a watch post, a public notice board, all enclosed by a fence.

Why it applies, and its limits: For drawing a checkpoint, the useful question is what buildings a barrier consists of, and this article answers it as an itemized list, which is why we use it. It is a tertiary encyclopedia summary, and Hakone was the most important barrier in the country on the most important road - a checkpoint on a lesser highway would have had a plainer program - so the list is a fully equipped ceiling rather than an average. The one thing we wanted from it and did not get is the arrangement: that the two guard houses faced each other across the road is not stated on any page we could read.

city-gate-enwiki

defensive-wall-enwiki

en.wikipedia "Defensive wall" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defensive_wall)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on the defensive wall of towns and cities; an encyclopedia article.

Why it applies, and its limits: It states that a wall is crossed only at its gates and that works stopped traffic circumventing the city so that tolls were paid, which is the chokepoint this page's gate station rests on; a general article.

chinese-city-wall-enwiki

en.wikipedia Chinese city wall (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_city_wall)

What it is: The English Wikipedia survey article on Chinese city walls: their construction from a rammed-earth core faced in brick or stone, their surviving examples at Nanjing, Xi'an and Kaifeng, and their plan, rectangular for capitals and important northern cities but irregular in broken country, where the line followed the terrain.

Why it applies, and its limits: It supports the shape rule our town walls obey, that a wall is a costly thing pulled tight around what must actually be defended and bent by the ground, rather than a tidy circle drawn around empty space. It is a tertiary summary covering the whole country over two thousand years, it gives no costs and no dimensions tied to a settlement's rank, and the economic argument our text makes from the shape is our own reasoning rather than the article's.

wujing-zongyao-wikisource

Zeng Gongliang and Ding Du, Wujing Zongyao (1044), first collection, juan 12, on Chinese Wikisource (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/武經總要/前集/卷十二)

What it is: The Northern Song imperial military compendium's chapter on building and defending a walled city, in the Wikisource transcription; a primary text.

Why it applies, and its limits: It prescribes four gates with two kept shut, three on awkward ground, and the enemy tower's bays, which are the counted openings and the tower this page draws; a prescription for the Song state's cities, not a survey of what was built.

chengchi-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 城池 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/城池)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on the chengchi, the walled city and its moat, with the gate counts of prefectural and county seats; an encyclopedia article.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the usual gate count by tier and what stood inside the wall, which sets this page's few grand gates; a tertiary summary; the tier gate counts (eight for a prefecture, four for a county) are an unreferenced generalization on the page, taken here for the shape and not the number.
