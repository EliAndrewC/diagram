Citations: Religion and the dead: the research behind the shrine, torii and funerary rules

The passages behind the footnotes of Religion and the dead: the research behind the shrine, torii and funerary rules - first the works they quote and why each one counts, then the notes themselves. Hover a footnote number on that page and the note appears beside it; the numbers here are the same ones.

The works cited on this page

religion-song-enwiki

en.wikipedia Religion in the Song dynasty (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_the_Song_dynasty)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article "Religion in the Song dynasty", a survey of Buddhism, Daoism and folk religion in China between 960 and 1279. The one figure we take from it is a dated count: records for the year 1221 gave 400,000 monks and 61,000 nuns in the dynasty, against a population the same article puts at around 100 million.

Why it applies, and its limits: Deciding how many monks a settlement of a given size can hold needs an order of magnitude for the share of a population living in religious orders, and this supplies one attributed to a dynastic record - under half a percent. The limits are real: an encyclopedia is a tertiary summary, so this figure is used precisely because it is stated as coming from a record and no further; a Song Chinese count is standing in for the whole setting; and an imperial count is a count of registered clergy, which is not the same as everyone living in a temple.

terauke-seido-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 寺請制度 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/寺請制度) - 「Of the 6,000 Jodo-sect temples in existence at the time of Genroku 9 (1696), those founded from the sixteenth century onward accounted for nine tenths.」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「元禄9年（1696年）当時存在した6000か所の浄土宗寺院のうち、16世紀以降の創建が9割を占めていた」); no all-sect total of Edo temples on the page

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 寺請制度 (terauke seido), on the Tokugawa requirement that every household register with a Buddhist temple, and on the growth in temple numbers that followed it.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for a single figure that tells us how recent a dense temple landscape actually is - six thousand Jodo-sect temples in existence in 1696, nine tenths of them founded from the sixteenth century onward - which is the check on how many precincts a settlement of a given age should carry. It is a tertiary summary whose figures we take only where its own references support them, the count covers one sect rather than all of them, and the page carries no all-sect total, so we do not put one on a map.

kanazawa-teramachi

VISIT KANAZAWA official travel guide, Teramachi Temple Area (https://visitkanazawa.jp/en/spot/detail_10182.html)

What it is: A visitor attraction listing on VISIT KANAZAWA, the official travel guide published by the Kanazawa City Tourism Association, describing the Teramachi temple district - one of three quarters of the old castle town into which the ruling Maeda family gathered temples when the town was laid out.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the scale a castle town's temple district could reach - some seventy temples close together in one of Kanazawa's three - which is the top of the range our temple quarters are measured against. Its limits are that it is a tourist board's page with no sources; that the seventy is a present-day count of what stands, not a period census; that Kanazawa was the seat of the largest domain in the country and one of its largest castle towns, so its temple stock is a ceiling and not a norm; and that it was three districts, not one.

takada-teramachi

Takada Teramachi tourism site / Joetsu Stories (https://joetsu-stories.jp/2025/02/03/takada-teramachi-part-1/)

What it is: An English-language local history article on Joetsu Stories, a bilingual site about the city of Joetsu in Niigata Prefecture, telling how the temple quarter (teramachi) of the castle town of Takada came into being after Matsudaira Tadateru moved his seat there in 1614. It describes the ground west of the castle that was set aside for temples, the first wave of temples that moved in, and what the domain expected of them.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives one dated case of a temple quarter made by a lord's decree - about twenty-five temples moved to ground west of the castle at Takada in 1614 - which sets the size of a castle town's temple quarter on our maps and the reading that it doubled as a defensive belt quartering foot soldiers. Its limits are that it is an anonymous local-history blog post that cites nothing; that its count is hedged by its own writer ("probably about 25"); that it is one town in one province at one date, a point in a range and not a rule; and that the reading of "protection" as spiritual support plus billets for soldiers is the writer's interpretation, unattributed.

takayama-teramachi

Japan Travel, "Takayama's historic 'Temple Town'" (https://en.japantravel.com/gifu/takayama-s-teramachi-gifu/13893)

What it is: "Takayama's historic 'Temple Town'", a visitor's guide article by Sandra Isaka on Japan Travel, a community travel site written by contributors, walking the reader through the temple quarter on the eastern edge of Takayama and noting who founded most of its temples.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives a size-matched low anchor for a town's temple quarter - Takayama's, with more than ten temples and shrines - which bounds the bottom of the range our town maps draw. Its limits are that it is community travel writing, a visitor's walk-through with no sourcing; that its count mixes Buddhist temples with Shinto shrines, so it is not a temple count; that it counts the quarter as it stands today rather than at any period; and that Takayama, its castle pulled down after 1692 and the town run from the intendancy office thereafter, is a particular kind of town.

jokamachi-wiki

Japanese Wiki Corpus, Jokamachi (https://www.japanesewiki.com/history/Jokamachi.html)

What it is: The English rendering of the Japanese Wikipedia castle-town article published in the Japanese Wiki Corpus, a collection of translated Japanese encyclopedia articles on history and culture. The passage we use covers the temple district placed at the town's outer rim, whose walled precincts were counted as part of the town's defenses.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives, in English, the temple quarter at the rim of the castle town with its precincts counted as part of the defenses, which is what puts the teramachi where our maps put it. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary at one further remove: the English is a machine-translation research corpus (the bilingual corpus of Wikipedia's Kyoto-topic articles made by NICT, Japan's National Institute of Information and Communications Technology), a frozen snapshot rather than the maintained article, which is why it reads as it does; and that two other entries in this registry point at the same article and at the Japanese original it translates, so the three cannot corroborate one another.

pingyao-chenghuangmiao

Wikipedia, City God Temple of Pingyao (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_God_Temple_of_Pingyao)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on the City God Temple of Pingyao, the Chenghuang temple in the walled county town of Pingyao in Shanxi, which describes the site as three separate temples standing together on one complex of more than 7,300 square meters and lists them individually.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for the shape of a county seat's civic temple - not a single hall but a compound holding several cults side by side, the City God's own temple among them - which is how our town maps compose a precinct rather than dropping one building. It is a tertiary summary used for what stands on the site rather than for a measurement, the surviving buildings are later rebuildings of a much older foundation, and Pingyao was a wealthy town whose temple is a generous example rather than an average county one.

tontine-monastery-lending

The Tontine Coffee-House, "Buddhist Monastery Lending" (https://tontinecoffeehouse.com/2024/02/26/buddhist-monastery-lending/)

What it is: A post, "Buddhist Monastery Lending," written by Daniel DeMatos in February 2024 for The Tontine Coffee-House, a financial-history blog running since 2018 that covers the history of financial institutions and innovations. It traces how Buddhist monasteries in China served as lenders and pawnbrokers from the arrival of Buddhism down to the nineteenth century, and closes with a short reading list that includes Jacques Gernet's Buddhism in Chinese Society.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for the institutional facts behind a temple that lends - the monastery as the great pawnbroker, and pledged goods held in an associated warehouse or granary for the life of the loan - which is why a temple compound on our maps can reasonably carry a strongroom rather than only a hall. It is a secondary blog summary rather than a scholarly work, so we take from it only what its own cited literature supports, and it is about China; we apply it to a Japanese-flavored setting on the strength of the monastic economy the two shared, not as a statement about any particular Japanese temple.

pawnbroking-history

Wikipedia, History of pawnbroking (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_pawnbroking)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article "History of pawnbroking", a tertiary summary of lending against pledged goods from antiquity to the present, with a short section on China that runs from the earliest monastic pawnshops through the Song.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use its statement that the earliest Chinese pawnbrokers were established and run by Buddhist monasteries, and its note that Southern Song laypeople formed partnerships with monasteries to open pawnshops, when deciding where a lending house belongs on a map - inside or beside a temple precinct rather than in a market row. It is a tertiary summary whose claims we accept only where its own references support them, and China occupies a few sentences of a global survey, so we take the institutional association it describes and not a chronology or a count.

kannushi-wiki

Wikipedia, Shinto priest / Kannushi (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kannushi)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on the kannushi, the priest who tends a Shinto shrine, covering the office, its duties, and the way it has been passed down within families.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for one structural fact that shapes who lives beside a shrine on our maps - that the office was hereditary, held by a married priest whose children succeeded him, so a shrine implies a resident priestly household rather than a celibate community. It is a tertiary summary and we take its figures only where its own references support them; note too that its most concrete statements are about the modern era, including the abolition of hereditary appointment in 1871, which is well after the period our setting is modeled on and which we use only as evidence of how deeply rooted the practice was before it.

tricycle-temple-wives

Tricycle, "Temple Wives of Japan" (https://tricycle.org/magazine/japan-temple-wives/)

What it is: "Temple Wives of Japan," an interview by Karen Jensen with the scholar Jessica Starling, published in the Spring 2022 issue of Tricycle: The Buddhist Review, a long-running nonprofit Buddhist magazine. It explains the bomori - literally the guardian or protector of the temple, in practice the wife of a Jodo Shinshu resident priest - and the household that a temple in that tradition actually is.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives us what a rural temple contains as a domestic institution: a married clergy exempted by the Tokugawa government so a temple head could be married and still hold priestly status, a wife who runs the temple, and succession from father to eldest son - which is why our temple compounds carry a priest's family dwelling rather than a celibate dormitory. It is a magazine interview rather than a study, and it describes one tradition, Jodo Shinshu, whose married clergy was the exception in the Edo period rather than the rule, so we use it for the shape of a temple household and not as a claim about every sect.

fushimi-inari-senbon

伏見稲荷大社, 千本鳥居 (https://inari.jp/sp/map/spot_07/)

What it is: The official website of Fushimi Inari Taisha in Kyoto, the head shrine of the Inari cult - its visitors' questions page and a map page on the Senbon Torii, the famous tunnels of vermilion gates on its mountain paths. The site gives the shrine's own account of the gates as votive offerings, a custom it dates from the Edo period onward, and its present count of about ten thousand across the whole mountain approach.

Why it applies, and its limits: It tells us that the gates are donations and roughly how thickly the most heavily endowed shrine in the country came to be lined with them, which is the upper bound a map needs when it draws a shrine approach. Its limits are that it is the institution describing itself, not a survey; that the count is a present-day round number accumulated since the Edo period, not a period census, and only the whole-mountain figure is readable on the site, so any density per stretch of path is derived by us; and that this is the most famous shrine of its kind, so it is a ceiling and never a typical case.

louzeyuan-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 漏澤園 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%BC%8F%E6%BE%A4%E5%9C%92)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on 漏澤園 (louzeyuan), the state-funded paupers' burial grounds of imperial China, from Cai Jing's proposal of 1104 through their continuation under the Yuan and Ming. It describes how they were sited on high, open, barren ground behind a fence on all four sides, and how each plot was numbered by the Thousand Character Classic and registered with the dead person's name, origin and dates.

Why it applies, and its limits: It answers, for a city map, where the poor were buried and what that ground looked like: bad land, deliberately walled off, laid out in numbered rows rather than left as an open field. It is a tertiary summary in Chinese, and it describes a Chinese institution, which suits the imperial-Chinese half of our setting more directly than the Japanese half; we take from it the siting and the layout, which its own sources support, rather than any count.

daxiangguo-enwiki

en.wikipedia Daxiangguo Temple (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daxiangguo_Temple)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on the Daxiangguo Temple, the great imperially patronized Buddhist monastery in Kaifeng, the Northern Song capital. It gives the temple's history and its size at its height, when it is said to have managed sixty-four departments with several thousand monks, and the rebuilt temple's grounds at about 540 mu (some 36 hectares).

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it as an upper anchor for how large a capital monastery could get, which is what keeps our own biggest temple from being drawn too small. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary which the encyclopedia itself flags as below its quality standards; that the sentence we quote is cited to the temple's own website, an institution's description of itself rather than a count or a study; and that this was one exceptional court-supported monastery, so it bounds the top of the range and says nothing about an ordinary temple.

meiji-jingu-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 明治神宮 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%98%8E%E6%B2%BB%E7%A5%9E%E5%AE%AE)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on Meiji Jingu, the large Shinto shrine in Tokyo, covering its grounds, its approach and its buildings.

Why it applies, and its limits: The article counts eight torii along the approach and gives no distances between them, so no spacing figure on our maps comes from it, so no spacing figure on our maps comes from it. Beyond being a tertiary summary whose figures we take only where its own references support them, it describes a modern shrine of exceptional scale, which makes it a poor model for the small country shrines our settlements mostly carry.

toribeno-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 鳥辺野 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%B3%A5%E8%BE%BA%E9%87%8E)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on Toribeno (鳥辺野), the great burial ground on the eastern hills of Kyoto, one of the three famous funerary fields of the old capital along with Adashino to the west and Rendaino to the north, attested in literature from the Heian period onward in works such as the Tale of Genji and Essays in Idleness.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for one point about form rather than any number: that the extent of such a field had no clear definition, which is our warrant for drawing a burial ground as an unbounded stretch of hillside rather than a walled or fenced plot. It is a tertiary summary, so we take from it only what its own references support; it also describes the capital's own enormous burial fields, so it tells us the shape of the thing and never the scale a village graveyard should have.

adashino-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 化野 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%8C%96%E9%87%8E)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 化野 (Adashino), on a district at the western edge of Kyoto. It records that the place was formerly used for exposure burial, that it later became known as a cremation ground alongside Toribeyama, and that Adashino in the west, Toribeno in the east and Rendaino in the north were called the city's three great burial grounds.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for one thing only: where a city put its dead, which turns out to be at the edge, in named districts on the outward side of the settlement, and that is a placement rule a map can follow. It is a tertiary summary of one city's arrangement, so it tells us the shape of the custom rather than any measured distance, and Kyoto is a capital, so the pattern is applied to smaller places by analogy rather than by evidence.
