The works cited on this page

religion-song-enwiki

en.wikipedia Religion in the Song dynasty (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_the_Song_dynasty)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article "Religion in the Song dynasty", a survey of Buddhism, Daoism and folk religion in China between 960 and 1279. The one figure we take from it is a dated count: records for the year 1221 gave 400,000 monks and 61,000 nuns in the dynasty, against a population the same article puts at around 100 million.

Why it applies, and its limits: Deciding how many monks a settlement of a given size can hold needs an order of magnitude for the share of a population living in religious orders, and this supplies one attributed to a dynastic record - under half a percent. The limits are real: an encyclopedia is a tertiary summary, so this figure is used precisely because it is stated as coming from a record and no further; a Song Chinese count is standing in for the whole setting; and an imperial count is a count of registered clergy, which is not the same as everyone living in a temple.

What it is: A visitor attraction listing on VISIT KANAZAWA, the official travel guide published by the Kanazawa City Tourism Association, describing the Teramachi temple district - one of three quarters of the old castle town into which the ruling Maeda family gathered temples when the town was laid out.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the scale a castle town's temple district could reach - some seventy temples close together in one of Kanazawa's three - which is the top of the range our temple quarters are measured against. Its limits are that it is an anonymous local-history blog post that cites nothing; that its count is hedged by its own writer ("probably about 25"); that it is one town in one province at one date, a point in a range and not a rule; and that the reading of "protection" as spiritual support plus billets for soldiers is the writer's interpretation, unattributed.

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on the kannushi, the priest who tends a Shinto shrine, covering the office, its duties, and the way it has been passed down within families.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for one structural fact that shapes who lives beside a shrine on our maps - that the office was hereditary, held by a married priest whose children succeeded him, so a shrine implies a resident priestly household rather than a celibate community. Tricycle, "Temple Wives of Japan" (https://tricycle.org/magazine/japan-temple-wives/)

What it is: "Temple Wives of Japan," an interview by Karen Jensen with the scholar Jessica Starling, published in the Spring 2022 issue of Tricycle: The Buddhist Review, a long-running nonprofit Buddhist magazine. It explains the bomori - literally the guardian or protector of the temple, in practice the wife of a Jodo Shinshu resident priest - and the household that a temple in that tradition actually is.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives us what a rural temple contains as a domestic institution: a married clergy exempted by the Tokugawa government so a temple head could be married and still hold priestly status, a wife who runs the temple, and succession from father to eldest son - which is why our temple compounds carry a priest's family dwelling rather than a celibate dormitory. It is a magazine interview rather than a study, and it describes one tradition, Jodo Shinshu, whose married clergy was the exception in the Edo period rather than the rule, so we use it for the shape of a temple household and not as a claim about every sect.

What it is: A study of the Daoist priestly families of northern Taiwan, reconstructed from the lists of lineage masters in their own liturgical manuals and from their family registers, with dated genealogical tables running from the eighth to the twenty-third generation. The author worked with his own master in Taipei and its district in 1986 and 1987 and then followed the lineages back to their mainland origin on the Guangdong and Fujian border country - the Liu to Jao-p'ing in Chaozhou prefecture, the Lin over the provincial line to Chao-an in Fujian, which he treats as one region because the two sub-prefectures adjoin.

Why it applies, and its limits: Where a general statement says a Daoist office is hereditary, this DEMONSTRATES it - names, dates and generations over about two hundred years - which is why the record hangs the hereditary half of its clergy rule here rather than on a summary. Its limits are that it is one regional tradition, probably Hakka in origin - the author hedges the word himself, since one of his lineages speaks the Chaozhou dialect and is not Hakka at all - in Taiwan and on the Fujian and Guangdong coast, so it is not evidence for Daoist practice across China; that a handful of lineages can show the office DID descend in families and can never give a rate; that transmission in its own tables runs to an adopted son, a son-in-law or a disciple as readily as to a son, so a strict father-to-son rule overstates it; and that it records at least two lines that simply stopped. Two things that cut the other way and belong here for balance: the Qing evidence is documentary rather than remembered - written family registers and liturgical manuscripts copied in 1908, 1928 and 1932, with masters dated from the middle of the eighteenth century - so for the hereditary claim this is period documentation and not a modern informant's account of the past; and the author is candid that the registers he reconstructs from contain manifest errors and gaps, which touches who descended from whom rather than whether the offices descended at all.
