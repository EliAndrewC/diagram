Citations: Water: the research behind the flow, channel and wetland rules

The passages behind the footnotes of Water: the research behind the flow, channel and wetland rules - first the works they quote and why each one counts, then the notes themselves. Hover a footnote number on that page and the note appears beside it; the numbers here are the same ones.

The works cited on this page

nougyoudoboku-matsutan

のうぎょうとぼく, 「Terminal irrigation channel / small irrigation channel within a paddy field」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「水田圃場内の「末端用水路・小用水路」」) and 「terminal drainage channel / small drainage channel」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「末端排水路・小排水路」) (https://nougyoudoboku.com/terminal-irrigation-canal/)

What it is: A page on the terminal irrigation canal - the last channel before the paddy plot - from のうぎょうとぼく, a self-published site on agricultural civil engineering written by a credentialed agricultural engineer and farmer, a former public servant in the field, posted in 2022 and updated in 2024. It sets out the three-tier supply hierarchy of a consolidated paddy district, the 300 to 600 m spacing of branch canals, and the height of the terminal channel's bed against the field surface; the matching terminal drain is the subject of a separate article on the same site, which this page links but does not contain.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the gravity logic of a paddy's water supply - the order of main, branch and terminal channel and how the last one sits against the field - which wet rice has not changed and which is what our canal trees are drawn from. Its limits are that it is present-day engineering guidance, self-published by a practitioner rather than a ministry standard or a peer-reviewed work; that it assumes consolidated, machine-worked paddy with precast concrete channels, so its 300 to 600 m branch spacing is the dimension of a modern land-readjustment block, the very thing a premodern field lacked, and only the order and the gravity transfer; and that the drainage side - the terminal drain's depth, the drain hierarchy, the sizing from catchment - is not on this page but on the linked companion, so the drainage claims the record took from this key rest on nothing readable at its link.

nougyoudoboku-suikou

のうぎょうとぼく (Japanese agricultural-engineering design notes), 「Design of the water inlet (minakuchi) and the mizujiri (the drain outlet) within a paddy field」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「水田内の「水口・水じり（落水口）」に関する設計について」) (https://nougyoudoboku.com/water-entrance-exit/)

What it is: A second page from the same Japanese agricultural civil engineering site, のうぎょうとぼく, covering the design of the two openings in a paddy plot's bund: the mizuguchi intake that admits water from the terminal channel and the mizujiri outlet that lets it go.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the plot-scale detail our maps draw at every bund - one intake per plot (two when the intakes would stand more than 50 m apart), an intake no wider than about half a meter, its sill just above the field surface and the outlet a little below it - so that water enters at one corner and leaves at another. The same limits apply as to its companion page: it is modern design guidance for engineered plots and concrete or pipe fittings, while a premodern plot's intake was a notch cut in an earth bund, so we take the arrangement and the relative heights and not the materials or the tolerances.

lacey-regime

Lacey's regime theory for unlined alluvial canals - the wetted-perimeter equation P = 4.8 * sqrt(Q), and the regime power-law exponents (width 0.5, depth 0.33, velocity 0.17 against discharge). Consulted via canal-design references and the Journal of Hydrology review "The width of a bankfull channel; Lacey's formula explained" (2003) (https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-1694(03)00069-6)

What it is: H. H. G. Savenije, "The width of a bankfull channel; Lacey's formula explained", a research paper in the Journal of Hydrology (2003) giving a physical derivation of the regime formula developed on the irrigation canals of colonial India and Pakistan: the width of a stable channel grows with the square root of its discharge.

Why it applies, and its limits: It supplies the law behind the way our channels taper, which is that width grows as the square root of flow rather than in proportion to it, and it gives an absolute check on drawn channel widths that caught a network drawn five or six times its true hydraulic size. The honest limits are that this is a modern engineering formula, calibrated largely on big colonial-era irrigation canals in sandy soil, applied here to small village ditches, so we use it for the relation between flow and width and not to pronounce the exact width of any one ditch.

fao-pond-water

FAO, Water for animals ch. 6 surface reservoirs (https://www.fao.org/4/r7488e/r7488e06.htm)

What it is: The surface-reservoir chapter of Water for animals, a UN Food and Agriculture Organization manual on watering livestock. It describes what a dug pond loses - evaporation from the surface, which it puts as high as 2 m a year, and seepage through the floor - and why such a pond needs a spillway to carry storm runoff past the dam.

Why it applies, and its limits: Our maps need to know that a standing pond is not a sealed tank and that it must have somewhere for a flood to go, both of which this states plainly. The limits are that it is a modern development manual written for hot, dry grazing country, so its evaporation figure is an outer bound rather than a number for a temperate rice valley, and that it gives these losses in prose rather than as a worked water balance.

sotobori-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 外濠 (東京都) (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/外濠_(東京都))

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 外濠 (東京都), on the outer moat of Edo castle in what is now Tokyo - how it was dug, which watercourses were pressed into it, and what has since become of it.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for a point about how a fortified city treats its water: the Sotobori was not a ditch dug beside a river but the river itself relocated, the Hirakawa's course moved into the excavation, which is the pattern our walled maps follow when a stream meets a defensive line. It is an encyclopedia article on one specific and unusually large city work - a shogunal capital, not a provincial town - so the principle scales down to our maps only as a principle, and the dimensions of Edo's moat are not numbers we carry across.

chang-jiang-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 長江 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%95%B7%E6%B1%9F)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the Chang Jiang, the Yangtze. Among much else it lists the separate names the river carries along its course, from the headwater Tuotuo He through the Jinsha and the Chuan, Jing and Yangzi reaches, the last of them taken from a ferry crossing.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is straightforward evidence for something our maps get wrong by default, that a real river is not one name from source to sea but a chain of local names, each belonging to the stretch the people beside it know. It is a tertiary encyclopedia summary, and it describes one enormous continental river in modern usage, so we take the naming habit from it as a principle and not the names or the reach lengths as anything to scale from.

shinano-gawa-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 信濃川 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E4%BF%A1%E6%BF%83%E5%B7%9D)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the Shinano River, the longest river in Japan, which notes that the same river is the Shinano within Niigata prefecture and becomes the Chikuma once it passes up into Nagano.

Why it applies, and its limits: It supports a small point of naming that our maps follow, that one river can legitimately carry different names along its course, so a river labeled one thing at a settlement need not be labeled the same thing upstream. It is a tertiary summary about a single modern river, used only for a naming convention it states plainly, and nothing on any map depends on a number from it.

sumida-gawa-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 隅田川 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%9A%85%E7%94%B0%E5%B7%9D)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 隅田川, on the river through eastern Tokyo, including the several older names by which different stretches of it were known - Ōkawa, Asakusagawa, Miyatogawa.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is cited for a small point of naming that matters on a map: one river can carry different names along different reaches and in different mouths, so a watercourse crossing a city need not be labeled the same at both ends. It is an encyclopedia article about one specific river in one city; we use it as an example of the naming habit rather than as evidence about any other river, and where it does not confirm a name we have seen elsewhere we do not claim it.

shizen-teibo-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 自然堤防 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/自然堤防) - 「On alluvial lowland, long-established settlements are sited first of all on the natural levees, which are also used as dry fields.」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「沖積低地において、古くからの集落はまず自然堤防上に立地し、また畑として利用される」), against the 後背湿地 behind it

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 自然堤防 ("natural levee"), on the low ridge of coarser sediment a river builds along its own banks when it floods, and on the wetter backswamp (後背湿地) that lies behind it on an alluvial plain.

Why it applies, and its limits: This is the single most useful fact for laying out a lowland village: houses and dry fields go up on the levee, which is the only naturally drained ground for miles, and the paddy goes into the backswamp behind - a rule the article states directly for long-established Japanese settlements. It is an encyclopedia summary of settled physical geography rather than a survey, so we take the siting principle from it and not any measurement; and it describes Japanese alluvial lowland, so it applies to our river-plain settlements and says nothing about hill or fan country.

yato-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 谷戸 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E8%B0%B7%E6%88%B8)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 谷戸 (yato, also read yatsu), a tertiary encyclopedia entry on the small spring-fed valleys that bite into the hill country of eastern Japan, how their floors were farmed and how the woods on the slopes above them were used in common.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for why a narrow valley floor makes easily worked paddy once it drains, and for the common rights villagers held over the neighboring woods, which is what puts a firewood wood on the slope beside a valley-bottom field on our maps. It is a tertiary summary, so we take the pattern from it and not a number; it speaks for eastern Japan in particular; and carrying those common rights on from firewood to a reed bed is a step our own record takes, not something the article says.

fukada-kotobank

コトバンク 深田 and ja.wikipedia ヨシ (https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%B7%B1%E7%94%B0-123763)

What it is: The Kotobank entry for 深田 (fukada, "deep paddy"), a Japanese online reference that reprints entries from published dictionaries and encyclopedias; here it defines the word as a paddy whose mud is deep, a swamp paddy (沼田, numata). It is cited together with the Japanese Wikipedia article on ヨシ (yoshi, the common reed), which covers reed beds and their cutting.

Why it applies, and its limits: Japanese dictionaries are good evidence that the deep-mud wet paddy we draw was a distinct and familiar kind of field, common enough to have its own everyday word. The limits are that a dictionary defines a word rather than measures a field, so there is no depth, no extent and no date here, and that the encyclopedia and Wikipedia entries beside it are tertiary summaries; anything more than the term and its sense comes from other entries in the record.

shitsuden-kotobank

コトバンク 湿田 (https://kotobank.jp/word/湿田-74168)

What it is: The Kotobank entry for 湿田 (shitsuden, "wet paddy"). Kotobank is a Japanese reference site that reprints entries from established published encyclopedias and dictionaries rather than writing its own, so the entry is a dictionary definition of the badly drained rice field that stays waterlogged the year round, with a note on the long conversion of such fields to the drained kind.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the wet paddy as a field class - the poorly drained paddy that never dries - and the direction of its conversion, which is what puts wet paddy on our maps at all. Its limits are that it is a very short tertiary aggregation of dictionary entries, carrying the distinction and the direction of change but no date or proportion; and that the conversion it documents at any scale is the Meiji-and-later national drainage program, a post-1868 undertaking outside the setting, so only the older, western-Japan beginning of the story it mentions bears on our fields.

hibiya-irie-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 日比谷入江 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%97%A5%E6%AF%94%E8%B0%B7%E5%85%A5%E6%B1%9F)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on 日比谷入江 (Hibiya Inlet), the tidal inlet that once reached inland to the foot of Edo Castle and is now dry land in central Tokyo. It records that filling began at the inlet's innermost northern end in 1592 with spoil from the works on the castle's western bailey, went on in earnest from 1603, and was finished in the early Edo period.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is our worked example of a waterfront settlement making new ground out of the earth dug for its own castle, which is the kind of thing that explains why a town's blocks near the water are newer and more regular than the ones behind them. It is a tertiary summary of one episode in one city, its dates are the traditional ones, and Edo's scale is far beyond anything we draw - so what carries across is the sequence, not the acreage.

bitchu-takamatsu-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 備中高松城 and 備中高松城の戦い (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/高松城_(備中国))

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on Bitchu Takamatsu Castle, a Sengoku-period fortress in what is now Okayama that stood on a belt of low, wet ground which formed its natural moats. Hashiba Hideyoshi's 1582 siege, in which an earth dam thrown around the castle drowned it in an artificial lake, has its own companion article, which is where the term "swamp castle" and the size of the lake are given.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a plain, well referenced account of a castle whose defense was its marsh rather than its stonework, which is exactly the question our wet defensive belt asks: whether low wetland was treated as a work of fortification and not merely as bad ground. It is a tertiary encyclopedia summary, so we take its figures only where its own references support them; the flooding is a single spectacular siege rather than routine practice; and it tells us that a wet belt was defensively real, not how wide an ordinary one was.

kagawa-tameike-structure

香川県, ため池の構造 (https://www.pref.kagawa.lg.jp/tochikai/about_tameike/repair/structure.html)

What it is: : that the intake is one facility, an inclined or vertical pipe drawing off stored water into a conduit through the dam base, with a sediment gate at the very bottom for clearing and for the last of the water, and that the water-facing slope of the bank is armored against wave erosion with riprap, stone pitching or concrete facing.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is why our reservoirs get a single intake works rather than scattered openings, and why one stretch of a pond's shore is deliberately bare stone while the rest can be planted, which makes the rule about a pond's margin a matter of position instead of one answer for the whole edge. It is a modern maintenance page: the concrete facing is of our time, the stone pitching and riprap are old, and we take the engineering reason for the armoring rather than the modern material.

tabayashi-1987

Akira Tabayashi, "Irrigation Systems in Japan", Geographical Review of Japan Series B 60(1): 41-65 (1987) (https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/grj1984b/60/1/60_1_41/_article/-char/en/)

What it is: A peer-reviewed article by Akira Tabayashi, "Irrigation Systems in Japan", in the Geographical Review of Japan, Series B volume 60 number 1 (1987), pages 41-65. It classifies Japan's irrigation systems by their water source - river, pond, lake, groundwater, torrent - maps where each kind predominates, and traces when each was built, from the Kofun and Nara period works of the west to the Sengoku and Edo works of the east.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the right kind of source for what it is used for - a Japanese geographer's account of Japanese irrigation from the third century to 1867, at national scale and with named systems at village scale, describing the gravity-fed logic that is premodern in origin - so the siting of a reservoir above its fields, the tapering of canals as they are tapped and the separation of supply from drain rest on it directly. Its limits are that its tail describes the modern land consolidation that followed the 1899 law, whose surveyed rectangular grid is the modern arrangement and not ours; that it gives no paddy depth; and that it does not say a storage pond is tapped by a single outlet, so that claim on our maps rests elsewhere.

jsidre-minumadai

JSIDRE, on Minuma-dai (1728) (https://www.jsidre.or.jp/tabata5-a/)

What it is: An article in the "history of regional development" series published by the Japanese Society of Irrigation, Drainage and Rural Engineering, on the Minuma-dai canal completed in 1728 on the Kanto plain, a shogunal reclamation carried out by Izawa Yasobei of the Kishu school of engineering. It describes the works and the reclamation layout that drained the old reservoir and watered the land in its place.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives us the comb layout our field blocks use, with the supply channels running along the higher rim and the drain down the lowest line through the middle, and it comes from the engineering profession that still maintains these systems. It is a society's popular history article rather than a peer-reviewed study and no author is named, and it describes one very large project, so we take the principle of the layout from it and not the dimensions, which belong to a scheme of thousands of hectares rather than to one hamlet's fields.

saitama-minuma-tsusenbori

Saitama City, 見沼通船堀のしくみ (https://www.city.saitama.lg.jp/004/005/006/008/p077111.html)

What it is: A page from the city of Saitama titled 見沼通船堀のしくみ ("how the Minuma boat canal works"), explaining the layout of the Minumadai irrigation system and the lock canal built to join its two arms. It states the arrangement in one line: the east-edge and west-edge canals run along the rim of the plateau, while the Shiba River runs down the center of the lowland.

Why it applies, and its limits: It describes an eighteenth-century Japanese irrigation scheme in the geometry our water layouts use - two supply canals carried high along the plateau rims on either side, and the river draining the lowland between - and it comes from the city that keeps the site. Its limits are that it is a heritage and education page rather than a technical study; that it describes one Kanto system as it survives; and that it gives the geometry only - that the fields could be fed from either side is our reading of that geometry, not the page's words.

aze-standard

Japanese land-improvement standard designs for 畦畔 (paddy bunds) - Aomori prefecture ほ場整備 standard drawing set and 大和川流域水田貯留技術基準 (https://www.pref.aomori.lg.jp/soshiki/nourin/noson/files/H2904_nnzusyu_H2911syuusei.pdf)

What it is: Aomori Prefecture's standard drawings for consolidated paddy (2017 revision), a public-works design standard: the outer bund drawn as a trapezoid 30 cm high above the higher field, 30 cm wide at the crest, with side slopes of 1:1 to 1:1.2, in three types by the difference in level between the fields it separates, up to 4 m, with berms on the tallest.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives an engineered cross-section for a paddy bund in numbers, which calibrates the most repeated line on a paddy map against the one to two feet the period sources give. Its limits are that it is a post-war land-consolidation standard for machine-formed embankments between fields whose surfaces may differ by meters, a different object from a hand-piled ridge between two level plots, so its figures are a modern engineered minimum and not a measurement of a premodern bund; that a hand-piled bund would have been less regular; and that the enlarged cold-region bund an enlarged cold-region bund of about 50 by 40 cm is not on the Aomori sheet - the berms on it are for tall bunds, and its only frost-heave provision concerns a flume's subgrade - and the other standard the entry names is neither linked nor quoted, so that figure stands as a guess.

fao-paddy-duty

FAO irrigation training manuals - "Scheme Irrigation Water Need and Supply" (u5835e) and "Determination of the Irrigation Schedule for Paddy Rice" (t7202e) (https://www.fao.org/4/u5835e/u5835e00.htm)

What it is: Two irrigation training manuals from the UN Food and Agriculture Organization - "Scheme Irrigation Water Need and Supply" and "Determination of the Irrigation Schedule for Paddy Rice" - which teach how to work out how much water a rice scheme must be fed: the large one-off soaking that turns dry ground into workable mud, the water that soaks away through the floor each day, the standing layer kept on the field, and the resulting flow per hectare.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it to size a channel against the fields it actually serves, and that sum is arithmetic of soil, sun and standing water, none of which a premodern valley did differently. The limits are that these are modern teaching documents whose numbers are planning generalizations across many countries, so they give a defensible band rather than the true duty of any particular valley, and that a premodern channel was cut by eye and by custom rather than calculated to a figure.

senjochi-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 扇状地, with MLIT land-classification notes (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/扇状地)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 扇状地 (alluvial fan), on the fan of gravel a mountain stream lays down where it leaves its valley: the three zones of the fan - its apex, its middle and its toe - and the line of springs at the toe where the water that sank into the gravel above comes back to the surface, along which settlement and paddy have clustered since antiquity.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the landform logic our foothill maps follow - dry gravel above, springs and paddy at the toe - and the physics of a fan does not change with the century. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary that carries the encyclopedia's own notice that it needs sources, so the zone terms, though standard in Japanese physical geography, are not attributed to any authority on this page; and that it is landform description only, with no dated settlement in it.

woods-westbrook-2006

Woods, Westbrook et al., "Hydrologic interactions between an alluvial fan and a slope wetland in the central Rocky Mountains, USA", Wetlands 2006 (https://www.cheriewestbrook.ca/publications/16523-hydrologic-interactions-between-an-alluvial-fan-and-a-slope-wetland-in-the-central-rocky-mountains-usa) - "Continuing ground-water discharge into the upper part of the wetland limited the water level declines"; stream seepage "is the primary source of ground-water recharge for the alluvial fan"

What it is: A peer-reviewed hydrology paper by Woods, Westbrook and colleagues, "Hydrologic interactions between an alluvial fan and a slope wetland in the central Rocky Mountains, USA", published in the journal Wetlands in 2006, which traces where the water in a wetland at the foot of an alluvial fan actually comes from.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for the mechanism behind a marsh drawn at the edge of a fan - groundwater discharging out of the fan keeps the wetland wet, and seepage from the stream is what recharges that groundwater - so the chain from stream to fan to marsh holds together on the map instead of each piece being placed by eye. Its limits are place and era: it measures one site in the Rocky Mountains in the 2000s, not an East Asian one, so we cite it for the physics, which does not change by country, and never for a measurement of a particular place. Only the abstract and the summary sections are on a public page, the full paper being behind a subscription.

kohai-shicchi-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 後背湿地 and 自然堤防 (with GSI notes) (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/後背湿地)

What it is: Japanese Wikipedia's article on 後背湿地 (kohai shicchi, the backswamp) read together with its companion article on 自然堤防 (the natural levee), explaining how a river builds raised banks along its own channel and leaves poorly drained, fine-grained lowland behind them, and how that lowland was farmed.

Why it applies, and its limits: The landform does not change with the century, so a modern description of it serves a premodern map directly, and we use it for what lies behind a river's bank - wet ground, traditionally worked as paddy, extended over time by draining marsh - which decides where our fields and our houses go on a floodplain. It is a tertiary summary, so we take the description of the landform and its traditional use and no number from it.

maff-drain-shape

MAFF, 排水路の形状・構造と適用条件 (技術書 20-23; https://www.maff.go.jp/j/nousin/noukan/tyotei/kizyun/pdf/04_hojou_hata_gijutsusho20-23.pdf)

What it is: A chapter of the Japanese Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries' land improvement technical standard, issued as a PDF, on the shape and structure of farm drainage channels and the conditions under which each is used. It gives the gradients such channels run at and how the interceptor, collector and outfall channels are laid out relative to the slope of the ground.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the layout of a drainage system on sloping upland fields - an interceptor channel laid nearly along the contours at a gentle gradient, and collectors falling at right angles to it at intervals of a few hundred meters - which follows from the fall of the land and is what our drained hem is drawn from. Its limits are that it is a present-day ministry design standard for upland fields created by modern land-forming earthworks; that the channel sections it standardizes are concrete drains, half-pipes and flumes, designed with tractor access in mind, so they have no premodern counterpart and only the layout and the gradient band transfer; and that its design gradients and sediment works are modern engineering.

mlit-senjochi-kurashi

MLIT land-classification teaching material, 「Alluvial fans and the lives of the inhabitants」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「扇状地と人々の暮らし」) (https://nlftp.mlit.go.jp/kokjo/inspect/landclassification/land/teaching_material_1.pdf)

What it is: A teaching document from the land-classification survey material of Japan's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism, "Alluvial fans and the lives of the inhabitants" (original: "扇状地と人々の暮らし"), explaining how an alluvial fan is built and how settlement and cultivation arrange themselves across it.

Why it applies, and its limits: It states the landform pattern our foothill maps follow - villages and paddies at the toe of the fan where water can be had, the dry middle of the fan given to crops that need little water, mulberry among them - in a ministry's own teaching material. Its limits are that it is modern educational material stating a general pattern rather than a study of any historical village; that the deck says water "can be obtained" at the toe and never speaks of springs, and says nothing about antiquity, so "the spring line" and "since ancient times" are the record's gloss and not the source's; and that its second half is about debris-flow hazard on fans, so the fan-life material is a few slides of a broader deck.

mineta-2007-tameike

嶺田拓也, 「Basic knowledge for ecosystem consideration (part 3): the flora of paddy fields and irrigation ponds」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「生態系配慮の基礎知識（その3）― 水田とため池の植物相 ―」), 農業農村工学会誌 (Journal of the JSIDRE) 75(8), 2007, pp. 745-748 (https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jjsidre2007/75/8/75_8_745/_pdf)

What it is: An article by Mineta Takuya in the Journal of the Japanese Society of Irrigation, Drainage and Rural Engineering (Japanese: 農業農村工学会誌) 75(8), 2007, part of a series on the ecological basics of farmland, dealing with the plants of paddy fields and of irrigation ponds.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the one source with numbers behind our rule that a farm pond's shallow shore is reeded, giving the zoning of reed and cattail by water depth and bank slope, and it is what shows that a working reservoir does not have a bare margin: a Kagawa study it reports found more emergent and floating-leaved species where ponds are dredged and their algae cut, and it is the ponds that have fallen out of use that lose their variety, so a working reservoir does not have a bare margin. It is a professional-society review reporting recent surveys of ponds still in use rather than primary evidence of its own or evidence about any earlier century, and the correlation it reports comes from a single prefecture, so we take a pattern of where plants stand from it and not a figure about how a premodern pond looked.

tameike-jawiki

ウィキペディア日本語版 ため池 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%81%9F%E3%82%81%E6%B1%A0)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article ため池 (tameike, "retention pond"), on the irrigation reservoirs that store water for rice fields across Japan - how they are built and managed, how the embankment is kept sound, and what grows on it as a result.

Why it applies, and its limits: It describes how a reservoir's embankment is kept - cultivation forbidden, the bank mown and burned, and the sun-loving grassland herbs that result - which the record offers as a confirmation of the rule that reeds keep off a raised, tended bank. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary, and the passage we use carries no citation on the page, so its weight comes from the prefectural and ministry sources cited beside it; that the practice is a maintenance regime with no date attached; and that the page never mentions reeds - it contrasts the tended bank's herbs with the tall grasses and kudzu of a neglected one - so "herbs, not reeds" is our inference from the regime and not the page's statement.
