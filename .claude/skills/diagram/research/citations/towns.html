Citations: The town tier: the research behind the layout, wall and market rules

The passages behind the footnotes of The town tier: the research behind the layout, wall and market rules - first the works they quote and why each one counts, then the notes themselves. Hover a footnote number on that page and the note appears beside it; the numbers here are the same ones.

The works cited on this page

song-architecture-enwiki

en.wikipedia Architecture of the Song dynasty (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture_of_the_Song_dynasty)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article "Architecture of the Song dynasty", a survey of Chinese building and city form in the tenth to thirteenth centuries, including the collapse of the walled-ward city plan that the Tang had inherited from earlier dynasties.

Why it applies, and its limits: It carries the one change that decides how a town map looks: a Tang city sorted its residents and its markets into separate walled wards, while a Song city let shops line the street in front of ordinary houses - so a settlement in our setting is drawn as one or the other, and the difference is visible at a glance. It is a tertiary summary, so we take this well-established structural contrast from it and check its own references before using any figure; and it is about China, which our setting draws on alongside Japan, so where the two diverge the Japanese sources govern.

guanxiang-zdic

漢典 zdic.net, 关厢 (https://zdic.net/hans/%E5%85%B3%E5%8E%A2)

What it is: The entry for 关厢 (guanxiang) in Zdic (漢典), an online Chinese character and word dictionary. It defines the word as the streets immediately outside a city gate and the area adjoining them.

Why it applies, and its limits: A dictionary is the right authority for what a word means, and what we take is the meaning: that Chinese had a settled term for the built-up strip outside the wall, which tells us such strips were a normal and named part of a walled city. That is all it supports - it says nothing about what stood there, and the commonly repeated claim that trade and inns concentrated in the guanxiang is not on this page and could not be read on any public page we reached, so nothing on our maps about what fills that strip rests on this source.

changan-enwiki

en.wikipedia Chang'an (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27an)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on Chang'an, the Han and Tang capital at modern Xi'an, which treats the two cities - built on different ground, centuries apart - as one subject. It describes the Tang city's walled residential wards and its two controlled markets opened and closed by drum and gong, the deliberately irregular rectangle of the Han city's outline, and the fact that Japan's ancient capitals at Nara and Kyoto were laid out in imitation of the Tang plan at a smaller scale.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives us both the walled ward as a real urban form and the historical bridge that lets a Chinese plan sit under a Japanese-flavored setting, since the Japanese capitals were consciously copied from this one. It is a tertiary summary, and it describes a planned imperial capital of extraordinary size, so it anchors form and not scale - never a size or a count for the places we draw. Two cautions: the irregular outline it describes is the Han city's, so it says nothing about whether a Tang or later wall was square; and the number of Tang wards is a figure the page gives only as "about 110", which is what our text follows.

jokamachi-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 城下町 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%9F%8E%E4%B8%8B%E7%94%BA)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on 城下町 (jokamachi, the castle town). It describes how such a town was zoned around its castle, with samurai quarters ranked by their distance from it, merchant and artisan quarters beyond them and a temple district on the outer edge, and how streets were deliberately bent into right-angle dog-legs and stopped with dead ends to lengthen an attacker's approach.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the source of the zoning and the street tricks that give our towns and cities their shape, and it states in one place what scattered town histories show one town at a time. It is a tertiary summary describing the castle town as a general type, so it gives us the pattern rather than any single town's plan, and Japanese castle towns were an Edo-period form, which is later than parts of the setting our maps also draw on.

kichinyado-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 木賃宿 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%9C%A8%E8%B3%83%E5%AE%BF)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the kichin-yado (木賃宿), the cheapest class of Japanese inn, whose name means "firewood-fee lodging" - guests brought their own rice and paid only for the fuel to cook it.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the cheapest kind of inn - the firewood-fee lodging on the edge of a post town, a common room, guests bringing their own bedding and rice and paying for the wood - which is what puts such an inn on our town maps. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary that carries the encyclopedia's own notice that it lacks sources; and that its passage about the inns spreading into a city's poor quarter, laborers and the unregistered sleeping a tatami apiece, describes the Meiji-era doss house after the post-station system ended, so the common room and the firewood fee are Edo while the poor-quarter crowding is a later condition being read back into a premodern town.

kichinyado-kotobank

コトバンク 木賃宿 (https://kotobank.jp/word/木賃宿-51029)

What it is: A page on kotobank, a Japanese reference site that reprints the entries several published dictionaries and encyclopedias give for a single word, gathering here the entries for 木賃宿 (kichinyado), the cheapest class of travelers' lodging in Edo-period Japan, where a guest paid a small charge for firewood and cooked their own food. The works reprinted on it include Shogakukan's Nipponica and Seisenban Nihon Kokugo Daijiten, Yamakawa's concise dictionary of Japanese history, and Heibonsha's revised Sekai Dai-hyakka Jiten.

Why it applies, and its limits: It tells us what the cheapest bed in a post town cost and who slept in one - a self-catering inn where the lodger paid for firewood and brought his own rice - which is what puts such an inn on a town map and tells a reader what kind of place it was. Its limits are that these are tertiary summaries of ordinances and records not themselves on the page, so only the dated figures they state can be taken; that the three prices - 3 mon under a 1611 ordinance, a 1658 figure, 24 mon in Edo in 1843 - are two centuries apart across a period in which the coin itself changed value, so they measure monetary change as much as the price of a bed and must never be averaged into one rate; and that the 1843 figure is the shogunal capital's, a different scale of place from the county seat we draw.

hutong-enwiki

en.wikipedia Hutong (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutong)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article "Hutong", on the alleys of Beijing that are formed by lines of siheyuan, the walled courtyard houses of the traditional city, and on how such neighborhoods grew.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for one structural point that shapes how our town blocks are laid out - the lane is the space left between rows of walled courtyard houses, and a quarter grows by joining one courtyard to the next and then one lane to the next, with smaller passages threading between. Its limits: an encyclopedia article is a tertiary summary, so we take only general description that its own references support and no figure of our own from it; and it is about Beijing, an imperial capital, so the pattern carries over to our towns while the scale and density do not.

chinese-city-wall-enwiki

en.wikipedia Chinese city wall (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_city_wall)

What it is: The English Wikipedia survey article on Chinese city walls: their construction from a rammed-earth core faced in brick or stone, their surviving examples at Nanjing, Xi'an and Kaifeng, and their plan, rectangular for capitals and important northern cities but irregular in broken country, where the line followed the terrain.

Why it applies, and its limits: It supports the shape rule our town walls obey, that a wall is a costly thing pulled tight around what must actually be defended and bent by the ground, rather than a tidy circle drawn around empty space. It is a tertiary summary covering the whole country over two thousand years, it gives no costs and no dimensions tied to a settlement's rank, and the economic argument our text makes from the shape is our own reasoning rather than the article's.

thepaper-city-walls

澎湃新闻, on the plan forms of Chinese city walls (https://m.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_1270378)

What it is: 圆形、方形、卵形？ ("Round, square, oval? What shape were China's ancient cities"), a 2014 article by the historical geographer Cheng Yinong (成一农) in 澎湃新闻 (The Paper), a Shanghai-based news outlet, surveying the plan forms that Chinese walled cities actually took and why they took them.

Why it applies, and its limits: It supplies the rule our walled outlines follow: capitals laid out in advance were essentially square, while cities in hill and mountain country had to bring the commanding high ground inside the wall for defense and so ended up markedly irregular, Beijing's 凸-shaped plan among the named examples. It is a popular article by a specialist rather than a peer-reviewed paper, and it generalizes over the whole span of imperial China at once, so we take the principle and its named examples from it rather than any measurement.

kyoto-machiya-isan

京都市 (City of Kyoto), 京都遺産, 京町家とその暮らしの文化 (https://kyoto-bunkaisan.city.kyoto.lg.jp/kyotoisan/nintei-theme/kyoumachiya.html)

What it is: The City of Kyoto's own heritage description of the Kyo-machiya, the Kyoto townhouse: the earth-floored passage running from the street to the back of the plot, the working front where customers are received, the cooking place behind it, and named surviving houses of the type.

Why it applies, and its limits: It states as a principle the thing our shop-house bands are built on, work and dwelling in one building, and it says which part of the plan is shop and which is home, so a merchant family living behind its own storefront is no longer general reading. Its limits are that it is a municipal heritage page rather than a study; that the Kyo-machiya is the most developed and most urban form of the type, so an ordinary county-seat shop would be plainer, smaller and less deep; and that it gives no dimensions a map could use.

dachedian-jilin-daily

王爽, "消失的大车店" (The vanished dachedian), 吉林日报, 25 April 2026 (http://jlrbszb.dajilin.com/pad/paper/c/202604/25/content_1049361.html)

What it is: A descriptive article in the Jilin Daily, a Chinese provincial newspaper, on the dachedian - the inn that took in passing carts and draft animals on the roads of the northeast - written partly from the author's memory of staying in one as a child, and describing the yard, the stable and its trough, the grooms' lean-to, the well and the yard wall.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the closest attested analogue to the caravan inn our town maps draw, and the only account read that describes the whole arrangement rather than just naming the institution; it is also what contradicts the drawn inn's second story, since it says the buildings in the yard were uniformly single-story. Its limits are that it is journalism and personal recollection rather than a survey; that it describes a twentieth-century survival in Manchuria rather than a Ming or Edo inn, and in the north rather than in the rice-growing south this setting also leans on; and that it counts nothing - no animals, no rooms, no dimensions - so every size on our sheet remains ours.

neixiang-xianya-zhwiki

内乡县衙 (the Neixiang County yamen), Chinese Wikipedia (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%86%85%E4%B9%A1%E5%8E%BF%E8%A1%99)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on the county yamen at Neixiang in Henan, the only complete Qing-dynasty county government office still standing, describing its layout and the regulation and ritual conception that layout follows.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the fallback orientation of our magistrate's manor as a rule rather than a preference - the seat sits to the north and faces south, by the regulation for a local government office and by ritual propriety - and the terms behind it mean proper rather than lucky, so the orientation is the formal one and not an auspicious one. Its limits are that it is a tertiary encyclopedia article; that it describes one surviving building of one dynasty; and that it is Chinese office regulation standing in for a setting drawn from pre-Meiji Japan as well as from imperial China.

okabe-hatago-jawiki

大旅籠柏屋 (the Ohatago Kashibaya, the preserved lodging house of Okabe-juku), Japanese Wikipedia (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%A4%A7%E6%97%85%E7%B1%A0%E6%9F%8F%E5%B1%8B)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on a lodging house of the Tokaido post station at Okabe, in Fujieda, Shizuoka - a rebuilding thought from an 1835 cost record to date from about 1836, registered as a national tangible cultural property in 1998 and open today as a museum. It gives the building's construction and its rooms floor by floor: two stories of wood under a tile roof, guest rooms above, and the main parlor, the kitchen and the proprietor family's own living space below.

Why it applies, and its limits: It settles that a highway inn of two stories is a real Japanese building and not an invention of this project's drawing. Its limits are the reason it does not settle the drawing. Nothing read describes a cart yard, a long stable, a feeding trough or a grooms' lean-to at a lodging house of this kind - this article describes the building and its rooms and says nothing of a yard at all - so it is no evidence for the fittings the wagon inn our maps draw is built around, and an analogue for a different institution. It is one building, and an exceptional one selected by its own survival: 大旅籠 names it the great hatago of its town, and its proprietors ran a pawnshop beside the inn and served as post-station officers, so it is the leading house of the street rather than the ordinary one on it. Its date, about 1836, is the very end of the period this setting draws on, and the station system it belongs to is that institution in its mature nineteenth-century form. It is a tertiary encyclopedia article rather than a survey, and the registration record it summarizes was not read. Nothing read says inns of its kind were usually two stories. And its ground-floor room list mixes the building's historical rooms with its present museum fabric - an exhibition room stands in the same list as the parlor and the kitchen, and the upper floor the citation rests on holds mannequins of two literary travelers at dinner.

kaogongji-wikisource

《周禮·冬官考工記》 (the Zhouli, Kaogongji chapter), Chinese Wikisource transcription (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%91%A8%E7%A6%AE/%E5%86%AC%E5%AE%98%E8%80%83%E5%B7%A5%E8%A8%98)

What it is: The artificers' chapter of the Rites of Zhou, the classical Chinese text that prescribes the ideal capital - nine li square, three gates a side, nine avenues each way, the court before the market - in a Wikisource transcription of the primary text.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the primary source for the square ideal every discussion of Chinese city plans starts from, quoted rather than paraphrased. Its limit is that it is an ideal, compiled long before the cities our maps are modeled on, and says nothing about how far real cities followed it - which is why the essay beside it is cited for the shape's actual cause.

chengchi-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 城池 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/城池)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on the chengchi, the walled city and its moat, with the gate counts of prefectural and county seats; an encyclopedia article.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the usual gate count by tier and what stood inside the wall, which sets this page's few grand gates; a tertiary summary.

kotobank-umayado

Seisenban Nihon Kokugo Daijiten (Shogakukan) and Digital Daijisen (Shogakukan), s.v. 馬宿, aggregated at kotobank (https://kotobank.jp/word/%E9%A6%AC%E5%AE%BF)

What it is: Two Shogakukan dictionaries shown together on kotobank for 馬宿 (umayado). Both define one sense of the word as an inn that takes a traveling guest's own horse into its keeping, or an inn with the facilities to do so; the Nihon Kokugo Daijiten, as a historical dictionary does, cites the word's earliest attested example for that sense - a sentence from Mori Ōgai's Nagashi of 1913 describing an inn with a travelers' lodging in front and a large stable behind.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is what the record has for whether a Japanese inn may honestly stand with a stable behind it the way the town maps draw, and it carries two claims of very different weight. The DEFINITION carries the institution: an inn that took a traveler's horse into its keeping was a named kind of place. Only the 1913 sentence carries the arrangement - inn in front, stable behind - and one sentence of fiction is the weakest positional evidence this record uses. Its date is Taisho, after the railway had begun displacing packhorse traffic, so it is an analogue for a premodern arrangement rather than a description of one; and the dictionary's marking it the word's earliest attested example cuts against antiquity, not for it - the compilers found no earlier written use of the sense, so this page does not establish that the word or the arrangement is of the Edo period. It says nothing of a cart yard, a feeding trough, grooms' quarters or any dimension, and nothing of the inn's story count. It is Japanese only; the wagon-inn analogue on the other side of the same knob owes it nothing. This entry does not rest on the closest physical evidence there is - a national cultural-property entry for an 1801 farmhouse in the mountains of northern Japan registered as a 馬宿, with a two-stall stable and a fodder hearth - and the same kotobank page also carries an encyclopedia passage on a homonymous place, a 馬宿 district in western Japan that was an ancient post station and not an inn, which corroborates nothing here.

jishi-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 集市 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/集市)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on the periodic rural market, its origins and its lodgings for traveling merchants; an encyclopedia article.

Why it applies, and its limits: It attests the merchants' inn-and-warehouse and the distance economics that made periodic markets, which are the second grade of lodging and the market catchment this page draws; a Tang-Song institution rather than a county seat's inn.

yashikirin-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 屋敷林 (居久根 redirects here) (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/屋敷林)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 屋敷林 (yashikirin, "homestead grove"), a tertiary encyclopedia entry on the belts of trees Japanese farmhouses planted around themselves against wind and snow, covering the regional names for the same thing - the northeastern igune (居久根) and the Izumo tsuijimatsu among them - along with the species used and the sides of the house they stand on.

Why it applies, and its limits: This is the backbone of how we plant a farmstead: which sides carry the grove (north and west against the wind, the east left open for the entrance and its ornamental garden), which trees make it up (sugi, matsu, hinoki and keyaki), and the part that turns a fact into a choice - that the Izumo form once enclosed the whole circumference of a house and was later cut back to a hook covering only the north and west, which is why our maps treat a full ring and a two-sided belt as two correct answers rather than one. It is a tertiary summary rather than a survey, so it gives us forms and species names and not counts or measurements, and the practice is Japanese and regional, the north-and-west rule following a wind that a warmer corner of the setting need not share.

machiya-shoka-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 町屋 (商家) (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%94%BA%E5%B1%8B_(%E5%95%86%E5%AE%B6))

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the machiya as a merchant house: the shop at the street front, the plot behind it, and the back-lane tenement entered through a wooden gate set between two front shops, with the number of tenement rows following the width of the shop's frontage.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the depth rule from the other end - what a plot's frontage buys is one row along the lane or two facing rows, never a third rank behind them - which is the historical reason our rows stack at most two deep, and it puts the single entrance where the map puts it. Its limits are that it is tertiary; that it describes the Japanese town house of Kyoto and Edo rather than the Chinese courtyard housing the setting also draws on; and that the frontage figures are typical rather than surveyed.

kotobank-uradana

Digital Daijisen and Kaitei Shinpan Sekai Dai-hyakka Jiten, s.v. 裏店, on kotobank (https://kotobank.jp/word/裏店-35109)

What it is: Kotobank's reproduction of the dictionary and encyclopedia entries on the uradana, the back-street tenement of the Edo town and its occupants.

Why it applies, and its limits: It defines the tenement as standing on a back street or behind a merchant house and names its occupants, which is the lower-status housing this page lines its back lanes with; dictionary lines.

caoshi-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 草市 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/草市)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on the caoshi, the commercial quarter that grew outside Chinese city walls from the Song; an encyclopedia article.

Why it applies, and its limits: It attests the market outside the wall and its descent from the rural periodic market, which is the gate market this page draws; a tertiary summary.

lifang-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 里坊制 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%87%8C%E5%9D%8A%E5%88%B6)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on the lifang system, the walled and gated residential wards of the Chinese city from the Han to the Tang: how the wards were closed at night, how the Song did away with the wards, the markets and the curfew together, and that the system did not return after the Song and Yuan.

Why it applies, and its limits: It dates the end of the walled ward, which is why the Chinese half of our block-gate argument turns on palings across streets rather than ward walls. It is a tertiary summary used for the dating, with the essayist Wu Gou's account of the same change beside it.

kotobank-hinomi-yagura

Nihon Dai-hyakka Zensho and Seisenban Nihon Kokugo Daijiten, s.v. 火の見櫓, on kotobank (https://kotobank.jp/word/火の見櫓-120180)

What it is: Kotobank's reproduction of the encyclopedia and dictionary entries on the hinomi-yagura, the fire-watch tower, its bell code and its Edo distribution.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the tower's height and bell and the one-tower-per-ten-blocks rule beside the block posts' ladders, which is the split between state and neighborhood watch this page now states; reference entries with no plan.

jishinban-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 自身番 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E8%87%AA%E8%BA%AB%E7%95%AA)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the jishinban, the Edo-period neighborhood watch house: its patrol and reporting duties, the ladder on the roof - which the article itself calls a small-scale fire tower - and the bell beside it, and the arrest and firefighting tools kept inside, down to the standard, the hook, the hand pump, the buckets and the ladders.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the source that puts the bell, the ladder and the gear at the TOWN's watch post, which is where our unwalled-seat fire posture places them and which no page had previously supported. Its limits are that it is tertiary and specific to Edo-period Japanese towns, and that it attaches all of it to the neighborhood watch house and nothing at all to an intendant's office, which is the half of our posture that stays unsupported.

edo-no-kaji-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 江戸の火事 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%B1%9F%E6%88%B8%E3%81%AE%E7%81%AB%E4%BA%8B)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the fires of Edo and the shogunate's fire-prevention measures: the great fires, the firebreaks, and the ordinances restricting bathhouse hours, fireworks and refuse burning.

Why it applies, and its limits: It records fire ordinances restricting fire-using trades inside the dense town, the fire law this page's kiln siting rests on. A tertiary summary; no passage names kilns.

kotobank-miyaji-shibai

Digital Daijisen and Seisenban Nihon Kokugo Daijiten, s.v. 宮地芝居, on kotobank (https://kotobank.jp/word/宮地芝居-1157374)

What it is: Kotobank's reproduction of the dictionary entries on miyaji-shibai, the plays licensed by occasion in shrine and temple precincts.

Why it applies, and its limits: It defines the precinct play, which is the stage this page gives a town; dictionary lines with nothing on the stage's orientation.

edo-sanza-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 江戸三座 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%B1%9F%E6%88%B8%E4%B8%89%E5%BA%A7)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 江戸三座 (the three theaters of Edo), on the playhouses licensed to stage kabuki by the Edo town magistrate's office from the middle of the period onward. It records that the city once had a great many playhouses, which were consolidated to four and finally to three.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives us the institution we need for a town map - that a theater is a licensed establishment, that the magistracy decided how many there were, and that the number ended up very small, capped because a big timber hall in a townhouse block was a fire the city could not fight - which is why a settlement gets one playhouse or none rather than a row of them. Its limits are that an encyclopedia is a tertiary summary, and this article carries the encyclopedia's own warning that its sources are unclear; that the licensing was an Edo city policy under one government, so we take the arrangement rather than the number itself; and that the count is dated - four houses by the 1670s, three only from 1714 - so "three" is not true of the whole period.
