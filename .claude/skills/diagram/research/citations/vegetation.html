The works cited on this page

forests-2020

Chen, Lin, Zhang et al., "Village Fengshui Forests as Forms of Cultural and Ecological Heritage: Interpretations and Conservation Policy Implications from Southern China", Forests 11(12):1286 (2020) (https://www.mdpi.com/1999-4907/11/12/1286; DOI 10.3390/f11121286; the page is public) - academic-sources/forests-11-01286.pdf) - read from the GM's downloaded copy in academic-sources/ (mdpi.com refuses automated fetches

What it is: "Village Fengshui Forests as Forms of Cultural and Ecological Heritage: Interpretations and Conservation Policy Implications from Southern China" by Jianling Chen, Weiming Lin, Yaoqi Zhang and colleagues, a peer-reviewed open-access paper in the journal Forests (2020). It reports a 2019 field survey of seven villages, one in each town of Youxi County in Fujian, with interviews of forestry staff, fengshui masters, village officials and clan heads, on what the groves contain, why they survived, and what protecting them would take.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is peer-reviewed work about the exact feature we draw. The figure of roughly two fengshui forests per village that we take from it is not the paper's own measurement: it quotes it, for Fujian province as a whole, from a 2012 Chinese study we have not read, so the number reaches us at second hand. It does not give us grove sizes: its own table records whole-village forest areas only, and it counts no trees or canopies, so the per-grove areas on our maps are labeled guesses. It is also a small interview-based study of the groves that survived in one county of one province, so it describes a remnant landscape rather than the ordinary one of several centuries ago, and China stands in for a setting modeled as much on Japan.

hu-2011-fengshui-patches

Hu, Li, Guo et al., "Values of village fengshui forest patches in biodiversity conservation in the Pearl River Delta, China", Biological Conservation 144 (2011) (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0006320711000450; the full text is paywalled: doi 10.1016/j.biocon.2011.01.023; unsupported by any readable page until then) - the ABSTRACT is public on that page and was read - 3403 plants/ha, 49.1 m2/ha, 32 well-protected patches, one 1200 m² transect each

What it is: A peer-reviewed article by Hu, Li, Guo and colleagues, "Values of village fengshui forest patches in biodiversity conservation in the Pearl River Delta, China", in Biological Conservation volume 144 (2011). The authors surveyed thirty-two well-protected village fengshui forest patches, sampling one 1,200 square meter transect in each, and report the species richness, stem density and basal area they found.

Why it applies, and its limits: A fengshui forest is the protected wood a village keeps at its back, which is exactly the feature our homestead and village groves are modeled on, and this is a direct measurement of how thickly such a grove actually stands (about 3,400 stems and 49.1 square meters of basal area per hectare, which the authors themselves put on a level with well developed evergreen broadleaf forest). Its limits: only the abstract is on a public page, so we take nothing beyond the figures the abstract states; the survey is of patches that survive in one region of southern China in the twenty-first century, and choosing the well-protected ones means these are the healthiest examples rather than an average grove; and southern China is not Japan.

coggins-minor-2018

Chris Coggins and Jesse Minor, "Fengshui Forests as A Socio-Natural Reservoir in the Face of Climate Change and Environmental Transformation", Asia Pacific Perspectives 15:2 (2018) (https://jayna.usfca.edu/asia-pacific-perspectives/pdfs/1-coggins-minor-fengshui-forests.pdf) - read from the PDF itself (pages 1-20) after the fetcher could not decode its text stream.

What it is: Chris Coggins and Jesse Minor, "Fengshui Forests as A Socio-Natural Reservoir in the Face of Climate Change and Environmental Transformation", a peer-reviewed article in Asia Pacific Perspectives 15:2 (2018). It is a field study of the village woodlands of southeastern China, mapping where each grove sits in relation to its village and arguing that these small protected forests carry an outsized ecological and social weight.

Why it applies, and its limits: It establishes what a village grove is: not a belt around the settlement but a set of separate patches at particular positions, behind the village on its hill, at the water head and the water mouth and in the mountain gaps, with the paddies lying open in front. It is a modern study of the groves that have survived to be surveyed, in one part of China, so it records the pattern rather than a full premodern inventory, and it stands for a practice our maps also give to Japanese-flavored villages, where the same siting logic is assumed rather than separately attested.

What it is: The English Wikipedia article "Fengshui woodland", a short encyclopedia entry on the patches of woodland kept behind and around villages in southern China and Hong Kong for geomantic reasons, giving the Chinese term 風水林 and some regional totals.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for one thing only - that the village grove is a recognized, named class of woodland rather than a decoration we invented. It is a tertiary summary whose figures we take only where its own references support them, and it carries no count of groves per village and no grove area at all, so the sizes and numbers on our maps come from the survey papers and, where those run out, are marked as guesses.

nougyoudoboku-keihan

のうぎょうとぼく, 水田圃場における畦畔について (https://nougyoudoboku.com/a-ridge-between-rice-fields/)

What it is: "On bunds in paddy fields" (水田圃場における畦畔について), an article published in December 2022 and revised since on のうぎょうとぼく ("Agriculture and Me"), a Japanese site about agricultural civil engineering written under the name Oimoya by a former prefectural officer in that field. It explains what a bund is and what it does - holding the water in, dividing the plots, giving a footway for weeding and spraying, growing fodder grass - and tabulates the standard cross-section: a trapezoid with a 1:1 batter, 30 cm high and 30 cm across the top, with heavier sections where the drop between plots is large and in cold regions.

Why it applies, and its limits: Those dimensions set the width of every bund we draw and the grassy strip on top of it, and they come from a trained agricultural engineer rather than a general-interest writer. The honest limit is era and method: this is a present-day design standard for consolidated, machine-worked paddy, published on a specialist's own site rather than in a reviewed journal, so we use it as the shape a soil bund naturally takes for the job, which a hand-built one also had to do, and not as a measurement of a premodern field.

What it is: Aomori Prefecture's standard drawings for consolidated paddy (2017 revision), a public-works design standard: the outer bund drawn as a trapezoid 30 cm high above the higher field, 30 cm wide at the crest, with side slopes of 1:1 to 1:1.2, in three types by the difference in level between the fields it separates, up to 4 m, with berms on the tallest.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives an engineered cross-section for a paddy bund in numbers, which calibrates the most repeated line on a paddy map against the one to two feet the period sources give. Its limits are that it is a post-war land-consolidation standard for machine-formed embankments between fields whose surfaces may differ by meters, a different object from a hand-piled ridge between two level plots, so its figures are a modern engineered minimum and not a measurement of a premodern bund; that a hand-piled bund would have been less regular; and that the enlarged cold-region bund an enlarged cold-region bund of about 50 by 40 cm is not on the Aomori sheet - the berms on it are for tall bunds, and its only frost-heave provision concerns a flume's subgrade - and the other standard the entry names is neither linked nor quoted, so that figure stands as a guess.

pmc7538448-levee

"Earthworm species and density in semi-natural grasslands on rice paddy levees in Japanese satoyama", PMC 7538448 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7538448/)

What it is: A research paper available through PubMed Central, "Earthworm species and density in semi-natural grasslands on rice paddy levees in Japanese satoyama", which counts earthworms in the grassy banks between paddy fields and, in describing its study ground, sets out what those levees are and how farmers keep them.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is fieldwork on Japanese paddies, and it states plainly what a levee is for - holding the water in and carrying the farmer and their tools between fields - and that the banks are kept as mown grass, which is exactly the paddy edge our maps draw. Its limits are that it is a modern study whose real subject is soil ecology rather than farming history, and that its account of upkeep includes present-day herbicide use, so we take from it the levee's purpose and its cut grassy surface and not the details of modern management.

phyllostachys-enwiki

en.wikipedia Phyllostachys bambusoides (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phyllostachys_bambusoides)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on Phyllostachys bambusoides, the Japanese timber bamboo known as madake, covering the plant itself and the things traditionally made from its culms and sheaths.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the traditional uses of the great timber bamboo - baskets, fans, food wrappings and clog coverings, the shakuhachi - which is what makes a bamboo grove on a map a working part of a farm rather than scenery. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary whose uses section rests on a single citation, with the encyclopedia's own request for citations on it, so the uses are taken as common knowledge rather than as referenced findings; that the uses are undated, and the article notes that the modern clog trade has moved to another species; and that it says nothing about how big a grove was.

bamboo-growers-hardiness

Grower pages on madake hardiness - completebamboo.com and two others (https://completebamboo.com/species_bamboo/Phyllostachys_bambusoides.html)

What it is: Species pages on the cold tolerance of madake, the timber bamboo: completebamboo.com, an unsigned reference site on bamboo carrying affiliate advertising, and two further pages the record names only as such. They give the temperature at which the species is damaged - about -15 C, or a USDA hardiness zone of 7 - with no method or source stated.

Why it applies, and its limits: A plant's cold tolerance is a constant of the species, so a present-day statement of the temperature at which madake dies bears on a premodern grove in a way a present-day yield would not, and that is the only thing we take from these pages: the northern limit of a bamboo grove on our maps, kept as a spread because the pages disagree. Their limits are that none states a method or a source; that the hardiness zone is a North American classification for ornamental planting, not a statement about managed Japanese stands; and that two of the three pages behind the key are unnamed and cannot be read, so the key rests on one reference site.

mlit-vegetation-classes

MLIT/NILIM river-environment vegetation classes (https://www.nilim.go.jp/lab/fbg/ksnkankyo/mizukokuweb/system/maegaki.files/shiryo2.pdf)

What it is: The vegetation-class reference list of the national river-environment survey run by the National Institute for Land and Infrastructure Management, part of Japan's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism, which defines each plant community the survey records and the ground it grows on.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it to decide what to draw at a wet margin, and it names the communities and their habitats plainly: reed in the lower reaches, the tachiyanagi willow association on the sand and mud deposits of lower rivers, and the alder community on waterlogged ground. It is a modern survey's classification key, so it records what grows along Japanese rivers now, after centuries of embanking and channel work; it gives habitats and not densities or sizes; and it says nothing about how inhabitants cut or used any of these plants.

lou-2016-floodplain-zones

Yanjing Lou, Yanwen Pan, Chuanyu Gao, Ming Jiang, Xianguo Lu and Y. Jun Xu, "Response of Plant Height, Species Richness and Aboveground Biomass to Flooding Gradient along Vegetation Zones in Floodplain Wetlands, Northeast China", PLOS ONE (2016) (https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0153972)

What it is: A 2016 research article in PLOS ONE by Lou and colleagues, "Response of Plant Height, Species Richness and Aboveground Biomass to Flooding Gradient along Vegetation Zones in Floodplain Wetlands, Northeast China". It measured three herbaceous marsh communities on the Sanjiang Plain and sorted them by how much of the year their ground stands under water: sedge marsh at the streamside, meadow marsh on the middle terraces, and wet meadow grass on the terraces that flood only occasionally.

Why it applies, and its limits: The ladder it measures - which plants hold the ground that floods every spring, which the ground that floods for part of the season, which the ground that floods rarely - is a physical response to water depth rather than a modern agricultural fact, so it is good evidence for what greens the wet margins of a floodplain on our maps. Two honest limits: it is a present-day ecological survey of one plain in northeastern China, not of Japan, and it is entirely about sedges and grasses, so it says nothing whatever about pine or any other tree and cannot be read as evidence about a tree's tolerance of soggy ground.

gymnosperm-densiflora

The Gymnosperm Database, Pinus densiflora (https://www.conifers.org/pi/Pinus_densiflora.php) with the Mt. Takao museum page

What it is: Two modern botanical descriptions of Pinus densiflora, the Japanese red pine: the species page in the Gymnosperm Database, a long-running online reference on the world's conifers, together with the species page from the Takao 599 Museum, the natural history museum at the foot of Mount Takao outside Tokyo. Both describe where the tree grows - rocky and sandy slopes, poor and dry soils, from sea level up into the hills.

Why it applies, and its limits: A species' tolerances are a fact of the species, so a modern description bounds what a premodern map may stand a pine on: red pine is a tree of dry, well-drained and disturbed ground, never a reed edge, and both pages also say what a human landscape does with it - the museum page that it is often planted for gardens and windbreaks, the database that its spread in Japan tracked the rise of agriculture. The limits are that the Gymnosperm Database is one editor's compilation rather than a peer-reviewed flora, though it cites its literature; that the habitat sentence our footnote quotes describes the species at its Russian northern edge rather than in Japan; and that neither page is a historical source, so how a village managed its pines rests elsewhere.

satoyama-enwiki

en.wikipedia Satoyama (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satoyama)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on satoyama, the managed border zone between the wooded hills and the farmed flat in Japan, covering its mosaic of woodland, paddy, dry field, grassland and ponds and the ways villagers worked it.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the definition of the managed belt between village and mountain, the mosaic of land uses in it, and two practices our maps draw - leaf litter gathered for the paddies and the coppice cut on a cycle. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary flagged as still being translated from the Japanese article; that the one Edo-period statement we lean on, the leaf gathering, carries no citation in the article; that the fifteen-to-twenty-year cutting cycle is stated in the present tense and cited to a 2017 ecology paper about present-day management, so a premodern rotation is being read from a modern study of a traditional practice; and that the word itself is largely a twentieth-century coinage, which is our knowledge rather than the page's.

jiao-2019-satoyama

Yuanmei Jiao, Yinping Ding, Zhiqin Zha and Toshiya Okuro, "Crises of Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services in Satoyama Landscape of Japan: A Review on the Role of Management", Sustainability 11(2):454 (2019) (https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/11/2/454)

What it is: A peer-reviewed review article by Yuanmei Jiao, Yinping Ding and Zhiqin Zha of Yunnan Normal University with Toshiya Okuro of the University of Tokyo, in Sustainability (2019), gathering the published work on how Japan's satoyama landscape was managed and what happened to its plants and animals once that management stopped. Its middle section tabulates the traditional handling of each part of the landscape, from coppice woods and grasslands to thatch fields, paddies and ponds, and what each of them yielded to the households that worked it.

Why it applies, and its limits: It states the practice we draw: farmers cut, coppiced, mowed and carried off the fallen leaves on a repeating round, and the woodland floor stayed light enough for grasses and spring flowers precisely because the litter went to the fields instead of lying where it fell. Because it is a review, what it gives us is the settled picture rather than a first-hand measurement: the individual figures belong to the studies it cites, its "traditional" means Japan before the 1960s rather than the Edo period alone, and the proof that the management keeps the floor open comes from modern ecological surveys of the woods that survive, so the practice is old while the measuring of it is recent. The later half of the paper is about industrialized farming, with chemical fertilizer, consolidated paddies and concrete channels, and has no bearing on these settlements.

takeuchi-2010-satoyama

Kazuhiko Takeuchi, "Rebuilding the relationship between people and nature: the Satoyama Initiative", Ecological Research 25 (2010) 891-897 (https://esj-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1007/s11284-010-0745-8)

What it is: A peer-reviewed article by Kazuhiko Takeuchi of the University of Tokyo and the United Nations University, in Ecological Research (2010), setting out the Satoyama Initiative: what the Japanese satoyama landscape is, how villagers worked it, and why it has come apart since the 1960s. Its opening pages give the definition the rest of the field uses - satoyama in the narrow sense as the secondary woodland and grassland beside a settlement, held in common and worked as coppice and meadow for fuel, fertilizer and fodder, and in the broad sense as the whole mosaic of woods, dry fields, paddies, canals, ponds and houses together.

Why it applies, and its limits: The land-use system it defines is the one our villages sit inside, and the author dates its fully wood-and-compost-dependent form to nineteenth-century Japan, before fossil fuels or chemical fertilizer reached the countryside. It is a modern policy review rather than a reading of period records, so we take the definition and the shape of the system from it and no measurement - it carries no cutting cycle or yield we could use, and the litter and the rotation on our maps rest on the 2019 review of satoyama management cited with it. It also corrects the pretty picture in a way worth knowing when reading one of our maps: the same author records that late Edo and early Meiji satoyama was often overused, denuded and eroding, so a well-kept coppice belt is the good case and not the average one.

uehara-2009-agris

Uehara et al. 2009, on Rhododendron in the abandoned satoyama coppice forest (AGRIS record) (https://agris.fao.org/search/fr/records/64724aabe17b74d2224f9430)

What it is: The bibliographic record, in the FAO's international agricultural database AGRIS, for Uehara, Shigematsu, Fujii and Iwamoto, "Succession of shrub-layer vegetation and situation of wild Rhododendron in the abandoned Satoyama coppice forest," Landscape Research Japan 72(5), 2009, pp. 511-516, with the paper's abstract. The study compares a coppice woodland in Hyogo Prefecture cut twenty-five years earlier with one left alone for fifty, counting 42 plant species and 13 floor herbs in the managed stand against 23 species and 3 floor herbs in the neglected one.

Why it applies, and its limits: This is our evidence that a managed coppice has an open, flowering, species-rich floor under a cut canopy while an abandoned one goes dark and bare, which is the difference between a village woodlot we draw as worked ground and a thicket - and it supplies the fifty-year figure for how long neglect takes to show. What we read is the database record and its abstract rather than the full Japanese article, and it is a single paired comparison in one prefecture measured in the 2000s, so we use its direction and rough magnitude, not its species counts, as a general rule about coppice woodland.

waldrand-dewiki

de.wikipedia Waldrand (https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waldrand)

What it is: The German Wikipedia article on the Waldrand, the forest edge, which sets out the structure of a fully grown one: from the open field inward, a herb fringe, then a belt of shrubs, then the forest mantle of trees whose crowns sweep down low on the field side.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for that three-layer profile, which is how we draw the boundary between woodland and cultivated ground as a graded edge rather than a hard line. It is a tertiary summary, so we take from it only what its own references support; it is also describing central European woodland, and it was consulted because the English-language article on woodland edges did not carry the layer terms - the layering is a general property of a sunlit forest margin, but the species that would fill each layer in East Asia are not taken from here.

pmc7898781-fukugi

"Distribution and utilization of homestead windbreak Fukugi trees", PMC 7898781 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7898781/)

What it is: A research paper available through PubMed Central, "Distribution and utilization of homestead windbreak Fukugi trees", a survey of the fukugi trees planted in strips around houses in the villages of Okinawa, which the authors trace to the Ryukyu Kingdom of roughly three hundred years ago and to the feng shui planning of its rural landscapes.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a survey of a surviving, dated example of the thing we plant on our maps - a maintained belt of trees set around a homestead to break the wind - so the form is attested rather than assumed. The limits are of place and of time: Okinawa is subtropical, and neither its tree species nor its Ryukyuan siting rules are those of the main Japanese islands or of imperial China, so we take the form and not the species or the spacing; and the trees measured today are the survivors of a much older planting, which makes the modern distribution an incomplete record of the old one.

woodland-edge-enwiki

en.wikipedia "Woodland edge" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodland_edge) - describes the edge generically ("trees are often different from those inside the wood, as well as hedge vegetation, brambles and low-growing plants") and does NOT carry the herb-fringe / shrub-belt / forest-mantle layers

What it is: The English Wikipedia article "Woodland edge", a short tertiary encyclopedia entry describing in general terms how the trees and plants at the margin of a wood differ from those growing inside it.

Why it applies, and its limits: This one is cited for an absence rather than for a figure. The layered vocabulary we use for the edge of a wood - herb fringe, shrub belt, mantle - comes from the German-language article on the same subject, and the English page is named so that a reader following the obvious English link is not left thinking those three layers were drawn from it.

ijc-yamaguni

International Journal of the Commons, "External impacts on traditional commons ..." - the Yamaguni district study (https://thecommonsjournal.org/articles/10.18352/ijc.348)

What it is: "External impacts on traditional commons and present-day changes: a case study of iriai forests in Yamaguni district, Kyoto, Japan" by Daisaku Shimada, a peer-reviewed article in the International Journal of the Commons, volume 8 issue 1 (2014). Working from interviews and archival documents, it traces how the eleven villages of the Yamaguni district hold and manage their customary common forest (iriai), and how those institutions have bent under depopulation and cheap imported timber.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for the finding that each of eleven neighboring villages runs its common woodland under its own distinct institutions, with detailed local rules on the method, season and quantity of what each household may take - which is why the villages on our maps differ from one another in how their woodland is held rather than sharing a single scheme. Its limits: it is a modern study whose main subject is recent change, though the institutions it describes are inherited ones; it covers a single district near Kyoto rather than the country; and it describes no boundary marks on the ground, so where a common forest ends on our maps does not come from it.

kichijoji-enwiki

en.wikipedia Kichijōji (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kichijōji)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on Kichijoji, on the west edge of Tokyo: a planned resettlement of the Musashino plateau after the great fire of 1657, where migrants were granted strips of land more than a kilometer long running from one road to the Tamagawa aqueduct, on poor soil that made every field a dry field, with rice stipends and house-building loans from the state.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives a real case of a village laid out on long strip lots from a road, which is the form one of our settlement types is drawn in. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary, lightly sourced on the point we use; that this was a planned, state-subsidized reclamation after a fire, a deliberate special case rather than a village that grew; that it was dry field only, so it carries nothing about a paddy village's form; and that the article says nothing about where the houses stood on the strips - the house at the road end with its fields behind is our placement, drawn from elsewhere, as are the woodlots.

pmc5723622-bamboo-range

The moso/madake range study, PMC 5723622 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5723622/)

What it is: A 2017 peer-reviewed paper in Ecology and Evolution by Kohei Takenaka Takano and seven co-authors, "Detecting latitudinal and altitudinal expansion of invasive bamboo Phyllostachys edulis and Phyllostachys bambusoides (Poaceae) in Japan to project potential habitats under 1.5C-4.0C global warming". The team surveyed bamboo stands near 145 weather stations across northern and eastern Japan, compared aerial photographs spanning three to four decades, and modeled where the two species could grow under warmer climates.

Why it applies, and its limits: Moso and madake are the two bamboos a Japanese village actually grew, and this is a measured field survey of where in Japan they can and cannot stand, with a stated cold limit, so it tells us whether a bamboo grove belongs beside a given settlement. The limits are that it is a modern survey of a modern and still-spreading distribution, and that its central subject is future warming, so the ranges it projects are not historical ranges and only its present-day survey figures and cold thresholds are safe to use.

ridgelineimages-gsi

Ridgeline Images, "Reading GSI Topographic Maps" (https://ridgelineimages.com/maps/reading-gsi-topographic-maps/) - the bamboo-forest symbol described as its own category beside broad-leaved forest, coniferous forest and the bamboo-grass (sasa) zone

yashikirin-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 屋敷林 (居久根 redirects here) (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/屋敷林)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 屋敷林 (yashikirin, "homestead grove"), a tertiary encyclopedia entry on the belts of trees Japanese farmhouses planted around themselves against wind and snow, covering the regional names for the same thing - the northeastern igune (居久根) and the Izumo tsuijimatsu among them - along with the species used and the sides of the house they stand on.

Why it applies, and its limits: This is the backbone of how we plant a farmstead: which sides carry the grove (north and west against the wind, the east left open for the entrance and its ornamental garden), which trees make it up (sugi, matsu, hinoki and keyaki), and the part that turns a fact into a choice - that the Izumo form once enclosed the whole circumference of a house and was later cut back to a hook covering only the north and west, which is why our maps treat a full ring and a two-sided belt as two correct answers rather than one. It is a tertiary summary rather than a survey, so it gives us forms and species names and not counts or measurements, and the practice is Japanese and regional, the north-and-west rule following a wind that a warmer corner of the setting need not share.

tsuijimatsu

tsuijimatsu.com (https://tsuijimatsu.com/)
