ogose-kosatsuba

Ogose town, cultural property H26-04 高札場 (https://www.town.ogose.saitama.jp/kamei/shogaigakushu/bunkazai/kaisetsu/explanation_cultural/H26_04.html)

What it is: A cultural property explanation page published by the board of education of Ogose town in Saitama prefecture, describing the site of the town's Edo period kosatsuba, the roofed board on which the shogunate's edicts and regulations were posted. It cites an 1830 provincial gazetteer for where the board stood and records that each of the seventeen villages that make up the modern town had one at a key point, with a surviving place name marking another at the foot of a pass on the Chichibu highway.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives an Edo-period source, at one remove, for where a village's notice board stood: at a key point in each of the seventeen villages, and in the post town itself in the lower town, the political and economic center, at the settlement's southern entrance - which is what puts a board at the center or the entrance of our villages. Its limits are that it is one town's own heritage office reading an 1830 gazetteer, not a comparative study; that Ogose itself is a post station rather than a plain farming village; that the board is gone and the evidence is a gazetteer at one remove; and that the ranked list of candidate sites our rule uses - the center, the shrine precinct, the assembly place, before the officials' houses - is our inference, since the sheet says only "a key point".

kosatsu-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 高札 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/高札)

What it is: Japanese Wikipedia's article on 高札 (kosatsu), the boards on which laws, price schedules and prohibitions were written up and posted publicly, and on the kosatsuba where those boards stood.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the edict-board as an institution, its siting rule - at the entrance or the center of a town or village, where everyone passes - and Edo's principal boards, which is what puts a notice board on every map and tells us where. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary that carries the encyclopedia's own notice that it lacks sufficient citations, so the siting rule that governs a feature on every map rests on thin references; that it is taken for the institution and the rule and not for a number; and that its counts - the six great boards and the rest - are the capital's, and hedged even there.

adachi-kosatsu

Adachi City museum, 高札 (https://www.city.adachi.tokyo.jp/hakubutsukan/chiikibunka/hakubutsukan/shiryo-kosatsu.html)

What it is: A collection page from the museum of Adachi City, one of Tokyo's wards, about 高札 (kosatsu), the public notice boards on which the Edo-period government posted its edicts. It lists the standing boards of the early Edo period and says where boards were put up: at bridge ends and on the ground before the gates of village officials' houses.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives where the boards stood - at bridge ends and before the village officials' gates - and names Edo's five great boards, which is what puts a notice board in the right place on our maps. Its limits are that it is a short museum page describing objects it holds, with no stated method and no figures; that the Edo region stands for Japanese practice generally; and that it says nothing about who read the board out or how circulars reached a village through it, so those rest on other sources or on our reading.

suzugamori-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 鈴ヶ森刑場 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/鈴ヶ森刑場)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 鈴ヶ森刑場, on the Suzugamori execution ground that stood on the Tōkaidō at the southern approach to Edo from the seventeenth century until its abolition in the early Meiji years, and on its northern counterpart Kozukappara on the Nikkō highway.

Why it applies, and its limits: It supplies the placement rule our city maps use for a thing that has to go somewhere unpleasant: execution grounds sat at the entrances to the city, on the great highways, just outside where the built-up area ended, deliberately in the sight of everyone arriving - and that they came in pairs, one at each approach, is the article's own statement. It is a tertiary summary describing one shogunal capital in the Tokugawa period, so we take the siting logic and the pairing and not any measurement; how a much smaller provincial settlement handled the same need is inferred from the principle rather than found here.

caishikou-enwiki

en.wikipedia Caishikou Execution Grounds (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caishikou_Execution_Grounds)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on the Caishikou execution grounds in Beijing, the Qing dynasty's public execution site, whose name means "vegetable market". It records that the ground lay at the crossroads of Xuanwumen Outer Street and Luomashi Street, and that crowds gathered there for notable executions.

Why it applies, and its limits: Our maps have to decide whether an execution ground is hidden away or is a named public place, and the Chinese answer is visible here: a market crossroads, chosen so that a crowd would see it. It is a tertiary summary of one site in the imperial capital, so it anchors one Qing site rather than the general Chinese rule - the classical marketplace execution, the qishi of the law codes, is not on this page and rests on other sources - and it is only half of the question our record has to settle, since Japanese practice pushed such grounds out of the settlement entirely.

qiushen-zhwiki

zh.wikipedia 秋审 (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/秋审)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on the qiushen (秋审), the Qing dynasty's autumn assize, in which the court decided each autumn which death sentences would be carried out. It describes the route a case took: the Three Judicial Offices - the Board of Punishments, the Court of Judicial Review and the Censorate - heard the provincial cases jointly, and the prisoners waiting in provincial jails were held over until the review returned.

Why it applies, and its limits: A magistracy on our maps has a jail and an office, and this gives the shape of what happens in them: a capital case climbs from a provincial cell to the capital and the confirmed sentence comes back down, which is why the jail holds prisoners for months rather than days. Its limits are that an encyclopedia article is a tertiary summary, so we take its procedure only where its own references support it, and that this is Qing Chinese law standing for the setting's justice generally, our setting drawing its officialdom from the Chinese pattern as well as the Japanese one.

Its limits are that it is a tertiary encyclopedia summary; that it is the shogunal capital's practice in one era, not a rule for a provincial town; and that the one figure we take from it, the ground's size of about 108 by 54 m, carries no citation on the page, so it is an uncited encyclopedia figure we rely on for want of a better one.

The Tontine Coffee-House, "Buddhist Monastery Lending" (https://tontinecoffeehouse.com/2024/02/26/buddhist-monastery-lending/)

What it is: A post, "Buddhist Monastery Lending," written by Daniel DeMatos in February 2024 for The Tontine Coffee-House, a financial-history blog running since 2018 that covers the history of financial institutions and innovations. It traces how Buddhist monasteries in China served as lenders and pawnbrokers from the arrival of Buddhism down to the nineteenth century, and closes with a short reading list that includes Jacques Gernet's Buddhism in Chinese Society.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for the institutional facts behind a temple that lends - the monastery as the great pawnbroker, and pledged goods held in an associated warehouse or granary for the life of the loan - which is why a temple compound on our maps can reasonably carry a strongroom rather than only a hall. It is a secondary blog summary rather than a scholarly work, so we take from it only what its own cited literature supports, and it is about China; we apply it to a Japanese-flavored setting on the strength of the monastic economy the two shared, not as a statement about any particular Japanese temple.

timber-rafting-wiki

Wikipedia, Timber rafting (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timber_rafting)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article "Timber rafting", a tertiary summary of how cut timber was moved on water before roads and railways, distinguishing rafting - logs bound together and navigated, sometimes with cabins and steering oars aboard - from log driving, and giving raft sizes from several regional traditions.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it to keep two different river systems straight on a map, since a raft needs a wide steady channel and a landing where it can be broken up, while driving loose logs does not, and to keep raft sizes within a plausible order of magnitude. It is a tertiary summary whose figures we take only where its own references support them, and its detailed dimensions come from European rivers such as the Rhine, so we treat them as the outer bound of what a raft can be rather than as a measurement of an East Asian one.

kiso-unzaihou

What it is: A page published by the Chubu Regional Forest Office of Japan's Forestry Agency, part of a public-history series on the Kiso forests, describing the Kiso-style felling and timber transport method - axe felling, the sledge and chute haul down to the water, loose-log drives, and rafting - as it was practiced from the late sixteenth century until 1920. What it is: A public-history article by Dan Ott, written in 2013 for the National Park Service's St. Croix National Scenic Riverway, on the log-handling works of the St. Croix river between Minnesota and Wisconsin from 1856 to 1914: the river divided lengthwise into log channels and holding pens beside a maintained navigation channel, the hinged sheer boom, and the lawsuits and negligence rulings the works drew.

hudson-big-boom

New York Almanack, "The Big Boom: Old Hudson River Chain Recalls Logging History" (https://www.newyorkalmanack.com/2016/10/the-big-boom-old-hudson-river-chain-recalls-logging-history/)

What it is: An article by Glenn Pearsall, "The Big Boom: Old Hudson River Chain Recalls Logging History", published on 18 October 2016 in New York Almanack, an online magazine of New York State history and natural history. It describes the log boom that held floated timber on the Hudson above Glens Falls - hewn timbers bolted together raftwise, the groups joined end to end by chains running over friction rollers, each end anchored to a heavy bank abutment - and the 1859 break that scattered logs forty miles downriver.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for how a boom is built and held, which is what the log booms on our timber-town rivers are drawn from: a floating fence across a current, jointed so it rides the water, tied to solid ground at both banks. Its limits: it is popular history rather than a technical or scholarly account, with the scholarly source one step further out; it is about industrial-era logging in North America rather than East Asia, and its fittings - bolted timber groups, chains run over iron rollers - are nineteenth-century hardware a premodern river works would replace with lashings and rope, so what transfers is the principle of anchoring to fixed ground and not the fastenings; and the Hudson operation was far larger than anything at our settlements, so we take the arrangement from it and not the dimensions.

sphere-2004-water

The Sphere Project, Humanitarian Charter and Minimum Standards in Disaster Response (2004), chapter 2, water supply, sanitation and hygiene promotion (https://ec.europa.eu/echo/files/evaluation/watsan2005/annex_files/Sphere/SPHERE2%20-%20chapter%202%20-%20Min%20standards%20in%20water,%20sanitation%20and%20hygiene%20prom.pdf)

What it is: A chapter of the Sphere humanitarian minimum standards - the water supply, sanitation and hygiene section, circulated here as a PDF on the European Commission's humanitarian aid site - setting out how many users a tap, a handpump or an open well can serve and how far anyone should have to walk to reach one.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives one planning figure - an open well serving about four hundred users - which the record uses only in the negative: a well's yield is never what limits how many wells a settlement has. Its limits are that it is a relief agencies' planning standard for present-day disaster response, not a historical or field measurement; that its four hundred rests on a survival ration of fifteen liters a person a day and a well open eight hours a day, so a settled household cooking, washing and watering stock would exhaust a well at a far smaller number - the figure flatters well capacity and is a ceiling on a ceiling; and that the handbook itself calls these rough guidelines to be used with caution. The bucket rate it assumes is one a premodern rope-and-bucket well shares; the modern part is the ration and the emergency, not the engineering.

nagaya-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 長屋 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%95%B7%E5%B1%8B)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the nagaya (長屋), the row tenement of Japanese towns - one long building divided across its width by party walls into separate rented dwellings. It covers the form in general and the Edo-period back-alley version in particular: the small unit of nine shaku by two ken, the shared privy in the alley, the shared well, and the townsmen and craftsmen who rented there while the better merchants kept shops on the main street.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the plain definition of the building type we draw along a town's back lanes, and of who lived in one, which is exactly what we take from it. It is a tertiary summary rather than a study, so we use its figures only where its own references support them, and what it describes best is Edo, the largest city in the country, so a provincial town's tenements on our maps are that pattern scaled down.

kichinyado-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 木賃宿 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%9C%A8%E8%B3%83%E5%AE%BF)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the kichin-yado (木賃宿), the cheapest class of Japanese inn, whose name means "firewood-fee lodging" - guests brought their own rice and paid only for the fuel to cook it.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the cheapest kind of inn - the firewood-fee lodging on the edge of a post town, a common room, guests bringing their own bedding and rice and paying for the wood - which is what puts such an inn on our town maps. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary that carries the encyclopedia's own notice that it lacks sources; and that its passage about the inns spreading into a city's poor quarter, laborers and the unregistered sleeping a tatami apiece, describes the Meiji-era doss house after the post-station system ended, so the common room and the firewood fee are Edo while the poor-quarter crowding is a later condition being read back into a premodern town.

guanxiang-zdic

漢典 zdic.net, 关厢 (https://zdic.net/hans/%E5%85%B3%E5%8E%A2)

What it is: The entry for 关厢 (guanxiang) in Zdic (漢典), an online Chinese character and word dictionary. It defines the word as the streets immediately outside a city gate and the area adjoining them.

Why it applies, and its limits: A dictionary is the right authority for what a word means, and what we take is the meaning: that Chinese had a settled term for the built-up strip outside the wall, which tells us such strips were a normal and named part of a walled city. That is all it supports - it says nothing about what stood there, and the commonly repeated claim that trade and inns concentrated in the guanxiang is not on this page and could not be read on any public page we reached, so nothing on our maps about what fills that strip rests on this source.

economy-song-enwiki

en.wikipedia Economy of the Song dynasty (replaces the Grokipedia page of the same title) (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_the_Song_dynasty)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article "Economy of the Song dynasty", a survey of Chinese agriculture, industry, trade and money in the tenth to thirteenth centuries. We use its account of the iron trade: Robert Hartwell's estimate that per capita iron output rose sixfold between 806 and 1078, and the description of iron-smelting households working with government support such as charcoal supplies and skilled craftsmen.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives us the scale and the organization of premodern heavy industry - how a smelting operation was staffed and supplied, and how much iron a large premodern state could make - which is what sizes and sites an ironworking quarter on a town map. wagner-ming-iron

Donald Wagner, Iron production in three Ming texts (https://donwagner.dk/MingFe/MingFe.html)

What it is: "Iron production in three Ming texts," a page by Donald B. Wagner presenting translations and commentary on Ming-dynasty Chinese accounts of the iron industry, among them Song Yingxing's description of the chao fining hearth and a passage numbering the workforce of one ironworks.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a translated and annotated set of Ming texts on ironmaking by the leading historian of Chinese ferrous metallurgy, cited for the fining hearth as Song Yingxing describes it and for the size of a large iron enterprise's workforce - core setting material, not a stand-in. Its limits are that it is a conference paper on the author's own site rather than a journal article; that the workforce figures come from Qu Dajun's account of about 1680 looking back at the late Ming; and that Wagner's own commentary warns that the passage describes a firm running several ironworks, which Qu Dajun seems to have misread as one, so the figures are an enterprise's and not one works' - a caution our text must carry with the numbers.

fires-in-edo-enwiki

en.wikipedia Fires in Edo (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fires_in_Edo)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article "Fires in Edo", an encyclopedia summary, well referenced to Japanese scholarship, of the repeated great fires of the Tokugawa capital - how often they came, what set them off, when in the year they fell, how the city fought them, and the thick-walled plastered storehouses (dozo) that survived them.

Why it applies, and its limits: We take from it the seasonal pattern - that the serious fires clustered in the dry months under the north and northwest winds, from January to April with March the worst - which is a matter of climate and so applies to a town as much as to the capital; and the construction of the storehouse, an earthen-walled, tile-roofed building a map draws differently from a timber house. It is a tertiary summary, used only where its own references support the point, and it is about the largest city in Japan: the season and the storehouse transfer to a smaller settlement, the scale and frequency of its conflagrations do not.

fao-charcoal-safety

FAO, Simple technologies for charcoal making, ch. 5 safety precautions (https://www.fao.org/4/X5555E/x5555e06.htm)

What it is: The safety chapter of Simple technologies for charcoal making, a technical handbook from the UN Food and Agriculture Organization on small-scale charcoal production. It explains that freshly made charcoal heats itself and can catch fire on its own, that the fine dust is the worst offender, and that a fresh burn should be left in the open air before it is bagged or stored.

Why it applies, and its limits: The chemistry of fresh charcoal is a property of the material and not of the century, so it explains why a charcoal burner's stack and store sit apart from thatched roofs - which is all we take from it. The limit is that this is a modern handbook: the specific waiting times it recommends are today's handling guidance, an anchor for the practice rather than a rule anyone in a premodern village would have stated in hours and days.

wagner-fining-puddling

Donald Wagner, Traditional Chinese fining and puddling (http://donwagner.dk/arch-iron/eu/fining-puddling-china-eu.html)

What it is: "Traditional Chinese fining and puddling," a page by Donald B. Wagner, the historian of Chinese iron and steel technology, on his own site. It describes how Chinese ironworkers turned cast iron into wrought iron: wood, charcoal and broken cast iron charged into a hearth and lit, the half-melted mass stirred with an iron rod under a blast of air, a family of methods known collectively as chao, "stir frying."

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives what happens inside the building - pig iron stirred under blast until it comes out as wrought iron - which is what makes a finery on our maps a finery and not a smithy. Its limits are that it is a specialist's web page rather than a peer-reviewed article; that it is about China, one half of the setting; that it describes a process rather than a workshop; and that the detail we quote comes from twentieth-century field reports of a surviving traditional process - a 1936 account and a 1958 report from southern Henan and Sichuan - which the author treats as continuous with the premodern practice, so it is a modern observation of an old technique and is labeled as such.

tetsu-to-hagane-91

History of Iron and Steel Making Technology in Japan, Tetsu-to-Hagané 91(1), JStage (https://doi.org/10.2355/tetsutohagane1955.91.1_2)

What it is: "History of Iron and Steel Making Technology in Japan", a review article by Mitsuru Tate published in 2005 in Tetsu-to-Hagane, the journal of the Iron and Steel Institute of Japan (volume 91, issue 1), tracing the tatara smelt and the refining that followed it across the long history of Japanese ironmaking.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a metallurgist's survey of Japanese ironmaking from the sixth century to 1923, in the field's own journal, cited for a seventeenth-century Japanese process - the two-stage refining in which pig iron from the tatara was worked to wrought iron by the great smithy as a separate operation - so era, place and method line up with the use exactly. Its limits are that it is an invited overview in a peer-reviewed journal, a specialist's synthesis rather than reviewed primary research; and that it describes a process and not buildings, so how a smithy stood on a map rests elsewhere.

mukoyama-linear-borders

Mukoyama, Naosuke, "The Eastern cousins of European sovereign states? The development of linear borders in early modern Japan", European Journal of International Relations 29(2) (2023) 255-282 (https://doi.org/10.1177/13540661221133206)

What it is: An argument, in the leading European journal of international relations, that early modern Japan developed linear borders of its own rather than importing the idea. It gathers the evidence out of the Japanese historians - boundary disputes, the mounds and stones and trees domains built along agreed lines, the guardhouses that policed them, and the provincial maps the shogunate ordered five times between 1605 and 1835 - and reproduces two period boundary maps and photographs of markers still standing.

Why it applies, and its limits: No other readable source assembles the whole case for drawing a clan border as a LINE: the agreed line, the hundred-odd numbered mounds along 130 km between Morioka and Sendai, the guardhouse of 102 soldiers, the domain currency that stopped at the boundary, and the shogunate’s own instruction that borders be depicted unambiguously on the map. Three limits, each the author’s own admission. He reads the historians rather than the documents, so on any single number the record is at one remove. He states that the change was uneven - furthest advanced in the peripheral outside-lord domains, weakest around Kyoto and Nara, where dozens of lords held ground together - so a border near a capital has weaker warrant than one in the marches. And the paper is about Japan alone; it lists China among its open questions, so it licenses the Japanese half of this setting and not the Chinese one.

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on the Nanbu-Date border mounds, the earth mounds raised in the early Edo period to mark the boundary between Morioka domain and Sendai domain - a line of about 130 km running from the Ou mountains east to the Pacific - which the shogunate re-confirmed along with the two clans' holdings in 1642.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for the fact that a domain boundary in this period was a made thing, marked on the ground by heaped earth, which is why such mounds can appear at the edge of a map. It is a tertiary summary whose details we take only where its own references support them, and it records one boundary in the far northeast standing for the general practice, so we draw the type of marker from it rather than any particular spacing.

ide-japanese-experience

IDE, Passing on "The Japanese Experience", rural society (https://d-arch.ide.go.jp/je_archive/english/society/wp_je_unu4.html)

What it is: "Development of local culture and the irrigation system of the Azusa Basin" by Akira Tamaki, published in 1979 by the Institute of Developing Economies as part of the “Japanese Experience of the UNU Human and Social Development Programme” series, and kept in the institute's online archive. It studies the Azusa River basin near Matsumoto in Nagano: the customary rules by which villages shared irrigation water, the difference between the dry fields on the river terraces and the wet paddy below them, and the repeated attempts to bring water up onto the terraces.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for the distinction between dry field (hatake) and wet paddy (suiden) and for why a settlement sitting above the water is stuck with the former: the late-Tokugawa schemes to irrigate the Hata terraces were defeated again and again by the downstream villages who would have lost the water. Its limits: it is a close study of one river basin in one province, so its customs are local before they are national, and the terrace-against-paddy contrast is a fact of that valley's landform rather than a general rule; and it runs from the Tokugawa period through twentieth-century modernization, so only its account of the older arrangements bears on our settlements.

harie-kabata

Harie Shozu no Sato, the kabata wells (https://ihcsacafe-en.ihcsa.or.jp/news/harie/)

What it is: A 2019 English-language article on IHCSA Cafe, the cultural information site of Japan's International Hospitality and Conference Service Association, written with the cooperation of the local Harie Shozu no Sato committee. It describes the Harie district in Shiga - "the village of living water" - where spring water rises into kabata, built basins inside and beside the houses, and explains how households use that water and how the community looks after it.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for the character of a village water point rather than for any measurement: that one source served drinking, cooking and washing, and that the place where water was drawn was also where villagers stopped to talk - which is why a well on our maps is drawn as somewhere people gather and not as plumbing. It is a short institutional piece written with the village's own preservation committee to introduce the place to foreign readers, and it describes a surviving village as it is today, so it is evidence for a continuing practice rather than a documented historical one; Harie is also unusual in having spring water at every door, which most villages did not.

kabu-ido-commons

Endo, Takahiro, "The Kabu-ido system and factors affecting local groundwater extraction control: case study of a customary groundwater management in Japan", Water History 14 (2022) 169-191 (https://doi.org/10.1007/s12685-022-00302-1)

What it is: A study of one Edo-period institution by which sixteen villages behind a single ring levee on the Noubi Plain limited how many wells they could sink, worked out of the villages’ own papers - the 1812 agreement between the upper and lower villages, and the Nishimatsu Diary, which records the well patrols year by year from 1821. It is the fullest account there is of a farming community policing its own water without an authority above it.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the clearest attested case of a community capping its own well count by agreement, which is exactly the practice this project draws more freely than the record attests, so it is evidence worth having against our own decision. Its limits cut that weight down a long way, and all four are the paper’s own. It is ONE district, not a country, and the paper says the institution is little known even in Japan. It is unusual rather than typical - the literature values it as a pioneering example. It runs from 1812 to the 1930s, the last half-century of the Edo period and the sixty years after it. And the wells it caps are ARTESIAN IRRIGATION wells whose free flow waterlogged the paddy of the villages downhill; the grievance is drainage nuisance, not scarcity, and a draw-well in a house yard raises none of it. One correction worth stating: the agreement’s only money runs the other way from a licence fee - the upper villages pay the well inspectors a ryo a year, and no charge for holding a well appears anywhere in it.
