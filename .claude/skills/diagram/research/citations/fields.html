Citations: Fields: the research behind the paddy, plot and crop rules

The passages behind the footnotes of Fields: the research behind the paddy, plot and crop rules - first the works they quote and why each one counts, then the notes themselves. Hover a footnote number on that page and the note appears beside it; the numbers here are the same ones.

The works cited on this page

kochi-seiri-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 耕地整理 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E8%80%95%E5%9C%B0%E6%95%B4%E7%90%86)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on 耕地整理 (kochi seiri, arable-land consolidation), the rearrangement of farm plots into regular rectangles squared to the compass, covering the Shizuoka method first carried out in 1872 and the national consolidation law of 1899.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for a date rather than a measurement: it places the neat rectangular grid of Japanese paddy in the Meiji era and after, which is why the fields on our maps keep the irregular shapes the ground gave them. It is a tertiary summary whose figures we take only where its own references support them, and here what we take is an institution and its statute, which is the kind of thing such an article states plainly.

nougyoudoboku-keihan

のうぎょうとぼく, 水田圃場における畦畔について (https://nougyoudoboku.com/a-ridge-between-rice-fields/)

What it is: "On bunds in paddy fields" (水田圃場における畦畔について), an article published in December 2022 and revised since on のうぎょうとぼく ("Agriculture and Me"), a Japanese site about agricultural civil engineering written under the name Oimoya by a former prefectural officer in that field. It explains what a bund is and what it does - holding the water in, dividing the plots, giving a footway for weeding and spraying, growing fodder grass - and tabulates the standard cross-section: a trapezoid with a 1:1 batter, 30 cm high and 30 cm across the top, with heavier sections where the drop between plots is large and in cold regions.

Why it applies, and its limits: Those dimensions set the width of every bund we draw and the grassy strip on top of it, and they come from a trained agricultural engineer rather than a general-interest writer. The honest limit is era and method: this is a present-day design standard for consolidated, machine-worked paddy, published on a specialist's own site rather than in a reviewed journal, so we use it as the shape a soil bund naturally takes for the job, which a hand-built one also had to do, and not as a measurement of a premodern field.

senmaida

Shiroyone Senmaida (Wajima, Ishikawa) - the Japanese government's Highlighting Japan feature (gov-online.go.jp, June 2025), the Wajima city cultural-property listing (wajima.hiddenheritage.jp), and JNTO's site page (japan.travel); (https://www.gov-online.go.jp/hlj/en/june_2025/june_2025-01.html)

What it is: The website of the Shiroyone Senmaida Scenic Preservation Council, the body that keeps the terraced paddies at Wajima on the Noto peninsula, whose office sits in the city's tourism division. Its "about" page describes the terrace as it is worked today, by hand, because machines cannot enter: 1,004 basins on about four hectares, an average of about 18 square meters each, the smallest about 50 centimeters square.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the small end of a really worked paddy - basins of a few square meters, the smallest a quarter of one - which is what tells us that no absolute minimum paddy area exists and lets our steepest fields be drawn tiny. Its limits are that it is a preservation body's own promotional page rather than a survey; that it is an extreme case, basins made tiny by a steep slope, so it is a floor and never a typical size; and that its counts describe the terrace's present state, not a documented historical layout - and the page gives the average and the minimum only, so how many basins run about a square meter is our reading, not its figure.

zennoh-mizukanri

Zennoh (National Federation of Agricultural Cooperative Associations), 水管理について (https://www.zennoh.or.jp/ty/einou/high_quality_rice/pdf/production_001_05.pdf)

What it is: A rice-growing leaflet, 水管理について ("On water management"), published by Zennoh, Japan's National Federation of Agricultural Cooperative Associations, as part of the advice it gives its member farmers on producing good rice.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for how deep the water in a paddy stands and when it is let out - about 2 to 3 cm held through the twenty days after the ear emerges, and the season-long sequence from deeper water through midseason drying and intermittent irrigation to the final drain a week or so before harvest - because that is what decides whether a given field on the map is drawn wet, drying or dry. The honest limit is era: this is present-day guidance written for cooperative farmers with modern varieties and modern control of the inlet, so we use it for the shape of the rice year, which the crop itself imposes and which is far older than the leaflet, rather than as a record of what a premodern farmer measured.

maff-suitou-mizu

MAFF (Japan, Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries), 水稲栽培のポイント (https://www.maff.go.jp/j/seisan/gijutsuhasshin/techinfo/attach/pdf/suitou-2.pdf)

What it is: An extension leaflet from the Japanese Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries, 水稲栽培のポイント ("points for paddy rice cultivation"), setting out how deep the water is held in a paddy at each stage of the season: somewhat deep until the transplanted seedlings root, shallower afterward to warm the water and encourage tillering, then the mid-season drain until the mud cracks and shows a footprint, with deep water held only as a guard against a cold snap.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the staged water depths of a rice year - a few centimeters until rooting, less after, the field drained in midsummer until it cracks and takes a footprint, intermittent watering after, deep water only against cold - which is the calendar our paddies are drawn to. Its limits are that it is present-day extension advice for modern cultivars in a machine-transplanted, herbicide-using system, its figures averaged from twenty-first-century demonstration plots; and that the mid-season drying in particular is documented here as modern practice - it appears in Edo-period farming texts only in places with water enough to re-flood, and spread generally in the twentieth century - so a drained, cracked field in midsummer on our maps is a guess resting on a modern document until a period source carries it.

azolla-enwiki

en.wikipedia Azolla (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azolla)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on Azolla, a genus of small floating water ferns. Its ecology section describes the fern's use on flooded rice paddies, where its symbiotic cyanobacterium fixes nitrogen from the air and the rotting mat releases that nitrogen into the water for the rice.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it to explain why a flooded paddy might be drawn carrying a green film rather than clear water, which is a visible surface feature with a reason behind it. It is a tertiary summary whose figures we take only where its own references support them, and the productivity numbers it quotes come from modern agronomy, so we cite the practice and the mechanism rather than any yield.

nabunken-azemame

Nara National Research Institute for Cultural Properties, Asuka Historical Museum, "畦豆" (2023-06) (https://www.nabunken.go.jp/asuka/info/2023/06/post-444.html)

What it is: A short explanatory article of June 2023 from the Asuka Historical Museum of the Nara National Research Institute for Cultural Properties on azemame (畦豆), the soybeans sown along the bund of a paddy field.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives, from a national research institute's museum, the practice of sowing beans along the paddy bund after transplanting and harvesting them with the rice - once done across Japan, now rare - which is what puts bund beans on our paddies. Its limits are that it is a short piece for a general audience with no proportion of bunds sown and no household count; that its "once across Japan" carries no date, so it fixes the practice in no particular century; and that the post itself says these bund plants are seen especially in the terraced hill country, where the bund is a larger share of the farmland than on the flat - so our flat-valley paddies are the case the source calls the less bund-intensive one.

seijoue-seika

精華町 せいか舎, 正条植え (https://seikasya.town.seika.kyoto.jp/essays/seijoue)

What it is: An essay on せいか舎, the local-history site of the town of Seika in Kyoto prefecture, about 正条植え, straight-row rice planting, and how it spread through the countryside.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a second and independent local voice agreeing with the same dating we take from the dictionary entry, that row planting spread in the Meiji period and brought weeder-based weeding and mid-season cultivation with it. It is a municipal essay written for residents rather than a scholarly study, and it speaks for one district, so we use it as corroboration of the general chronology and not as a measurement of anything.

seijoue-kotobank

コトバンク 正条植 (https://kotobank.jp/word/正条植-85971)

What it is: The entry for 正条植 (seijoue, straight-row rice planting) on Kotobank, a Japanese reference site that reprints entries from published dictionaries and encyclopedias, describing the practice and the national campaign that spread it.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it to date the practice, because it tells us straight-row planting was rare before Meiji and was promoted nationally through the 1890s and 1900s together with the hand-pushed inter-row weeder (田打車), planting ropes and rulers, which is why our paddies are not drawn as tidy grids of aligned seedlings. It is a tertiary reference aggregating dictionary entries, so we take the dating from it rather than fine detail, and because it is written about a Meiji reform, what it tells us about earlier practice comes by contrast rather than by direct description.

tabayashi-1986

Tabayashi 1986, Geographical Review of Japan 60(1) (https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/grj1984b/60/1/60_1_41/_article/-char/en/)

What it is: A peer-reviewed article by Akira Tabayashi, "Irrigation Systems in Japan", in the Geographical Review of Japan, Series B volume 60 number 1 (1987), pages 41-65. It classifies Japan's irrigation systems by their water source - river, pond, lake, groundwater, torrent - maps where each kind predominates, and traces when each was built, from the Kofun and Nara period works of the west to the Sengoku and Edo works of the east.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the right kind of source for what it is used for - a Japanese geographer's account of Japanese irrigation from the third century to 1867, at national scale and with named systems at village scale, describing the gravity-fed logic that is premodern in origin - so the siting of a reservoir above its fields, the tapering of canals as they are tapped and the separation of supply from drain rest on it directly. Its limits are that its tail describes the modern land consolidation that followed the 1899 law, whose surveyed rectangular grid is the modern arrangement and not ours; that it gives no paddy depth; and that it does not say a storage pond is tapped by a single outlet, so that claim on our maps rests elsewhere.

kagawa-tameike

Kagawa prefecture tameike structure pages (https://www.pref.kagawa.lg.jp/tochikai/about_tameike/repair/structure.html)

What it is: Pages from the Kagawa prefectural government's Land Improvement Division describing the structure of a tameike, the irrigation reservoir dammed across a valley head, part by part: the inclined intake on the dam's upstream face, the bottom conduit at its base, and the spillway that keeps a flood from overtopping the dam. Kagawa, dry and densely ponded, has one of the largest counts of such ponds in Japan.

Why it applies, and its limits: It tells us where each part of a pond sits and what it does, which is what the pond glyph on our maps is drawn from. Its limits are that it is modern engineering and maintenance guidance for ponds as they are kept today - its spillway list includes forms that are unambiguously modern - so we use it for the arrangement of parts rather than for what a premodern pond was built of; that it is one prefecture; and that it says nothing about one pond feeding another below it, so a chain of ponds on our maps rests on other sources or is a guess.

jsidre-minumadai

JSIDRE, on Minuma-dai (1728) (https://www.jsidre.or.jp/tabata5-a/)

What it is: An article in the "history of regional development" series published by the Japanese Society of Irrigation, Drainage and Rural Engineering, on the Minuma-dai canal completed in 1728 on the Kanto plain, a shogunal reclamation carried out by Izawa Yasobei of the Kishu school of engineering. It describes the works and the reclamation layout that drained the old reservoir and watered the land in its place.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives us the comb layout our field blocks use, with the supply channels running along the higher rim and the drain down the lowest line through the middle, and it comes from the engineering profession that still maintains these systems. It is a society's popular history article rather than a peer-reviewed study and no author is named, and it describes one very large project, so we take the principle of the layout from it and not the dimensions, which belong to a scheme of thousands of hectares rather than to one hamlet's fields.

beitang-studies

Nature Communications 2023; Jiang-Huai pond irrigation (PMC6695888) (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-39454-w)

What it is: A peer-reviewed article in Nature Communications (June 2023) on reactivating small water bodies for irrigation and drainage in Chinese rice farming. It describes the beitang (陂塘) pond systems of the Jiang-Huai region as a pond-based irrigation paradigm with more than 2,500 years of history, and records that in the 1950s some 8.3 million such decentralized systems served 39 percent of China's irrigated area.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the source for our assumption that a village in rice country waters itself from ponds it holds locally, rather than only from a canal running past it, which changes what a settlement map has to show. It is a modern paper whose real subject is present-day sustainability, and its one big number is a 1950s count rather than a premodern one, so we take the system's antiquity and its village scale from it and treat the share as an indication that the pond mode was ordinary rather than marginal.

japanese-wiki-corpus

Japanese Wiki Corpus + Tsukuba field-trace surveys (https://www.japanesewiki.com/history/Jori%20sei%20(system%20of%20land%20subdivision%20in%20ancient%20Japan).html)

What it is: The English rendering of the Japanese Wikipedia article on jori-sei 条里制, the ancient grid system for subdividing farmland, hosted on the Japanese Wiki Corpus, a collection of Japanese Wikipedia articles translated into English by Japan's National Institute of Information and Communications Technology. The article sets out the geometry: a basic unit about 109 meters square, thirty-six of them making a ri, each unit divided into ten strips, the grid lines generally north-south and east-west but tilted in a fair number of regions, with land first recorded this way in 743.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for that grid module and its proportions, which is the frame our regular field patterns are laid out on and the reason a long-settled plain reads as ruled rather than as a patchwork. Its limits: it is an encyclopedia article, a tertiary summary, so we take the plain geometry and dates and not its interpretation; it is a translation rather than the Japanese original; and jori was an ancient state land system whose regularity survived only where the ground and the centuries allowed, so a grid this tidy suits a broad old plain and not every valley we draw.

kokudaka-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 石高 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%9F%B3%E9%AB%98)

What it is: Japanese Wikipedia's article on 石高 (kokudaka), the rice-volume measure used to rate land and to pay retainers, including what one koku was taken to represent and what a retainer was actually issued.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use its rule of thumb - one koku as an adult's rice for a year - to turn a settlement's assessed yield into a number of mouths it could feed, and the article supplies its own hedge, that a standard stipend ran nearer 1.8 koku of brown rice, which keeps the round figure from being treated as exact. It is a tertiary summary whose figures we take where it states them with that qualification, and the koku equivalence is in any case a convention of assessment rather than a measured diet.

gokogomin-kotobank

コトバンク 五公五民 (https://kotobank.jp/word/五公五民-64520)

What it is: The Kotobank entry for 五公五民 - "five to the lord, five to the commoner" - the rice tax rate. Kotobank is a Japanese reference site that reprints entries from published dictionaries and encyclopedias, and this one records the shift from a four-to-six split in the early Edo period to a five-to-five split after the Kyoho era of 1716-36.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the nominal tax rate in the words of five published reference works, which is what our rent and yield arithmetic starts from. Its limits are that it is a tertiary aggregator; that the rate is a nominal one, and the sources themselves say the real burden varied with soil, harvest and region; and that one of them hedges the very transition we quote - the shift to five-five after the Kyoho era is "said to have" happened "but is not certain" - so the later rate is a convention rather than a settled fact.

mdpi-kunisaki

Jansing, Mahichi et al., "Sustainable Irrigation Management in Paddy Rice Agriculture: A Comparative Case Study of Karangasem Indonesia and Kunisaki Japan", Sustainability 12(3):1180 (2020) (https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/12/3/1180; the page is public))

What it is: Matthew Scott Jansing, Faezeh Mahichi and Ranahansa Dasanayake, a peer-reviewed open-access article in Sustainability (2020) comparing two small irrigation communities - a Balinese subak and Tsunai ward on the Kunisaki peninsula of Oita, whose five or six reservoir systems water fifty hectares for eleven farmers - from farmer surveys, interviews and water tests of the ponds.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives us a real, counted case of how much land a cluster of small farm ponds waters and how many households share it - one ward's systems serving about fifty hectares for eleven farmers - which is the scale at which we draw a pond against the fields below it. It describes irrigation as it is managed today, in one peninsula, with modern equipment and a modern number of farmers; its own counts of the ward's ponds differ between passages; and it states no typical or maximum command area at all, so the upper bound our maps work to is our guess rather than its finding.

satoyama-enwiki

en.wikipedia Satoyama (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satoyama)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on satoyama, the managed border zone between the wooded hills and the farmed flat in Japan, covering its mosaic of woodland, paddy, dry field, grassland and ponds and the ways villagers worked it.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the definition of the managed belt between village and mountain, the mosaic of land uses in it, and two practices our maps draw - leaf litter gathered for the paddies and the coppice cut on a cycle. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary flagged as still being translated from the Japanese article; that the one Edo-period statement we lean on, the leaf gathering, carries no citation in the article; that the fifteen-to-twenty-year cutting cycle is stated in the present tense and cited to a 2017 ecology paper about present-day management, so a premodern rotation is being read from a modern study of a traditional practice; and that the word itself is largely a twentieth-century coinage, which is our knowledge rather than the page's.

kotobank-shitsuden

Seisenban Nihon Kokugo Daijiten (Shogakukan), Nihon Dai-hyakka Zensho / Nipponica (Shogakukan) and Kaitei Shinpan Sekai Dai-hyakka Jiten (Heibonsha), s.v. 湿田, aggregated at kotobank (https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%B9%BF%E7%94%B0-74168)

What it is: Three reference works shown together on kotobank for 湿田 (shitsuden, the wet paddy) - Shogakukan's Seisenban Nihon Kokugo Daijiten and Nipponica, and Heibonsha's revised Sekai Dai-hyakka Jiten. They define it as a badly drained paddy that stays waterlogged even when rice is not growing in it, and set out what that costs: no winter crop, heavy going for the farm work, soil that is colder and starved of air, and unstable, generally low yields.

Why it applies, and its limits: This is the class our blue plots belong to, and these entries are what let a reader clicking one of them learn why a wet field is a poorer field rather than simply a wetter one. They are tertiary summaries that characterize a type rather than measure any particular field, so which of a settlement's plots are wet is our own placement, made from the ground the map puts them on.

wikipedia-soybean

Wikipedia, "Soybean", Description (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soybean)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article "Soybean", and in particular its Description section - a tertiary encyclopedia entry on the plant, its habit and its cultivation.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the two facts a bean plot on a map turns on - that the plant is an annual, replanted every year, and how tall it stands - and the first is a constant of the species. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary; and that the height it gives, 50 to 125 cm, describes the crop as bred and grown now - the article gives a separate modern-cultivar figure and says nothing about landraces - so the drawn height is a modern-crop range used because no premodern measurement of the plant could be read.

bench-terrace-riser

FAO STI-portal and ICIMOD pages on traditional irrigated / rainfed paddy rice terraces, plus general bench-terracing references (https://sti-portal.fao.org/innovations/traditional-irrigated-rice-terraces)

What it is: A technology profile of traditional irrigated rice terraces on the Food and Agriculture Organization's Science, Technology and Innovation portal, compiled from WOCAT survey data and describing hand-built level bench terraces on steep slopes in Nepal, read alongside pages from ICIMOD and general references on bench terracing. It gives the dimensions of the structural face: risers 0.8 to 1.5 m high at slopes of 80 to 160 percent, stone-faced where stone is available, with a small lip of 20 to 25 cm holding 10 to 15 cm of water on the platform.

Why it applies, and its limits: It supplies the physical distinction our terraced maps depend on, that the tall riser is a retaining wall holding up the soil while a separate low lip holds in the water, which is why micro-basins belong on a hillside and not on a valley floor. The clear limitation is place: these are Himalayan terraces in Nepal standing in for East Asian ones because no comparable published measurements of Japanese or Chinese terrace risers could be read on a public page; the profile also describes a working present-day system that has taken up some modern methods, and parts of its text are automatically compiled, so we use it for the geometry of the structure and not for the details of how it is farmed.

kotobank-kanden

Digital Daijisen (Shogakukan) and Kaitei Shinpan Sekai Dai-hyakka Jiten (Heibonsha), s.v. 乾田, aggregated at kotobank (https://kotobank.jp/word/%E4%B9%BE%E7%94%B0-49311)

What it is: The Digital Daijisen and Heibonsha's revised Sekai Dai-hyakka Jiten entries for 乾田 (kanden, the dry paddy), shown together on kotobank: a paddy that drains well and dries out to the condition of a dry field when the water is let off, together with the national program that converted waterlogged paddy into it.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the contrast term for the wet paddy - the dry paddy that drains and can carry a winter crop - and the scale of the Meiji conversion, which is what tells us that much of the premodern paddy was the wet kind. Its limits are that these are tertiary dictionary summaries; that the two-thirds figure is hedged in the source itself as what is commonly said; and that it is a modern figure about a modern program - the entry gives no premodern share of wet paddy, so the step from "two thirds were converted after Meiji" to "a premodern map carries wet paddy" is the record's inference and not the source's statement.

kotobank-yatsuda

Encyclopedia entry s.v. 谷津田 / 谷地田, aggregated at kotobank (https://kotobank.jp/word/%E8%B0%B7%E6%B4%A5%E7%94%B0-400607)

What it is: The encyclopedia entry for 谷津田 / 谷地田 (yatsuda, the valley-bottom paddy) on kotobank, from Heibonsha's Sekai Dai-hyakka Jiten, describing paddy lying in the low ground of valleys cut into a plateau: wetland, opened up long ago, and in many cases dependent on rainfall because such valleys are not well supplied with water sources.

Why it applies, and its limits: Where our maps run fields up a valley floor, this is the field type they are, and the entry gives both the setting and its character - old, wet, and often rain-fed rather than fed by a channel, which bears on whether we draw a supply ditch to it. It is a tertiary summary describing a landform-and-field type rather than measuring one, so we take the character of the ground from it and no dimension.

kotobank-fukada

Digital Daijisen and Seisenban Nihon Kokugo Daijiten (Shogakukan), s.v. 深田, aggregated at kotobank (https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%B7%B1%E7%94%B0-123763)

What it is: Two Shogakukan dictionaries, the Digital Daijisen and the Seisenban Nihon Kokugo Daijiten, shown together on kotobank for the word 深田 (fukada), which both define as a paddy with deep mud, a swamp paddy, the opposite of a shallow one.

Why it applies, and its limits: A dictionary is the right authority for what a word means, and this word settles a distinction our paddy classes depend on: fukada names how deep the mud underfoot is, not where in the landscape the field sits. Nothing in these entries ties the word to a valley bottom, and none of them attaches a depth to it, so we use it for the sense of the term and take our depths from elsewhere.

fao-rice-water

Thierry Facon, "Water Management in Rice in Asia: Some Issues for the Future", FAO (https://www.fao.org/4/x6905e/x6905e0g.htm)

What it is: "Water Management in Rice in Asia: Some Issues for the Future", a paper by Thierry Facon published by the UN Food and Agriculture Organization, surveying how rice irrigation in Asia is run and where it is heading. Along the way it describes the ordinary mechanics of a paddy landscape: water passing from plot to plot so that irrigation and drainage cannot be told apart, and runoff being taken up again further down.

Why it applies, and its limits: That plot-to-plot cascade is the behavior our field layouts depend on, and it is a consequence of flat, bunded, gravity-fed paddies rather than of any modern works. The limits are that the paper is written about contemporary irrigation systems and their reform, and that what we draw from the cascade - which plot ends up wettest - is our own inference, labeled as such where we use it, not something the paper says.

cropfarming-soybeans

cropfarming.org, "What do soybeans look like growing in the field?" (https://cropfarming.org/what-do-soybeans-look-like-growing-in-the-field/)

What it is: A growers' advice page on cropfarming.org, an unsigned farming trade site, headed "What do soybeans look like growing in the field?" It describes the soybean plant as a field crop looks: an erect, bushy plant on a central stem, two to four feet tall, with broad, pointed, medium green leaflets.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it only for the shape and the color of the plant, so that a bean plot on a map is drawn as something a reader could tell from rice, and a plant's habit and leaf color are not much changed by the century. Its limits are that it is a commercial advice page with no named author, no references and an affiliate-link business model, so nothing on it can be traced to a study; that it describes modern varieties in the American Great Plains and Corn Belt, bred shorter and more even than the landraces an East Asian farmer would have sown, so we treat the height band as an approximation rather than a measurement of our setting's crop;

aze-standard

Japanese land-improvement standard designs for 畦畔 (paddy bunds) - Aomori prefecture ほ場整備 standard drawing set and 大和川流域水田貯留技術基準 (https://www.pref.aomori.lg.jp/soshiki/nourin/noson/files/H2904_nnzusyu_H2911syuusei.pdf)

What it is: Aomori Prefecture's standard drawings for consolidated paddy (2017 revision), a public-works design standard: the outer bund drawn as a trapezoid 30 cm high above the higher field, 30 cm wide at the crest, with side slopes of 1:1 to 1:1.2, in three types by the difference in level between the fields it separates, up to 4 m, with berms on the tallest.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives an engineered cross-section for a paddy bund in numbers, which calibrates the most repeated line on a paddy map against the one to two feet the period sources give. Its limits are that it is a post-war land-consolidation standard for machine-formed embankments between fields whose surfaces may differ by meters, a different object from a hand-piled ridge between two level plots, so its figures are a modern engineered minimum and not a measurement of a premodern bund; that a hand-piled bund would have been less regular; and that the enlarged cold-region bund an enlarged cold-region bund of about 50 by 40 cm is not on the Aomori sheet - the berms on it are for tall bunds, and its only frost-heave provision concerns a flume's subgrade - and the other standard the entry names is neither linked nor quoted, so that figure stands as a guess.

kubota-transplanting

Kubota, 稲作の歴史 - 田植え (https://www.kubota.co.jp/kubotatanbo/history/tools/transplanting.html)

What it is: A page from the public-education series on the history of Japanese rice growing published by Kubota, the farm machinery manufacturer, covering transplanting: how seedlings were set out by hand, and how the older scattered pattern the page calls 乱雑植え ("disorderly planting") gave way to marked straight rows laid out with ropes and rolled frames.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for the date at which straight-row transplanting spread and for the simple tools that marked the lines, which is what tells us our premodern paddies should still show the older irregular clumps rather than a ruled grid. It is a company's outreach page rather than scholarship, and it is written looking back from modern Japanese farming, so we take its sequence and its rough date and not any figure of yield or spacing.

maff-water-history

MAFF agricultural-water history PDF (https://www.maff.go.jp/j/nousin/mizu/kurasi_agwater/k_agri/pdf/detail_jp.pdf)

What it is: A twelve-page briefing deck of May 2012 from the Water Resources Division of the Rural Development Bureau of Japan's Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries, titled "On the history of agricultural water and water rights" (original: "農業用水の歴史と水利権について"). It sets out how the right to take irrigation water came to be settled in Japan, the other uses a village canal served besides watering the crop, and worked historical cases, among them the long quarrel over the Takina river in Iwate.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives a ministry's own account of how water disputes ran - on one Iwate river, thirty-six recorded quarrels, some of them deadly - which corroborates the frequency and the severity of the disputes our lore hooks draw on. Its limits are that it is a modern government briefing whose live argument is present-day water rights, with compressed historical cases whose own sources are not reproduced; that the detailed case is one valley in northern Japan; and that it says nothing about an upstream village's leverage over a downstream one - that rule rests on the irrigation study cited beside it, not on this deck.
