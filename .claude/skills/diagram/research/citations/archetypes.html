aburana-jawiki

ja.wikipedia アブラナ and zh.wikipedia 油菜 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/アブラナ)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article アブラナ and the Chinese Wikipedia article 油菜, both on rape (oilseed rape, the yellow-flowered brassica grown for oil and as a green vegetable). The Japanese page's calendar - seedlings transplanted before winter, the crop cut in March and April - is that of nabana, the flowering-greens vegetable grown as a market crop; the Chinese page describes rape sown into the harvested paddy and plowed in before the next transplanting, a present-day Taiwanese double-crop practice.

Why it applies, and its limits: Between them they give the shape of the rice-and-rape year - rape in the paddy over winter, cut in spring before the rice goes in - which is what lets a plot on our maps carry two crops. Their limits are that they are tertiary summaries; that the calendar we quote is a modern market-garden regime for the vegetable, transplanted at spacing, not the broadcast oilseed stand our maps draw, so its dates are taken as plausible for oilseed rape rather than shown for it; and that the green-manure practice is an explicitly modern Taiwanese one, not an attested premodern rotation.

What it is: A paper in the Chinese Journal of Eco-Agriculture (中国生态农业学报) by Liu Dan, Tian Jun and Duan Licheng on how a warming climate has shifted rice planting within the rice and rapeseed rotation of Jiangxi Province, built on weather records from 85 stations and concentrating on the years 2011 to 2022. It prints the district-by-district calendar: rapeseed harvested in early April in the south of the province and early May at Jiujiang, with rice transplanted from mid-April to early May.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives dated points in a plot's year for a rice-and-rapeseed rotation - rape harvested in early April in southern Jiangxi and early May at Jiujiang, rice transplanted from mid-April to early May - which is what decides what a plot on the map is carrying in a given month. Its limits are that it is a modern calendar, and the paper's own subject is that warming has moved these dates, so an older calendar sits later than the one it prints; that one southern Chinese province stands in for our rice country; and that it carries only the spring transitions - it gives neither the rice harvest month nor the rapeseed sowing window, which the record marks as unsourced.

Guangming Daily, 【这些文化遗存现今的模样87】二千多岁的桑基鱼塘仍未老 (赵玙, 侯楠楠), 2024-03-19 (https://epaper.gmw.cn/gmrb/html/2024-03/19/nw.D110000gmrb_20240319_7-01.htm)

It is a newspaper feature for a general reader rather than a study, its dating is the paper's own compressed summary, and the site it is standing in is a working heritage landscape today, so the pond it describes is a present-day one whose form we are treating as continuous with the old one.

fei-1939

Fei Hsiao-tung, Peasant Life in China: A Field Study of Country Life in the Yangtze Valley (Routledge, 1939) - https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.76327, full text

What it is: Peasant Life in China: A Field Study of Country Life in the Yangtze Valley by Fei Hsiao-tung (Routledge, 1939), the classic village ethnography of Kaixiangong, a silk-and-rice village on the lower Yangtze delta near Lake Tai. It records the place household by household: how the houses stand along the streams, what land a family held, boats, manure pits, and silkworms reared indoors on stacked trays.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a first-hand, room-by-room account of exactly the kind of settlement these maps draw, and almost everything we take from it - where the manure pit sits, that nearly every household kept a boat, that the village grew along its water, where rice was threshed - is arrangement rather than technology, and so belongs to the older village as much as to the one Fei walked through. The limits are real: it is 1936, with railways, motor roads and a silk economy already disrupted by mechanized filatures, and Fei chose this village because an industrial reform of its silk-making was under way there, which is a bias toward exactly the silk arrangements we take from it; his fieldwork was two months of one summer, by his own statement not a full year's cycle, so the seasonal claims we draw on are a summer observer's; it is one village in one delta rather than a survey; it is Chinese, standing beside the Japanese material for a setting built on both; and the copy we can read is a scanned text whose garbles the footnotes carry.

What it is: "The Circular Economy of the Dyke-Pond System" by Mae-Wan Ho, published on 9 October 2006 by the Institute of Science in Society.
