Citations: Ways: the research behind the roads, lanes, bridges and planks

The passages behind the footnotes of Ways: the research behind the roads, lanes, bridges and planks - first the works they quote and why each one counts, then the notes themselves. Hover a footnote number on that page and the note appears beside it; the numbers here are the same ones.

The works cited on this page

ritter-timber-bridges

Michael A. Ritter, Timber Bridges: Design, Construction, Inspection, and Maintenance, USDA Forest Service EM 7700-8 (1990), 944 pp (https://www.ltrc.la.gov/ltap/pdf/timber_bridges_design_construction_inspection_maintenance.pdf)

What it is: The United States Forest Service's standard manual for timber bridges, written by a Forest Service engineer: the timber itself, the design of beams, decks and railings, the kinds of abutment, and inspection and maintenance, with worked design examples running to 944 pages.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for the one thing our own record had only reasoned about, what a timber beam actually needs to bear on, and for what an abutment is there to do, which is to hold the roadway embankment. Its limits are large, and they are why it settles only half of the question it was fetched for: it is a modern manual, and its worked examples are highway-loaded glulam beams on spans up to about 94 feet, far heavier and longer than a plank over a farm ditch, so its bearing lengths are an upper bound and not a premodern measurement; and in 944 pages it never gives a distance from the water's edge to the abutment, which is the figure our maps actually draw.

lowtech-chinese-wheelbarrow

Low-Tech Magazine, "The Chinese wheelbarrow" (https://solar.lowtechmagazine.com/2011/12/the-chinese-wheelbarrow/)

What it is: A long illustrated article from Low-Tech Magazine, an independent magazine on pre-industrial and low-energy technology, on the Chinese one-wheeled barrow - what it carried, how it was built, and the road network that grew up around it rather than around the cart. It quotes and credits its two main authorities by name, Rudolf Hommel's 1937 fieldwork on Chinese hand tools and Joseph Needham's history of Chinese technology.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is what our whole picture of a Chinese country lane rests on - narrow paths wide enough for a walker and a barrow, cart tracks only on the eastern plains, and paths built for pedestrians, carrying-pole porters, barrow-pushers and litter-bearers - which decides the vehicle we draw a lane for. Its limits are that it is a popular magazine article rather than a peer-reviewed study, so it is a readable route to Hommel and Needham rather than a primary source itself; that it is an advocacy publication whose standing thesis is that pre-industrial transport was more capable than it is remembered to be, so it reads its evidence in that direction; that it carries no width measurement at all - the paths it describes are 「scarcely wide enough for foot passengers and wheelbarrows」, which is a description and not a figure, so the width we draw a lane at is ours and not the article's; that its observations are early twentieth-century fieldwork used as a witness to a much older practice; and that its cart-track sentence speaks for the eastern plains, not for the rice-growing south our settlements are modeled on.

toyama-1988-road-undevelopment

外山科夫, 二宮公紀 and 馬場俊介, "我が国の道路未整備の背景 - 江戸から昭和初期まで -" (The background to the undeveloped state of our country's roads, from Edo to the early Showa), 日本土木史研究発表会論文集 8 (1988) 96-103 (https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/journalhs1981/8/0/8_0_96/_pdf)

What it is: A refereed paper in the proceedings of Japan's civil engineering history conference asking why Japan built no roads for wheeled vehicles between the Edo period and the early twentieth century: what moved on the roads, what was forbidden on them, and what the prohibition was protecting.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the direct evidence for the countryside our lanes are drawn for - no horse-drawn carriage at all before Meiji, inhabitants on foot or on horseback, goods on a horse's back - It is also precise where the general account is loose: the highway cart prohibition was to keep the post-horse system supplied with horses, not to spare the road surface, and the causes it names include the kind of horse rather than the size of one. Its limits are that it speaks for Japan alone, one half of this setting; that it is a short conference paper rather than a monograph; and that it gives no road widths and no lane dimensions of any kind.

daihachiguruma-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 大八車 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/大八車)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the daihachiguruma, the large two-wheeled hand cart of Edo-period Japan - a tertiary encyclopedia entry covering what the cart was, who was allowed to use it, where it was allowed, and the smaller beka-guruma of the Kamigata region.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for one thing only, the legal confinement of the freight hand cart to Edo, Owari and Sunpu, which is what makes a cart the exception rather than the norm on a country lane, and for the article's note that freight carts were not permitted on the Tokaido until the Meiji period. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary rather than a document or a study; that it speaks for Japan alone, one half of this setting; and that it is about the regulated cities and highways, so it tells us what was forbidden outside them and nothing about what an ordinary village lane looked like.

stone-routes-enwiki

en.wikipedia, Stone routes (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stone_routes)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on the stone routes, the stone-paved ox-cart roads laid in Japan to carry goods and building materials into Kyoto - the stone is the road's surface, not its cargo - a tertiary entry that gives the road's construction: its width, the two rows of laid stones the wheels ran on and the earth track between them for the beasts.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the one measured wheeled way we could read for premodern Japan, 2.7 m of route with a 90 cm center for the bulls, which sets the upper bound our village lanes are kept under: if a purpose-built cart road was nine feet across, an ordinary farm track was not wider. Its limits are that it is a tertiary encyclopedia article citing no survey in the passage we quote; that a paved road built to carry freight into the capital is the most exceptional kind of way there is and is not evidence of what an ordinary lane measured; and that it is Japanese, while China is this setting's leading reference.

ctie-michi-nazenaze

株式会社建設技術研究所 (CTI Engineering Co., Ltd.), 道のなぜなぜ 1-4, "江戸時代の道路はどのようなものだったのですか？" (https://www.ctie.co.jp/service/publishing/img/michi-nazenaze/1-4.pdf)

What it is: A short explanatory sheet on Edo-period roads from a question-and-answer series published by a large Japanese civil engineering consultancy, covering the Five Highways, their construction and their width.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the width of the trunk highways in ken, and it is careful in exactly the way that mattered to us: it states the ordinary figure with mountain roads expressly excluded, so the two are not to be confused. Its limits are that it is a company's popular publication and cites no survey behind the number; that it is Japanese; that it gives no figure at all for the mountain roads it excludes, so that narrowing remains unsourced; and that a second readable account of the same highways gives a wider ordinary width, so the two bracket the figure rather than confirm it.

itabashi-kotobank

Encyclopedia entry s.v. 板橋, aggregated at kotobank (https://kotobank.jp/word/板橋)

What it is: The dictionary and encyclopedia entry for 板橋 (itabashi) at kotobank, which aggregates the major Japanese reference works, giving the word's plain sense: a bridge made of boards.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it to fix the name of the small crossing our maps draw over a farm ditch, so that the glyph and the word a reader clicks mean the same object - a planked deck and not an earthen one. Its limits are that it is a dictionary definition and nothing more: it gives no construction, no size, no period and no picture of how such a bridge was used, so everything else our entry says about the plank comes from elsewhere.

dobashi-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 土橋 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/土橋)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the dobashi, the earthen-decked bridge - a tertiary entry describing how one is built, logs laid side by side and earth thrown over and trodden down until the surface is level, and noting that in the Edo period plank bridges were limited to an important few while earthen bridges were the great majority.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the other half of the naming question: it tells us what a dobashi actually is, which is what lets our record say that the plank bridge and the earthen bridge are two different objects rather than two words for one. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary with no measurements and no dating of the technique, and that it is Japanese, so it names the Japanese form of a thing our maps place in a setting drawn from both China and Japan.

chinaknowledge-caoyun

ChinaKnowledge.de, "caoyun 漕運, tribute grain transport" (http://www.chinaknowledge.de/History/Terms/caoyun.html)

What it is: A reference page on caoyun, the imperial tribute-grain transport, from ChinaKnowledge.de, a scholar-run encyclopedia of Chinese history: what the grain was, which provinces produced it, the canal system that carried it, and the fleet and troops that worked it.

Why it applies, and its limits: It carries the fact our lanes depend on - that bulk goods in Ming China moved by water, in quantities no road could have taken - with the 1472 decree of four million shi a year to the canal terminal outside Beijing and the abandonment of the sea route. Its limits are that it is a tertiary reference page rather than a study, so its figures reach us without the gazetteer or archive behind them; that it describes the state's own grain traffic rather than ordinary trade; and that it does not characterize the feeder canals as a dense net; and that it says nothing about Japan, whose water freight stays uncited.

nrcs-ts14q-abutments

United States Department of Agriculture, Natural Resources Conservation Service, "Technical Supplement 14Q: Abutment Design for Small Bridges", National Engineering Handbook Part 654 (2007) (https://directives.nrcs.usda.gov/sites/default/files2/1712931168/7375.pdf)

What it is: A design supplement in the United States Department of Agriculture's engineering handbook covering the abutments of small rural bridges - the single-span, single-lane kind carried on timber stringers and simple strip footings - with the loads, the footing, the slope and what to do about scour.

Why it applies, and its limits: It describes the bridge our maps draw, and it states the mechanism that sets an abutment back from the water: a shallow strip footing cannot be sunk below the depth the current will scour, so the bank is armored instead and the footing kept clear of it. Its limits are that it is modern engineering guidance written for modern materials, loads and construction; that the only distance in it is an illustrative worked example rather than a rule, and is measured from the crest of the bank slope rather than from the water; and that it is American, standing in for a practice no premodern East Asian source we could read describes.

nippon-com-gokaido

nippon.com, 大名行列が通った道: 五街道と脇往還 (https://www.nippon.com/ja/japan-topics/c08604/)

What it is: A general-audience article from nippon.com, the news and culture site of a Japanese public-interest foundation, on the Five Highways and the secondary routes beside them, and on the daimyo processions that traveled them.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the second readable account of a trunk highway's width, and it disagrees with the first - 4 ken as the ordinary width, rising to 7 or 8 ken in places - which is why our record brackets the figure instead of stating one. Its limits are that it is journalism with no survey cited; that the explanation it offers for the width is a military one, which suits the greatest highways and not an ordinary road; and that it says nothing about mountain roads or about the barriers.
