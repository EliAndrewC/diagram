The works cited on this page

kashima-kainyo-1987

The 1987 (昭和62年) survey of Kashima (鹿島), Tonami city, 46 households, as reported by Tonami City's digital archive 砺波正倉, 「The homestead groves (yashikirin) of Tonami City」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「砺波市の屋敷林」) (https://1073shoso.jp/www/sankyo/detail.jsp?id=18666): 「According to a survey in Kashima, Tonami City (46 households) in Showa 62 (1987), of the 1,542 trees in all of 10 centimeters or more in diameter, 735 were sugi, accounting for 48 percent, about half of the whole, an average of 16 per household.」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「昭和６２年の砺波市鹿島（世帯数４６戸）での調査によると、直径１０センチ以上の全樹木数１，５４２本の内、スギは７３５本で全体の約半数の４８パーセントを占め、１戸当たり平均１６本であった」) - 1,542 trees of DBH >= 10 cm over 46 households (33.5 per homestead), 735 sugi (48%), 16 sugi per house. The primary 1987 report is not online; . (123,647 / 4,773 = 25.9 trees (>= ~3 m tall) per grove-owning household in 2020 - same order of magnitude as 33.5 trees >= 10 cm per homestead in 1987, consistent with the documented post-2000 decline (wind-throw, felling).); https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/esj/ESJ51/0/ESJ51_0_271/_article/-char/ja/ (Sugi as the dominant kainyo species in Tonami; ~18 woody species per homestead when shrubs are counted (the 1987 figure of ~6 species is for >= 10 cm trunks only).); https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jjsrt/46/1/46_154/_pdf/-char/ja (A single typical grove (S family, 2020) also has sugi as the dominant species by basal area; confirms a body of Tonami grove surveys from ~1975 onward and names the Research Institute bulletin where the 1987-era survey data would have been published.)

What it is: A 1987 survey of the homestead groves (yashikirin, locally kainyo) of Kashima in Tonami city, Toyama prefecture, counting the trees in the grounds of 46 households - 1,542 trees of at least 10 centimeters trunk diameter, of which 735 were sugi, an average of 16 sugi and about 33 trees per homestead - as reported by Tonami city's own digital archive 砺波正倉 in its account of the city's homestead groves.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the best hard count of a homestead grove's stand the record could read - trunks, species and heights, household by household - and it is what the size of our groves and the dominance of cedar in them rest on; a later city count and two published ecological studies of Tonami groves agree with it in order of magnitude, which is why we trust the scale rather than the exact number. Its limits are that it is a 1987 measurement of a living practice, and the same page says the composition had changed markedly over the preceding thirty years, with ornamental garden species brought in to replace the cedar, so a 1987 species list is already part modern garden; that the Tonami plain is the dispersed-farmstead snow country of Hokuriku, where an isolated farmstead's grove is a wind and snow break at the developed end of the Japanese range, not a national average nor the form a nucleated village takes; and that the primary 1987 report is not online - what a reader opens is a 1996 institute publication's excerpt on the city archive.

visit-toyama-sankyoson

Visit Toyama, the Tonami dispersed settlement (https://visit-toyama-japan.com/en/travel-inspiration/sankyoson)

What it is: The English page on the Tonami Plain dispersed settlement (散居村, sankyoson) from Visit Toyama, the tourism site for Toyama Prefecture. It describes an alluvial fan on which more than 7,000 farmhouses, each ringed by its own kainyo homestead grove, are scattered across roughly 220 square kilometers, a landscape it dates to more than five hundred years of formation.

Why it applies, and its limits: It describes the dispersed-farmstead country of the Tonami plain - thousands of farmhouses each standing in its own fields inside its own grove, and what the grove did for the house - which is the pattern one of our settlement forms is drawn from. Its limits are that it is a tourism organization's promotional page that cites no survey or study; that it describes a living landscape in the present tense and gives modern round counts, used here as an anchor for a premodern pattern; that it is one plain in one prefecture; and that the density our text gives, about thirty farmsteads to the square kilometer, is our own division of its two round figures and not a number the page states.

yashikirin-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 屋敷林 (居久根 redirects here) (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/屋敷林)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 屋敷林 (yashikirin, "homestead grove"), a tertiary encyclopedia entry on the belts of trees Japanese farmhouses planted around themselves against wind and snow, covering the regional names for the same thing - the northeastern igune (居久根) and the Izumo tsuijimatsu among them - along with the species used and the sides of the house they stand on.

Why it applies, and its limits: This is the backbone of how we plant a farmstead: which sides carry the grove (north and west against the wind, the east left open for the entrance and its ornamental garden), which trees make it up (sugi, matsu, hinoki and keyaki), and the part that turns a fact into a choice - that the Izumo form once enclosed the whole circumference of a house and was later cut back to a hook covering only the north and west, which is why our maps treat a full ring and a two-sided belt as two correct answers rather than one. It is a tertiary summary rather than a survey, so it gives us forms and species names and not counts or measurements, and the practice is Japanese and regional, the north-and-west rule following a wind that a warmer corner of the setting need not share.

isa-auf-fukugi-2011

Chen and Nakama, on the Fukugi homestead windbreaks of Okinawa, Arboriculture & Urban Forestry 37(1) (https://auf.isa-arbor.com/content/37/1/19) - "House-embracing Fukugi trees were functionally laid out with denser forests on the northern side of the houses and on the sides facing the sea"; "the greatest number of large Fukugi trees stand to the north of the house, followed by the eastern side"

What it is: A peer-reviewed article by Chen and Nakama on the Fukugi homestead windbreaks of Okinawa, in Arboriculture & Urban Forestry volume 37 issue 1 (2011). The authors surveyed the old Fukugi trees still standing around houses in several villages, recording their heights and which side of the house each one stands on.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for the shape and orientation of a homestead windbreak - a line of trees set around the house, thickest on the sides the damaging wind comes from, most of the large trees to the north and then to the east - which is how the shelter belts around our farmsteads are drawn. Its limits: it measures trees that were planted long before the survey, so it records a surviving form rather than a documented historical practice; and Okinawa is subtropical and typhoon-swept, standing in here for the main islands of Japan, so the species and the exact wind sides are local while the principle we carry over is shelter on the weather side.

kitamoto-mushiro-niwa

北本市史 民俗編, the farm yard measured in straw mats (https://kdas.jp/test2/detail_display.php?t_cd=1&acc_cd=3&aclc_cd=0&chap=6&hp_page=74&bc_cd=1)

What it is: A page of 北本市史 民俗編, the folklore volume of the published city history of Kitamoto in Saitama prefecture, read in that city's own digital archive. The passage records local farming custom, among it how big a farmhouse's work yard was, counted in the straw mats that could be laid out on it, with the conversion of two mats to one tsubo.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the one source we could read that states a farm work yard's size directly rather than leaving it to be inferred from a house plan, and it adds the reason the size varies - the yard tracked how much grain the household had to thresh and dry - which is why our yards scale with the holding. Its limits are era and place: a twentieth-century folklore volume recording remembered practice in one district of the Kanto plain, whose crop was barley, and a remembered "usually forty to sixty mats" is the range of a custom rather than a survey measurement.

yonekura-mushiro

Yonekura district museum note, Okayama, on the straw mats a farm kept (https://townweb.e-okayamacity.jp/yonegura/minna/hakubutukan/hakubutu012.htm)

What it is: An exhibit page from the 米倉町内博物館, a small neighborhood museum of old farm, fishing and household tools kept by residents of the Yonekura district of Okayama City and published on the city's community web pages. The page describes the mushiro, the woven rice-straw mat - how it was made on a mat loom, its size, and what a farm did with it. No author or date is given.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for a single household number, that a farm kept about fifty straw mats, which matters because mats spread out to sun-dry grain need floor space and a work yard has to be drawn large enough to hold them. Its value is that it is an independent local account agreeing with a count recorded elsewhere; its limits are what kind of thing it is - a community museum's note written from local memory rather than a survey - and how narrow it is, speaking for one district of Okayama and for the last farming generations before machines took the work over.

tobunken-mushiro

今石みぎわ, むしろ織り, Tokyo National Research Institute for Cultural Properties (https://tobunken.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/3166/files/06_55_Imaishi.pdf)

What it is: 今石みぎわ (Imaishi Migiwa), "莚と莚織りの技術" (The straw mat and the technique of mat weaving), a research report in the National Research Institute for Cultural Properties, Tokyo's series on intangible cultural heritage, 2012, freely downloadable. Half of it records the craft as it survived in a three-household hamlet in Yamaguchi, measured and photographed from 2010; the other half is a documentary study of mat looms in Edo-period sources - the Kaga agricultural treatise of 1707, the illustrated encyclopedia of 1713, a craftsmen's picture scroll and a farmhouse survey of about 1830 - and of the Meiji machines that replaced them.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the measured size of the ordinary straw mat - about three shaku by six, 90 by 180 cm - which is what turns a count of mats in a period document into an area on our maps. Its limits are that the measurement is of a present-day weaving of a craft that was once universal, so it is a modern measurement of a premodern form; the same paper, though, carries the premodern corroboration - the 1707 Kaga treatise it quotes gives the same three-shaku width, and the paper's point is that the loom was essentially unchanged from at least the early 1700s - so the figure is better founded than a single village's practice would be. The one size variation it reports is the Ryukyu rush mat at three shaku two sun.

ndl-kokumori

National Diet Library reference on 石盛 (the assessed yield per tan) (https://crd.ndl.go.jp/reference/entry/index.php?id=1000296656&page=ref_view)

What it is: Another entry in the National Diet Library's Collaborative Reference Database, on kokumori (石盛), the assessed yield per tan of land fixed at the cadastral survey. It gives the grades that had largely settled into use: 1.5 koku for upper paddy, 1.3 for middle, 1.1 for lower and 0.9 for the poorest, while noting that this varied by region.

Why it applies, and its limits: The middle-paddy figure is the first input to the yard area we derive from a household's crop, so we need a period number for what a tan of ordinary paddy was reckoned to yield, and this is one. Two honest limits: it is a tax assessment rather than a measured harvest, and the entry itself says the numbers differed from region to region, so we use the middle grade as a plausible ordinary case and not as a precise yield.

irri-drying-floor

IRRI Rice Knowledge Bank, sun drying and drying-floor area (https://www.knowledgebank.irri.org/grainQuality/module_4/popups/pu_drying.htm)

What it is: A page from the Rice Knowledge Bank of the International Rice Research Institute, the agricultural research organization based in the Philippines, explaining how paddy is dried in the sun: how thin the grain is spread and how much floor a given tonnage of it needs.

Why it applies, and its limits: It carries the only plain statement of area we could find on a public page - grain spread a few centimeters deep, five hundred square meters of drying floor handling six tons of paddy - a depth-to-area ratio from which the size of the drying yard in front of a house is derived. Its limits: this is modern extension advice for today's farmers and varieties, written in the tropics, and we flag it as such wherever it is used; and its ratio is for a whole lot spread at once on a hard floor, while our households dry on racks first and spread in batches, so the yard on the map is a fraction of the ratio's answer, worked out in the record rather than taken from the page. Sun-drying grain on a floor is nonetheless an old technique whose arithmetic does not change with the century, so we take the ratio from it and nothing else.

kamikanai-1771-houses

上金井村 (Meiwa 8, 1771) main-house and holding histograms, 日本建築学会論文報告集 418 (https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/aijax/418/0/418_KJ00004075360/_pdf)

What it is: 津田良樹 (Tsuda Yoshiki), a peer-reviewed paper in the Architectural Institute of Japan's journal of planning (1990), on the size and plan of Edo-period farmhouses as recorded in the billeting documents made when the shogun's procession to Nikko was lodged along the road: five villages in Shimotsuke across three periods, about thirty houses each, with the houses' sizes read from the procession records and, for Kamikanai in 1771, each household's assessed holding from the village's tax ledger beside them.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the shape of the whole house-size distribution of a village, from a documentary base that counts the small and the poor whom surviving buildings leave out, which is what our farmhouse sizes are spread by. Its limits are that these are highway villages, chosen because they lodged the shogunal procession, not a random draw; that the coupling of house size to holding - about ten tsubo more for five koku more - is stated for Iwahara in the Kyoho period, while at Kamikanai the paper finds the relation not marked, with sizable houses among the landless, so the two findings must be kept apart as the research page keeps them; and that it is one province of eastern Japan.

kikoba-kenchi

木古庭村 (Miura, Sagami) Bunroku 1594 and Genroku 1699 検地帳, 日本建築学会計画系論文集 222 (https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/aijsaxx/222/0/222_KJ00003747888/_pdf)

What it is: A 1974 research paper in the Transactions of the Architectural Institute of Japan (number 222), the second part of a study called "近世村方文書よりみた相模国三浦郡木古庭村の屋敷と家屋" ("The homesteads and houses of Kikoba village, Miura district, Sagami province, seen through early-modern village documents"). It works through that one village's own paperwork, including a 1594 land survey and a homestead-area register submitted to the magistrate in 1691, to reconstruct how large peasant house plots and houses were and who held them.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the one thing a homestead layout most needs and can least often find - the measured spread of house-lot areas across a whole village, from a register of 1699 - so the sizes and the spread of the plots on our maps rest on it; it also shows that the small end of the distribution was occupied, since every household in the village owned a main house. Its limits are that it is one village of 58 households in one province, so the spread is a pattern rather than a national rate; that the lots run from about 20 to 100 bu with peaks at 30 and 50, and the paper gives no separate figure for the great holders' lots - its "twice" and "three times" count buildings per household, not lot area - so any larger lot on our maps is our reading and not its measurement; and that the public copy is a scanned Japanese paper we read for its tables and its stated figures.

santome-shinden-allotment

三富新田 (Genroku 9, 1696) founding allotment, 日本建築学会論文報告集 436 (https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/aijax/436/0/436_KJ00004077200/_pdf)

What it is: A 1992 peer-reviewed paper by Yoshiro Yamamori in the Journal of the Architectural Institute of Japan, Planning Series, no. 436, on the row settlements of the Musashino plateau west of Tokyo. It reconstructs how Santome Shinden (三富新田), a planned reclamation colony finished in 1696, was surveyed and handed out: every settler got a long strip running back from the road, with a thatched house set at the road end of it.

Why it applies, and its limits: Our homesteads normally vary in size, and this paper is the attested counter-case for the setting where they do not, because a planned colony issued every household an identical house lot even though the finished strips themselves came out uneven. Its limits are that it is a modern architectural study reading the survey record rather than an Edo document, and that it describes one deliberately planned colony on one plateau, so it is evidence for what a laid-out settlement looked like and never for an ordinary village that grew on its own.

kayabuki-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 茅葺 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E8%8C%85%E8%91%BA)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on kayabuki (茅葺), thatched roofing, describing the materials used, how a thatch roof is built and maintained, and the constraints the material puts on the roof's form.

Why it applies, and its limits: Our farmhouses are thatched, and this article gives the physical reason their roofs are drawn steep: thatch is pitched steeply to shed rain, and the coarser and thicker the stems the wider the gaps and so the steeper the pitch required. The limit is that it gives no number - no angle, no degrees - so the specific pitch on our roofs is our own choice within a requirement the article states only in words, and as a tertiary summary its claims are ones we take where its own references support them.

sendai-igune-list

Sendai City, 居久根 conservation page, which links the city's PDF list of trees suited to an igune (https://www.city.sendai.jp/ryokuchihozen/kurashi/shizen/midori/hyakunen/hozen.html; )

What it is: The city of Sendai's conservation page for igune, the farm windbreak groves of the Sendai plain, which links the city's own PDF list of tree species suited to one, sorted by height: tall trees of 15 m and over (sugi, black pine, the hinoki group, keyaki), sub-canopy and middle trees around 10 m, and shrubs around 3 m.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the species and the height classes of a real windbreak grove - tall trees of fifteen meters and more, a middle story around ten, shrubs around three - which is what sets the drawn height and the mix of our homestead belts. Its limits are that it is a modern city's conservation list, guidance about what to plant and keep rather than a survey of what stands; that it is one region's list - the cold, snowy, salt-windy Sendai coastal plain, where the black pine is a coastal species - standing in for a windbreak anywhere in the setting; and that the list is a PDF that does not render as text for every reader who follows the link.

minami-2022-igune

Minami, Yonezawa and Okaze 2022, the Osaki igune flow-field study, J-STAGE (https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jass/38/2/38_37/_article/-char/en) - : the abstract places the 10 m igune "on the prevailing wind direction side" and names no compass side

What it is: Kento Minami, Chinatsu Yonezawa and Tsubasa Okaze, a 2022 paper in the Journal of the Japanese Agricultural Systems Society (open access on J-Stage), which models the wind around one dispersed farmstead's igune - its homestead grove - in Osaki, Miyagi: a computer simulation of the flow field over a digital model of the site built from drone imagery, with the trees represented as a canopy. The paper describes the grove as standing on the west and north sides of the site, against the west-northwest winter wind, with tall cedar and dense bamboo on the west.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives a measured-looking picture of what a homestead belt does to the wind and, in its description of the site, which sides the belt stands on, which is what our belts' height and siting rest on. Its limits are that it is a computer simulation of the flow rather than a measurement, with only the geometry taken from the site; that it is one modeled house, so "west and north" is that farmstead's arrangement against that site's wind and not a general rule; and that the belt modeled is a surviving modern one, which the paper itself says has been in decline since the 1970s.

magariya-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 曲屋 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%9B%B2%E3%82%8A%E5%AE%B6)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the magariya (曲屋), the L-shaped farmhouse of northern Japan in which the stable is built onto the dwelling as a wing of the same roof.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the L-shaped farmhouse with the stable under the same roof, projecting from the dwelling and warmed by the kitchen hearth, which is what our cold-country farmhouse variant is drawn from. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary, though a well-cited one; that it is a regional house type of Iwate, not the ordinary farmhouse of the country; that the stable's projection from the SOUTH face is the article's description of the Tono basin in particular, where the article also records both right- and left-handed forms, so the compass side is a local observation and not a property of the type; and that the article associates the form with the upper stratum of the village - headmen and prosperous farmers - so drawing it as the ordinary farmhouse would misstate its standing.

desire-path-enwiki

en.wikipedia Desire path (cites Hampton and Cole 1988 for the fifteen-passage figure) (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desire_path)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article "Desire path", a general account of the informal tracks worn by people walking the route they prefer rather than the one laid out for them. It states that as few as fifteen passages over a site can be enough to make a distinct trail, and that such a path usually takes the shortest or most easily walked line, citing Hampton and Cole's 1988 handbook Soft Paths.

Why it applies, and its limits: Our maps draw the worn tracks between a farmhouse, its yard and its fields, and this tells us how little traffic it takes to make one visible and what line it follows around a slope or an obstacle, which is exactly the rule we needed. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary, and that the fifteen-passage figure comes from a modern wilderness-recreation handbook about soft ground in American backcountry rather than from any study of a premodern farmyard, so we use it as an order of magnitude and not as a threshold.

ninety-nine-pi-desire

99% Invisible on desire paths (https://99percentinvisible.org/article/least-resistance-desire-paths-can-lead-better-design/)

What it is: An article from 99% Invisible, the American design magazine and podcast, on desire paths - the informal tracks that people wear across open ground where the built route does not go, which the article says can form after as few as fifteen crossings.

Why it applies, and its limits: It corroborates the reading that a path is where feet have found the shortest or easiest line - a mechanism of walking that is not bound to a period - which is the only thing we take from it. Its limits are that it is a design magazine's popular article, modern, Western and non-scholarly, with no East Asian or premodern content; that it supplies none of our distances; and that the one number the record quotes from it, that a path forms after about fifteen crossings, is given there without attribution, so the number's anchor is the 1988 study the record names separately, not the magazine.

pmc7538448-levee

"Earthworm species and density in semi-natural grasslands on rice paddy levees in Japanese satoyama", PMC 7538448 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7538448/)

What it is: A research paper available through PubMed Central, "Earthworm species and density in semi-natural grasslands on rice paddy levees in Japanese satoyama", which counts earthworms in the grassy banks between paddy fields and, in describing its study ground, sets out what those levees are and how farmers keep them.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is fieldwork on Japanese paddies, and it states plainly what a levee is for - holding the water in and carrying the farmer and their tools between fields - and that the banks are kept as mown grass, which is exactly the paddy edge our maps draw. Its limits are that it is a modern study whose real subject is soil ecology rather than farming history, and that its account of upkeep includes present-day herbicide use, so we take from it the levee's purpose and its cut grassy surface and not the details of modern management.

paddy-field-enwiki

en.wikipedia Paddy field (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paddy_field)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on the paddy field, a tertiary summary of flooded rice cultivation, its history across Asia and the physical form of the fields themselves.

Why it applies, and its limits: One sentence in it, describing prehistoric Korean paddies laid out as squares and rectangles separated by bunds about 10 cm high, is where the height of the low earth walls our maps draw between plots comes from. Its limits are kind and place: an encyclopedia is a tertiary summary whose figures we accept only where its own references support them, and the example is Korean and very early rather than Japanese, cited because it is a readable public statement of a bund height and because a bund's height is set by how deep a flooded plot is held, which varies little across the rice-growing world.

fao-ac180e-rice-fish

FAO, Fish Culture in Rice Fields, chapter 7 "Culture Operations" (https://www.fao.org/4/ac180e/AC180E07.htm) - "the height of the dry bund should be at least 25 cm, but a height of 60 cm is recommended". , and the basin-irrigation chapter (fao.org/4/s8684e/s8684e03.htm) speaks of "permanent bunds" of 60-90 cm and never of a "dry bund"

What it is: A chapter of Fish Culture in Rice Fields, a technical manual published by the UN Food and Agriculture Organization on raising fish in flooded rice paddies. The "Culture Operations" chapter covers how such a field is prepared and managed, including the earth bund that holds the water in, which it says should stand at least 25 cm high and better still 60 cm.

Why it applies, and its limits: A paddy bund is a wall of piled mud whose height is set by how deep the water must stand, which has nothing to do with modern machinery, so the figure carries back to a premodern field. The honest limits are that this is a present-day extension manual rather than a historical record - the same chapter assumes modern fertilizer, pesticides and bred varieties; that it was written for tropical fish-farming projects in Africa and Southeast Asia rather than for a Chinese or Japanese paddy; and that a bund built to keep fish in is taller than one built only to keep water in, so we treat its 60 cm as the upper end of the range a paddy bund occupies rather than as a measured premodern height.

sugiura-1973-fuzoku

Sugiura Tadashi, 「On the outbuildings of farmhouses in rural settlements: the case of Miyazaki town, Miyagi prefecture」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「農村集落における農家の付属建物について - 宮城県宮崎町の例」), Tōhoku Chiri 25(3): 145-152, 1973 (https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/tga1948/25/3/25_3_145/_article/-char/ja)

What it is: A peer-reviewed geography article by Sugiura Tadashi, "農村集落における農家の付属建物について - 宮城県宮崎町の例" ("On the outbuildings of farmhouses in rural settlements: the case of Miyazaki town, Miyagi prefecture"), published in Tōhoku Chiri 25(3), 1973, pages 145-152. It counts, household by household, which outbuildings each farmstead in the surveyed town actually had, and what stood inside the older main houses.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a household-by-household count of the outbuildings of three hamlets, by function, which is the strongest kind of evidence the record has for what stood on a farmstead and is what populates our farmyards. Its limits are that the survey was made in July 1972, a quarter century after the postwar land reform and well into mechanization, when the draft ox and horse had largely left Tohoku farms, so the barn and livestock-shed frequencies are a floor rather than a premodern figure and some sheds are twentieth-century equipment; that it is the cold, snowy northeast and not the whole country; that it is three hamlets of one town, 87 households, a sample the author himself calls unusually rich in outbuildings; and that its table gives average counts of buildings per household, which the record reads as the share of households owning one - the two agree only where no household kept two.

ido-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 井戸 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/井戸) - the shaft's lining: 「If the ground is firm a bare dug shaft will do, but where it is liable to collapse a well lining (idogawa, 井戸側) becomes necessary.」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「地盤が堅固であれば素掘りでもよいが、崩れやすい場合には井戸側が必要となる」)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 井戸 (ido, "well"), covering how wells in Japan are dug, lined and owned.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for the construction of the shaft that the well glyph in a yard stands for - firm ground will hold a bare dug shaft, while ground liable to collapse needs a lining (idogawa) - and for the related point that wells were often held jointly rather than by one household, because digging one was expensive.
