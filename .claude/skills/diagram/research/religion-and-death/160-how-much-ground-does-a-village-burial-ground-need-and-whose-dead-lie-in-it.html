What the research found. Edo Japan actually buried PER SETTLEMENT. A Fukui record of the funeral custom puts the sanmai, the cremation and burial place, at the edge of the hamlet while the visiting graves stand in two temples inside it, and the Japanese graveyard vocabulary keeps a category for exactly that ground: the graveyard a village settlement's own inhabitants hold in common and manage themselves. The form is the usual one - most often a graveyard held in common by each settlement, its ground sometimes divided into plots by household or by age and sometimes under no rule at all - and a national folklore survey counted the settlements keeping the two-grave custom prefecture by prefecture: 60 in Shiga, 59 in Nara, 45 in Mie, 42 in Kyoto, 39 each in Hyogo and Fukui, 22 in Shizuoka, few elsewhere. Those counts are of the two-grave custom rather than of village graveyards in general, they lean heavily to the Kinki region, and they count settlements confirmed at survey time in the twentieth century, so they are a floor rather than a rate. Whether a twelve- to fifteen-household hamlet kept one, and the grandfathering that leaves them standing today, are in no page read. The two-grave regions (ryobosei) formalize the split: the body goes to a burial place mostly of earth burial, often in mountain forest away from habitation, while the visiting grave - a stone pagoda holding neither body nor bone, raised for grave-visiting at Bon and for the memorial of the ancestors - commonly stands inside a temple's precinct. Ming and Qing China is the deliberate contrast - family graves sited by feng shui and scattered through the farmland, with communal ground only as pauper charity; a farm survey of the 1920s (buck-1930-farm-economy) found graves on 2.6 per cent of the farm land of its five level-land localities, and on 9.1 per cent of the fertile land of one of them, where, with no hill land to take them, they stood anywhere in a field whatever the trouble to the plow. The Chinese burial-ground measure here is the Qing charity graveyards in How much ground does a grave take, and how large is a burial ground drawn? So the single discrete communal ground these maps draw is the Japanese surface convention, which is the setting's China-geography, Japan-surface split working as intended.

Two things make one central ground MORE defensible here than in historical Japan. The setting cremates and then inters, and an urn is carried to the district ground in a way a corpse procession is not. And a hamlet's own patch would be tiny - roughly 750 to 2,450 sq ft for full-body burial, less for urns (a GUESS: this project's own rough reckoning of a hamlet's deaths over a 30-year reuse at the grave sizes below, which no page read gives), which is a blob thirty to fifty ft across on a village sheet - legitimately below the resolution of these maps, so a hamlet that draws nothing stays honest even where local patches exist in the fiction.

The decision. The drawn village ground is the marked, ritually active DISTRICT ground, and it is sized to the district rather than to the village that hosts it: about 800 inhabitants, at roughly 25 to 30 deaths per 1,000 a year, with a plot reused after about 30 years, gives some 600 to 720 active urn plots; at 10 to 20 sq ft each that is about 0.15 to 0.30 acre. The plot figure is of the order the graves excavated at an Edo temple graveyard reach: jar burials in wooden burial chambers run 85 cm to 1.4 m along the major axis, and the simple jar burials and wooden coffins, the kind that match the graves of farmers found in villages, 65 cm to 2.3 m, most of them under 1.4 m. One excavation does give a density: a 40 m² trench at a temple graveyard in Edo's Honjo held the traces of ten graves, one box coffin and nine barrel coffins - about 4 m², some 43 sq ft, a grave. That is the trench rather than the whole ground, and those are body burials in coffins rather than the packed urn plots drawn here, so it sits above the 10 to 20 sq ft band and corroborates the Chinese full-body figure there (about 50 sq ft a grave in a Qing charity ground) instead; the step from a grave's size to an urn plot's footing is still this project's own. Sizing the same ground to the village's own roughly 350 inhabitants would give about half of that, and it is the district the ground serves. A planner's reckoning of 1934 by households rather than by urns lands inside the same band; it is at How much ground does a grave take, and how large is a burial ground drawn?, and how far from the houses the ground lies is at How far from its houses does a village bury its dead, and on which side?

The rule the map follows: a village draws ONE burial ground, between about 0.12 and 0.38 acre (the district band with glyph rounding allowed both ways), and a ground sized only to the village itself is too small. A hamlet draws none: its dead go to the village district's ground, just as it has no shrine and no headman. The ladder rises steadily with the population served - the district's ~800 sits below a town's ~1,200, and 0.15 to 0.30 acre nests under a town's 0.25 to 0.75.
