What the research found. Edo Japan actually buried PER SETTLEMENT. A Fukui record of the funeral custom puts the sanmai, the cremation and burial place, at the edge of the hamlet while the visiting graves stand in two temples inside it,59 and the Japanese graveyard vocabulary keeps a category for exactly that ground: the graveyard a village settlement's own inhabitants hold in common and manage themselves.60 The form is the usual one - most often a graveyard held in common by each settlement, its ground sometimes divided into plots by household or by age and sometimes under no rule at all - and a national folklore survey counted the settlements keeping the two-grave custom prefecture by prefecture: 60 in Shiga, 59 in Nara, 45 in Mie, 42 in Kyoto, 39 each in Hyogo and Fukui, few elsewhere.61 Those counts are of the two-grave custom rather than of village graveyards in general, they lean heavily to the Kinki region, and they count settlements confirmed at survey time in the twentieth century, so they are a floor rather than a rate. Whether a twelve- to fifteen-household hamlet kept one, and the grandfathering that leaves them standing today, are in no page read.94 The two-grave regions (ryobosei) formalize the split: the body goes to a burial place mostly of earth burial, often in mountain forest away from habitation, while the visiting grave - a stone pagoda holding neither body nor bone, raised for grave-visiting at Bon and for the memorial of the ancestors - commonly stands inside a temple's precinct.62 Ming and Qing China is the deliberate contrast - family graves sited by feng shui and scattered through the farmland, with communal ground only as pauper charity;116 Buck's pre-mechanization farm survey of Chinese farming of 1929-33 (buck-survey) is said to put some 2% of ALL farm area under graves, and no readable copy of it carries the figure, so it is carried here as a guess.63 The Chinese burial-ground measure here is the Qing charity graveyards below. So the single discrete communal ground these maps draw is the Japanese surface convention, which is the setting's China-geography, Japan-surface split working as intended.

Two things make one central ground MORE defensible here than in historical Japan. The setting cremates and then inters, and an urn is carried to the district ground in a way a corpse procession is not. And a hamlet's own patch would be tiny - roughly 750 to 2,450 sq ft for full-body burial, less for urns, which is a blob thirty to fifty ft across on a village sheet - legitimately below the resolution of these maps, so a hamlet that draws nothing stays honest even where local patches exist in the fiction.

The decision. The drawn village ground is the marked, ritually active DISTRICT ground, and it is sized to the district rather than to the village that hosts it: about 800 inhabitants, at roughly 25 to 30 deaths per 1,000 a year, with a plot reused after about 30 years, gives some 600 to 720 active urn plots; at 10 to 20 sq ft each that is about 0.15 to 0.30 acre.139 The plot figure is of the order the excavated Edo commoner graves themselves reach: jar burials in wooden burial chambers run 85 cm to 1.4 m along the major axis, and the wooden coffin graves that match the graves found in villages 1 to 2.4 m.64 One excavation does give a density: a 40 m² trench at a commoner temple graveyard in Edo's Honjo held the traces of ten graves, one box coffin and nine barrel coffins - about 4 m², some 43 sq ft, a grave.65 That is the trench rather than the whole ground, and those are body burials in coffins rather than the packed urn plots drawn here, so it sits above the 10 to 20 sq ft band and corroborates the Chinese full-body figure instead; the step from a grave's size to an urn plot's footing is still this project's own. Sizing the same ground to the village's own roughly 350 inhabitants would give about half of that, and it is the district the ground serves.

The rule the map follows: a village draws ONE burial ground, between about 0.12 and 0.38 acre (the district band with glyph rounding allowed both ways), and a ground sized only to the village itself is too small. A hamlet draws none: its dead go to the village district's ground, just as it has no shrine and no headman. The ladder stays monotone with the population served - the district's ~800 sits below a town's ~1,200, and 0.15 to 0.30 acre nests under a town's 0.25 to 0.75.
