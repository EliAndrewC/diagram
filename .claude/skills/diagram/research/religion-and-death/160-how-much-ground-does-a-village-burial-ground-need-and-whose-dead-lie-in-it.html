Whose ground it is. Why the maps draw a village one communal burial ground at all is How our maps choose where a village's and a hamlet's dead lie. This question is how large that ground is, and which households it serves.

What a hamlet's own ground would be. The setting cremates and then inters, so what a hamlet carries to a distant ground is an urn rather than a body. A hamlet's own patch would be small - roughly 750 to 2,450 sq ft for full-body burial, less for urns (a GUESS: this project's own rough reckoning of a hamlet's deaths over a 30-year reuse at the grave sizes below, which no page read gives), a ground thirty to fifty ft across, which is drawable at the hamlet tier's scale. The decision. The drawn village ground is the marked, ritually active ground of the village, and it is sized to the population it serves: the village's own households and those of the hamlets whose dead lie in it, so between the village's own ~350 inhabitants and the whole district's ~800. At roughly 25 to 30 deaths per 1,000 a year (the rate of Edo villages, below), with a plot reused after about 30 years (the reuse period is this project's choice; no page read gives one), a population of P keeps 0.75 to 0.9 P active urn plots; at 10 to 20 sq ft each that is P × 7.5 to 18 sq ft - some 260 to 320 plots and about 0.06 to 0.15 acre for the village's own ~350, some 600 to 720 plots and about 0.14 to 0.33 acre for the district's ~800. The plot figure is of the order the graves excavated at an Edo temple graveyard reach: jar burials in wooden burial chambers run 85 cm to 1.4 m along the major axis, and the simple jar burials and wooden coffins, the wooden-coffin graves among them the same as the graves of farmers found in villages, 65 cm to 2.3 m, most of them under 1.4 m. One excavation does give a density: a 40 m² excavation at a former temple graveyard in Higashi-Komagata, in Tokyo's Sumida ward, held the traces of ten graves, one box coffin and nine barrel coffins - about 4 m², some 43 sq ft, a grave. That is the trench rather than the whole ground, and those are body burials in coffins rather than the packed urn plots drawn here, so it sits above the 10 to 20 sq ft band and corroborates instead the Chinese full-body figure of about 50 sq ft a grave in a Qing charity ground, given at the grave-size question below; the step from a grave's size to an urn plot's footing is still this project's own. A modern check, not a ground for the size: a planner's reckoning of 1934 for city cemeteries, by households rather than by urns - one family plot of about 36 sq ft a household, at about five inhabitants a household, times 1.5 to 2 for the paths, or P × 10.8 to 14.4 sq ft - lands inside the same band at both ends (about 0.09 to 0.12 acre at 350, 0.2 to 0.26 at 800); it is at How much ground does a grave take, and how large is a burial ground drawn?, and how far from the houses the ground lies is at How far from its houses does a village bury its dead, and on which side?

The death rate, from Edo villages' own registers. Three farming villages of the Nihonmatsu domain, followed in their population registers from the early 1700s to 1870, died at about 30 per 1,000 a year in the decade of the Tenmei famine and 29 in that of the Tenpo famine, and at about 18 in the decades after them. The birth rates estimated from the same kind of register run from 18.4 to 31.9 per 1,000 across seven villages between 1671 and 1871, while the country's surveyed population stayed nearly flat from 1721 to 1846, falling in the three great famines and growing in ordinary years; over those years, then, deaths ran close to births. One Mino village's registers give a life expectancy at birth of about 37 years for 1773 to 1869, with its infant mortality assumed rather than counted, and a population that neither grows nor shrinks loses each year one over its mean length of life - about 27 per 1,000. So the premodern figure the ground is calibrated to is about 20 to 30 deaths per 1,000 in ordinary years, more in a famine decade, and the 25 to 30 used here sits in its upper half. The grave sizes are Edo too; the reuse period and the step to an urn plot are this project's own, and the hamlet's figure is the same arithmetic, a guess.

The rule the map follows: a village draws ONE burial ground, sized to the population P it serves - its own inhabitants and those of the hamlets whose dead lie in it - at P × 7.5 to 18 sq ft, with glyph rounding allowed both ways: about 0.06 to 0.15 acre when it serves the village's own ~350, about 0.14 to 0.33 acre when it serves the whole district's ~800. A hamlet's dead lie either in a burial ground of its own at its edge, about 750 to 2,450 sq ft (a guess), or in the village's ground, rolled per hamlet (How our maps choose where a village's and a hamlet's dead lie). The ladder rises steadily with the population served - the district's ~800 sits below a town's ~1,200, and the village ground's band nests under a town's 0.25 to 0.75 acre.
