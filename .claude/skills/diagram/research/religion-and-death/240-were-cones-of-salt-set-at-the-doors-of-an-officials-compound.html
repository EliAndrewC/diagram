Were cones of salt set at the doors of an official's compound?

Sources: kotobank-morijio (salt heaped at the doorways of the customer trades, first recorded about 1838-40), jawiki-morijio (the heap's shape), ise-miyachu-morijio (a pair, outside the door)

The small heap of salt at a doorway, morijio, is recorded in the late Edo period, and where it is recorded is telling: the standard historical dictionary of Japanese places it at the doorways of the customer trades - restaurants, assignation houses and variety halls - set out for good fortune, and its earliest cited use is in a popular novel of about 1838-40. Today the salt is pressed into a cone or a three-sided pyramid, at the entrance or inside the house, a shape the article describes from present-day practice and does not date, and at an entrance today it commonly stands outside, one heap on each side, though that is a seller of ritual goods describing present-day custom, not a record of the Edo period.

No page read puts morijio at the gate of a samurai residence, a magistrate's office or any government building, and the one dated record ties it to trades that wanted customers at the door, which an official's compound does not.

The rule the map follows: where a compound's plan sets salt wards at its doors, the form follows the present-day custom the record describes, since no page read gives an Edo-period shape - a small cone of salt (the record says only small; a few inches across is the map's own choice), a pair outside each warded opening, one on each side - and the use itself is a guess: no record read puts salt at an official's gates, so a sheet that draws them says so in its notes.
