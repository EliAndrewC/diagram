Were cones of salt set at the doors of an official's compound?

Sources: kotobank-morijio (salt heaped at the doorways of the customer trades, first recorded about 1838-40), jawiki-morijio (the heap's shape), ise-miyachu-morijio (a pair, outside the door)

The small heap of salt at a doorway, morijio, is recorded in the late Edo period, and where it is recorded is telling: the standard historical dictionary of Japanese places it at the doorways of the customer trades - restaurants, assignation houses and variety halls - set out for good fortune, and its earliest cited use is in a popular novel of about 1838-40. Today the salt is pressed into a cone or a three-sided pyramid, at the entrance or inside the house, a shape the article describes from present-day practice and does not date, and at an entrance today it commonly stands outside, one heap on each side, though that is a seller of ritual goods describing present-day custom, not a record of the Edo period. No page read gives the heap's shape, or a pair at a door, before modern times.

No page read puts morijio at the gate of a samurai residence, a magistrate's office or any government building, and the one dated record ties it to trades that wanted customers at the door, which an official's compound does not.

The decision (the GM's rule of 2026-09-28, that anything attested only in modern times is eliminated). What is premodern is a small heap of salt at the doorway of a trade that wanted customers, in the late Edo period (accurate); the cone and the pair at each side of a door are known only from present-day custom, and salt at an official's gates from no record at all. So the maps draw no salt at an official's compound, and no map draws the cone or the pair.
