Where do a hamlet's dead lie?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-09-27; kofukuroman-sanmai (a hamlet's burial ground at its edge), bochi-jawiki (the ground a settlement's inhabitants hold in common), kotobank-ryobosei (the two-grave split and where each grave lay), haka-jawiki (graves on a household's own land or kept by a neighborhood group), danka-jawiki (the grave at the parish temple), oikawa-2008-kikaijima (settlement graveyards on one island after 1868), nakajima-2006-huizhou (a Chinese lineage's grave-hill)

The setting. The GM's ruling of 2026-09-27: a village district is a main village and its hamlets, usually about half a dozen; the country monk lives in the main village and performs the funerary rites for the whole district; the village alone keeps the shrine, the headman's house and the cremation ground, and a hamlet has none of them. So a hamlet's dead are burned at the village's cremation ground. Where their bones then lie the ruling leaves to the history, and asks that the maps follow it where it agrees and roll between its forms where it does not.

What the history says. Two answers stand side by side. Most forms found put the dead by their own settlement. In Wakasa a hamlet's burial ground, the sanmai, stood at the hamlet's edge, while the graves its families visited stood in the temples inside it. Japanese keeps a name for a graveyard that a settlement's inhabitants own in common and manage themselves. Where the dead had two graves, the one where the body lay was usually on common ground - a riverbank, a hillside, a beach - reused in the order of death and marked with no more than a stone, and only the grave for the rites stood in the precinct of a temple or a hall in the village; one dictionary centers the custom on Kansai and has it hardly found in Kyūshū. Until the Second World War many graves were also built on a corner of a household's own land, or kept by a neighborhood group - the household grave that Does a farm household bury its dead on its own plot, and where on it? treats. On Kikaijima, after the Meiji government banned exposure burial, graveyards of each settlement began to form outside the old burial caves, the island's settlements being assumed to have differed widely in custom. The other answer is the parish temple: making one's grave at one's parish temple was half an obligation - and most villages' households were registered with temples outside the village, as Does a village keep a Buddhist temple of its own? finds, so outside the two-grave country the family's grave stood at a temple away from the hamlet; only in that country was the temple's the visited grave and the body's grave by the settlement. In Qing China, graves lay on family and lineage land: what brothers are often said to have left undivided was grave land and land for the rites, and one Huizhou lineage kept its founding ancestor's grave-hill, its ridge in the ancestral hall's name and its slopes shared among the lineage's members.

What is not known. No page read counts how many burial grounds a village of several hamlets kept, or how often a hamlet's dead went to the temple's ground rather than their own. The rule the map follows: the history does not point one way, so each hamlet's seed chooses: a burial ground of its own - a small common ground, organic in outline as the village's is, at the hamlet's edge beyond its last house, holding the urns brought back from the village's cremation ground - or none, its dead lying in the village's ground by the country monk's shrine, as the setting's parish temple. The odds are a guess: even. No hamlet draws a cremation ground, a shrine or a headman's house.
