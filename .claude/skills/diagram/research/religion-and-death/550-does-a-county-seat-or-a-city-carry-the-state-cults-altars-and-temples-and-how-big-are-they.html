Does a county seat or a city carry the state cult's altars and temples, and how big are they?

Sources: zhifangdian-yanzhou-altars (where one prefecture's county seats put their altars and City God temples), lin-2001-miaohui (a City God temple at every seat), chenghuang-zhwiki (the City Gods' four ranks), temple-of-confucius-enwiki (a Confucian temple for every county school), wenmiao-zhwiki (temple and school built as one), shanghai-wenmiao-zhwiki (a county's Confucian temple and its hall), shaozhou-xuegong-zhwiki (a prefecture's school-temple and its hall), ningbo-chenghuangmiao-zhwiki (a prefecture's City God temple), pingyao-chenghuangmiao (a county's City God temple), l7r-rites-and-temples (the setting's temples at each seat)

What the research found - where they stood. A Chinese county seat carried a set of state foundations beside its yamen. Every seat of local government had a City God temple, and the Ming founder gave the City Gods ranks at four levels, from the capital down to the county. Every county school was to have its Confucian temple, the wenmiao, by a decree of 630. The gazetteers of Yanzhou prefecture, in Shandong, place them against the wall: at Juye the altar of soil and grain stood outside the west gate, the altar of wind, clouds, thunder, rain, mountains and rivers outside the south gate, the altar of the unquiet dead north of the wall, and the City God temple northwest of the yamen, inside it; Yuncheng repeats the pattern, its City God temple west of the yamen. It is a tendency, not a rule: Shouzhang put its altar of the unquiet dead east of the seat, and Dong'e its altar of soil and grain to the southwest. A Confucian temple and its school were built as one complex, the temple in front of the school or beside it on either hand; Shanghai's, rebuilt in 1855 inside the county seat's west gate, runs on two axes side by side, one the temple's and one the school's.

How big. The City God temple of Ningbo prefecture covers about 4,700 m² (some 50,600 sq ft), nearly all of it built over, on an axis of screen wall, gate, ceremonial gate, stage, main hall and rear stage, with side halls to either hand; the county seat of Pingyao's covers over 7,300 m² (78,600 sq ft), but as three temples on one site. Of the Confucian temples, the main hall of Shanghai county's is about 260 m² (2,800 sq ft), three bays wide and 14 m (46 ft) tall; the hall of the prefectural school at Shaozhou is 374 m² inside (some 4,000 sq ft), five bays by three and 16.4 m (54 ft) tall, and that school once held a stone balustrade, a gateway, a semicircular pool, a second gate, a lecture hall, two side galleries, the main hall, a rear hall, a library pavilion and two shrines, one to good officials and one to local worthies. No readable page gives an altar's size or the whole site of a county's Confucian temple.

The setting. The GM's canon names no Confucian temple, no City God and no state altar. What it puts at every seat is the Ministry of Rites' own ladder of temples: every county town has at least one preceptor, serving in a town monastery; every provincial city at least one provincial abbot, in a subordinate temple; every domain capital at least two grand abbots.

The decision it drove. A deviation: no map draws a Confucian temple, a City God temple or a state altar in a county town or a city, because the setting has none of them. The building that plays the state cult's part at a seat is the Ministry of Rites' temple - the preceptor's monastery in a county town, the provincial abbot's temple in a provincial city - and it is drawn as a temple precinct, holding what "What does a temple precinct hold, and how big are its halls?" describes.
