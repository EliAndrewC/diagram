What kind of shops stand at a temple gate?

Sources: the GM's correction; xiangyouqian-zhwiki (incense and lamp oil given to a Chinese temple as a donation, and then money in their place); kotobank-monzenmachi (the eating houses of the gate town); miyagegashi-jawiki (the sweets sold at the teahouses along the highway); kotobank-miyage (the early-modern souvenir shops that formed a gate town, and what they sold); lin-2001-miaohui (the diviners at a village temple fair); omamori-enwiki (the amulet as a donation to the shrine or temple that issues it); monzenmachi-jawiki (who gathers before a great temple's gate, and how the trades there shifted); qingming-enwiki (the spirit money and paper replicas a family burns for its dead); Chinese temple accounts, traced to no readable page; this project's own anchor for the lay-house band

What the temple sells itself. Amulets, charms, talismans, incense and lamp oil reach the temple directly, with nobody in between, and, in this page's reading, are a major part of what keeps it. On the Chinese side that flow is a DONATION rather than a sale: believers gave incense sticks, candles, lamp oil and gold paper to temples and monasteries for use in the offerings, and later gave money in their place, which is why the money itself is called incense-oil money. What no page read carries is a temple's own accounts booking an annual oil-lamp and incense income. The Japanese side is the same relation: an omamori is issued by a shrine or a temple and is viewed mainly as a donation to the place that issues it, so it is a gift of the institution rather than a line of stock. Manufacture away from the precinct, and charms issued by no shrine at all, belong to the modern trade and to no part of this setting. So there is no third-party religious-goods shop competing at the temple's own gate, and none is drawn - the GM's correction of 2026-07-20, and the easiest thing here to get wrong.

What did cluster at the gate. A gate town forms when tradesmen and craftsmen doing business with the temple's own people and with its worshippers gather in front of a large shrine or temple that draws many pilgrims; lodging is the oldest of those trades, and the eating houses and souvenir shops a visitor meets today are a later composition, after the railways brought day-trip worship. So the gate carries food, drink and lodging - the inn above all, and the eating-and-drinking establishments a reference work names as what was added to the gate town's permanent festival market - because worshippers and pilgrims need feeding and resting and the temple does not do that itself. The sweets are attested a step away: the teahouses along the highway sold the confections travelers carried home and talked about, which is how a province's named sweet grew, and no page read puts a sweet-seller at the gate itself. Souvenirs follow, and they are premodern: a signed encyclopedia entry has the Kumano shugenja selling souvenir goods in the early modern period, and specialist souvenir shops coming into being as demand grew and forming the gate town - talismans and amulets above all, with lucky dolls, toys, picture books and sacred medicines beside them. That sits against the gate-town article's attribution of the eating houses and souvenir shops a visitor meets today to the railway era: the early-modern shop is attested, the crowd of them is later. The word meibutsu for them is this project's own and stands on no page read. Then the funeral trades, which cluster by the graveyard rather than by the hall - coffin-makers, grave-marker cutters, and the China-first detail, the makers of the spirit money and the paper replicas of houses, servants and goods that a family burns for its dead - serving the parish death economy the temple presides over but does not manufacture; that those makers stood in a row of shops by the burial ground is this project's reconstruction, and rests on nothing read. Then the service trades. Diviners are attested at a temple: a Qing county gazetteer has the physicians, diviners, astrologers and physiognomists putting up sheds at a village temple's annual fair, beside the storytellers and the conjurors - a fair rather than a permanent shopfront, and a village temple rather than a great urban one. The retained temple artisans (carpenters, gilders, painters) who work for the temple on its endless repair cycle and live in the district in workshops that are also dwellings, not storefronts, are this project's own: nothing read describes a temple housing the trades it retains.

A map drawing convention. At this scale none of those has a glyph of its own - a teahouse and an eatery are both the generic shop - so the story is told by PLACEMENT (a shop beside the temple reads as a teahouse) and never by the icon. Nothing on the map tries to be an amulet shop; everything sacred is inside the compound.

The rule the map follows: on a city sheet, size the lay service band at about 40 to 80 houses around the two complexes of a provincial city (30 to 60 resident monks between them), which is roughly one to two lay service households per resident monk for a city whose worship traffic is regional. That band is ordinary zoning inside the existing caste bands, not extra population. A pilgrimage destination inverts the ratio to ten lay households or more per monk - the gate-town pattern - and that is what an especially pious city looks like on the ground. No source gives a ratio: the band is this project's own anchor, a guess calibrated on the drawn cities.
