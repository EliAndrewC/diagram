What kind of shops stand at a temple gate?

Food, drink and lodging. A gate town forms when tradesmen and craftsmen doing business with the temple's own people and with its worshippers gather in front of a large shrine or temple that draws many pilgrims; at the pilgrimage temples the inn came first, and the eating houses and souvenir shops a visitor meets today replaced it after the railways brought day-trip worship. A city temple's gate had them far earlier: a reference work names the eating-and-drinking establishments added to the gate town's permanent festival market, and Edo's new gate towns of the first half of the seventeenth century already had rest teahouses and souvenir shops. On Sensoji's approach in the Edo period twenty water teahouses stood in a row nearer the inner gate, and the shops nearer the outer gate sold toys, sweets and souvenirs; the right to trade there began as a privilege given to the local residents who owed the temple the sweeping of its precinct and approach. So the gate carries food, drink and rest, because worshippers need feeding and resting and the temple does not do that itself. Sweets were also sold at the teahouses along the highways, which is how a province's named sweet grew.

Souvenirs. A signed encyclopedia entry has the Kumano shugenja selling souvenir goods in the early modern period, and specialist souvenir shops coming into being as demand grew and forming the gate town - talismans and amulets above all, with lucky dolls, toys, picture books and sacred medicines beside them. Those talismans are a mountain pilgrimage's; at a city temple the amulets are the temple's own, as Does a temple's gate have shops selling its amulets and incense? sets out. What a worshipper looks for at the gate is a meibutsu, the place's famous specialty: the soba shops on Zenkoji's approach seem to owe their name to their density and to worshippers seeking a place to rest and a meibutsu.

The funeral trades cluster by the graveyard rather than by the hall - coffin-makers, grave-marker cutters, and the China-first detail, the makers of the spirit money and the paper replicas of houses, servants and goods that a family burns for its dead - serving the parish death economy the temple presides over but does not manufacture. One ordinary Edo temple's gate did house a stonemason whose family had been there some 150 years, and a florist, among its twenty-two households, as How many houses stand at a temple's gate? lists; that the funeral makers stood in a row of shops by the burial ground is this project's reconstruction, and rests on nothing read.

The service trades and the temple's own craftsmen. Diviners are attested at a temple: a Qing county gazetteer has the physicians, diviners, astrologers and physiognomists putting up sheds at a village temple's annual fair, beside the storytellers and the conjurors - a fair rather than a permanent shopfront, and a village temple rather than a great urban one. Temples did retain carpenters for their building and repair: a carpenters' house served Shitennoji on the temple's stipend up to the early modern period, and the carpenters Dogen is said to have brought from Song China made a village near Eiheiji's gate and handled its enlargements and repairs for generations. So the retained carpenters live in the district in workshops that are also dwellings, not storefronts. That a temple kept gilders and painters the same way is this project's own extension, on no page read.

A map drawing convention. At this scale none of those has a glyph of its own - a teahouse and an eatery are both the generic shop - so the story is told by PLACEMENT (a shop beside the temple reads as a teahouse) and never by the icon.
