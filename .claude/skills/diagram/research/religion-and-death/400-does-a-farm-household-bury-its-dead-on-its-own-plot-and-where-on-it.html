Does a farm household bury its dead on its own plot, and where on it?

Sources: kotobank-haka (the household's own grave, once common), nireyama-yashiki-bochi (where on the plot such graves stood, and where they went), eisi-yashikibaka (a household grave in the field or the front garden), ikikata-yashiki-bochi (where the form survives)

A grave of the household's own. A shared burial ground was not the only form. Formerly many households each held a graveyard of their own, or a branch house set its grave beside the main house's so that the kin shared one, and some set the grave within their own house land - the yashiki-baka, the house-plot grave; from Meiji on, the graveyards were often merged. Yanagita Kunio found houses with a graveyard within or beside the plot common in the Kanto, in the northeast and in the mountain villages of southern Kyushu, and today such graves are few across the country, few in the Kansai and said to be many in the Kanto. In the Meiji period the authorities advised gathering each district's graves into one communal ground, and many districts did so while others did not.

Where on the plot. One family's history, set down by a descendant in the former Hatara district, where many houses keep a graveyard beside the house plot, gives the seat. Its graveyard before the Genroku era (1688-1704) stood at the southeast corner of the house plot, and another at the northwest corner, ground now counted as woodland; the writer believes the dead there were buried without gravestones in the woods behind the house's own tutelary shrine, part of the grove round the house. When such graves were moved off the plot, by the writer's reading of their own district, they went no great distance: many houses used part of their own fields or woods within the hamlet. In the hills, where flat land is scarce, the graveyard is said often to be set part-way up the slope behind. A visitor to one Kanto town today sees the same thing: each household's grave on part of its own land, in the middle of a field along the road or in the front garden.

What is not known. No page read says what share of households, or of hamlets, buried on their own ground rather than in a shared one, or how that differed between hamlet and village.

The decision. The household grave is an attested form beside the shared burial ground the record describes for a village (How much ground does a village burial ground need, and whose dead lie in it?), so a hamlet's household graves are a knob, never a choice made once for every hamlet: each hamlet's seed decides whether its farmsteads keep graves of their own, and if they do, each such grave sits at a back corner of its house plot, in or at the edge of the grove round the house, or on the household's own field or wood close by. A household grave is a few stones, drawn at the scale of the plot, not a burial ground.

The rule the map follows: on a hamlet sheet, the seed decides whether the farmsteads keep household graves; where they do, some farmsteads draw a small grave of a few stones at a back corner of the house plot, in or against its grove, or on the household's own field or wood beside it. The share of hamlets keeping household graves, and of farmsteads in such a hamlet that have one, is a guess: about half of hamlets, and about a third of their farmsteads.
