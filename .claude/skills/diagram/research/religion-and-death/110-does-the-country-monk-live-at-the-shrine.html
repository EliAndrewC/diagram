Does the country monk live at the shrine? Yes - and that is the historically accurate form

Sources: kuri-jawiki and kuri-kitchen-enwiki (the kuri, the priest's dwelling), jushoku-jawiki (the unstaffed temple), jinja-jawiki, kannushi-wiki and miyaza-jawiki (the village shrine with no priest), shake-jawiki (the hereditary priest's house near the shrine), betto-jawiki, bettoji-jawiki, jinguji-enwiki and shinbutsu-bunri-enwiki (the shrine-temple and its resident monk, and its end in 1868), mianning-village-temples, tudimiao-zhwiki and temple-chinese-enwiki (China); the RPG's own setting as its fan wiki summarizes it, unverifiable from here (fn-159); terauke-seido-jawiki, shumon-ninbetsu-jawiki and jaanus-shamusho (the registers and the office a small shrine did not have); the GM's ruling of 2026-09-19

What the research found. Three bodies of evidence agree once the setting's fusion of the Fortunes and Shinsei is read as what it is - the pre-1868 Japanese fusion of the kami and the Buddhas. At a Japanese village's Buddhist parish temple the priest lived on the premises: the kuri is "the place where a temple's monks live", in origin "a kitchen which prepared meals only for the abbot and his guests", and the one whose form its record describes resembles the region's farmhouse - which is to say, the priest lived in a house. The one limit is the unstaffed temple, where "no one resides and a head priest from far away commutes". At a village's Shinto shrine, by contrast, nobody lived: "small shrines often had no dedicated priest; the parishioners managed them in rotation", "most village shrines during this period were too poor to have a dedicated priest", the miyaza "keeps no full-time priest; the members ... take the priest's role in yearly rotation", and where a hereditary priestly family existed it "set up their houses near the shrine" - near, in houses of its own.

The form the setting draws is the third one, common before 1868 - shrine-temples "remained common for over a millennium" and at small shrines "the monks of the jingūji sometimes did the managing and performed the rites" - the shrine with a small temple attached, a jingūji, "the temple where the bettō lived", from which the shrine's own rites "such as sutra-reading before the kami, were performed in Buddhist form" by "the head of the shrine monks". That monk "was tellingly called shasō (社僧) or 'shrine Buddhist monk', and was both a shrine priest and a Buddhist monk". What it took to end the arrangement says how widespread it was: the 1868 order "ordered the defrocking of shasō and bettō (shrine monks performing Buddhist rites at Shinto shrines)", who "were told to become 'shrine priests' (kannushi) and return to their shrines", and "an estimated 30,000 Buddhist structures were demolished between 1868 and 1874". A country monk living at a village shrine and serving its rites is that arrangement; how many village shrines had a temple of their own, no page read counts.

China, the setting's first model, has both forms side by side. The village temples of a Qing county in Sichuan "generally engaged monks and Daoists to reside and keep watch, responsible for guarding the property, cleaning, and managing the temple's incense offerings", under heads "chosen by election from among the congregation"; the earth-god shrine proper, "from a few square meters at the small end to over ten", housed nobody, and popular temples are "established and administered by local managers, village communities, lineage congregations". The tabletop game's own setting, as its fan wiki summarizes the rulebooks, says the same of Rokugan: no village without some manner of shrine, priests as the shrine's administrators and primary caretakers, and at most temples a resident monk - pages a browser opens and this project's tools cannot, so the summary stands unverified here and the GM's own notes are the canon.

The decision. The country monk lives at the shrine, and the sheet draws the fused site: a sanctuary, a hall for the villagers, and the monk's home - under the hall's roof by default (the GM's words: "the shrine is both their home and the place where the villagers come"), which the next section shows is an attested if uncommon form. This is labeled accurate: the form is attested and the GM's ruling and the sources coincide, so no knob is needed for residence - with the disclosure that its prevalence at village scale is uncounted. Disclosed beside it are the two forms history also had and these sheets do not draw - the pure Shinto village shrine with no resident, tended by the parishioners' rota, and the unstaffed rural temple with a commuting priest.

The rule the sheet follows: a country shrine's precinct holds the monk's dwelling; in the one-roof form (the default) the dwelling is the rear end of the hall, and in the two-building form it is a farmhouse-class house beside it. The district's registers - under the terauke system the temple issued the travel certificate, the village headmen compiled the ledger - are kept, on the sheet, in a writing room or a record chest in the dwelling (the sheet's own arrangement), never in an office building, because a small shrine had none: "business was frequently carried on at the private home of the administrator".
