Does the country monk live at the shrine? Yes - and that is the historically accurate form

Sources: kuri-jawiki and kuri-kitchen-enwiki (the kuri, the priest's dwelling), jushoku-jawiki (the unstaffed temple), jinja-jawiki, kannushi-wiki and miyaza-jawiki (the village shrine with no priest), shake-jawiki (the hereditary priest's house near the shrine), betto-jawiki, bettoji-jawiki, jinguji-enwiki and shinbutsu-bunri-enwiki (the shrine-temple and its resident monk, and its end in 1868), yuya-2007-kanazawa-shaso and kotobank-bettoji (how common it was); l5r-fandom-seido and l5r-fandom-shinden-tcg (the tabletop game's own setting, as its fan wiki summarizes it); the GM's ruling of 2026-09-19

What the research found. Three bodies of evidence agree once the setting's fusion of the Fortunes and Shinsei is read as what it is - the pre-1868 Japanese fusion of the kami and the Buddhas. At a Japanese village's Buddhist parish temple the priest lived on the premises: the kuri is "the place where a temple's monks live", in origin "a kitchen which prepared meals only for the abbot and his guests", and the one whose form its record describes resembles the region's farmhouse - which is to say, the priest lived in a house. The one limit is the unstaffed temple, where "no one resides and a head priest from far away commutes". At a village's Shinto shrine, by contrast, nobody lived: "small shrines often had no dedicated priest; the parishioners managed them in rotation", "most village shrines during this period were too poor to have a dedicated priest", the miyaza "keeps no full-time priest; the members ... take the priest's role in yearly rotation", and where a hereditary priestly family existed it "set up their houses near the shrine" - near, in houses of its own.

The form the setting draws is the third one, common before 1868 - shrine-temples "remained common for over a millennium" and at small shrines "the monks of the jingūji sometimes did the managing and performed the rites" - the shrine with a small temple attached, a jingūji, one of those "in which the bettō resided", from which the shrine's own rites "such as sutra-reading before the kami, were performed in Buddhist form" by "the head of the shrine monks". That monk "was tellingly called shasō (社僧) or 'shrine Buddhist monk', and was both a shrine priest and a Buddhist monk". What it took to end the arrangement says how widespread it was: the 1868 order "ordered the defrocking of shasō and bettō (shrine monks performing Buddhist rites at Shinto shrines)", who "were told to become 'shrine priests' (kannushi) and return to their shrines", and in the purge of Buddhism that followed, of temples of every kind and not only shrine-temples, "an estimated 30,000 Buddhist structures were demolished between 1868 and 1874". A country monk living at a village shrine and serving its rites is that arrangement. How common it was below the great shrines is said in words, not numbers: an encyclopedia says many provincial shrines also had such a temple, and a study of the Kanazawa area gives as the received view that shrines served by lay priests - Shinto priests who were not Buddhist monks - were, if anything, rare there before 1868, the many shrine monks and mountain ascetics becoming Shinto priests after it - a view the study itself questions in part. How many village shrines had a temple of their own, no page read counts.

China, the setting's first model, has both forms side by side - resident keepers at a county's village temples, nobody at the earth god's shrine - under "Who kept a Chinese village temple?". The tabletop game's own setting, as its fan wiki summarizes the rulebook, says the same of Rokugan: "There was no village without some manner of shrine", and "Priests were the shrine's administrators and primary caretakers"; and of temples, that the smallest "relied on visiting monks, priests, shugenja, or even local volunteers", "but most had at least one resident monk". The GM's own notes are the canon.

The decision. The country monk lives at the shrine, and the sheet draws the fused site: a sanctuary, a hall for the villagers, and the monk's home - under the hall's roof by default (the GM's words: "the shrine is both their home and the place where the villagers come"), which "How big was the country monk's dwelling, and did it share the hall's roof?" shows is an attested if uncommon form. This is labeled accurate: the form is attested and the GM's ruling and the sources coincide, so no knob is needed for residence - with the disclosure that its prevalence at village scale is uncounted, the received view in one region putting shrines without monks as the rare case. Disclosed beside it are the two forms history also had and these sheets do not draw - the pure Shinto village shrine with no resident, tended by the parishioners in rotation, and the unstaffed rural temple with a commuting priest.

The rule the sheet follows: a country shrine's precinct holds the monk's dwelling; in the one-roof form (the default) the dwelling is the rear end of the hall, and in the two-building form it is a farmhouse-class house beside it. Where the district's registers are kept is under "Where does the country monk keep the district's registers?".
