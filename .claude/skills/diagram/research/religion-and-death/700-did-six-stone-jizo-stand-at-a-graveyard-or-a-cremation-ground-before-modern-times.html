Did six stone jizo stand at a graveyard or a cremation ground before modern times? At a graveyard's entrance, yes

Sources: hirahaku-rokujizo (where six jizo stand, and two sets dated by inscription), agematsu-shinden-rokujizo (a set of 1678 at a graveyard), nagoya-museum-edo-boseki (the six jizo as a monument of the Edo-period graveyard), higashiueta-sanmai (six jizo at one cremation ground, undated)

At a graveyard. The six jizo stand in a row at the entrance of a temple or a graveyard, and stone ones are said to appear at temples and graveyards from the end of the Muromachi period; two sets at a temple gate in Hiratsuka carry inscriptions of 1753 and 1797. A set of six at the Shinden graveyard in Agematsu, in the Kiso valley, is cataloged as Edo-period work of 1678, the oldest jizo in the town. A city museum's exhibition of Edo-period gravestones names the six jizo at the entrances of graveyards and temples as a representative stone monument of the graveyard. So six stone jizo at a graveyard's entrance are premodern.

At a cremation ground. The one page that puts six jizo at a cremation ground is a community page on a Takamatsu district's old ground, which says six jizo were always set at a cremation ground but gives no date for the ground or the figures, only that it was used "formerly". No page dated before modern times puts them at a cremation ground standing on its own. That where a village's cremation ground lies beside its burial ground the jizo at the burial ground's entrance serve both is this project's reading, and so is the decision it drives: the maps draw the six jizo at a graveyard, where they are attested before modern times, and not at a cremation ground standing on its own, where they are not.

The rule the map follows: six small stone jizo stand in a row at the entrance of a village's burial ground. A cremation ground beside the burial ground shares them; a cremation ground on its own at the village's edge draws none.
