How many houses stand at a temple's gate?

Sources: matsui-2014-edo-religious-space (an average Edo temple's twenty-two gate households, their trades and their depth, and the count of Edo's gate towns); nakamise-jawiki (the shops on Sensoji's approach today); this project's own anchor for the ratio to the monks

An ordinary temple's gate. Kyozenji in Azabu, with a precinct of about 1,000 tsubo, was a temple of average size, and in 1843 twenty-two households stood on its gate-town land, half of them owned by the families living in them and the rest let. They were a neighborhood's trades rather than a pilgrim's: sake-sellers, a rice-polisher, a pawnbroker, a fruit-seller, dealers in secondhand goods and in secondhand clothes, a fishmonger, a sundries shop, a seller of stewed food, a stonemason and a cooper each there some 150 to 180 years, a lantern-maker, a tofu-maker, a shingle roofer, a hairdresser, a florist and a pouch-maker. The houses were shallow, about three ken deep, the usual look of a gate town at a temple of that size. Such gate towns were everywhere in the shogun's capital: by the middle of the eighteenth century there were 440 gate-town wards, which the shogunate then turned into town land with 227 temple and shrine precincts. The form was Edo's: houses built on land belonging to the temple's own precinct, and the paper says it was not generally found in Kyoto or Osaka. Who owned the ground does not show on a map, so the band is drawn the same either way.

A great temple's gate. The greatest pilgrimage temple of the capital had far more: today about 90 shops line the roughly 250 m of Sensoji's approach, the street rebuilt in the 1880s and the 1920s. That is a present count, not an Edo one. How many lay households a temple's gate carried for each of its monks is on no page read.

The rule the map follows: on a city sheet, size the lay service band at about 40 to 80 houses around the two complexes of a provincial city (30 to 60 resident monks between them), which is roughly one to two lay service households per resident monk for a city whose worship traffic is regional, and draw them as a single shallow row about 18 feet (3 ken) deep facing the temple's approach. That band is ordinary zoning inside the existing caste bands, not extra population, and its trades are a neighborhood's. A pilgrimage destination inverts the ratio to ten lay households or more per monk - the gate-town pattern - and that is what an especially pious city looks like on the ground. The twenty to forty houses a complex gets sits beside the twenty-two at one average Edo temple, and the shallow row is that temple's; the ratio to the monks is this project's own anchor, a guess calibrated on the drawn cities.
