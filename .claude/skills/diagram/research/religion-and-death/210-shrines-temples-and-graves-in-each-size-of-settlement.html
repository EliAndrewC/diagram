Shrines, temples and graves in each size of settlement

In this setting, the clergy a settlement keeps follows its size. Every village district has a country monk, every county town at least one preceptor and every provincial city at least one provincial abbot, all of them overseen by the Ministry of Rites; the preceptors serve in town monasteries, the abbots in the cities' temples. A village's shrine, a town's monasteries and a city's temples follow from that ladder.

Real Japanese and Chinese settlements kept more than this, and varied more. A county town had a shrine of its own, a city a principal shrine, a Chinese county seat the altars and temples of the state cult, and a village none, one or several Buddhist temples. The small wayside shrines, for their part, stood where a way crossed a boundary.

The smallest shrines of all were not a settlement's to keep by rank; they stood where the way crossed a boundary, whatever the size of the place.

  • The deity of the way was enshrined at boundaries.
    It stood at a village's entrance and exit, a mountain pass, a crossroads and the foot of a bridge, most often as a stone monument or a stone image.
  • A small shrine could stand beside a well.
    At Mikurahana a dragon-god shrine, a shrine to a water deity rather than to the way, stands near a named well, and the people of nearby Amasegawa keep a day of their own for its festival.
