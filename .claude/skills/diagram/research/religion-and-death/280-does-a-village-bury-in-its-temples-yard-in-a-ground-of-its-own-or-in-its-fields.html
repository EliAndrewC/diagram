Does a village bury in its temple's yard, in a ground of its own, or in its fields?

Sources: takeuchi-2017-bochi-hosei (the forms of graveyard standing side by side at the end of the Edo period), kaf2-kinsei-bo (the temple graveyard's spread, and its crowding), bochi-jawiki (the ground a village holds in common), ryobosei-jawiki (the settlement's common ground as the usual form of the burial ground), meiji-1884-bochi-saimoku (the village graveyard open to all its inhabitants), buck-1930-farm-economy (Chinese family graves in the fields)

More than one form, side by side. At the end of the Edo period Japan's graveyards took several forms at once - an individual's, a lineage's, a settlement's and a temple's - each governed by local custom. The temple's had grown with the Edo system that tied every household to a temple: many temples were founded, graveyards attached to them spread, and the dead came more often to be buried at the temple, in grounds so cramped that burials overlapped. Beside it stood the village's own: a graveyard its inhabitants held in common and ran themselves, which is the usual form of the burial ground where the body and the grave stone were kept apart. When the state first regulated graveyards, in 1884, it opened a village's graveyard to everyone registered or dying there, whatever their sect, while letting an older custom stand. China went further from the hall: with no hill land to take them, family graves stood anywhere in a field, and a well-to-do family's graveyard in the north could fill almost a whole field.

What is not known. No page read says how common each form was, village by village, or which regions favored which.

The decision. Two Japanese forms are attested for a village's one ground, so the map rolls between them per village rather than choosing: the ground in the yard of the hall that tends it, which is this setting's churchyard (Does a graveyard stand beside its temple or its shrine?), and a ground of the village's own, apart from the hall. The individual's and the lineage's graves are a house's own and too small to show at a village's scale, and the Chinese family graves in the fields are the form these maps deliberately do not draw (How much ground does a village burial ground need, and whose dead lie in it?).

The rule the map follows: each village's seed chooses where its one burial ground lies: in the shrine's own yard, or as a ground of its own apart from the shrine, downstream of the houses and beyond the last of them (How far from its houses does a village bury its dead, and on which side?). A village whose shrine stands on a hill always takes the second. The share of villages taking each form is a guess: even odds.
