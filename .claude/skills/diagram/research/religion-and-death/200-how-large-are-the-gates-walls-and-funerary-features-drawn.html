Why this matters at all. A walled compound - a manor or estate, the governor's yamen, a merchant estate, the mausoleum - draws its wall, its gate and a deliberately blank court, because the interior is the subject of its own building plan. The wall and the gate ARE the feature, so they have to be honest, and a funerary feature drawn to the wrong size reads as a different kind of place.

The anchors. A samurai residence gate is drawn at about 9 to 12 ft and a grand yamen gatehouse at about 18 to 24 ft. Neither figure is a finding: the official commentary on the nagaya-mon, the gate a samurai of middle rank and above was permitted, describes its servants' quarters and its stable and gives no width,69 and the best-preserved county magistrate's office in China is described by its forty thousand square meters and its hundred-meter axis and not by its gate,70 so both are this project's own calibration on the drawn sheets. What the anchors exist to prevent is real enough: a gate gap drawn as a fixed distance on the sheet rather than as a real width opens 204 ft at city scale , which is most of a wall missing. Wall thickness is the one figure in this paragraph the record does measure, and what it measures is a heavier wall. A Japanese rammed-earth tsuijibei was built a full meter thick and three meters high, and the lighter neribei about 30 cm71 - castle walls, both, and they bracket the 1.5 to 2 ft these compounds are drawn at without containing it, so the drawn figure is a choice about a lesser wall and not a measurement of one. A cremation ground's cleared core is drawn at 30 to 80 ft at village and town scale and about 80 to 160 ft at city scale, and the recorded sites bracket that band: village cremation grounds of 10 tsubo 5 go (34.65 m²) and 42 tsubo, and the large urban one at 600 tsubo (1,980 m²), about 145 ft square.72 Those are Meiji through Showa municipal facilities rather than Edo ones. The one real extent is Edo's own: the crematory at Yoyogi is recorded in the Musashi gazetteer at 900 tsubo, some 180 ft square, with three houses of the men who worked it.73 The pyre itself is not a built platform at all: in open country it was firewood stacked on the ground, the permanent form was a shallow trench about one size larger than a coffin framed in stone and earthenware, and the roofed forms are two: a fire bed on leveled ground with posts at its four corners carrying a simple roof, and, in snow country, a hut of about four to six tatami with a full tiled roof on stacked cut-stone walls and a channeled fire bed dug at its center.74 A pauper bone mound is drawn at 10 to 30 ft across with nothing behind the band,75 while the base of Kyoto's monumental ear mound is measured: 30 ken, about 49 m, across, standing 4 ken (about 7.2 m) high under a 5-ken stone pagoda, on a regional encyclopedia site that names no source for the figures.76 Cremated bone takes almost no volume, which is why the great Edo execution ground - more than two hundred thousand put to death between its founding and its abolition, on a plot about 108 m across the front and a little over 54 m deep77 - is not remembered for a mound; no page read describes one raised there.78 Burial grounds size to the population served at about 10 to 20 sq ft per packed urn grave, with about one generation of active plots before reuse.109 The excavated Edo commoner graves are of that order - jar burials in wooden chambers 85 cm to 1.4 m along the major axis, wooden coffin graves 1 to 2.4 m79 - and the four-to-six-times multiple for Chinese full-body burial is measured now: a Kangxi endowment of 4 mu of Jiangnan field took 573 coffins, about 4.7 m² - some 50 sq ft - a grave, inside the 40 to 120 sq ft the multiple implies, in charity graveyards that ranged from 3 fen to 10 mu.80 Those are grounds for the unclaimed and the drowned, packed more tightly than a family grave sited by fengshui.110 The acreage ladder those plots build to (a village district 0.15 to 0.30 acre, a town 0.25 to 0.75, a city 0.75 to 2 split across its yards) is still this project's own arithmetic, on no source.97 This setting follows the Japanese cremate-then-inter mode.

A to-scale map does not enlarge a feature for legibility; where a glyph would otherwise vanish, the help is the thinnest mark the sheet can carry, disclosed as the drawing convention it is.

The rule the map follows: a compound's gate opening is drawn at its real width for the building it serves, about 9 to 12 ft for a samurai residence and 18 to 24 ft for a yamen gatehouse. A compound wall is drawn at its true thickness, or at the thinnest line the sheet can carry where that is wider , and a gate post at about 2 ft square under the same kind of floor - map drawing conventions, so that a wall one and a half ft thick still reads at 3 ft per pixel. A cremation ground's cleared core is 30 to 80 ft at village and town scale and 80 to 160 ft at city scale, with a fire bed about a coffin's length and a little wider, roofed where it is permanent - four posts and a simple roof over the bed, or, in snow country, a walled hut of about four to six tatami, roughly 8 to 10 ft square; a pauper bone mound is drawn at about 22 ft, with a small floor under it so that it never vanishes , and anything outside 8 to 32 ft is wrong. Burial grounds are sized to the population they serve, and the ladder stays monotone: a village district ground below a town's, a town's below a city's.
