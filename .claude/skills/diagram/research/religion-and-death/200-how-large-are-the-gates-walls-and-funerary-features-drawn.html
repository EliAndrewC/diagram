Why this matters at all. A walled compound - a manor or estate, the governor's yamen, a merchant estate, the mausoleum - draws its wall, its gate and a deliberately blank court, because the interior is the subject of its own building plan. The wall and the gate ARE the feature, so they have to be honest, and a funerary feature drawn to the wrong size reads as a different kind of place.

This question holds the compound's gate and wall; each funerary feature is sized in a question of its own: the cremation ground and its fire bed at How large is a cremation ground, and what was its fire bed?, the bone mound at How large is a bone mound drawn?, and the single grave and the burial ground it builds to at How much ground does a grave take, and how large is a burial ground drawn?

The anchors. A samurai residence gate is drawn at about 9 to 12 ft and a grand yamen gatehouse at about 18 to 24 ft. The first falls within what surviving Japanese gates measure - a one-bay gate of about 6 to 8.5 ft, or a nagaya-mon opening in two ken, about 12 ft - though the official commentary on the nagaya-mon, the gate a samurai of middle rank and above was permitted, describes its servants' quarters and its stable and gives no width. The second is this project's own calibration on the drawn sheets: a yamen's gate was a roofed building of three bays, but the best-preserved county magistrate's office in China is described by its forty thousand square meters and its hundred-meter axis and not by its gate, and no page read gives such a gate's width. Both are set out at How wide was the main gate of a magistrate's post? What the anchors exist to prevent is real enough: a gate gap drawn as a fixed distance on the sheet rather than as a real width opens 204 ft at city scale , which is most of a wall missing. Wall thickness comes in two forms. A Japanese rammed-earth tsuijibei was built a full meter thick and three meters high, and the lighter neribei about 30 cm, and a dye shop's surviving neribei of stones laid in red earth measures 0.6 m, about 2 ft, across its base - so the 1.5 to 2 ft these compounds are drawn at is the lighter wall's measured range (How thick was a compound's earth wall?).

A to-scale map does not enlarge a feature for legibility; where a glyph would otherwise vanish, the help is the thinnest mark the sheet can carry, disclosed as the drawing convention it is.

The rule the map follows: a compound's gate opening is drawn at its real width for the building it serves, about 9 to 12 ft for a samurai residence and 18 to 24 ft for a yamen gatehouse. A compound wall is drawn at its true thickness, or at the thinnest line the sheet can carry where that is wider , and a gate post at about 2 ft square, a figure of this project's own, under the same kind of floor - map drawing conventions, so that a wall one and a half ft thick still reads at 3 ft per pixel.
