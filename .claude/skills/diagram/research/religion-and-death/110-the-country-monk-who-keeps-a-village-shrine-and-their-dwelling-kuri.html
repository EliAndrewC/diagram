The country monk who keeps a village shrine, and their dwelling (kuri)

Before 1868, in the Edo period, a Japanese village shrine was kept in one of several ways. Many shrines had a small Buddhist temple attached, whose monk lived there and performed the shrine's rites in Buddhist form. Many small shrines had no priest at all, and the parishioners took turns keeping them. Where a hereditary family of Shinto priests served a shrine, it lived near it, in houses of its own. How common each form was in the villages, no source counts.

A monk's home at a temple was its kuri, "the place where a temple's monks live". At an ordinary temple it was much like an ordinary house, and often the temple's office as well; the kuri that have been measured are all rich temples'. The temple certified its parishioners under the Edo registration of households, its precinct was exempt from tax, and its parish households paid it for their rites of the dead.

  • A temple's monks lived at the temple, in its kuri.
    The Japanese encyclopedia defines the kuri as "the place where a temple's monks live". In origin it was "a kitchen which prepared meals only for the abbot and his guests".
  • Some temples had no one living at them.
    The encyclopedia's page on the head priest speaks of temples where "no one resides and a head priest from far away commutes".
  • Small Shinto shrines often had no priest of their own.
    "Small shrines often had no dedicated priest; the parishioners managed them in rotation", and "most village shrines during this period were too poor to have a dedicated priest". A village's shrine guild, the miyaza, "keeps no full-time priest; the members ... take the priest's role in yearly rotation".
  • A hereditary family of Shinto priests lived near its shrine, not at it.
    Such families "set up their houses near the shrine" they served - near it, in houses of their own.
  • Before 1868 many shrines had a small Buddhist temple attached, whose monk kept the shrine.
    These shrine-temples, the jingūji, "remained common for over a millennium". At small shrines "the monks of the jingūji sometimes did the managing and performed the rites". The shrine's rites, "such as sutra-reading before the kami, were performed in Buddhist form" by the bettō, "the head of the shrine monks", who lived at the temple; some of the jingūji "in which the bettō resided" were called bettō-ji.
  • The shrine's monk was both a Shinto priest and a Buddhist monk.
    That monk "was tellingly called shasō (社僧) or 'shrine Buddhist monk', and was both a shrine priest and a Buddhist monk".
  • The arrangement was ended by order in 1868.
    The first order separating the kami from the Buddhas, in April 1868, "ordered the defrocking of shasō and bettō (shrine monks performing Buddhist rites at Shinto shrines)", who "were told to become 'shrine priests' (kannushi) and return to their shrines". In the purge of Buddhism that followed, of temples of every kind and not only shrine-temples, "an estimated 30,000 Buddhist structures were demolished between 1868 and 1874". What it took to end the arrangement says how widespread it was.
  • How common was a monk at a village shrine?
    The sources say it in words, not numbers. An encyclopedia says that many provincial shrines of rank had a bettō-ji, which cannot show that a village shrine had one. A study of the Kanazawa area gives as the received view that shrines served by lay priests - Shinto priests who were not Buddhist monks - were, if anything, rare there before 1868, the many shrine monks and mountain ascetics becoming Shinto priests after it; the study itself questions that view in part. No page we read counts how many village shrines had a temple of their own.
  • The kuri that have been measured are all rich temples'.
    One designated kuri is "9 ken along the ridge and 5 ken across the beams", with "the head priest's living room ... a twelve-mat room", beside a main hall of "7 ken" by "7 ken". The largest is "12 ken along the ridge by 8 ken". No small village kuri has been measured.
  • An ordinary temple's kuri was much like an ordinary house.
    At an ordinary temple, the encyclopedia says, the kuri often doubles as the temple's office, and many are buildings much like ordinary houses. Even the largest measured kuri resembles "the honmune-zukuri farmhouse characteristic of this region".
  • Did a temple's hall and dwelling ever share one roof?
    Once that we know of, and it was called unusual. At Kaie-ji, a Zen temple in the city of Sakai, the "main hall and kuri" are "one building: ridge span 25.2 meters, beam span 8.0 meters on the left side, 13.0 meters on the right side" - 83 ft long by 26 to 43 ft deep, some 200 to 330 m² (~2,150-3,550 sq ft). "A main hall and kuri in one building is unusual for temple architecture of the beginning of the Edo period". No second example was found.
  • A domain lord's family temple had a main hall of about 84 by 53 ft.
    The hall of a temple "protected as the family temple of the Ōzeki clan, who were the domain's lords" is "25.5 meters" by "16.3 meters".
  • Under the Edo registration, a household's temple certified that it belonged to it.
    "The temple issued the terauke certificate to its parishioners, to prove that they were its parishioners", and "for travel or a change of residence its certificate ... was required".
  • The village headmen, not the temple, compiled the yearly register.
    "The compiling of the register was to be done every year, town or village by town or village, by the nanushi or shōya headmen and the town elders".
  • A small shrine often had no office of its own.
    An online dictionary of Japanese architecture says that at shrines "business was frequently carried on at the private home of the administrator ... especially at small shrines".
  • A temple's or a shrine's precinct was exempt from tax.
    Exempt land, jochi, was land "exempted from annual tax by the shogunate and the daimyo in the Edo period, other than the vermilion-seal and black-seal lands", among it "the precincts of temples and shrines and fields and homesteads holding a document of tax exemption".
  • The sealed land grants to temples and shrines ran from about 10,000 koku down to 1.
    Of the shuinchi, "Even the largest, Kōyasan and Nikkō Tōshōgū, were about 10,000 koku, and the smallest a mere 1 koku".
  • A parish household paid its temple for the rites of the dead.
    "A parish household belongs to a particular temple, entrusts all of its funerary and memorial rites to that temple, and pays fuse."

China, the setting's first model, had both forms side by side: village temples with keepers who lived at them, and small earth-god shrines too small to house anyone.

  • In one Qing county of Sichuan, the village temples engaged monks and Daoists to live at them as keepers.
    The village temples of Mianning "generally engaged monks and Daoists to reside and keep watch, responsible for guarding the property, cleaning, and managing the temple's incense offerings".
  • The congregation elected the heads of its village temple.
    "The association heads, as leaders of the village-temple organization, are chosen by election from among the congregation."
  • Temples to local gods were run by local managers and village communities.
    Such temples "are established and administered by local managers, village communities, lineage congregations and worship associations".
  • Did anyone live at the earth god's small shrine?
    Probably not at the smallest. An earth-god shrine runs "from a few square meters at the small end to over ten" - some 30 to over 100 sq ft - and at the small end it is too small to house anyone. That nobody kept one is a GUESS from its size: no source we read names a keeper at one, or says there was none.
  • One Song-dynasty earth-god temple has a main hall of about 33 by 23 ft.
    At Xietang, "first built in the Southern Song", the surviving main hall is "three bays wide and one bay deep, 10 meters long and 7 meters wide" - large only against the smallest earth-god shrines, a comparison the page itself does not make.
  • A Chinese village temple's land was given or bought for its deity's upkeep.
    In Mianning, donors gave the temples land outright, or "partly bought and partly donated, to serve as a permanent resource for the deity's incense and lamps".
  • China registered its villagers through the lijia, not through a temple.
    The lijia's "greatest function ... lay in allocating corvée labor and collecting tax by head-count", and nothing we read puts a village temple in the registration business.
  • Was a Chinese village temple's land exempt from tax?
    No page we could read says whether a Ming or Qing village temple's land was exempt.
