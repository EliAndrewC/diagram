The fire bed. The pyre is not a built platform at all: in open country it was firewood stacked on the ground, the permanent form was a shallow trench about one size larger than a coffin framed in stone and earthenware, and two roofed forms appeared around the middle of the Edo period: a fire bed on leveled ground with posts at its four corners carrying a simple roof, and, in snow country, a hut of about four to six tatami with a full tiled roof on stacked cut-stone walls and a channeled fire bed dug at its center. The roofed forms were the exception: until the end of the Edo period the great majority of cremation grounds were open-air burning grounds, with no building or furnace. All of these forms are premodern.

The size. The one extent of a cremation ground before modern times is a city's: the crematory at Yoyogi, outside Edo, is recorded in the Musashi gazetteer at 900 tsubo, some 180 ft square, with three houses of the men who worked it. Three recorded sites in the Tama district west of Tokyo, each with a size, are all modern: a village cremation ground of 10 tsubo 5 go (34.65 m²) built in Taisho 11 (1922), a three-village union's ground of 42 tsubo set up in Showa 22 (1947), and Hachioji's of 600 tsubo (1,980 m²), about 145 ft square, set up in Meiji 43 (1910), probably as a private facility, and taken over by the city in 1920. No premodern figure for a village's or a town's burning ground was found. So the maps do not calibrate a village or a town ground to the modern sites: the premodern record gives the fire bed and, where it was roofed, its posts a few shaku out from the bed, and the open ground around it is a guess.

The rule the map follows: the fire bed is about a coffin's length and a little wider; most grounds leave it open, and a permanent one may roof it - four posts a few feet out from the bed carrying a simple roof, about 10 to 12 ft square , or, in snow country, a walled hut of about four to six tatami, roughly 8 to 10 ft square. A village or town ground's cleared core around it is 30 to 80 ft, a guess with no premodern figure behind it. A city's ground is calibrated to Yoyogi's: up to about 180 ft square.
