Where does a village put its shrine, and how big is it?

Sources: tudigong-enwiki (the earth-god shrine inside a walled village's entrance gate), fengshui-woodland-enwiki (the head and mouth of a village, and the earth-god shrine grove), kejia-taiwan-zhwiki (a Hakka deity's hall built at the village water mouth, with a named instance of 1760), sun-2014-zhejiang-village-entrances (temples at Zhejiang villages' water mouths and entrances, read as auspicious sites), kotobank-chinju-no-mori (the Japanese tutelary shrine within the village or at its edge), hie-jinja-kurihara-jawiki (a village shrine's own two halls, by their bays); the GM's ruling of 2026-07-21 on the footprint band; the water-mouth grove, recorded in the vegetation research

What the research found. A Chinese village's tutelary (guardian of that one place) earth-god shrine (tudi miao, 土地庙) at a village's entrance is attested as one instance rather than as a rule: a walled village of the Hong Kong New Territories keeps its earth-god shrine within the entrance gate, while the same page puts the god's other shrines inside temples and houses, beneath the main altar or below the door. The fengshui vocabulary of village groves keeps head for a village's entrance and mouth for its exit, and files both as the water gate, the shuikou, which can mean the water at either, and it files the earth-god shrine grove among the temple groves rather than among the village ones. Seating the hall AT the water mouth is attested: the temples of Minzhu Gongwang, a deity of the Hakka, are usually built at the village's shuikou, and a named instance of 1760 stands on a stream bank in what is now New Taipei. In Zhejiang's old villages temples stand at the water mouth, the head or the entrance - a mountain-god temple at one village's water mouth, a Guandi temple (to the deified general Guan Yu) at another's head, a local temple at a third's entrance - and a survey of those entrances reads their siting as borrowing the idea of an "auspicious site" from the worship of nature spirits; its table puts the earth god's own temple at one village's entrance. What is not attested is that the earth god's shrine took that site as a rule, or that it was the most strategic position of all: that reading is this project's own. On these maps the entrance is therefore the shuikou: the point where the water leaves and the connector track sets off for the road, and where the water-mouth grove already grows. The Japanese surface of the same idea is the tutelary chinju standing in its grove, which ordinarily stands within the village or at its edge, its ground the village's gathering place for its main observances.

The decision. The village's one shrine nestles there inside its sacred wood, fronted by an arch facing the village, and it is drawn SMALL: a modest hall, not a temple. It carries no label - a vermilion hall with an arch in front of it reads as a shrine on sight, and the marker rows beside it read as a graveyard, so neither is labeled. A village site is never called a temple; where the word is used of one, it means a shrine.

The rule the map follows: on a village sheet (2 ft per pixel) the ordinary earth-god or water-mouth hall is about 60 by 48 ft, some 275 m² , two farmhouses' worth under one roof; a showcase primary hall runs to about 490 m² , and 600 m² is the ceiling, because the monastery and temple tier of 1,000 m² and up belongs to towns and cities. The drawn hall is the country shrine in its one-roof form - the villagers' hall and the monk's home in one building, the form the GM chose - and its 275 m² lies inside the 200 to 330 m² of the one attested example, Kaie-ji's hall-and-kuri of 25.2 by 8.0 to 13.0 m, a form its own heritage listing calls unusual, at a Zen temple in the city of Sakai (labeled accurate; the measured halls are on this page under "How big is a country shrine, and what stands in its precinct?", the one-roof example under "How big was the country monk's dwelling, and did it share the hall's roof?"). A village shrine's own halls, with no dwelling, are far smaller: Hie Shrine in Kurihara, ranked a village shrine in 1874, keeps a worship hall of 3 bays by 2, thought late Edo, and a sanctuary of 2 bays by 1, early Edo at the latest - about 18 by 12 ft and 12 by 6 ft at a bay of 6 shaku, this project's conversion, the page giving bays only. The 490 and 600 m² figures above it, and the 1,000 m² tier line, remain this project's own calibration: no source read gives a Japanese village shrine's hall a ceiling. The hall stands BESIDE a lane, never on one, since a building sits off the way; the arch is the deliberate exception, because a gateway straddles the approach and the way runs under it. A shrine's water is drawn beside the lane (its stone basin stands beside the approach - under "What else stood in a village shrine's precinct?"), never on the hall and never under an arch. Hall and arches are drawn in a cleared opening in the grove rather than under the canopy - a drawing decision.
