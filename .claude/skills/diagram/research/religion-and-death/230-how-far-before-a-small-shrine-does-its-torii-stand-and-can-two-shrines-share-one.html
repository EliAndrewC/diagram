How far before a small shrine does its torii stand, and can two shrines share one?

Sources: jinjahoncho-keidai (the torii stands at the precinct's boundary, and subsidiary shrines stand inside it), jawiki-torii (several gates on one approach, and a subsidiary shrine with its own), jawiki-yashikigami (a household shrine may even have a torii)

Where it stands. A torii is put up at the boundary between the shrine and the world outside; inside it is the kami's sacred ground. So how far it stands from the hall is simply how deep the shrine's ground is in front of the hall, and a household shrine's ground is small: most such shrines are small stone or wooden shrines, and one that is carefully kept may be given a hall the size of a subsidiary shrine and "may even have a torii" - an arch is a mark of care, not a fixture every household shrine has. No page read gives a distance from any torii to its hall, for a household shrine or any other, so the distance is a guess.

Two shrines, one arch. Both arrangements are attested - one arch for both shrines, and an arch for each. The precinct's torii marks the ground of everything inside it, and a precinct keeps small subsidiary shrines besides the main hall, so a second shrine inside the same ground stands behind the same arch; several arches on one approach are counted from the outermost in. But a subsidiary shrine may also have an arch of its own: the list of noted torii includes the stone torii of Banshi-sha, a subsidiary shrine within the precinct of Kitano Tenmangu. Whether two shrines share one arch is therefore a knob.

The rule the map follows: a compound shrine's torii stands in front of its hall, facing the approach; how far is a guess, kept short because a compound shrine's ground is small - about 5 ft where the plan is tight, up to the village shrine's 20 ft where it has room. Two shrines standing on one shrine ground may share one arch at its entrance, or each may have its own, rolled from the map's seed; a compound shrine may also have no arch at all.
