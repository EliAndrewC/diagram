The economy. Chinese Buddhist monasteries were major economic actors: their land and dependent labor funded oil mills, spinneries and breweries; the Inexhaustible Treasuries of one Tang sect held donated wealth, and the scheme spread as temples lending it out at a profit; the chief one, at a temple in Chang'an, was destroyed on the order of the Tang emperor Xuanzong in 713, for a reason no page read gives; the earliest pawnbrokers, in the fifth century, were Buddhist monasteries, and by the Song dynasty, lay investors partnered with monasteries specifically to open pawnshops inside monastic tax exemption (a 1202 document records ten people forming a ju to support one). The GM's campaign notes' Fox economy - the Seven Temples holding forest usufruct, moneylending with Fukurokujin and Ebisu (the setting's Fortunes of wisdom and mercy, and of honest work), wedding loans with Benten (the Fortune of romantic love) - is a direct match, down to which institution lends.

The decision it drove. The eight precincts distribute by trade across the quarters rather than gathering into one belt, because each is an economic house sited where its business is. The Japanese rim-belt teramachi is the considered alternative and is deliberately NOT taken: it is a castle-town DEFENSIVE arrangement - at Hirosaki the temples were gathered for the defense of the castle town and the control of its people, and these temples are commercial institutions first. A trade-sited precinct is sometimes the only temple in its quarter, so the rule that a temple neighborhood carries shrines applies per CLUSTER and a lone precinct is exempt - the same treatment Tango's converted-estate Bishamon already gets.

The housing inversion. China first: most Zhengyi Daoist priests lived a married life at home rather than in a temple, and the office often passes from generation to generation within a family; they RESIDE IN HOUSEHOLDS rather than in a monastery, normally affiliated with a temple without living in it. All three works describe clergy their authors met or read of in China and Taiwan in the twentieth century and after - though one of them has the state questioning priests who lived at home under the Ming and Qing, and a hereditary master another interviewed counts himself the twenty-second generation - and what they carry back is the shape of the institution rather than its numbers. Japan corroborates twice: Shinto priests marry and their children inherit, the chief priesthood of the Izumo shrine is counted past eighty generations, its present holder succeeding his father (the hereditary office was abolished in 1871 but persists by preference); and Jodo Shinshu was the one tradition the Tokugawa exempted so a temple head could marry and keep priestly status, producing the bomori and eldest-son succession. The GM's campaign notes' Fox rule - only the Three Bonds celibate, every other position hereditary, "the temple's revenue sources are effectively family businesses passed down along bloodlines" - is essentially that arrangement with a small celibate administrative core. So a Fox precinct is a small walled workplace ringed by its temple families.
