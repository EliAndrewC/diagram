Shrine woods and fences (chinju no mori, tamagaki)

A Japanese village shrine stood in its own wood, the chinju no mori, kept in and around its precinct so as to surround the sanctuary, the approach and the place of worship. Which sides of the hall the wood took followed the ground: all around on a rise or amid the paddy of the plain, and on a slope behind the hall, at its sides, or both.

The wood and the arch where the approach entered, not a wall, marked a village shrine's ground. Japan's shrine fence, the tamagaki, is old at the great shrines, but every dated fence found at an ordinary shrine rings the sanctuary alone or lines the approach, and those round a sanctuary date from 1918 and 1928.

  • A shrine's wood is kept so as to surround its sanctuary, its approach and its place of worship.
    That is how the Japanese encyclopedia defines the chinju no mori: a wood set up and kept within the precinct and around it. A small shrine with houses built right up to its sanctuary is not counted as having one.
  • In Japan's rice country, a shrine's wood with paddy all around it is still a common sight.
    So says the caption of the encyclopedia's lead photograph, a shrine wood in Hyōgo surrounded by paddy fields.
  • A naturalist puts a shrine's big trees in two places: single trees in front and along the approach, and a grove behind the main building and around its sides.
    The grove is in many cases the remnant of the forest that grew there before. The account is general, of shrines and temples today, and its named examples are great shrines; that it holds at a village shrine is this project's reading.
  • Which sides of the hall did the wood stand on?
    It depends on the slope. The one survey read that sorts shrine woods by where they stand, of the 27 wooded shrines on slopes inside Tokyo's Yamanote loop, ties the form to where the hall sits. Where the hall stands at the foot of a slope, at its lower break of slope, the wood is on the slope behind it - behind only, or behind and at the sides. Where the hall stands at the top of a slope and the approach climbs to it, the wood is behind and at the sides, or at the sides only. None of its forms is a wood in front of the hall alone.
  • On a hill-shaped rise, the wood stood all around the hall.
    The survey found few shrines of this form. At the other extreme, where the hall stands at the top of a gentle slope, a shrine may keep no more than a row of trees along its approach, with buildings or other land beyond it.
  • How far can a modern city's survey speak for the countryside before 1868?
    Only as to which forms occur, not how often. The survey sorts the woods a modern city has left: many of them burned in the war and were replanted, and their grounds have shrunk and been built on, so any of its forms may be in part what the city left, and its shares do not come out as whole counts of its 27 shrines. The paper names only one form for its weakest, built-up shrines, the row of trees along the approach.
  • So was a village shrine wooded on every side?
    Not always. It was wooded all around on a rise and, by the definition and the common sight in the rice country, in the paddy plain; on a slope it was wooded behind the hall, at its sides, or both, with the approach running up through open ground.
  • What shape was a shrine wood's outline?
    No source read gives a wood's plan, and how village maps before 1868 drew a shrine's trees was not found. The picture modern writers hold up, since the ecologists of the late 1970s, is "a clearly demarcated, broccoli-shaped patch" of natural broad-leaved forest - "broccoli-shaped" is the canopy seen from the side, and only "clearly demarcated" speaks to the plan - and the same article says at once that many real shrine woods are conifers planted in the premodern or modern period. A remnant of old forest has the edge its trees give it.
  • A shrine's precinct came in time to be surveyed land.
    Shrine precincts were among the grounds exempted from the annual tax, and though at first left out of the land survey, they came in time to be surveyed and entered in the survey register, the kenchicho, as exempt. No source read says the wood's edge ran along that surveyed line.
  • Where a wood meets paddy fields, could its foot run straight along them?
    One picture shows it. The encyclopedia's photograph of a shrine wood surrounded by paddy, taken at ground level, shows the wood's crowns ragged against the sky but its foot running level along the edge of the fields, as straight as a view from the ground can show, the arch at that edge and the approach a grass strip across the field. The photograph is modern and shows one side.

The other way a shrine's ground might be bounded is a fence, and Japanese has a word for it.

  • Japanese calls a shrine's fence a tamagaki, or a mizugaki.
    A tamagaki is "a fence set up around a shrine or sacred precinct", and the encyclopedia's example is Ise, the imperial shrine, with four fences in concentric rings, the innermost called the mizugaki.
  • The shrine fence is old at the great shrines.
    The historical dictionary quotes the word from the Kojiki, the chronicle of 712, and a shrine's "tamagaki, mizugaki, aragaki and so on, layer upon layer" from a collection of Buddhist tales of 1283.
  • The older fences were wooden rails or hedges, and stone ones spread later.
    The page that says so gives no date for the change.
  • Before 1868, a fence round a sanctuary is known only where the shogunate or a lord built the shrine.
    The shogunate's rebuilding of Iwashimizu Hachimangu in 1634 enclosed its sanctuary with a fence, and at Iwakiyama Shrine the lord of the Hirosaki domain built, between 1686 and 1694, a fence that runs from the inner gate "once round the main sanctuary". No fence round a village shrine's sanctuary dated before 1868 was found.
  • At ordinary shrines, the dated fences ring the sanctuary alone or line the approach.
    At Yahira Shrine a roofed board fence of 1918 stands around the sanctuary, "built with its height kept down, so that the sanctuary's roof can be seen from around it", on a sanctuary built in 1700 - 218 years before its fence. At Shirahata Shrine a stone fence carved with a date in 1928, raised to mark the emperor's enthronement, "surrounds the shrine building at the top of the stairs". The one late-Edo stone fence found, at Yasuda Hachiman Shrine, stands "on both sides of the shrine's approach", 0.6 m (~2 ft) high.
  • Some neighborhood shrines had nothing round them but an arch.
    A patron asking the Fukui Prefectural Library about shrine fences, in a 2008 entry of the National Diet Library's reference database, explained that "my neighborhood shrine has only a torii", that someone was thinking of building something, and that other shrines had hedges or stone. Where the arch stands on the approach is at Shrine gateways and the approach to the hall (torii, sando).
  • Was a village shrine's whole precinct ever fenced?
    No example was found, and none of the dated fences rings a whole precinct. That no village shrine's precinct was fenced whole is this section's reading of the dated examples; no page states it as a general rule.
  • Where a wall marked rank, the recorded case is a temple's.
    Temples built the striped earthen wall, whose number of white lines expressed the temple's rank, five the highest.

China's village shrines agree at the bottom of the scale.

  • The humblest Chinese earth-god shrine is two stones for walls and one for a roof.
    It stands under a tree or by a road, with no room for a courtyard.
  • The one Chinese village temple read with a courtyard does not say what bounded it.
    Gantang Temple, the village temple of Shui village, stood on about 2.5 mu (~0.4 acre, reckoning the modern mu).
