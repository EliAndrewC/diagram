How large was a village shrine's precinct, and how much of it was built on?

Sources: ranzan-shrine-register (fourteen village shrines of one Saitama district), sano-shrine-gazetteer (fifty village shrines of one Tochigi district), kanzaki-hamlet-shrines (hamlet shrines of one Saga district, with Edo site areas), agechi-rei-jawiki (the Meiji land orders), rokusha-jinja-fuchu (a precinct and its buildings in one entry), chinju-no-mori-jawiki (the wood), gantang-temple-thepaper (China)

What the research found - the area. Japan's shrine registers give each precinct's area in tsubo, and three districts' registers can be read, transcribed shrine by shrine. In Hiki district, Saitama, the fourteen village-rank shrines, the sonsha, run from 55 tsubo to 708 tsubo, with a median of 403 tsubo, about 14,300 sq ft, the area of a square some 120 ft on a side. In Sano, Tochigi, fifty village shrines run from 142 to 2,700 tsubo, with a median of 655, about 23,300 sq ft. One step down, the unranked hamlet shrines, the mukakusha, and a few "miscellaneous" ones of Kanzaki district, Saga, from the registers of about 1880 and 1915, run from 50 to 662 tsubo with a median of 148, about 5,300 sq ft. A village shrine's ground, then, ran typically from about 150 to 650 tsubo, some 5,000 to 23,000 sq ft, and a hamlet's smaller one lay at the bottom of that band. Fujian's village temples are put at around 100 m² in the south of the province and over 400 m² in the north-east, figures that no readable page confirms; one Zhejiang village temple's compound stood on about 2.5 mu, some 500 tsubo.

Why the registers may not give an Edo precinct. Every register figure above is Meiji or later, and each was taken after the government took shrine lands: the domains that temples and shrines had held in the Edo period were confiscated by the orders of 1871 and 1875, and on the Kanzaki page the land-tax reform of 1876 split a shrine's ground into precinct and outside land and put the outside land into state hands, a survey of 1877 keeping 1 tan 13 bu of one shrine's former grounds of 7 tan 5 se 27 bu as its present precinct. So a register can understate an Edo precinct. It can also overstate one: some precincts were later enlarged by returned land - a Hiki shrine took back 1 tan 5 se 1 bu of forest in 1903 - and by mergers, Sano's Wakukama Shrine growing fivefold in 1911 when another shrine's ground was joined to it. The one Edo set on any page read is Kanzaki's: for several of the same hamlet shrines the page quotes the Saga domain's temple-and-shrine returns of 1663 to 1859, which give each site in se and bu - mostly 2 to 8 se, about 60 to 240 tsubo, one as small as 1 se 15 bu and a few 1 tan (300 tsubo) or more, a se being 30 tsubo. That set is a Kyushu plain's hamlet shrines, the bottom of the band.

How much of it was built on. Very little. Rokusha Shrine in Hiroshima, a village shrine from 1872, lists a worship hall of 9 tsubo and an office of 30 inside a precinct of 285 tsubo: 13.7 per cent built. The Tochigi register's Wakukama Shrine has a worship hall 5 ken by 2 ken and a sanctuary under 1 tsubo, with an offering hall of 1 ken by 1 and a half, on 457 tsubo, under 3 per cent. The buildings of a village shrine covered somewhere between a fortieth and a seventh of its ground. What covered the rest, no source measures. The registers single out old, dense trees - one unranked shrine's 1,848 tsubo are praised as unmatched in the region for "their luxuriant, dense trees" - and a shrine's wood, the chinju no mori, is by definition kept around its sanctuary, its approach and its place of worship. That the unbuilt ground was mostly that wood is a guess from those two, not a measurement.

The decision. The precinct is drawn as the grove (the GM's ruling of 2026-09-27, choosing to add a grove around the shrine on the village map): the wood surrounds the hall and the sanctuary and runs out along the approach to the outermost arch, its area inside the register band. The band is the registers as found, which may understate or overstate an Edo precinct. The Hoshigaoka precinct, about 860 tsubo, drawn as the map's grove from its well to its outermost arch, sits above Saitama's largest and above Tochigi's mean of 817 (accurate as a band; its place in the band a guess; the wood's share of it a guess). The swept ground is a small clearing at the hall and the sanctuary, not a yard. Why no fence bounds it is under "Was a village shrine walled or fenced?", and what else stands in it under "What else stood in a village shrine's precinct?".
