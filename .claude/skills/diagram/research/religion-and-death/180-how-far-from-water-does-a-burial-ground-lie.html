How far from water does a burial ground lie?

Sources: meiji-1884-bochi-saimoku (the Home Ministry's siting rule of 1884: away from great rivers, on high, dry ground, clear of drinking water), louzeyuan-zhwiki (the Chinese state burial grounds on high, open, barren ground), kawazoe-2010-ryobosei (the burial ground downstream of the houses, and across the water from them), isesaki-bochi-kijun (a present-day set-back from rivers and lakes), paddy-field-enwiki (the flooded field)

What the sources say: high and dry, and downstream. A Home Ministry instruction of 1884 on siting graveyards keeps a new one off the banks of great rivers and puts it on high, dry ground where it does no harm to drinking water. China's state burial grounds for paupers were set the same way, generally on high, open, barren ground. A survey of 22 two-grave settlements finds the village's burial ground placed along its watercourse: it may lie below the houses, downstream, but never above them, and the surveyor suggests that putting it downstream may also have been meant to avoid a hygiene problem. That is not the same as keeping it far from water: sometimes, mainly in fishing villages, the burial ground lies across a stream from the houses, and those by the sea stand on unstable ground that tsunami wash away.

What no source gives: a distance. None of these puts a figure on how far from water a grave must lie. The only figure found is modern: one Japanese city today requires a graveyard to stand 20 m, about 65 ft, from a river or a lake. The ladder below is therefore reasoned from the hazard, and its distances are a guess, set well beyond that modern floor. The reasoning, this project's own and on no page read, is that a burial ground by big water floods out, so the bigger the watercourse, the further back the dead lie; and because even narrow open water floods a grave, the ladder has a solid floor rather than starting at nothing. A flooded paddy counts as open water - rice stands in water, and a grave against a field edge is a real flood hazard - while a thin irrigation channel does not. Two refinements earn their place. The CREMATION ground may sit nearer the water than the graves, because fire and its washing rites want water at hand and a fire site is not flood-sensitive, so the burial ground naturally lands beyond the crematory as seen from the water. And a ground INSIDE the wall is exempt from the moat's set-back, the moat being outside and the rampart between; streams and ponds still count on either side of a wall. A walled crypt precinct is exempt entirely - a crypt compound is not a flood-sensitive open plot. The rule the map follows: the set-back scales with the watercourse and never falls below its floor. On a city sheet (3 ft per pixel) that floor is about 225 ft from a stream, rising to about 390 ft from the moat, which is the heaviest water a map carries at about 78 ft wide, and capped at about 420 ft for a river or a canal; a flooded field edge takes about 150 ft of its own. The same drawn bands read about two thirds of those figures on a village sheet at 2 ft per pixel . A cremation ground keeps a fixed modest margin of about 90 ft instead and is exempt from the field-edge margin altogether. The distances are a guess.
