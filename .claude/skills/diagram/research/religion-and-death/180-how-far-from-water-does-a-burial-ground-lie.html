How far from water does a burial ground lie?

Sources: meiji-1884-bochi-saimoku (the Home Ministry's siting rule of 1884: away from great rivers, on high, dry ground, clear of drinking water), louzeyuan-zhwiki (the Chinese state burial grounds on high, open, barren ground), sohu-xuancheng-yizhong (Song-dynasty county pauper grounds on high, barren ground, one to ten li outside the city), yagura-jawiki-weblio (Kyoto's commoners' burial ground on the lower Kamo River, in 871), kotobank-ryobosei (the burial grave on riverbeds, hills and beaches), kawazoe-2010-ryobosei (the burial ground downstream of the houses, and across the water from them), isesaki-bochi-kijun (a present-day set-back from rivers and lakes), paddy-field-enwiki (the flooded field)

What the sources say: high and dry, and downstream. A Home Ministry instruction of 1884 on siting graveyards keeps a new one off the banks of great rivers and puts it on high, dry ground where it does no harm to drinking water. China's state burial grounds for paupers were set the same way, generally on high, open, barren ground. A survey of 22 two-grave settlements finds the village's burial ground placed along its watercourse: it may lie below the houses, downstream, but never above them, and the surveyor suggests that putting it downstream may also have been meant to avoid a hygiene problem. That is not the same as keeping it far from water: sometimes, mainly in fishing villages, the burial ground lies across a stream from the houses, and those by the sea stand on unstable ground that tsunami wash away.

Before modern times: high ground in China, and in Japan often by the water. The high, barren site is premodern in China. The Song dynasty's pauper grounds, ordered from 1104, lay one to ten li outside the county towns, local gazetteers of several Zhejiang counties record, and some were made where they "chose high, barren ground". In Japan the 1884 instruction is the earliest rule on water found, and it is modern. Japan's older practice ran the other way: in 871 the Council of State (the Daijokan) kept the lower reaches of Kyoto's Kamo River from being turned to farmland because they were "the burial ground of the common people and a place for grazing", though the same article says most commoners of that age were laid out in the open rather than buried, so it shows where the dead were taken more than where dug graves lay; and the burial grave of the two-grave villages, a custom the Heibonsha encyclopedia dates to the early Edo period, was set on riverbeds, in the hills and on beaches. The downstream side is attested only in today's villages, by the survey above.

What no source gives: a distance. No source before modern times puts a figure on how far from water a grave must lie. The only figure found is modern: one Japanese city today requires a graveyard to stand 20 m, about 65 ft, from a river or a lake. A ladder of set-backs that grew with the watercourse, set beyond that modern floor, would be a guess resting on a modern rule alone, so under the GM's ruling of 2026-09-28 that the maps keep nothing only modern, the maps do not draw it. What stands is the site: a burial ground takes the higher ground to hand, as China's pauper grounds did (dry ground is asked only by the modern 1884 rule, so the maps do not rest on it), and it is never drawn in open water or in a flooded field, where a grave would fill with water. A paddy is a flooded field; a thin irrigation channel is not open water. The cremation ground may sit nearer the water than the graves, because fire and its washing rites want water at hand and a fire site does not flood out.
