How far from water does a burial ground lie?

Sources: paddy-field-enwiki; this project's own funerary doctrine - death pollution and flooding - with no measured set-back found in any source

A burial ground by big water floods out, so the bigger the watercourse, the further back the dead must lie; and because even narrow open water floods a grave, the ladder has a solid floor rather than starting at nothing. A flooded paddy counts as open water - rice stands in water, and a grave against a field edge is a real flood hazard - while a thin irrigation channel does not. Two refinements earn their place. The CREMATION ground may sit nearer the water than the graves, because fire and its washing rites want water at hand and a fire site is not flood-sensitive, so the burial ground naturally lands beyond the crematory as seen from the water. And a ground INSIDE the wall is exempt from the moat term, the moat being outside and the rampart between; streams and ponds still count on either side of a wall. A walled crypt precinct is exempt entirely - a crypt compound is not a flood-sensitive open plot. No source was found giving a distance: the ladder is reasoned from the hazard, and its numbers are this project's own.

The rule the map follows: the set-back scales with the watercourse and never falls below its floor. On a city sheet (3 ft per pixel) that floor is about 225 ft from a stream, rising to about 390 ft from the moat, which is the heaviest water a map carries at about 78 ft wide, and capped at about 420 ft for a river or a canal; a flooded field edge takes about 150 ft of its own. The same drawn bands read about two thirds of those figures on a village sheet at 2 ft per pixel . A cremation ground keeps a fixed modest margin of about 90 ft instead and is exempt from the field-edge margin altogether.
