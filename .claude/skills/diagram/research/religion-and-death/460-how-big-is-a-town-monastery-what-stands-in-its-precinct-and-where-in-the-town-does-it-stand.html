How big is a town monastery, what stands in its precinct, and where in the town does it stand?

Sources: ranzan-temple-register (nineteen temples of one Saitama district), ranzan-anyoji (the smallest precinct of them), ranzan-koshoji (the largest, with its gates), ranzan-soshinji (a bell tower and a storehouse), ranzan-kinsenji (a graveyard held outside the precinct), eijuji-shinshiro (a training temple's hall and plan), amagasaki-teramachi (the temple quarter in a castle town's corner), miyazu-teramachi (a planned temple quarter), pingyao-gucheng-zhwiki (a Chinese county seat's temples inside its walls); the GM's campaign notes (a monastery just outside a town)

How big. The Hiki district register gives each temple's precinct in tsubo and its main hall's frontage in ken. Its nineteen temples run from 285 tsubo to 1,944 tsubo, with a median of 462 tsubo, about 16,400 sq ft, the area of a square some 128 ft on a side; their main halls run from 4 to 10 and a half ken across the front, about 24 to 63 ft, with a median of 7 ken, about 42 ft. These are the temples of a rural district's villages, set down in a register of the Meiji era carried into 1963, so they may not be an Edo precinct (why a register can understate or overstate one is under "How large was a village shrine's precinct, and how much of it was built on?"); a county seat's monastery is not likely to have been smaller, which is this page's inference. A Zen training temple in Aichi, a larger house, has a main hall of 6 ken by 7 ken, 16.57 m by 14.29 m, about 54 by 47 ft, built in 1697.

What stands in the precinct. Besides the main hall and the kuri, the register lists what each temple held: a bell tower 1 ken square and an earthen storehouse at one, and at the largest a treasure house, a main gate, a middle gate, an outer gate and a side gate. The Aichi temple sets its gate at the south end of the precinct's axis. The graveyard was not always inside: one temple's register entry lists its graveyard, 4 se 10 bu, about 130 tsubo, among the land it held outside the precinct, at another named place in its village. Whether a town temple's precinct was walled, fenced or hedged is on no page read.

Where in the town. Two forms are attested. A Japanese castle town gathered its temples into a temple quarter at its edge: Amagasaki's lies in the town's northwest corner, north of the samurai quarter, and is thought to have been set apart from the townsmen's quarter to weaken the temples and to use their great buildings in the castle's defense; Miyazu's twelve lie in the town's southwest part, a quarter thought to have been planned with the castle town itself. A Chinese county seat kept its temples inside its walls, placed by its plan: at Pingyao the Daoist temple stands in the east and the Buddhist temple in the west, symmetrical about the main street. In the setting's own campaign, one town's monastery stands just outside the town.

The decision. A town monastery's precinct and hall are rolled within the register's bands (accurate as bands; their fit to a county town is this page's inference). What stands in it follows the register (accurate). Its boundary is a guess: the register's gates imply an enclosure it does not describe. Where it stands is a knob, rolled per town from its seed between the two attested forms (accurate). Where its graveyard lies is ruled under "Does a graveyard stand beside its temple or its shrine?".

The rule the map follows: on a town sheet (1 ft per pixel) each monastery's precinct is about 10,000 to 69,000 sq ft, about 16,400 sq ft by default ; its main hall is 24 to 63 ft across the front, about 42 ft by default; the precinct holds the hall, a kuri beside it, a bell tower about 6 ft square and a gate at the end of its axis, and is bounded by a line broken only at its gates (a guess). Where it stands is rolled per town: EDGE, at the rim of the town, both monasteries near each other and apart from its street of shops, or AXIAL, inside the town, set either side of its main street.
