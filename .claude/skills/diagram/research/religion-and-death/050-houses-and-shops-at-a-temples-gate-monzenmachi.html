Houses and shops at a temple's gate (monzenmachi)

Before a large temple or shrine that drew many worshippers, tradesmen and craftsmen gathered to do business with its own people and its visitors, and the houses they built made a gate town. A city temple's gate sold food and rest from the start: a reference work says a gate town grew when a temple's festival market became permanent and eating houses were added to it, and Edo's new gate towns of the first half of the seventeenth century already had rest teahouses and souvenir shops.

An ordinary temple's gate held a short row of shallow houses - some twenty households at one Edo temple of average size, most of them a neighborhood's trades rather than a pilgrim's - while a great pilgrimage temple's approach carried rows of teahouses and souvenir shops. The temple's own amulets were, in this project's reading, not among the gate's wares: a temple issued them itself, and what a visitor paid was taken as a donation to it.

  • One ordinary Edo temple's gate held twenty-two households.
    In 1843, late in the Edo period, twenty-two households stood on the gate-town land of Kyozenji in Azabu, a temple of average size with a precinct of about 1,000 tsubo (~0.8 acre).
  • Half of those households owned their houses.
    A 2014 geography paper on Edo's religious spaces counts 11 of Kyozenji's 22 gate houses as lived in by their owners, and the rest let.
  • Kyozenji's gate households followed a neighborhood's trades rather than a pilgrim's.
    The geography paper's table of the 1843 households lists sake-sellers, a rice-polisher, a pawnbroker, a fruit-seller, dealers in secondhand goods and in secondhand clothes, a fishmonger, a sundries shop, a seller of stewed food, a stonemason, a cooper, a lantern-maker, a tofu-maker, a shingle roofer, a hairdresser, a florist and a pouch-maker.
  • Some of those families had kept their place at the gate for generations.
    The same table has the stonemason's household there about 150 years by 1843, and the cooper's about 180.
  • Gate-town houses at a temple of average size were shallow, about 3 ken (~18 ft) deep.
    The 2014 geography paper gives Kyozenji's houses that depth and calls it the usual look of a gate town at a temple of Kyozenji's size.
  • By the middle of the eighteenth century Edo had 440 gate-town wards.
    The shogunate then made them town land, and 227 temple and shrine precincts with them.
  • Edo's gate towns stood on the temple's own precinct land, a form rarely seen in Kyoto or Osaka.
    The 2014 geography paper calls the form characteristic of Edo and not generally found in Kyoto or Osaka.
  • No page we read counts a gate town's households against its temple's monks.
    Kyozenji's paper counts its gate households and not its monks, and the pages on other great temples' gates describe their streets without counting them.

At a temple that drew pilgrims from far off, the gate's first business was feeding, resting and lodging them.

  • At the pilgrimage temples the inn came first.
    Inns gathered before the gate for the worshippers and their pilgrimage confraternities (kō); only after the railways brought day-trip worship did eating houses and souvenir shops replace them.
  • Twenty water teahouses stood in a row on Sensoji's approach in the Edo period.
    They stood nearer the inner gate, and the shops nearer the outer gate sold toys, sweets and souvenirs.
  • The right to trade on Sensoji's approach began as a privilege of the residents who swept it.
    Early in the Edo period it was granted to the local residents who owed the temple the cleaning of its precinct and approach.
  • About 90 shops line Sensoji's approach today.
    They stand along roughly 250 m (~820 ft) of street, rebuilt in the 1880s and again in the 1920s, so this is a present count, not an Edo one.
  • Did the gate's shops, rather than the temple, do most of the feeding?
    Most likely, in this project's reading, a GUESS: the gate towns on record - Edo's teahouses, Sensoji's approach, the pilgrimage inns - carry food, drink and rest because worshippers need feeding and resting, and no page read weighs the gate's share against the temple's.
  • At Kumano, a mountain pilgrimage site, souvenir shops grew into a gate town.
    A signed encyclopedia entry has the Kumano shugenja selling souvenir goods in the early modern period, and specialist souvenir shops coming into being as demand grew and forming the gate town - talismans and amulets above all, with lucky dolls, toys, picture books and sacred medicines beside them. Those talismans are a mountain pilgrimage's; at a city temple the amulets are the temple's own, as the bullets below say.
  • Why is the approach to Zenkoji, a great temple, known for soba today?
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on soba says the name seems to have settled from the many soba shops on the street and from worshippers seeking a place to rest and a meibutsu, a place's famous specialty. It describes the present street and gives no date.
  • Sweets sold at the highway teahouses grew into a province's named sweets.
    Travelers spread word of the sweets they found on the road in the Edo period, and that is how local sweets developed.

What passed between a temple and its believers - amulets, and the incense and oil given for its offerings - passed directly, with no shop between.

  • An omamori is issued by the shrine or temple itself, and what a visitor pays is viewed mainly as a donation.
    The English Wikipedia article on omamori calls the payment mainly a donation to the place that issues it, rather than the price of a line of stock.
  • Factory-made charms, and charms issued by no shrine, are a modern trade.
    The same article gives both as modern: popular shrines turning to factories when they cannot meet demand, and commercial charms issued by no shrine or temple.
  • In China, incense and lamp oil were given to a temple, and later money in their place.
    Believers gave incense sticks, candles, lamp oil and gold paper to temples and monasteries for the offerings, and later gave money instead, which is why the money itself is called incense-oil money.
  • How much of a temple's keep did its amulets, incense and oil bring in?
    A major part, as a GUESS: the relation between temple and believer is attested, but no page read carries a temple's own accounts booking a yearly income from lamp oil and incense. How a temple earned the rest of its keep is at Temple clergy, their families, and how a temple earned its keep.

Beyond the shops, two other trades worked for a temple: the craftsmen who kept its buildings, and, in this project's reconstruction, the makers of funeral goods.

  • Where did the funeral trades stand?
    By the graveyard rather than by the hall, in this project's reconstruction, a GUESS: coffin-makers, grave-marker cutters, and, a detail drawn from China, the makers of the spirit money and the paper replicas of houses, servants and goods that a family burns for its dead, serving the parish's deaths, over which the temple presides without making the goods. Kyozenji's gate did house a stonemason and a florist, but no coffin-maker, and no page read puts a row of funeral makers by a burial ground.
  • Two great temples kept carpenters of their own, one of them in a village by its gate.
    A carpenters' house served Shitennoji on the temple's stipend up to the early modern period, and the carpenters Eiheiji's founder Dogen is said to have brought from Song-dynasty China made a village near Eiheiji's gate and handled its enlargements and repairs for generations.
  • Did a temple keep gilders and painters the same way?
    This project extends the carpenters' pattern to them, a GUESS: no page read describes gilders or painters retained by a temple.

In China the record is of fairs: on a fair day a temple's own ground became the market.

  • Diviners and physicians put up sheds at a Chinese village temple's yearly fair.
    A Qing county gazetteer, quoted in a study of Chinese temple fairs, has the physicians, diviners, astrologers and physiognomists putting up sheds at a village temple's annual fair, beside the storytellers and the conjurors - a fair rather than a permanent shopfront.
  • A great Chinese temple, the Xiangguo, opened for trade five times a month under the Song dynasty.
    The same study quotes a Song-dynasty account of the temple opening five times a month for the common folk to trade.
  • Did the fair's traders rent the temple's ground?
    A study of Chinese temple fairs says most of the fair-going traders rented the temple's buildings and plots of ground and came back to them between fairs. That sentence rests on a 1991 compilation on Chinese society under the Republic (1912-1949), while the fair's span from Tang to Qing rests on Tang tales at one end and on Qing and Republican gazetteers at the other.
