Houses and shops at a temple's gate (monzenmachi)

Before a large temple or shrine that drew many worshippers, tradesmen and craftsmen gathered to do business with its own people and its visitors, and the houses they built made a gate town. A city temple's gate had them early: a reference work names the eating houses added to a gate town's permanent festival market, and Edo's new gate towns of the first half of the seventeenth century already had rest teahouses and souvenir shops.

An ordinary temple's gate held a short row of shallow houses - some twenty households at one Edo temple of average size, most of them a neighborhood's trades rather than a pilgrim's - while a great pilgrimage temple's approach carried rows of teahouses and souvenir shops. The temple's own amulets were not among the gate's wares: a temple issued them itself, and what a visitor paid was taken as a donation to it.

  • One ordinary Edo temple's gate held twenty-two households.
    In 1843, late in the Edo period, twenty-two households stood on the gate-town land of Kyozenji in Azabu. Kyozenji, with a precinct of about 1,000 tsubo (~0.8 acre), was a temple of average size, and half of the households on its gate-town land were owned by the families living in them and the rest let.
  • They were a neighborhood's trades rather than a pilgrim's.
    Sake-sellers, a rice-polisher, a pawnbroker, a fruit-seller, dealers in secondhand goods and in secondhand clothes, a fishmonger, a sundries shop, a seller of stewed food, a stonemason and a cooper each there some 150 to 180 years, a lantern-maker, a tofu-maker, a shingle roofer, a hairdresser, a florist and a pouch-maker.
  • The gate's houses were shallow, about 3 ken (~18 ft) deep.
    The paper calls that the usual look of a gate town at a temple of Kyozenji's size.
  • By the middle of the eighteenth century Edo had 440 gate-town wards.
    The shogunate then turned them into town land, with 227 temple and shrine precincts.
  • Houses built on the temple's own precinct land were Edo's form.
    The paper says the form was not generally found in Kyoto or Osaka.

At a temple that drew pilgrims from far off, the gate's first business was feeding, resting and lodging them.

  • At the pilgrimage temples the inn came first.
    Inns gathered before the gate for the worshippers and their pilgrimage confraternities; only after the railways brought day-trip worship did eating houses and souvenir shops replace them.
  • Twenty water teahouses stood in a row on Sensoji's approach in the Edo period.
    They stood nearer the inner gate, and the shops nearer the outer gate sold toys, sweets and souvenirs. The right to trade there began as a privilege given to the local residents who owed the temple the sweeping of its precinct and approach.
  • About 90 shops line Sensoji's approach today.
    They stand along roughly 250 m (~820 ft) of street, rebuilt in the 1880s and again in the 1920s, so this is a present count, not an Edo one.
  • Did the gate's shops, rather than the temple, do most of the feeding?
    That is this record's reading of the gate towns quoted above, a GUESS: they carry food, drink and rest because worshippers need feeding and resting, and no page read weighs the gate's share against the temple's.
  • Sweets sold at the highway teahouses grew into a province's named sweets.
    Travelers spread word of the sweets they found on the road in the Edo period, and that is how local sweets developed.
  • At Kumano, souvenir shops grew into a gate town.
    A signed encyclopedia entry has the Kumano shugenja selling souvenir goods in the early modern period, and specialist souvenir shops coming into being as demand grew and forming the gate town - talismans and amulets above all, with lucky dolls, toys, picture books and sacred medicines beside them. Those talismans are a mountain pilgrimage's; at a city temple the amulets are the temple's own, as the bullets below say.
  • A worshipper may look for a meibutsu at the gate.
    The soba shops on Zenkoji's approach today seem to owe their name to their density and to worshippers seeking a place to rest and the place's famous specialty.

What a temple sold of its own - amulets, and the incense and oil of its offerings - passed between the temple and the believer directly, with no shop between.

  • An amulet was issued by the shrine or temple itself, and paid for as a donation.
    An omamori is issued by a shrine or a temple and is viewed mainly as a donation to the place that issues it, rather than the price of a line of stock. Manufacture away from the precinct, and charms issued by no shrine at all, belong to the modern trade.
  • In China, incense and lamp oil were given to a temple, and later money in their place.
    Believers gave incense sticks, candles, lamp oil and gold paper to temples and monasteries for the offerings, and later gave money instead, which is why the money itself is called incense-oil money.
  • How much of a temple's keep did its amulets, incense and oil bring in?
    A major part, as a GUESS: the relation between temple and believer is attested, but no page read carries a temple's own accounts booking a yearly income from lamp oil and incense. How a temple earned the rest of its keep is at Temple clergy, their families, and how a temple earned its keep.

Beyond the shops, a temple drew two other kinds of trade near it: the makers of funeral goods, and the craftsmen who kept its buildings.

  • Where did the funeral trades stand?
    By the graveyard rather than by the hall, in this project's reconstruction, a GUESS: coffin-makers, grave-marker cutters, and, a detail drawn from China, the makers of the spirit money and the paper replicas of houses, servants and goods that a family burns for its dead, serving the parish's deaths, over which the temple presides without making the goods. Kyozenji's gate did house a stonemason and a florist, but no coffin-maker, and no page read puts a row of funeral makers by a burial ground.
  • Temples kept carpenters of their own, living near the gate.
    A carpenters' house served Shitennoji on the temple's stipend up to the early modern period, and the carpenters Eiheiji's founder Dogen is said to have brought from Song-dynasty China made a village near Eiheiji's gate and handled its enlargements and repairs for generations.
  • Did a temple keep gilders and painters the same way?
    This project extends the carpenters' pattern to them, a GUESS: no page read describes gilders or painters retained by a temple.

On a fair day the temple's own ground became the market.

  • Chinese temples held fairs on their own ground.
    A Qing county gazetteer has the physicians, diviners, astrologers and physiognomists putting up sheds at a village temple's annual fair, beside the storytellers and the conjurors - a fair rather than a permanent shopfront. A Song account has the Xiangguo Temple opening five times a month for the common folk to trade.
  • Did the fair's traders rent the temple's ground?
    A study of Chinese temple fairs says most of the fair-going traders rented the temple's buildings and plots of ground and came back to them between fairs. That sentence rests on a 1991 compilation on Republican-era society, while the fair's span from Tang to Qing rests on Tang tales at one end and on Qing and Republican gazetteers at the other.
  • How many gate households did a temple have for each of its monks?
    No page read says. Kyozenji's paper counts its gate households and not its monks, and the pages on other great temples' gates describe their streets without counting them.
