City temple size - the setting's deliberate liberty

Sources: religion-song-enwiki (the 1221 census: 400,000 monks and 61,000 nuns); the Edo temple count and the Shaolin count each say at their own sentence that no page read carries them (ja.wikipedia's page on the terauke temple-registration system (寺請制度) gives only 6,000 Jōdo temples in 1696); daxiangguo-enwiki ("several thousand" monks).

Historically the median temple was tiny: Edo Japan ran something on the order of 90,000 parish temples at one or two priests each, a figure on no page read, and small hereditary Chinese temples held a handful of monks; only the famous pilgrimage complexes ran large (the great Song public monasteries several hundred, Daxiangguo and Shaolin held a thousand or more, on no page read either). The setting deliberately over-sizes - every city temple is a major complex in a way history would usually lack. A provincial city (~3,000 inhabitants) has ~15-30 monks per complex (this project's own scale - no page read gives a monk count for any Ming urban monastery); a domain capital (~12,000) has ~50+ monks per complex. That puts clergy at ~1-2% of city population against the historical ~0.46% (Song China: 461,000 clergy in 1221 against ~100 million inhabitants) - a deliberate 2-5x density, softened in-world by the temples serving the whole province's worship, not just the walled city.

The census behind that ratio: 1221, ~461,000 clergy (400,000 monks + 61,000 nuns).
