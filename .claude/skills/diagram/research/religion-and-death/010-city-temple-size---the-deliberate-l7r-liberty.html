City temple size - the setting's deliberate liberty

Sources: the GM's campaign notes, l7r-castes (monks under 1% of the population); religion-song-enwiki (the 1221 census: 400,000 monks and 61,000 nuns); ukai-2021-otera-chizu (some 90,000 Edo temples); terauke-seido-jawiki (6,000 Jōdo temples in 1696); tokyo-archives-jiin (who an Edo temple's monks were); siming-zhi-buddhism-dfz (the Yuan count of temples and clergy, and Tiantong's monks); jingshan-zhwiki (Jingshan's monks under Dahui); daxiangguo-enwiki ("several thousand" monks); shaolin-zhwiki (Shaolin's monks at its height); kojima-sensoji-bettodai (Sensoji's thirty-three sub-temples); hongwu-emperor-enwiki (the Ming cap); honghua-zhwiki (a Ming temple's fifty-five official monks)

Historically the median temple was tiny. Edo Japan is thought to have had some 90,000 temples, a figure its writer gives as one view among several, and how many priests the ordinary one kept is on no page read: the one or two drawn here is a guess. What is read is who they were: a temple's clergy were its head priest, a retired head priest and disciples, many of them younger sons of farming or samurai households, ordained in their early teens and moving among their sect's temples before rising to head one. On the Chinese side a Yuan count found 42,318 temples and 213,148 monks and nuns across the realm, about five to a temple on average; how many a single small hereditary temple kept is on no page read. The famous great houses ran large: Tiantong went from under two hundred monks to twelve hundred under one Southern Song abbot, Jingshan passed 1,700 under Dahui and built a Thousand-Monk Pavilion in 1140 to hold them, Daxiangguo at its height held several thousand, and Shaolin over 2,000 by the Tang-Song period. A great Japanese city temple was a complex of small temples rather than one house: Sensoji's more than a hundred temples within the precinct were cut to thirty-three, twelve of scholar-monks and twenty-one of ritual monks, and the paper, after a legal historian, sorts Edo temples into three kinds: the single temple, the complex of sub-temples, and the sub-temple within one. The Ming founder went further the other way and capped each county at one monastery of at most two monks - a legal cap, not a census, and reading the median from it is this record's own inference.

The setting deliberately over-sizes - every city temple is a major complex, a deliberate deviation from a history in which most temples were small. A provincial city (~3,000 inhabitants) has ~15-30 monks per complex and a domain capital (~12,000) ~50+ monks per complex - this project's own scale, a guess: no page read gives a monk count for any Ming city monastery. The nearest Ming figure read is a frontier one - a Tibetan Buddhist monastery founded by imperial order in 1442 at Hezhou and given fifty-five official monks - the size of a domain capital's complex, as an establishment rather than a count. A provincial city has two complexes, 30 to 60 monks between them, which puts clergy at ~1-2% of its population against the historical ~0.46% (Song China: 461,000 clergy in 1221 against ~100 million inhabitants) - a deliberate 2-4x density, softened in-world by the temples serving the whole province's worship, not just the walled city: the GM's notes put monks at under 1% of the population as a whole.

The census behind that ratio: 1221, ~461,000 clergy (400,000 monks + 61,000 nuns).
