Where do the crematory, the ossuary and the clan mausoleum stand?

Sources: the GM's campaign notes for the cremate-then-inter canon and the caste standing of a religious order; kasoba-jawiki (burning in open country away from habitation, and the fire bed beside a burial ground), tokyo-kasoba-history (the 1884 set-back from dwellings and crowded ground, and the 1667 removal of the temple cremation grounds), shinagawa-kaizoji-muentou (the bone mound with its image, raised over the unconnected dead); the funeral path itself, traced to no readable page, zuihoden-jawiki

The setting always cremates and then inters the bones, so a graveyard here is a compact field of stone markers rather than a body-burial sprawl. Smoke, fire risk and pollution put the crematory OUTSIDE the walls, at a town's edge and clear of the dwellings, and the marginal siting is attested: the dead were burned in open country away from habitation, and where a permanent fire bed was made it lay beside a burial ground. The set-back is a stated rule where the record speaks: the model standard issued with the burial regulations of 1884 put a cremation ground at least 120 ken, about 216 m, from dwellings and from places where people congregate, on ground that is not upwind, walled, with a chimney and a device against the foul smoke. The premodern instance of the same hazard is Kanbun 7 (1667), when the smoke of the cremation grounds scattered through Asakusa and Shitaya reached the shogun on his way to visit a grave, and some twenty-odd of them were moved together to designated ground by the Kozukappara execution place. That the pyre is reached by a minor funeral path rather than by high-street frontage is this project's own reasoning from the same hazard, on nothing read. It also never stands between a temple and the road, because nobody should walk past the pyre to reach worship - behind or beside the hall, never on its ceremonial approach. And it adjoins an EXTRAMURAL burial ground, because the body is burned and its bones interred next door: the two are one funerary complex, and a graveyard inside the walls does not answer for it. Monks officiate with burakumin assistants, a religious order standing outside the caste system, so handling the dead does not pollute its caste.

Note the split that is easy to miss. A temple's own graveyard sits beside the temple wherever the temple is, and may perfectly well face the road; it is the CREMATORY that is the marginal exception, kept off the road and off the hall's approach. Minor town streets trigger neither rule - only the trunk road does.

The other two. The pauper OSSUARY is a low earthen mound with one weathered stupa holding the communal bones of the poor and the unconnected dead, and that form is on the record: a ward's cultural-property listing has the bones of those who died in the Shinagawa holding jail gathered up at a temple in 1708, a burial mound raised over them and a Kannon image set on top, with stupas for the unconnected dead raised beside it in 1865 and 1873. Where it stands - outside the walls by the crematory - is this project's own; it is a city feature, which is why a town's common ground has to absorb the same dead itself. The clan MAUSOLEUM is the opposite in every way: the ruling clan's ancestral crypts in a precinct with its own halls and gates - the attested instance, Sendai's Zuihoden, faces the castle's main keep across the river - drawn here INSIDE the walls by the samurai and government quarter as a prestige monument near the seat of power, a placement of this project's own; that ancestor veneration is central to samurai identity is on no page read. City only.

The rule the map follows: a TOWN draws a cremation ground at its edge, clear of the dwellings; a CITY draws the cremation ground and the pauper ossuary outside the walls, beyond a gate, and the clan mausoleum inside them by the samurai quarter. The crematory stands at least about 390 ft from the trunk road and, where a monastery or temple is within about 1,200 ft of it , at least as far from that road as the hall is, less a tolerance of about 120 ft for "beside it, a little away" . Its burial ground is extramural and within about 210 ft of it edge to edge . Where a wall of a walled compound (a mausoleum, a manor, a merchant estate) abuts a neighborhood fence, the compound YIELDS that side: the fence is drawn over the coincident wall and is that side of the precinct, the compound's own wall running underneath - a map drawing convention that keeps two clashing parallel walls off the sheet.
