When one shrine hall serves several kami, does each have its own altar?

Sources: kotobank-aidono (several kami in one hall, the lesser ranked below the main one, and the two-bay hall with two doors), jawiki-saijin (a shrine ordinarily keeps several kami), genbu-honden-styles (small halls joined under one roof), jawiki-goshi (a second kami in the main hall or in a shrine of its own), tokyo-jinjacho-kamidana (the order of talismans on a three-shrine and a one-shrine household altar shelf)

A shrine keeping more than one kami was the ordinary case, not the exception: one is the main kami and the rest are secondary. Keeping several in one hall has its own name, aidono, "enshrining two or more kami together in the same shrine hall", and the secondary kami are often honored below the main one. What the pages read do NOT settle is a single answer to whether each kami gets its own altar, because three forms are attested:

  • Side by side, a bay or a hall to each. A hall built as two bays joined into one, with two doors, was a recognized form, the two-bay shrine; another joined two or more small halls side by side under a single roof. The household version is the three-shrine altar shelf, its talismans side by side, the highest in the middle, the next to the right as you face it, the third to the left.
  • One altar, the kami ranked behind it. The one-shrine household shelf keeps all its talismans in one place, stacked in rank from front to back.
  • A shrine of its own. A kami brought in from elsewhere could be enshrined in the main hall, or given its own small shrine inside the precinct.

None of the pages says which form was usual, and none describes a shrine kept inside a samurai residence or an official's compound, so nothing read ranks the three for a magistrate's shrine.

The rule the map follows: the form is a knob, not a choice. A compound shrine that serves more than one kami takes one of the three forms, rolled from the map's seed: altars side by side across the hall, one to a bay, the main kami's in the middle or on the right as you face it; a single altar for the main kami, the others kept behind it and not drawn apart; or a second small shrine of its own inside the compound. Where a sheet draws several altars side by side in one hall, that is the first form; what the pages read attest is the two-bay hall and small halls joined under one roof, not separate altars inside one undivided hall, so drawing them in one undivided hall is a guess.
