What religious and funerary features does each size of settlement carry?

Sources: the GM's tier rule and the clan patron Fortunes, from the campaign notes; dosojin-jawiki (the wayside deity enshrined at a village's entrance, a pass, a crossroads and a bridge's foot), sanko-densetsu-jawiki (a small shrine beside a well); each cell of the table below is answered in the section it names

Type and rank track each other, and the two faiths divide the work. A HAMLET has no religious building of its own and shares its village district's, exactly as it has no headman. A VILLAGE has a shrine, tended by the one tax-free religious figure of the district, the country monk, whose dwelling may double as the shrine itself. A TOWN has monasteries, each dedicated to a patron Fortune of the clan whose holdings include it - a preceptor for each Order, serving in its town monastery. A CITY has full temples, its temple neighborhood dotted with small wayside shrines. An arch fronts any shrine, monastery or temple that a way runs straight up to. The GM's ruling of 2026-09-27 sets the district's funerary line: monks perform the funerary rites in the countryside and much of them in the cities; a town and a village each have a cremation ground; the country monk lives in the main village and serves its whole district, the village and its hamlets, usually about half a dozen; and within its district the village alone keeps the shrine, the headman's house and the cremation ground - its hamlets have none of them. Where the small shrines go. The floor of three wayside shrines near a cluster of temples is a minimum, not a pattern to repeat: additional ones placed at meaningful spots elsewhere read truer than one more shrine crowded by the halls. Historically a wayside shrine stood at a threshold: the deity of the way is enshrined at spatial and social boundaries - a village's entrance and exit, a mountain pass, a crossroads, the foot of a bridge - and a small shrine could stand by a well, as a dragon-god shrine does near a named well at Mikurahana. That gives the gate, the crossing, the bridge and the well, and a landing is the same kind of threshold, between water and land - so a shrine at the wharf, fittingly to the Fortune of fishermen and honest commerce, or at a bridge, a gate or a public well, is placed where the city's life turns.

The rule the map follows: each tier draws what its column of the table names, sized and sited as the section each cell names specifies. In short: a HAMLET draws its wayside stones and no shrine, arch, burial ground or cremation ground. A VILLAGE draws one shrine with its arches, one burial ground, one cremation ground, its wayside stones and, on its knob, its wayside halls. A TOWN draws its monasteries - two by default, one for each patron Fortune of the clan that holds it - with one or two graveyards in their precincts and, if it is walled, a common graveyard outside the walls as well, a cremation ground at its edge and a street shrine for each block. A CITY draws its temples - two major complexes by default, more only under a declared exception - with two to four graveyards and one in every temple precinct that has not opted out, at least three small wayside shrines by a cluster of two or more temples (a lone precinct among houses needs none), the clan mausoleum inside the walls, and the cremation ground with the pauper ossuary outside them, an extramural funerary complex beyond a gate.
