Salt heaps at doorways (morijio)

A small heap of salt set at a doorway for good fortune, morijio, is recorded in the late Edo period at the doors of the customer trades - restaurants, assignation houses and variety halls. Its shape as a cone, and a heap on each side of a door, are known only from present-day custom.

  • The earliest recorded use is in a popular novel of about 1838-40, late in the Edo period.
    The standard historical dictionary of Japanese places the heap at the doorways of restaurants, assignation houses and variety halls, and cites it first from a ninjōbon, a popular romance, of about 1838-40.
  • Was the heap a cone, and were there two at a door?
    Today the salt is heaped into a cone or a three-sided pyramid, at the entrance or inside the house, a shape the Japanese Wikipedia article describes from present-day practice without a date, in an article that flags itself as short of sources. At an entrance today it commonly stands outside, one heap on each side, though that is a seller of ritual goods describing present-day custom, not a record of the Edo period. No page we read gives the heap's shape, or a pair at a door, before modern times.
  • Was salt set at the gates of an official's compound?
    No page we read puts morijio at the gate of a samurai residence, a magistrate's office or any government building. The one dated record ties it to trades that wanted customers at the door, which an official's compound does not.
