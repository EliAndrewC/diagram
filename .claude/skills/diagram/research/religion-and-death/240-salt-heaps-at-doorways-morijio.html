Salt heaps at doorways (morijio)

A small heap of salt set at a doorway for good fortune, morijio, is recorded in the late Edo period at the doors of the customer trades - restaurants, assignation houses and variety halls. No page we read gives it the shape of a cone, or a heap on each side of a door, before present-day custom.

  • The standard historical dictionary of Japanese first records the salt heap in a popular novel of about 1838-40, late in the Edo period.
    The dictionary places the heap at the doorways of restaurants, assignation houses and variety halls, and cites it first from a ninjōbon, a popular romance, of about 1838-40.
  • Was the salt heaped into a cone in the Edo period, as it is today?
    Today the salt is heaped into a cone or a three-sided pyramid, at the entrance or inside the house. The Japanese Wikipedia article describes that shape from present-day practice, gives it no date, and flags itself as short of sources. No page we read gives the heap's shape before modern times.
  • Were two heaps set at a door, one on each side, in the Edo period?
    At an entrance today the salt commonly stands outside, one heap on each side, but that is a seller of ritual goods describing present-day custom, not a record of the Edo period. No page we read gives a pair of heaps at a door before modern times.
  • Was salt also heaped at the gates of samurai houses and government offices?
    No page we read puts morijio at the gate of a samurai residence, a magistrate's office or any government building. The one dated record ties it to trades that wanted customers at the door, which a samurai house or a government office did not.
