How much ground does a grave take, and how large is a burial ground drawn?

Sources: tanigawa-1992 (excavated grave sizes), feng-yizhong-jiangnan (mu and coffin counts for Qing charity graveyards), kimura-1934-bochi-menseki (a household's family plot, and the ground a cemetery needs beyond its plots)

Why a funerary feature is drawn to its real size is at How large are the gates, walls and funerary features drawn?; how little cremation leaves to bury is at How large is a bone mound drawn?

Burial grounds size to the population served at about 10 to 20 sq ft per packed urn grave, with about one generation of active plots before reuse. The excavated graves the paper measures are of that order - jar burials in wooden chambers 85 cm to 1.4 m along the major axis, jar burials and wooden coffins of another category 65 cm to 2.3 m, most under 1.4 m - and a Chinese full-body grave, which this project reckons at four to six times an urn grave's ground (40 to 120 sq ft), is measured: a Kangxi endowment of 4 mu of Jiangnan field took 573 coffins, about 4.7 m² - some 50 sq ft - a grave, inside that reckoning, in a region where one county's charity graveyards (Baoshan's, in a Guangxu-era count) ranged from 3 fen to 10 mu. That one is a ground for the drowned, packed - by this project's comparison - more tightly than a family grave sited by fengshui. This setting follows the Japanese cremate-then-inter mode.

Turning plots into a ground. One source read does it, for exactly that mode. A landscape architect sizing the common cemeteries of Japan's cities in 1934, from Tokyo's figures, quotes a North American rule - the area of a plot, times the burials a year, times the years before a grave is reused, times a factor for the paths and buildings - and replaces it, for cities that cremate and inter a household's urns together, with one family plot for each registered household, times the same factor. He advises a plot of 1 tsubo, about 36 sq ft, and a factor of 1.5 to 2. A 1-tsubo plot easily holds 20 to 30 urns. The plots actually in use ran from 0.5 tsubo a user in one Tokyo cemetery to 2.61 in another, 2.075 on average; the factor measured in the older five averaged 1.78. By that reckoning, a village district of about 800 inhabitants at five to a household (How many inhabitants does a map's house count stand for?) is some 160 households, whose plots and paths come to about 240 to 320 tsubo - roughly 0.2 to 0.26 acre, inside the village band. The whole ladder (a village district 0.15 to 0.30 acre, a town 0.25 to 0.75, a city 0.75 to 2 split across its yards) is this project's own arithmetic, from the plot figure and the population served; the 1934 reckoning is a check on it drawn from modern city cemeteries, and no source read gives a premodern Japanese burial ground an area against the population it served.

The rule the map follows: burial grounds are sized to the population they serve, and the ladder stays monotone: a village district ground below a town's, a town's below a city's.
