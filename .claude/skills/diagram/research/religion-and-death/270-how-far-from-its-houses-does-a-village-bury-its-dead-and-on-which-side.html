How far from its houses does a village bury its dead, and on which side?

Sources: kawazoe-2010-ryobosei (measured distances of the burial ground and the visiting graves from the houses, and the side they lie on), kofukuroman-sanmai (the burial place at the hamlet's edge), meiji-1884-bochi-saimoku (the 1884 national minimum distance from houses), kotobank-ryobosei (the two-grave custom's date, and where each grave lay), ryobosei-jawiki (the two graves often close together), yoro-sosoryo-sol (the ancient code's ban on burial near the capital and beside roads)

Two grounds at two distances. In the villages that kept the two-grave custom, the body went to a burial ground and the grave stone stood elsewhere, so a survey of 22 such settlements could measure each. The visiting graves lay on average about 130 m, some 430 ft, from the middle of the houses, and 18 of the 22 within 200 m, about 650 ft. The burial grounds lay on average about 370 m, some 1,200 ft, away, and in some villages about 900 m, over half a mile. The surveyor reads the burial ground's distance as a compromise: the village wanted its dead as far off as it could, and stopped where carrying them became too hard. A folklore record from Fukui gives the same picture in words, the burial place at the hamlet's edge and the visiting graves in the temples inside it. Under a national rule of 1884, a new graveyard had to stand about 60 ken, some 360 ft, or more from houses.

The side: downstream, never upstream. The same survey finds the burial ground never above the houses on the village's watercourse: below them, or, in some fishing villages, across the water from them. Its model puts the shrine at the upper end of that line and the visiting graves with the temple. What the survey cannot say. Its villages are two-grave ones, in three regions - the Shiwaku islands of the Inland Sea, Shima and Wakasa - and many of them small fishing villages on the coast; it measures them as they stand today, choosing villages that still keep the shape they had when their two graves were made, untouched by large postwar development, while granting that when the custom formed cannot be clearly dated; and it gives averages, not a village-by-village table. Whether a village that buried in one ground kept it at the burial ground's distance or the visiting grave's is in no page read.

Before modern times: the form, not the distance. The two-grave custom is old: the Heibonsha encyclopedia holds that it arose in the early Edo period, being closely tied to the spread of grave stones, and sets the visiting grave in the grounds of the temples and halls within the village; Mypedia puts the burial grave away from the houses and the visiting grave inside a house plot or a temple's grounds. Across Japan the two are often quite close - side by side across a single road, or on one piece of ground. None of these gives a distance. The one older rule found names none either: the ancient code forbade burial near the capital and beside roads. So the visited ground within the village, beside its hall, is premodern; the 650 ft is a measure of villages as they stand today, and the 60 ken of 1884 is a Meiji rule for new graveyards.

The decision. The setting cremates and then inters, so the ground a village visits and the ground its dead lie in are the same ground, and it takes the visiting grave's place: within the village, with the hall that tends it - in this setting the village's shrine, where the history has a temple. The two-grave body ground has no counterpart in a cremating village. Where a village's ground stands apart from its hall (see Does a village bury in its temple's yard, in a ground of its own, or in its fields?), it lies at the burial ground's side of the village: downstream of the houses, never upstream. The surveyed 650 ft is a modern measure with no premodern figure behind it, so under the GM's ruling of 2026-09-28 that the maps keep nothing only modern, the maps do not draw a set distance.

The rule the map follows: a village's burial ground lies within the village, beside the hall that tends it or at the edge of the houses, and never upstream of them on the village's stream. A ground that stands apart from the shrine lies downstream of the houses, just beyond the last of them. No set distance from the houses is drawn. The ground within the village is attested before modern times; the downstream side is attested in today's villages only.
