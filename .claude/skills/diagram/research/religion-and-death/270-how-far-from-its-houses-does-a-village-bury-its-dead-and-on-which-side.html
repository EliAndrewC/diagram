How far from its houses does a village bury its dead, and on which side?

Sources: kawazoe-2010-ryobosei (measured distances of the burial ground and the visiting graves from the houses, and the side they lie on), kofukuroman-sanmai (the burial place at the hamlet's edge), meiji-1884-bochi-saimoku (the first written minimum distance from houses)

Two grounds at two distances. In the villages that kept the two-grave custom, the body went to a burial ground and the grave stone stood elsewhere, usually by the temple, so a survey of 22 such settlements could measure each. The visiting graves lay on average about 130 m, some 430 ft, from the middle of the houses, and 18 of the 22 within 200 m, about 650 ft. The burial grounds lay on average about 370 m, some 1,200 ft, away, and in some villages about 900 m, over half a mile. The surveyor reads the burial ground's distance as a compromise: the village wanted its dead as far off as it could, and stopped where carrying them became too hard. A folklore record from Fukui gives the same picture in words, the burial place at the hamlet's edge and the visiting graves in the temples inside it. When the state first wrote a rule, in 1884, a new graveyard had to stand about 60 ken, some 360 ft, or more from houses.

The side: downstream, never upstream. The same survey finds the burial ground set along the village's watercourse, below the houses, never above them. Its model puts the shrine at the upper end of that line and the visiting graves with the temple. What the survey cannot say. Its villages are two-grave ones, a custom concentrated in and around the Kinki region, and many of them fishing villages; it measures them as they stand today; and it gives averages read from its graphs. Whether a village that buried in one ground kept it at the burial ground's distance or the visiting grave's is in no page read.

The decision. The setting cremates and then inters, so the ground a village visits and the ground its dead lie in are the same ground, and it takes the visiting grave's place: close to the houses, with the hall that tends it. The two-grave body ground has no counterpart in a cremating village. Where a village's ground stands apart from its hall (see Does a village bury in its temple's yard, in a ground of its own, or in its fields?), it lies at the burial ground's side of the village: downstream of the houses, never upstream.

The rule the map follows: a village's burial ground lies within about 650 ft of the middle of its houses , and never upstream of them on the village's stream. A ground that stands apart from the shrine lies downstream of the houses, beyond the last of them. The 650 ft is the surveyed reach of the visiting graves, carried over to an urn ground by this project's reasoning; the downstream side is attested.
