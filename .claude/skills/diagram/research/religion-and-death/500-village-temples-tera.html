Village temples (tera)

A village temple was a small Buddhist temple of a village's own, with which its households were registered as parishioners. When, in the first half of the seventeenth century, early in the Edo period, the Tokugawa state made every household register with a Buddhist temple, not every village had one, and many villages' houses were registered with temples outside them. Some villages had a temple of their own, and some had several. A village's own temple was small. Most temples of any kind were small ones of only a main hall and a bell tower, and a village temple's hall was about 24 to 36 ft a side, for a parish of a few dozen to a couple of hundred households, with a small house for its monk.

The setting our maps are drawn for has no separate village temple. Each village district, a village and the hamlets round it, has one country monk, who serves in the village's shrine and keeps the district's records of births and deaths. So the village's shrine does the work of its temple, and the district's hamlets are served from it, much as a village with no temple of its own was served by a neighbor's. How that shrine and its monk were kept in history is at Keeping a village shrine or temple: its monk, the monk's dwelling (kuri), its records and its land.

  • Not every village had a temple when every household had to register with one.
    When the state demanded the certificates of registration, not all of Japan's villages, close to 65,000 of them, had a temple that could take parishioners.
  • Were a village's houses most often registered at several temples outside it?
    Many of the new parishioners chose a temple by nearness rather than by doctrine. A volume in a series on Buddhist folklore, looking at temple ties across the country, found that a village's houses were not all registered at one temple inside it, and judged the form in which several temples outside the village divided its houses between them safe to call the most common, especially early in the Edo period.
  • Some village temples were made from a hall the village already had.
    The Buddhist sects raised halls dedicated to a local deity, or to Amida, Shakyamuni or Fudo, to the status of temples - halls that religionists from the village had served season by season. In the Shin sect, the meeting hall of the faithful became a temple as the farming households round it became its parishioners.
  • In one Fukuoka village a small chapel became the temple of the whole village.
    Jōrakuji, founded in 1596 when it was a hall-hermitage, took the whole village as its parishioners in 1688, and the next year a villager helped pay for its founding as a temple.
  • Some villages kept several temples.
    Medieval Sugaura, a lakeshore settlement in Shiga, had only about 70 households in the fifteenth century, but three temples and twelve hermitages and cloisters. Takenami, a hamlet of Wakasa, buried its dead at its edge and kept the graves its families visited in the two temples inside it. A temple register of one Saitama district lists nineteen temples in fourteen old villages, two apiece in five of them (Town monasteries).
  • No page we read counts the villages that kept no temple, one, or several.
    Nor does any say whether the shares differed by region; the one district register we read, in Saitama, covers only fourteen villages.
  • How many households did a village temple serve?
    The village temples of one Fukuoka town counted between 24 and 216 parish households each - 35, 41 and 88 at three of them. That this is of the order of one village's households is this project's reading: no page we read gives the households of these villages themselves.

What stood in a village temple's grounds is known from a handful of temples, most of them in that one Fukuoka farming town, whose gazetteers measured their halls.

  • Most temples were small ones of a main hall and a bell tower.
    Only the large temples where many monks lived had the full arrangement of seven halls; small temples of only a main hall and a bell tower were by far the most common.
  • How big was a village temple's main hall?
    About 24 to 36 ft a side. The main halls of the Fukuoka town's five village temples ran from 4 to 6 ken across and 4 to 6 deep, at a ken of about 6 ft.
  • The smallest temple the Fukuoka town's gazetteers measured had a hall of about 27 by 18 ft and a kuri of about 12 by 15 ft.
    It was a Kōshin hall in the same Fukuoka town that had once belonged to the shugen order. Its hall was 4.5 ken wide and 3 deep, and its kuri, the monk's dwelling, 2 ken wide and 2.5 deep.
  • How big was a village temple's precinct?
    The precincts of nineteen village temples in one Saitama district ran from 285 to 1,944 tsubo, about 10,000 to 69,000 sq ft (Town monasteries). The Fukuoka town's Kōshin hall stood on a site of 185 tsubo, about 6,600 sq ft. The town's gazetteer also gives each temple's land, but in two cases that figure is smaller than the temple's own main hall, so it is not the precinct.
  • A village temple might keep lesser halls in its grounds.
    One of the Fukuoka town's temples had a Yakushi hall and a Kannon hall in its grounds.
  • Some village temples had their graveyard inside the precinct.
    The great flood of 1891 scoured out the graveyard inside the precinct of another of the town's temples. Whether a village buried in its temple's yard or in a ground of its own is a choice history makes both ways (Does a village bury in its temple's yard, in a ground of its own, or in its fields?).
  • Where did a village temple stand in relation to its houses?
    One of the Fukuoka temples is described as facing east and standing at the edge of the houses, on slightly higher ground. No page we read gives a general rule for where a village temple stood.
