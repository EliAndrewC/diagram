Torii spacing - two regimes and nothing in between

Sources: yutoku-inari-sagatv (a donation row almost post to post), fushimi-inari-enwiki (Fushimi's gates on the main path), manzo-inari-note (a small rural row, counted and walked), nara-nagao-jinja (about 300 m between Nagao Shrine's first and second gates), meiji-jingu-access (the ~10-minute walk from each entrance to the main hall) and meiji-jingu-jawiki (the ranked set among Meiji Jingu's eight), ndl-crd-senbon-torii, kato-kawataka-torii and kudamatsu-stone-torii (the row's Edo dating, and single early stone arches), sanuki-kokubunji-jawiki and shimotsuke-kokubunji-jawiki (a great precinct's size)

What the research found. The spacing of real arches that could be read falls into two regimes. (1) Donation rows stand almost post to post: at Yutoku Inari in Saga the arches of the row "appear to be lined up at intervals of almost 10 centimeters", and Fushimi Inari has around 800 on its main path. Yutoku's own figures give the whole path's average: 223 arches on the 300 m climb to the inner shrine, about 1.3 m apiece - this project's arithmetic on the page's figures. The one small rural row that can be estimated at all is Manzo Inari's, a remote mountain shrine with no house in sight, where a visitor counted 114 arches on the way back along a walk of about seven minutes up a mountain path from the entrance to where the view opens at the shrine, the arches continuing along the way; no page gives the path's length, and if the row runs the whole walk, an ordinary walking pace gives a mean of about 3 to 4.4 m, some 10 to 14 ft, between arches - this project's arithmetic on a walking time, not a measurement, and a remote mountain shrine's, not a village's. (2) Ranked gates (ichi-no-torii, ni-no-torii, san-no-torii) are landmarks strung along a whole approach: Nagao Shrine's first to second is about 300 m, and Meiji Jingu's three span a ~10-minute walk from the entrance to the main hall - "three" there is the ranked set among its eight arches - while Kasuga Taisha's first gate is said to stand 1.3 km from the shrine, which no page read states. A ranked pair is off the map at every settlement scale. Nothing read attests a row at 30 ft or more.

When. Fushimi's rows "began to be erected from the Edo into the Meiji period, through worshipers' donations", and a single stone arch at an ordinary shrine is thought to date from as early as the late Muromachi or early Edo. One city's inventory counts 64 stone arches and lists its dated ones up to the Kansei era (1789-1801) year by year from 1679, the earliest, of 1679, raised by the parishioners at the solicitation of the priest of the Myōken shrine-temple whose approach it marked, and several later ones a single donor's gift, each arch listed on its own. No page shows a village shrine with a row of arches before 1868; that absence is recorded, not proved. A great precinct, by contrast, has ground to spare for a long approach: Sanuki's Nara-period provincial temple, its kokubunji, stood on 240 by 220 m, and Shimotsuke's ran 457 by 413 m - two Buddhist temples, whose sizes show the scale of a great precinct, not where any arch of theirs stood.

The decision (the GM's ruling of 2026-09-27, choosing "All maps, ~10-13 ft"): every avenue of arches along a shrine's approach, at every scale and in every tier, the village's included, is laid at one pitch, the distance from arch to arch, of 12 ft, two ken, the middle of the ruled band - a guess inside the GM's ruling, bounded by Manzo's estimated row. The band's floor, 10 ft, was declined because at three feet per pixel it would set arches barely three pixels apart, too close for the drawn arches to read as separate; its top, 13 ft, had nothing to prefer it. The count of a shrine's arches is its patronage, recorded above, and the seven at Hoshigaoka are the GM's particular.

A donation row is a designated-site special case (the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25). The Fushimi-style row - the 800 or so arches on its main path, donated by worshipers from the Edo period into Meiji, both quoted above - is not how an ordinary approach in this setting works. It belongs to a handful of named great sites: Shinden Togashi, the Temple of Amaterasu, the Ki Rin Shrine and others of that rank. A map of one of those sites is drawn as an explicit outlier, exempt from the pitch; a real donation row is packed tighter than an arch can be drawn apart at these scales, and that limit is the drawing's, not a finding about how gates were spaced.
