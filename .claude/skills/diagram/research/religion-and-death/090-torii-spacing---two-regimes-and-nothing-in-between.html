Torii spacing - two regimes and nothing in between

Sources: yutoku-inari-sagatv (a donation row almost post to post), fushimi-inari-enwiki (Fushimi's gates on the main path), manzo-inari-note (a small rural row, counted and walked), nara-nagao-jinja (about 300 m between Nagao Shrine's first and second gates), meiji-jingu-access (the ~10-minute walk from each entrance to the main hall) and meiji-jingu-jawiki (the ranked set among Meiji Jingu's eight), torii-enwiki, sando-enwiki and kokugakuin-eos-torii (the first arch where the approach enters the grounds), ndl-crd-senbon-torii and kato-kawataka-torii (the row's Edo dating, and a single early stone arch), sanuki-kokubunji-jawiki and shimotsuke-kokubunji-jawiki (a great precinct's size)

What the research found. The spacing of real arches that could be read falls into two regimes. (1) Donation rows stand almost post to post: at Yutoku Inari in Saga the arches of the row "appear to be lined up at intervals of almost 10 centimeters", and Fushimi Inari has around 800 on its main path. Yutoku's own figures give the whole path's average: 223 arches on the 300 m climb to the inner shrine, about 1.3 m apiece - this project's arithmetic on the page's figures. The one small rural row that can be estimated at all is Manzo Inari's in Miyagi, a remote mountain shrine with no house in sight, where a visitor counted 114 arches on the way back along a walk of about seven minutes up a mountain path from the entrance to where the view opens at the shrine, the arches continuing along the way; no page gives the path's length, and if the row runs the whole walk, an ordinary walking pace gives a mean of about 3 to 4 m, some 10 to 13 ft, between arches - this project's arithmetic on a walking time, not a measurement, and a remote mountain shrine's, not a village's. (2) Ranked gates (ichi-no-torii, ni-no-torii, san-no-torii) are landmarks strung along a whole approach: Nagao Shrine's first to second is about 300 m, and Meiji Jingu's three span a ~10-minute walk from the entrance to the main hall - "three" there is the ranked set among its eight arches - while Kasuga Taisha's first gate is said to stand 1.3 km from the shrine, which no page read states. A ranked pair is off the map at every settlement scale. Nothing read attests a row at 30 ft or more.

Where the first arch stands. The approach "is almost always straddled by one or more torii", "its point of origin" being "usually straddled" by one, and the first, the largest, "stands at the approachway to the grounds of the shrine", further arches standing within the precinct. The first arch is therefore where the approach enters the shrine's ground, at the edge of its wood (under "How large was a village shrine's precinct, and how much of it was built on?").

When. Fushimi's rows "began to be erected from the Edo into the Meiji period, through worshippers' donations", and a single stone arch at an ordinary shrine is as old as the late Muromachi or early Edo. No page shows a village shrine with a row of arches before 1868; that absence is recorded, not proved. Nor does any page give a village approach's length, or the distance from its innermost arch to the hall. A great precinct, by contrast, has ground to spare between its outermost arch and its hall: Sanuki's Nara-period provincial temple stood on 240 by 220 m, and that is the small end - Shimotsuke's ran 457 by 413 m.

The decision (the GM's ruling of 2026-09-27, choosing "All maps, ~10-13 ft"): every avenue, at every scale and in every tier, the village's included, is laid at one pitch of 12 ft, two ken, the middle of the ruled band - a guess inside the GM's ruling, bounded by Manzo's estimated row. The band's floor, 10 ft, was declined because at three feet per pixel it would set arches barely three pixels apart, too close for the drawn arches to read as separate; its top, 13 ft, had nothing to prefer it. The count of a shrine's arches is its patronage, recorded above, and the seven at Hoshigaoka are the GM's particular.

A donation row is a designated-site special case (the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25). The Fushimi-style row - the 800 or so arches on its main path, donated by worshipers from the Edo period into Meiji, both quoted above - is not how an ordinary approach in this setting works. It belongs to a handful of named great sites: Shinden Togashi, the Temple of Amaterasu, the Ki Rin Shrine and others of that rank. A map of one of those sites is drawn as an explicit outlier, exempt from the pitch; a real donation row is packed tighter than an arch can be drawn apart at these scales, and that limit is the drawing's, not a finding about how gates were spaced.

And the avenue STARTS at its hall (the GM's ruling of 2026-07-27): "the distance from the front of the temple should be the same as the distance between each torii arch." An avenue can be perfectly spaced and still stand yards away from the temple it serves - three arches up a street on the far side of a flophouse, or with the caption and two rows of houses between it and its hall - and then it does not read as that temple's approach; it reads as red marks near some other building. So the threshold is set alongside the count and the stride: the whole run is moved bodily, keeping its direction, its curve and its stride and changing only its distance, until the innermost arch stands one pitch off the nearest FACE of the hall (measured to the footprint rather than to the center, so a run drawn off to one side slides onto the flank it stands off rather than diagonally into the middle of the building). A village's run is laid through the same pitch and seated by the same rule.

And a caption never stands in the approach (the same conversation: an arch must "never be covered by the 'temple of X' label"). A hall's own caption and its approach both want the ground at the hall's face, which is where the collision came from. The caption tries three baselines in a STRICT ORDER - the side the map's author asked for, that same side pushed past the far end of the avenue, then the opposite side - and takes the first that touches no arch. Stepping PAST the avenue has to exist because a parish graveyard may sit deliberately at the back of a monastery, so flipping the caption off the approach would only bury it in the cemetery.
