How many temples a city keeps, and its temple quarter (teramachi)

A Japanese Buddhist temple was a foundation of its own, kept by the households that were its patrons, so a city had many temples rather than one or two. The castle towns of the Edo period gathered theirs into a temple quarter (teramachi), commonly at the town's outer edge, where it is usually read as part of the town's defenses, and a quarter held from over ten temples and shrines, as at Takayama, to about seventy temples, as at Kanazawa. Kanazawa's held town houses and shrines among its temples.

A Chinese county seat kept, besides its Buddhist and Daoist houses, two state foundations of its own: a Confucian temple at its school and a temple to its City God.

  • Did a Japanese city's temple count follow its patrons rather than its population?
    Each temple was a separate foundation, supported by its own patron households (danka), which it served in return. So, on this project's reasoning, a city's temple count tracks how many distinct patrons it had gathered over its history, not how many inhabitants it had.
  • How many temples did a castle town gather into its temple quarter?
    From about ten to about seventy. Kanazawa's temple quarter holds about 70 temples. On a map of 1689 the port city of Sakai had some 60 temples in its northern ward and 50 in its southern, and Akita's plan of 1663 lays out 43. About 25 temples moved to Takada in one wave in 1614. The lowest count found is Takayama's, a castle town whose size no page we read gives: its temple quarter has over ten temples and shrines.
  • Many castle towns set their temple quarter at the town's edge.
    Many castle towns gathered their temples and graveyards at the outer edge of the town, on the castle town's outer ring. One town after another shows it: Fukuchiyama gathered its temples as the town's northern guard, Shibata set its temples outside the river and moat on its southeast edge, at the town's entrance, and Yonago gave a stretch of sand hills northwest of the castle to temples as the town's line of defense. At Takada an area west of the castle was set aside for them.
  • The usual reading is that a temple quarter was placed for defense.
    Accounts of the castle town say the quarter's spacious precincts formed part of the town's defenses. A Japanese reference work's entry for Tokushima says a temple quarter in a castle town is generally held to have been for massing troops in wartime and for defense, and its entry for Matsue calls that town's long line of temples a defensive position against an enemy from the east or south, or a muster ground for the army. An article on Wakayama's temple quarter gives the concrete argument: a temple's tile-roofed buildings stood inside stone or earthen walls, so in an emergency each became a fort, and a temple could build or mend its stone walls without the shogunate's leave.
  • Was defense really what the temple quarter was for?
    The reading is contested. Miyazu's city history notes that some temple quarters hold the lord's own family temple, or lie beside a pleasure quarter or a port, so their function varied by region and over time. A scholar of Takada's temple quarter grants that the defensive reading is nearly the settled view, reports a recent objection that it is likely a product of the long peace under the Tokugawa, and judges that Takada's plan, nearly undefended as seen from the highway, did little for defense in practice. At Takada the "protection" the quarter gave the castle is described as spiritual and moral support, with room to house the domain's foot soldiers.
  • This project guesses that the temples' graveyards and groves also served as a firebreak.
    It is a GUESS: none of the pages on the temple quarters speaks of fire.
  • A castle town's temples stood in one group, in a line, or in several groups.
    Fukuchiyama's temples stood in one group on the north, and Miyazu's twelve in one in the southwest. Matsue's ran in a long line from south to north. Kanazawa's lords gathered theirs in three areas. No page we read shows temples ringing the whole of a town's edge.
  • A street named for temples might hold hardly any.
    Hikone's street called Teramachi was lined with samurai houses on two of the town's Edo-period maps, with only three temples to its north.
  • A lord gathered the temples of a quarter and sorted them by sect.
    At Hirosaki the lord gathered the temples in 1610, for the castle town's defense and the control of its population: a street of Soto temples round his own family's Soto temple and, after the original quarter burned in 1649, a new street for the other sects. Thirty-three Soto temples line the first street's avenue. At Kanazawa most temples stand on ground the lord granted them, chiefly Nichiren and Hokke temples along one of the quarter's two old roads and chiefly Soto and Rinzai along the other, which the city's plan reads as the domain's deliberate placement.
  • A temple quarter held town houses as well as temples.
    Kanazawa's Teramachidai district, one of the town's three temple areas, stretching about 1,220 m (~4,003 ft) north to south and 1,000 m (~3,281 ft) east to west but only 22 ha (~54 acres) in area, holds 52 temples, 2 shrines and about 320 town houses. Hirosaki's second temple street, laid out in 1650, had 45 town houses and one samurai house beside its temples in 1678, and early in the Meiji era 14 of its 234 houses were temples.
  • Along Kanazawa's wide straight street, the temples stood in a walled row.
    Along Kanazawa's wide, straight old Noda road, temples with large precincts stand in an orderly unbroken row, most with an earthen wall and a gate on the street, and the main hall, the priest's quarters (kuri) and the bell tower are seen over the wall.
  • Along Kanazawa's narrower street, town houses fronted the temples.
    Along Kanazawa's narrower, gently curving old Tsurugi road, the lots of the old gate lands survive with town houses lining the street, so the temples are hard to see from it and have few walls; their gates stand back from the street, and the main hall, the kuri and the bell tower stand at the end of an approach. The town houses' lots are narrow and deep, and face each other across the street.
  • In Kanazawa's quarter, most main halls faced the street.
    In Kanazawa's quarter most main halls face the street, and where the kuri stands depends on how big the precinct is, with no one pattern.
  • In Kanazawa's quarter, the hip-roofed main halls were all large, 8 ken (~48 ft) or more across.
    A little under a fifth of the quarter's main halls are hip-roofed, and the plan finds every one of them large.
  • In Kanazawa's quarter, a temple on sloping ground terraced its garden.
    Where the ground falls away from the reception rooms, a garden is built up with stone walling to the building's level and set off from the graveyard or the neighbor's lot behind.
  • How much ground did a temple in a temple quarter take?
    No page we read gives a single ordinary temple's frontage and depth. An ordinary Edo temple's precinct was about 1,000 tsubo, some 3,300 m² (~35,600 sq ft) (What does a temple precinct hold?). The Edo-period blocks that survive in Tokyo's Taito ward, all in its Yanaka and Negishi-Shitaya districts, average about 7,300 to 9,000 m² (~79,000-97,000 sq ft), against about 2,800 m² (~30,000 sq ft) for the ward's blocks as a whole, room for two or three such precincts to a block. Kanazawa's 22 ha (~54 acres) come to about 4,100 m² (~44,000 sq ft) for each of its 54 temples and shrines, streets and town houses included. Both are this project's arithmetic; the first takes those blocks to be temple blocks, which the paper does not say of each.
  • Great houses kept family temples of their own.
    A family temple (bodaiji) is one whose sect a household has followed for generations, and at which it holds the memorial services for its ancestors; the Tokugawa's were Kan'eiji and Zojoji. Clan temples (ujidera), built by a particular clan for the repose of its house, go back to the Asuka period, nine centuries before the castle towns.
  • Did a lord's retainers keep their family temples in the temple quarter?
    Perhaps, but no page we read says so: the pages give the lord's patronage of a quarter, or the lord's own family temple, and none the retainers'.
  • A temple's approach ran from the busy road to its gate.
    In its broad sense an approach (sando) is the whole way to a shrine or temple from a place of heavy traffic such as a highway. Gate-front towns (monzen) grew up along the approaches of influential temples, and a highway was not seldom bent toward one for the pilgrims' sake.
  • A neighborhood might hold several small shrines of the fire god Akiba.
    A small shrine (hokora) is a simplified shrine, put up on land a settlement or an individual owned. The shrines of the fire-quelling Akiba cult are often small, several of them may stand within a single neighborhood, and some are built of stone, because stone does not burn easily.
  • Did small shrines stand in the ground between a quarter's temples?
    Kanazawa's temple quarter holds two shrines among its 52 temples, but no page we read says what stood in the ground between them. A town's shrines as a whole are at Shrines in towns and cities.

A Chinese walled city's temples are recorded differently.

  • Every Chinese county seat kept a Confucian temple and a City God temple.
    In 630 the Tang dynasty decreed a Confucian temple for the school of every province and county. The Ming founder gave the City Gods titles at four levels, from the capital down to the county, and under the Qing every seat of local government had its City God temple. These were state foundations, counted here apart from the Buddhist and Daoist houses. Where they stood in a seat and how big they were is at Does a county seat or a city carry the state cult's altars and temples?
  • A City God temple could be several temples on one site.
    Pingyao's City God temple complex holds the temple of the City God, a temple of the god of wealth (Caishen) and a temple of the kitchen god (Zaojun), together over 7,300 m² (~78,600 sq ft).
  • How many temples did a Chinese city keep?
    For a county seat, no page we read gives a count. For one great city there is one: the Song-dynasty gazetteer of Suzhou files the shrines of the cults under a heading of their own, the Daoist abbeys and then the Buddhist temples of the prefectural city under theirs, and the temples outside the wall under a third. It has thirty entries for Buddhist houses inside the city, and the sites of some were no longer known. The count is this project's, made on the page, and it is a prefectural city's, not a county seat's. The range of temples a city keeps therefore rests on the Japanese temple quarters.
