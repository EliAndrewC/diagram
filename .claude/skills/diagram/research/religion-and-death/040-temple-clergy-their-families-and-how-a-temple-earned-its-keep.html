Temple clergy, their families, and how a temple earned its keep

A large temple was an economic house as well as a religious one, and what it owned decided the trades it carried on. In China, Buddhist monasteries were the largest moneylenders, most of them lending as pawnbrokers, and monastic estates carried oil mills, spinneries and breweries as well as fields.

Who lived at a temple followed its rule of ordination. Monks lived together inside the walls. Where the office passed from parent to child, the clergy married and kept households of their own: the priest families of Japan's shrines, near the shrine they served, most priests of China's Zhengyi Daoist tradition, married and living at home and, today at least, in families of hereditary priests, and, in the Edo period, the head priests of Japan's Shin sect.

Chinese monasteries earned much of their keep by lending and from their estates.

  • Chinese Buddhist monasteries lent money, mostly as pawnbrokers, until the nineteenth century.
    They were the largest lending institutions, they generally lent by playing the pawnbroker, and they made loans from the time Buddhism arrived in China until the nineteenth century.
  • The earliest Chinese pawnbrokers, in the fifth century, were Buddhist monasteries.
    The monasteries founded, owned and ran them, and only later were pawnshops more widely seen.
  • Under the Southern Song dynasty, lay investors opened pawnshops in monasteries to avoid property tax.
    Monasteries were exempt from it, and wealthy lay people went into partnership with them. A document of 1202 records ten people forming a ju, a partnership of investors, to support the founding of a pawnshop in a monastery.
  • Temples lent out donated wealth from "inexhaustible treasuries".
    The inexhaustible treasury began as a facility inside the temples of one sect, the Three Stages sect, and the scheme spread to other Buddhist sects, lending the wealth gathered from donations to the public for profit.
  • The chief inexhaustible treasury, at Chang'an, was destroyed by imperial order in 713.
    The treasury of the Huadu temple in Chang'an was destroyed by order of the Tang emperor Xuanzong, for a reason no page we read gives.
  • Monastic estates carried workshops as well as fields.
    Under the Tang dynasty, manors owned by the imperial family, by high officials and by monasteries grew grain, fruit, mulberry and tea, raised animals, and carried oil mills, spinneries and breweries, worked by slaves, craftsmen and tenant farmers. Many of the great estate owners were Buddhist monasteries.
  • The monasteries' wealth drew the state's persecutions of Buddhism.
    Under the Tang, the great monastic estates were among the reasons the state turned on Buddhism.

Celibate monks lived in the monastery itself, though not always to the end.

  • A Zen temple's monks lived together in its monks' hall.
    The monks' hall of a Japanese Zen temple is the place where its monks carry on a communal life of practice.
  • Monks grown old on Mount Hiei retired to residences at the town at its foot.
    In the Edo period the Tendai head granted monks who had trained on the mountain into old age a retirement residence at Sakamoto, and many were built there.

In China the married, hereditary clergy were the priests of the Zhengyi Daoist tradition.

  • Most Zhengyi Daoist priests lived married, at home.
    The Quanzhen order of the twelfth century followed the Buddhist ideal of a celibate, monastic life, but most Zhengyi priests lived married at home, putting on vestments to perform their rites.
  • Under the Ming, the Qing and the Republic, the state kept pressing priests who lived at home to move into a temple or give up their status.
    Through the Ming, the Qing and the Republic of China (1912-49) the state kept questioning priests who lived at home, offering them a temple life or a return to lay status.
  • Today a Zhengyi priest is normally affiliated with a temple but does not live in it.
    A modern encyclopedia of Chinese culture says so, and that such priests come from families of hereditary priests and serve local communities.
  • A Zhengyi priest's office passed within his family; one master counted himself the twenty-second generation.
    Dated family registers show a lineage's fathers giving all their sons the next character of its generational poem, and knowledge handed down from father to son. A master interviewed on the Chinese mainland counted himself the twenty-second generation of hereditary masters. The office also went to adopted sons, sons-in-law and grandsons who were disciples.
  • How far do the modern accounts of Zhengyi priests describe the priesthood before modern times?
    In its shape more than its numbers. The three works cited above, a 2003 survey of Daoism, a 1988 study of priestly lineages and an encyclopedia of contemporary Chinese culture, describe clergy their authors met or read of in China and Taiwan in the twentieth century and after. Two reach further back: the state's objections to priests living at home run through the Ming and Qing, and a hereditary master counts himself the twenty-second generation.

Japan's hereditary clergy served its shrines, and one Buddhist sect.

  • Shinto priests could marry, and their children usually inherited the office.
    A priest's children usually took the position after him. Hereditary priesthoods were outlawed in 1871, under the new Meiji government, but many shrine family lines stayed intact and the succession continues in practice.
  • The chief priesthood of the Izumo shrine counts eighty-four generations.
    Its eighty-fourth kokuso, the eldest son of the eighty-third, succeeded his father in 2002.
  • In the Edo period the Shin sect was the one Buddhist sect whose temple heads could marry.
    Under the Tokugawa (1603-1867) the government exempted Jodo Shinshu alone, so that the head of a temple could marry and keep the status of priest.
  • A Shin priest's wife had a title of her own, bomori, the "guardian of the temple", by the fourteenth century.
    The word means the keeper of a temple lodging, and in the Shin sect the head priest's wife; a register dated 1343 names a meeting hall's head and his bomori. The sect still calls the resident priest's wife the bomori.
  • A Shin temple passes to its priest's child, usually the eldest son.
    A modern account of Japan's temple wives says the resident priest is expected to be succeeded by one of his children.
  • Did Shin priests' families live round the temple at Yoshizaki in the 1470s?
    Rennyo moved to Yoshizaki in 1471. A town grew up there, with lodging houses that quartered the priests stationed there and the believers who came to worship, and the ground round the temple was lined with priests' quarters and believers' lodgings. Rennyo's letters speak of "the wives of the priests of the lodging houses"; that those lodging houses held the priests' wives is this record's reading of the two sources together.
  • Shrine priest families lived in quarters of their own near the shrine.
    Priest families often built their houses near the shrine they served, and a place where their houses gathered was called a shake-machi, a priests' quarter. At Kamigamo in Kyoto the shrine's priests lived in such a quarter from the Muromachi period until the Meiji Restoration, clustered together with farmers. At Takabatake in Nara the Kasuga shrine's priests' quarter survived until the Meiji era, and paths led from it up to the shrine.
  • At Kamigamo in Kyoto, the shrine priests' houses looked unlike the town houses beside them.
    At Kamigamo the priest families' main houses were as a rule one-story, gabled and tiled, where the town houses were built a little higher to hold an upper room. Each stood inside an earthen wall with a gate at its entrance, and the quarter's preservation plan names the earthen bridges over the Myōjin river and the earthen walls along it among its features, and divides its old buildings into a priest-house style and a townhouse style.
  • No page we read compares a temple family's house with its neighbors'.
    Nothing we read says. The Kamigamo houses are a shrine's priests'; no page compares a Chinese married priest's house, or a Japanese temple family's, with its neighbors'.
  • Did a village temple's head priest live with his family in its kuri?
    A district register of temples made after 1868 lists a kuri with a frontage of six ken three shaku among a village temple's buildings, and the register's present-day editors gloss the kuri as the building in which the temple's head priest lives with his family. The gloss is modern usage.
  • No source we read counts the clergy homes round one temple.
    Nor does any give their distance from it.
