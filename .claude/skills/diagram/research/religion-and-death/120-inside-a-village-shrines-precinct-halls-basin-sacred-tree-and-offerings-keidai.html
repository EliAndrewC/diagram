Inside a village shrine's precinct: halls, basin, sacred tree and offerings (keidai)

A village shrine's precinct, its keidai, held two buildings on one line: at the back a tiny sanctuary, the honden, where its kami were kept, and in front of it on the same axis a larger worship hall, the haiden, where the villagers came to pray. The sanctuary was often a single bay wide - about 6 ft square at one rural shrine - and the worship halls and small temple halls of the Edo period that have been measured run from about 21 to 35 ft on a side.

Beyond its halls, the pages we read name a few fixtures and gifts. A sacred tree, roped with straw, stood in the precinct, and a stone basin for rinsing the hands and mouth by the approach; stone guardian dogs and lanterns were given by commoners and parishioners.

  • The commonest form of shrine sanctuary is the flowing-roof style, nagare-zukuri.
    The Japanese Wikipedia calls it the most common form of shrine sanctuary in the whole country. More than half of the sanctuaries designated national treasures or important cultural properties are in this style - a count of famous buildings, not of village shrines.
  • How wide was a sanctuary?
    Most often one bay or three. A front of one ken, a single span between two pillars, makes the one-bay form of the style, and the English Wikipedia gives 1 and 3 ken as its commonest sizes.
  • One rural shrine's Edo-period sanctuary, built in 1789, is about 6 ft square.
    The Hachiman shrine at Naose, in the Ehime highlands, built its sanctuary in 1789, and as a listed cultural property it has been measured: 1.98 m (~6 ft) along the ridge and 1.82 m (~6 ft) across the beams.
  • How big was a village shrine's worship hall?
    About 21 to 35 ft on a side, on the few Edo-period halls measured. The same Ehime shrine's worship hall, built in 1790, is 9.78 m (~32 ft) by 10.62 m (~35 ft), some five times its sanctuary's width. Two temple halls give the same scale: a thatched Kannon hall of 1778 in Saitama, three bays square, is 6.54 m (~21 ft) on each side, and a Nara temple's main hall of the early 17th century is 5 ken by 5 ken. No village hall three bays square of the Edo period was found measured.
  • The smallest Edo-period hall we found measured, a temple's side hall, is about 13 ft square.
    It stands part-way along a temple's approach, rebuilt in 1685, and measures 3.83 m (~13 ft) on each side - a subsidiary hall, not one where a village gathered.
  • The sanctuary stands at the rear, on the worship hall's axis.
    An online dictionary of Japanese art and architecture puts the sanctuary at the rear of the shrine complex, and the worship hall usually on the same axis as the offering hall and the sanctuary.
  • A small sanctuary was sometimes housed under a covering building.
    The covering house, the ōiya, is sometimes built over a small sanctuary to keep off wind and rain.

A Japanese shrine often kept more than one kami, and the pages we read describe three ways of housing them.

  • The Japanese Wikipedia says a shrine ordinarily kept several kami, one of them the main one.
    The article, which flags itself as short of sources, calls the principal one the main kami and the rest secondary kami.
  • Keeping several kami in one hall has its own name, aidono.
    A dictionary defines aidono as enshrining two or more kami together in the same shrine hall, and an encyclopedia adds that the secondary kami are in many cases honored below the main one.
  • Some halls gave each kami a bay, or a small hall, side by side.
    A hall built as two bays joined into one, with two doors, was a recognized form, the two-bay shrine. Another joined two or more Kasuga- or Nagare-style halls under a single roof.
  • A household's altar shelf, the kamidana, sets its talismans side by side in rank.
    On a three-shrine shelf the highest stands in the middle, the next to the right as you face it, the third to the left.
  • Did a hall ever keep one altar, with the other kami ranked behind it?
    A household's one-shrine altar shelf does: its talismans stand in one place, in rank from front to back. No page we read describes a shrine hall kept this way, so for a hall this form is a GUESS.
  • Could a kami brought in from elsewhere be given a small shrine of its own?
    Yes, says a stub article flagged as short of sources, which gives three kinds of joint enshrinement: in the main hall, in the old shrine moved into the precinct, or in one moved to ground outside it.
  • Which way of housing several kami was usual?
    No page we read says.

Where a country monk kept the shrine, the monk's dwelling stood with it; its size, and the one building known to put a hall and a dwelling under one roof, are at The country monk who keeps a village shrine, and their dwelling (kuri).

  • Did a village shrine kept by a country monk have a bell?
    No page we read says. At small temples a main hall and a belfry alone are by far the commonest arrangement, but whether a village shrine kept by a monk had its bell, or how many did, no page we read tells.

Two fixtures stood in the precinct.

  • A shrine's sacred tree is roped with a shimenawa.
    A sacred tree, the shinboku, is in general a tree in a shrine's precinct or the like, with a straw rope or the like strung around it, and revered. No page we read counts how many village shrines kept one.
  • Were most shrines built where a sacred tree already stood?
    So says the Japanese Wikipedia article on sacred trees, which carries the encyclopedia's own banner for lacking sources: almost all of Japan's shrines were first built where a tree or a wood already stood as a seat of the kami, so the tree came before the shrine.
  • A rinsing place, the temizuya, stands beside the approach.
    The Japanese Wikipedia's article on shrines puts the temizuya, for purifying the body, among the buildings beside the approach.
  • Is the roofed pavilion over the basin as old as the rite of rinsing?
    No: one writer on the temizuya finds the rite of rinsing the hands and mouth very old, and the temizuya as a roofed building much newer.
  • A parish gave its shrine a basin in 1828, before 1868.
    Nagao Shrine's basin, in Kawasaki, is inscribed as dedicated by the whole body of the shrine's parishioners in 1828, in the Edo period, before Japan began to modernize in 1868.
  • The one basin pavilion found carved with a date before 1868 stands at a shrine with a following across the Kantō, not a village's own.
    At Yakyu Inari Shrine, in Saitama, a pavilion carved with the date 1836 covers a basin of 1826, both given by a donor living in Edo, and the city's page reads the donor's home as a sign of how far through the Kantō the shrine's following reached.
  • Did a village shrine's basin stand in the open before 1868?
    No page we read says, either way. A shrine without a pavilion is described only today: a shrine writer observes that many shrines have no temizuya at all, unstaffed shrines being especially many in the provinces, and does not say whether such a shrine keeps a basin in the open.

Most of what else stood in a precinct was given to it, piece by piece.

  • Commoners gave stone guardian dogs, the komainu, from the Edo period on.
    Stone pairs given by commoners multiplied from the Edo period on, and their forms diversified.
  • Did a temple of Bishamon set stone tigers in place of guardian dogs?
    At Zenkoku-ji, a city temple in Kagurazaka, a pair of stone tigers flanks the entrance, "unlike the more common guardian lion-dogs"; a collector's page gives it as the general form: "Stone tigers like Komainu in front of Bishamon temples". Whether a village shrine of Bishamon had them, no page we read says.
  • A village's parishioners gave their shrine stone lanterns in 1799.
    A pair of lanterns given to the old Hikawa Shrine of Ishigami, in Saitama, is inscribed as dedicated by the village's parishioners in 1799.
  • Some shrines kept strength stones, the chikaraishi.
    At Hikawa Shrine in Nerima, eight oval river stones lie beside the approach, lifted by the young men of the Kantō in trials of strength from the late Edo period into the Meiji era.

A fence round the sanctuary was not among a village's gifts: before modern times one is known only where a lord or a great shrine built it (Shrine woods and fences (chinju no mori, tamagaki)).

A few larger things stood in some precincts and not in others.

  • 1,777 farmers' stages are known across Japan.
    The count covers the whole country, includes stages since lost, and includes noh and puppet stages.
  • Farmers built stages for plays on shrine ground.
    They were often built on shrine grounds, sometimes at temples, and seldom on sites of their own.
  • When did villages build their stages?
    Permanent stages, from the early 1800s. Kabuki and puppet plays came to be performed in farming villages across the country from the 1750s to the 1780s, and permanent stages are said to have been set up in the early 1800s.
  • Farmers' stages are densest in Tokushima, Hyōgo and Nagano.
    Each of the three prefectures has more than 200 permanent stages.
  • Sumo was offered at shrines across the country.
    The rite that consecrates a sumo ring, the dohyō, is performed for the sumo offered at shrines across the country; the page gives no size for a village's ring.
  • Did a village shrine have subsidiary shrines?
    Subsidiary shrines are small shrines that belong to a main one, within its precinct or near it, and are sometimes seen in a precinct; the standard dividing their two kinds was set at the high-ranking government shrines. No page we read places one at a village shrine.
  • A small shrine's business was often done at its administrator's home.
    An online dictionary of Japanese art and architecture says that at small shrines business was frequently carried on "at the private home of the administrator".
  • Did a village shrine have a votive-plaque hall or a store for its portable shrine?
    No page we read places either at a village shrine.

Villages in China staged plays for their gods too, at the god's temple.

  • A Chinese temple's opera stage was often built facing the temple's front.
    Plays of thanks to the gods were staged chiefly for the gods themselves, so the stage was often put up in front of the temple, and the audience kept to its sides and rear.
