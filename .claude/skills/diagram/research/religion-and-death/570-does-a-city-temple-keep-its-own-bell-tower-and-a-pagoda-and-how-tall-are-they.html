Does a city temple keep its own bell tower and a pagoda, and how tall are they?

Sources: shoro-jawiki (what a temple bell tower is and where it stood), ryosenji-shoro (a bell tower's size in bays), zhihuasi-zhwiki (a walled city temple's own bell and drum towers), tokinokane-list-jawiki (town time bells hung at temples), kokubunji-edict-jawiki (a pagoda ordered for every province), kohfukuji-sanjunoto (a three-story pagoda's height and base), sanjunoto-jawiki (three-story heights), gojunoto-jawiki (five-story heights), wenfengta-zhwiki (a Chinese town's fortune pagoda)

What the research found - the bell tower. The bell tower (shoro) is the building in a temple where its great bell hangs, and it is one of the seven halls of the old full plan (see "What does a temple precinct hold?"). In the Nara period it faced the sutra house across the plan; from the Heian period its place grew less fixed; and from the Muromachi period some temples joined it to the gate as a bell gate. One early-Muromachi bell tower, Ryosenji's in Nara, is a single bay square under a hip-and-gable roof, its lower story boarded in and flared outward like a skirt. A Chinese city temple could keep its own towers apart from the city's: in Beijing's Dongcheng district, the Zhihua temple has a two-story bell tower east of its inner gate and a drum tower of the same form west of it, the drum tower 7.1 m (23 ft) square; its bell, cast in 1444, is 1.05 m (3.4 ft) across the mouth and 1.60 m (5.2 ft) tall. In several Japanese towns the town's time bell hung at a temple - at Tenryuji in Shinjuku, at Sensoji in Asakusa, at Nionji in Kaizu. The civic bell-and-drum tower of a Chinese seat is "The bell-and-drum tower - one per walled seat".

What the research found - the pagoda. In the 740s Emperor Shomu ordered a seven-story pagoda built in every province, with a monastery and a nunnery in each. Kohfukuji's three-story pagoda in Nara stands 19.1 m (63 ft), its first story three bays square and 4.8 m (16 ft) across; other three-story pagodas run to about 24 m (79 ft) at Hokkiji and about 34 m (112 ft) at Yakushiji. Five-story pagodas run from 16.1 m (53 ft) at Muroji, the lowest standing in the open, to 54.8 m (180 ft) at Toji in Kyoto, the tallest before the modern era. Every measured pagoda read stands at a great or famous temple. In southern China a town might raise a pagoda of its own to pray for its literary fortune, a fengshui building with nothing to do with Buddhism, most often to its southeast.

What was not found. No readable page says how many ordinary city temples kept a bell tower or a pagoda.

The decision it drove. A city temple's own bell tower is part of the plan in both countries - accurate - so every temple precinct carries one near its gate, apart from the city's bell-and-drum tower. That a pagoda stands only in a great temple's precinct is a guess, from every measured pagoda standing at a great temple and the provincial temples' pagodas of the 740s.

The rule the map follows: every temple precinct carries a bell tower within its gate, about 20 ft square; a pagoda stands only in a great temple's precinct (the temples of the domain capital's grand abbots and of a provincial city's provincial abbot, the senior monks the Ministry of Rites posts to each), about 16 to 20 ft square at its base and 60 to 110 ft tall for three stories. Both sizes are guesses from the towers measured here: the bell tower from one Chinese city temple's 23 ft tower and a Japanese tower a single bay square, the pagoda from the three-story ones.
