How big is a town's own shrine, and does a city keep a principal shrine?

Sources: ranzan-kamagata-hachiman (the 1871 district-shrine rule, and a district shrine's precinct), jinja-goshi-jawiki (the 1897 promotion floors), sano-shrine-gazetteer (a county-office town's district shrine and its buildings), shinoda-2004-edo-shrines (Edo's principal shrine and where it stood), bicchu-sojagu-jawiki (a provincial capital's shrine), lin-2001-miaohui (a City God temple at every seat of government), licheng-chenghuangmiao-zhwiki, sanyuan-chenghuangmiao-zhwiki, pingyao-chenghuangmiao (three county seats' City God temples), zhangde-chenghuangmiao-zhwiki (a prefectural city's), pingyao-gucheng-zhwiki (where one stood in its city); the GM's campaign notes (the tier ladder)

A town's own shrine, in Japan. Above the village shrine, the sonsha, stood the district shrine, the gōsha. The rule of 1871 fixed one for each household-register district - twenty villages of about a thousand households, say, with five shrines among them, the chief of which became the district shrine and the other four its village shrines. The promotion rules of 1897 set its precinct floor at 500 tsubo, about 17,800 sq ft, against 300 tsubo for a village shrine and 600 for a prefectural one, with parishioners of at least 1,000 households against 200 and 2,000. One district shrine in Hiki district, Saitama, raised to that rank in 1927, has a precinct of 3,373 tsubo, about 120,000 sq ft; village shrines in the same register ran to a median of 403 (under "How large was a village shrine's precinct, and how much of it was built on?"). Its halls were of a village shrine's scale, whose sanctuaries run most often to 1 or 3 ken (under "How big is a country shrine, and what stands in its precinct?"): Sano, the seat of its district's office, whose two ranked shrines counted over 7,600 parishioners, kept the district shrine Asahimori, whose sanctuary was 1 and a half ken by 2 and a half, about 9 by 15 ft, and whose worship hall was 3 and a half ken by 3, about 21 by 18 ft, with a Chinese-style gate, two stone arches and ten lesser shrines in its ground. What set a town's shrine apart was the ground, the gate and the lesser shrines, not the hall.

A city's principal shrine. Edo kept one for the whole town, its sōchinju: the Sannō shrine, the peak of Edo's shrines; enshrined in 1478 on a hilltop where Edo castle was built, it was moved outward as the castle grew, and each new site kept the old one's slightly raised ground. Edo's shrines as a whole drifted from high ground - plateau tops, their edges, hilltops - toward flat ground in the town and along its back streets, though many stood on flat ground from the first. A province kept a shrine near its capital that gathered the province's deities, the sōja; Bitchū's stands in the center of the city that grew around it as its gate town and post town.

In China. Under the Qing, wherever a local government office was set up there was a City God temple, the chenghuangmiao. At county seats they ranged widely: Licheng's is one courtyard, 36.23 by 52.65 m, 1,892 m², about 20,400 sq ft, with a gate and a main hall on its axis; Pingyao's, three temples on one site, covers over 7,300 m², about 78,600 sq ft; Sanyuan's, with three gates, four archways, five towers and six courtyards on its axis, covers 13,390 m², about 144,000 sq ft. The prefectural city of Zhangde's covers 6,773 m², about 72,900 sq ft, so rank did not fix size. At Pingyao it stands in the east of the city, the county office in the west, about the main street.

The decision. Both histories give a county town a shrine of its own and a city a principal one. The setting's tier ladder gives a town its monasteries and a city its temples and wayside shrines, and names neither a town shrine nor a city's principal shrine, and the campaign notes have no City God; so none is drawn (deviation, the setting's own). Were the GM to add one, the history would draw a town's shrine on a precinct of 500 tsubo or more, about 17,800 sq ft, with village-sized halls, a gate and lesser shrines, and a city's on raised ground near its seat of government.
