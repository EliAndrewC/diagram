Who lives inside a city temple's walls, and who lives outside them?

Sources: sodo-jawiki; ranzan-kinsenji (a temple's priest's house inside its precinct); kotobank-bomori (a Shin priest's wife from 1343, and the priests' wives at Yoshizaki); koshoji-yoshizaki-taya (priests' houses round a temple in the 1470s); kamigamo-shake-plan (a quarter of priest families' houses of their own style); takabatake-naragoround (a shrine's priests' quarter until Meiji); sakamoto-satobo-otsu (old monks' residences apart from their monastery); the GM's ruling of 2026-07-24; the GM's campaign notes for the vows and the initiate tier; the householder-clergy finding recorded above

The decision. The GM ruled on 2026-07-24 that a city temple draws exactly like the governor's yamen: a walled compound with a deliberately blank court, because the interior is the subject of its own building plan when the players visit it. On the city sheet the big rectangle IS the temple.

Who is implied inside. Celibate monks, resident initiates and a few lay servants - some 25 to 45 of them in a provincial-city complex - live in dormitories and cloisters implied within that wall. They are implied population, counted on top of the dwellings the map draws rather than housed in the street fabric, and they sit outside the lay caste bands, because clergy are not a lay caste.

Who lives out. Adepts may marry and raise children, so a share of each complex's 15 to 30 monks are householders whose families keep two or three ordinary homes in the temple's own neighborhood. The setting's campaign notes put married monks' families in or next to the temple complex. In the registers made after 1868 a Japanese temple's head priest lived with his family inside the precinct, in its kuri. The married priest is far older in the Shin sect: a list of 1343 already calls the wife of a meeting hall's head priest bōmori, and Rennyo's letters speak of the wives of the priests in the lodging houses at his temple of Yoshizaki, where from 1471 a town grew up and houses for the priests stationed there were built round the temple. Hereditary priest families of shrines lived in quarters of their own before modern times: the Kamigamo shrine's from the Muromachi period until the Restoration, in a quarter they shared with farmers, in houses of a style of their own - set behind earthen walls, entered by their own gates, reached over earthen bridges across the stream - which the quarter's preservation plan tells apart from the townhouses; and the Kasuga shrine's at Takabatake in Nara, a priests' quarter that lasted until Meiji, from which paths led up to the shrine. An old monk's residence apart from the monastery is Edo too: monks grown old in training on Mount Hiei were granted retirement residences at Sakamoto, the town at its foot. How many clergy homes stood round one temple was not found in any source read, so the counts below are the GM's ruling and a guess in degree. Deviation, disclosed: those homes are drawn deliberately identical to a laborer's house, with no label and no glyph of their own, so that the eye cannot pick the temple families out of the fabric, though a real priest family's house could be picked out. Each complex also carries about twice its monk count in initiates ("many are called, few are chosen"), of whom only a small fraction sleep inside: roughly 5 to 10 at provincial-city scale, 10 to 20 at a domain capital. The rest are laborers, carters, servants and shopkeepers engaged in religious study while they work ordinary jobs, and on the map they are ordinary houses of their own caste, never buildings of their own.

The rule the map follows: on a city or capital sheet (3 ft per pixel) a major temple is a walled compound of wall, gate and empty court. Two or three clergy homes stand within about 500 ft of each major complex , and none stands stranded away from every temple; a precinct whose clergy are hereditary householders carries five to nine instead, ringing its wall. Beyond those, at most two exception features stand apart - a retired abbot's hermitage, or a small daughter chapel with two to four monks of its own - drawn precisely because they are exceptions.
