Who lives inside a city temple's walls, and who lives outside them?

Sources: sodo-jawiki; the GM's ruling of 2026-07-24; the GM's campaign notes for the vows and the initiate tier; the householder-clergy finding recorded above

The decision. The GM ruled on 2026-07-24 that a city temple draws exactly like the governor's yamen: a walled compound with a deliberately blank court, because the interior is the subject of its own building plan when the players visit it. On the city sheet the big rectangle IS the temple.

Who is implied inside. Celibate monks, resident initiates and a few lay servants - some 25 to 45 of them in a provincial-city complex - live in dormitories and cloisters implied within that wall.131 They are implied population, counted on top of the dwellings the map draws rather than housed in the street fabric, and they sit outside the lay caste bands, because clergy are not a lay caste.

Who lives out. Adepts may marry and raise children, so a share of each complex's 15 to 30 monks are householders whose families keep two or three ordinary homes in the temple's own neighborhood. Departure, disclosed: those homes are drawn deliberately identical to a laborer's house, with no label and no glyph of their own - the eye should not be able to pick the temple families out of the fabric, because in reality it could not.130 Each complex also carries about twice its monk count in initiates ("many are called, few are chosen"), of whom only a small fraction sleep inside: roughly 5 to 10 at provincial-city scale, 10 to 20 at a domain capital. The rest are laborers, carters, servants and shopkeepers engaged in religious study while they work ordinary jobs, and on the map they are ordinary houses of their own caste, never buildings of their own.

The rule the map follows: on a city or capital sheet (3 ft per pixel) a major temple is a walled compound of wall, gate and empty court. Two or three clergy homes stand within about 500 ft of each major complex , and none stands stranded away from every temple; a precinct whose clergy are hereditary householders carries five to nine instead, ringing its wall. Beyond those, at most two exception features stand apart - a retired abbot's hermitage, or a small daughter chapel with two to four monks of its own - drawn precisely because they are exceptions.
