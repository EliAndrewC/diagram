How many wayside shrines does a village or a town's streets carry, how big are they, and where do they stand?

Sources: dosojin-jawiki (the thresholds, and the stones' size), jizo-bosatsu-jawiki (where a stone jizo stands, and a jizo for each town block), jizobon-jawiki (a block with no jizo, and the dosojin in its place in the east), matsumoto-dosojin (a stone at each hamlet's entrance, standing with others), fukuyama-tsujido-jawiki (a wayside hall for every 20-30 households, and its size), onga-choshi-jiin (a jizo at a crossroads, and one tied to a well), sanko-densetsu-jawiki (a small shrine beside a well), inari-shin-jawiki (Inari shrines everywhere in Edo), wangjian-miaojie (the Chinese village's altar and earth-god temple)

Where they stand. The deity of the way, the dosojin, is enshrined at spatial and social boundaries - a village's entrance and exit, a mountain pass, a crossroads, the foot of a bridge - and the stone jizo, merged with it, stands at the same places and in graveyards. In Matsumoto, whose city holds more than 600 of the stones, most stand at a settlement's entrance, the village boundary. One Fukuoka town's jizo stands at a crossroads, moved there from a household's grounds. A small shrine could also keep a well: a dragon-god shrine stands near a named well in northern Tōhoku, still kept with a festival, and a Fukuoka village's jizo was held to have been angered when rubbish fouled the village's great well.

How many. Where the stones are thick, a VILLAGE kept one to each of its hamlets: in one Matsumoto district each of three settlements has its own dosojin, and at another settlement's entrance two dosojin stand in a row with a koshin tower and a memorial tower for the nenbutsu, so what a map sees is a group of stones rather than one. In the Fukuyama domain the settlements also built small open wayside halls, the tsujido, along the roads between villages, at passes and in busy places, about one for every 20 to 30 households, each kept by its settlement and none built far from habitation; one district had 17 of them in 1700. The halls are a regional form: a gazetteer of 1809 calls them rare in other provinces and common in the province of Bingo alone, some 600 of them in the Fukuyama domain. A TOWN, in the Kinki region, kept a jizo for each town block, though a block with none borrowed one from a temple for its festival, and from the Kantō eastward it is also said that the dosojin did the street jizo's work; in Edo Inari shrines were one of the three things met everywhere in the city. China's counterpart is the earth god: the Ming statutes ordered one altar to the gods of soil and grain for every hundred rural households, and a count from the gazetteers of the Yangzi delta, which the historian reporting it doubts as too low, found about one earth-god temple to every ten-odd lowland villages.

How big. A dosojin runs from a stone small enough to sit on a palm to a standing figure nearly 2 m, about 6 ft, tall. A wayside hall is most often four corner posts under a roof, with at most one wall and a board floor of about two mats, some 6 ft square; one 1862 account puts such halls on Shōdoshima at 2 to 3 ken, about 12 to 18 ft, on a side.

The rule the map follows: at each place a road or lane enters a hamlet or a village, a group of one to three wayside stones stands beside the way, drawn at about 4 ft across - a map drawing convention, the real stones being palm-sized to about 6 ft tall; a crossroads, a bridge's foot or the village's well may take one more. Each village then draws, at random, whether it also keeps wayside halls: if it does, one open hall about 6 to 18 ft square stands at a crossroads or beside the road between settlements for every 20 to 30 of its households, and never away from the houses and roads. The odds of the choice are a guess: even. A TOWN keeps a small street shrine, a jizo's or an Inari's, for each of its blocks; that the count holds outside the Kinki is a guess.
