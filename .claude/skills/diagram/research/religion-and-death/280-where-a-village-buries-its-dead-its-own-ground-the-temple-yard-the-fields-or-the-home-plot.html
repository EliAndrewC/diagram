Where a village buries its dead: its own ground, the temple yard, the fields or the home plot

At the end of the Edo period, before 1868, a Japanese village's dead did not all lie in one kind of ground. A temple's graveyard, a graveyard the settlement held in common, a lineage's ground and a single person's grave all stood across the country, each left to local custom. Where the dead had two graves, the body lay in common ground on a riverbank, in the hills or by the shore, while the grave the family visited stood at a temple or a hall in the village. And many households kept a grave on their own land.

Chinese graves lay on family land instead, and often out in the working fields: a farm survey of the 1920s found graves on between 2 and 3 per cent of the farm land of the level-land localities it measured.

  • Graveyards of several forms stood side by side in Japan at the end of the Edo period.
    At the end of the Edo period and in early Meiji, graveyards of many forms - an individual's, a lineage's, a settlement's and a temple's - stood across the country. The idea of private ownership in them was very weak, and like the customs of funerals they were left to the custom of each locality.
  • Temple graveyards spread in the Edo period.
    Supported by the temple-certification system, many temples were founded, the graveyards attached to them spread, and the dead came more often to be buried at a temple. At one excavated temple ground in Sagamihara, reburied when its temple moved, the ground was so cramped that burials overlapped.
  • A household was registered with the temple that buried it.
    Under the Tokugawa parish system the registered unit was the household, not the person, and what the temple gave it was the funerals and the continuing memorial rites for all its members. The system grew out of the government's campaigns against Christianity of 1613 and 1614. Making one's grave at one's parish temple was half an obligation. Most villages' households were registered with temples outside the village (Village temples (tera)), so where that obligation was kept, a family's grave stood at a temple away from its own settlement.
  • A village could hold a graveyard in common.
    Japanese has a name for a graveyard that the inhabitants of a settlement own in common and manage themselves. New ones are not permitted today.
  • A Meiji law of 1884 opened a village's graveyard to all its inhabitants.
    Under the state's first systematic graveyard law, a Home Ministry instruction of November 1884, anyone registered in a town or village, or dying there, could be buried in its graveyard whatever their sect, unless an older special custom stood.
  • Where the dead had two graves, the body's grave was usually on common ground.
    The two-grave custom separates the ground where the body is buried from the ground where its grave is visited. The burial grave lay on a riverbank, in the hills or by the shore, most often on common land, filled in the order of the deaths and marked with no more than a stone. The visiting grave stood in the precinct of a temple or a hall within the village. Many burial grounds lay in mountain forest far from the houses, and the visiting grave was a stone pagoda holding neither body nor bone. The burial ground was most often a graveyard held in common by each settlement, sometimes divided into plots by household or by age and sometimes under no rule at all.
  • In one Wakasa hamlet the burial ground stood at the hamlet's edge.
    A Fukui record of the funeral custom puts the sanmai, the burial ground or the hall at one, at the edge of the hamlet of Takenami, while the visiting graves stood in two temples inside the hamlet.
  • How common was the two-grave custom?
    A national folklore survey counted the settlements keeping it prefecture by prefecture: 60 in Shiga, 59 in Nara, 45 in Mie, 42 in Kyoto, 39 each in Hyogo and Fukui, 22 in Shizuoka, and few elsewhere. Two dictionaries center it on the Kansai region and find it hardly at all in Kyushu. The counts are of the two-grave custom rather than of village graveyards in general, and of settlements confirmed when surveyed in the twentieth century, so they are a floor rather than a rate.
  • Did every village, or every hamlet, keep a burial ground of its own before 1868?
    No page dated before modern times says so. Every page that dates a settlement's own graveyard before 1868 names it beside other forms; that each village and each hamlet kept one rests on folklore records and surveys of the twentieth century. No page we read says how common each form was village by village, or which regions favored which, and none attaches a count of households to a village's graveyard.
  • How many burial grounds did a village of several hamlets keep?
    No page we read counts them, or says how often a hamlet's dead went to the temple's ground rather than to a ground of their own.
  • On Kikaijima, graveyards of each settlement formed after 1868.
    When the Meiji government banned exposure burial, where to make the burial place varied from settlement to settlement, and graveyards of each settlement began to form outside the old cliff caves. The island's settlements are assumed to have differed widely in custom.
  • A village's shrine stood above its houses, and the body's grave below them.
    A survey of 22 two-grave settlements puts the shrine at the upper end of the village, the end its watercourse makes the upper one, and the burial grave below the settlement but never above it.
  • Shinto treats death as a source of defilement.
    Death and everything to do with it is a primary source of defilement, kegare, which a shrine's priests are bound to avoid.
  • One person's remains could be divided among several graves.
    Divided remains - one person's cremated remains shared among two or more graves or ossuaries - is an attested practice.

A household could also keep a grave of its own, on its own land, apart from any shared ground.

  • Many Japanese households once kept a grave on their own land.
    Formerly many households each held a graveyard, or a branch house set its grave beside the main house's so that the kin shared one, and some set the grave within their own house land - the yashiki-baka, the house-plot grave. Until the Second World War many graves were still built on a corner of a household's own land, or kept by a neighborhood group.
  • Where were house-plot graves common?
    Yanagita Kunio, as a local-history blog reports him, found houses with a graveyard within or beside the plot common in the Kanto, in the Ou region of the northeast and in the mountain villages of southern Kyushu, among other places. Today such graves are few across the country, and are said to be especially few in the Kansai and many in the Kanto.
  • Where on its plot did a household set its grave?
    One family's history, set down by a descendant in the former Hatara district, puts its graveyard before the Genroku era (1688-1704) at the southeast corner of the house plot, and another at the northwest corner, ground now counted as woodland. The writer believes the dead there were buried without gravestones in the woods behind the plot's shrine to its guardian god, part of the grove round the house. In that district, the writer says, many houses have a graveyard adjoining the plot or close by it, though not within the grounds, while in one area of Saitama's Kodama district many are said to have one beside the plot.
  • A household's grave could also stand in its own field.
    A stonemason who visited Fukaya, in the north of Saitama, saw each household's grave built on part of its own land: in the middle of a field along the road, or in a house's front garden, among other places. In Ibaraki such graves are still often seen within the house grounds, in the garden, before the entrance, or in a corner of a field. In the hills, where flat land is scarce, the graveyard is said often to stand part-way up the slope behind.
  • When graves were moved off the plot, they went no great distance.
    The Hatara writer surmises that the graves of their district were moved out of the house plots, and says that no house moved its grave far: many used part of their own fields or woods within the hamlet. In some areas of western Tokyo the burial graves on the home's own land were later moved to a communal graveyard.
  • From the Meiji period on, household graves were often gathered into shared grounds.
    Because households setting up graveyards here and there as they pleased, all on tax-exempt land, ran against the common interest, graveyards were often consolidated from Meiji on. By Takatori Masao's account, as the Hatara blog reports him, the authorities advised gathering each district's graves into one communal ground, and many districts did so while others did not.
  • Some Japanese burial graves stood beside the bunds of a family's own fields.
    In the Tenryu river basin and in the Otawa district of Narusawa village in Yamanashi, the burial graves are reported to have once stood on private land such as beside the bunds, and to have moved to common land by order of the Meiji government. In 1872 a Finance Ministry notice forbade burying the dead at the bund edge of one's own fields, having heard that people were doing so. The notice does not say how common the practice was.
  • Did a Japanese grave ever stand out in the middle of a paddy?
    No Japanese page we read puts one there: every placement read is at the bund edge, in a field's corner or on the house plot.
  • What share of households buried on their own ground?
    No page we read says what share of households, or of hamlets, buried on their own ground rather than in a shared one, or how that differed between hamlet and village.

In China the dead lay on family and lineage land, and in the level country that land was often the family's own fields.

  • Chinese graves stood anywhere in a field.
    A farm survey of the 1920s found that in most regions with no hill land to take them, graves stood in every conceivable position in a field, whatever the trouble to cultivation, and it shows a well-to-do family's graveyard in north China filling almost a whole field. Hillside burial is attested in southern China as well (In-field features), so the field grave is one of two southern forms.
  • How much of the farm land did graves take?
    In the same survey's five localities of level land only, graves took 2.6 per cent of the farm land, and at most 9.1 per cent, on the fertile land of Wuchin county in Kiangsu. Two student samples it reports beside these run higher still.
  • Around Shanghai, many villagers buried their dead one by one in the open fields.
    Villagers usually had a small burial ground, often a lineage's, but many buried their dead in single graves in the middle of open fields, at a spot a Taoist priest or a geomancer chose. A column of soldiers that marched into Shanghai from Wusong in 1842 reported graves in every field, as mounds of earth.
  • How common were graves in the fields?
    No source gives a rate: the Shanghai account says outright that there is no way now to know how many such graves there were or where they stood. What it does give - "many" villagers, graves "in every field" on one march - argues that where the custom held they were common, not rare.
  • Did the delta's field graves stand in flooded paddy?
    The Shanghai account does not say what the fields grew, so it shows graves standing inside working fields in rice country without saying that any one of them stood in a paddy.
  • Graves were still scattered across the farmland of Henan in 2012.
    In the spring of 2012 the province's party secretary saw many graves scattered across farmland, and the province later announced it had leveled more than two million graves to make fields. It is a modern report.
  • Rich households' graves took good farmland, and the shared grounds were for the poor.
    In the Jiangnan region most of the many surviving old graves occupy good farmland and prime scenic ground, and a study of Ming and Qing Jiangnan calls the seizure of good land and feng shui ground by rich households astonishing. The communal grounds it describes are chiefly charity graveyards: the Ming code required every prefecture and county to bury the destitute, and the charity graveyards of benevolent societies stood mostly in the towns.
  • A lineage kept its grave land undivided.
    In traditional China what brothers left undivided is often said to have been only grave land and land for the rites. One Huizhou lineage kept its founding ancestor's grave-hill with only its ridge in the ancestral hall's name, its slopes worked in common and shared among the lineage's members.
