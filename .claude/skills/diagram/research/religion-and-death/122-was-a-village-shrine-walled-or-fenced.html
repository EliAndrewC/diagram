Was a village shrine walled or fenced? No - its arch and its wood marked it

Sources: tamagaki-jawiki (what the fence is called, and Ise's four rings), shinshiro-yahira-jinja, shirahata-jinja-tamagaki and yasuda-hachiman-tamagaki (three dated fences, and what each rings), jinja-mamoru-tamagaki (wood and hedge first, stone later), ndl-crd-tamagaki (a neighborhood shrine with only an arch), chinju-no-mori-jawiki (the wood around the precinct), tsuijibei-jawiki (the striped wall as a temple's rank), tudimiao-zhwiki and gantang-temple-thepaper (China)

What the research found. Japanese has a word for a shrine's fence, the tamagaki, also called the mizugaki, "a fence set up around a shrine or sacred precinct", and the example the encyclopedia gives is Ise, the imperial shrine, with four fences in concentric rings. But every fence at an ordinary shrine that could be dated rings the sanctuary alone, or lines the approach, and none rings the whole precinct. At Yahira Shrine a roofed board fence of 1918 stands around the sanctuary, "built with its height kept down, so that the sanctuary's roof can be seen from around it", on a sanctuary built in 1700; at Shirahata Shrine a stone fence carved with a date in 1928, raised to mark the emperor's enthronement, "surrounds the shrine building at the top of the stairs"; and the one late-Edo stone fence found, at Yasuda Hachiman Shrine, stands "on both sides of the shrine's approach", 0.6 m high. The older fences were wooden rails or hedges, and stone spread later. Yahira's fence came 218 years after its sanctuary, and Shirahata's marked a national occasion.

The other side of the picture is a shrine with nothing around it. A patron asking the National Diet Library about shrine fences explained that "my neighborhood shrine has only a torii", and that someone was thinking of building something, since other shrines had hedges or stone. What sets a village shrine's ground apart is its wood, the chinju no mori, kept "so as to surround the sanctuary, the approach and the place of worship"; and a small shrine with houses built right up to its sanctuary is not counted as having one. Where a wall marked rank, the attested case is a temple's: the striped earthen wall, whose number of white lines expressed the temple's rank, five the highest. China agrees at the bottom of the scale. The humblest earth-god shrine is two stones for walls and one for a roof, under a tree or by a road, with no room for a courtyard. The one village temple read with a courtyard stood on about 2.5 mu, and its account does not say what bounded it. That no village shrine's whole precinct was fenced is this page's reading of these dated examples; no page states it as a general rule.

The decision. The country-shrine sheet draws no fence, wall or hedge around its precinct (accurate). The ground is marked the way the record marks it: the first arch where the approach enters it (the spacing of the arches is under "Torii spacing" on this page), and the grove around it, whose extent is under "How large was a village shrine's precinct, and how much of it was built on?". A fence around the sanctuary alone is a later addition: the dated ones are of 1918 and 1928, the second raised for a national occasion. It belongs with the other gifts on the wealth knob (under "What else stood in a village shrine's precinct?") and is not drawn at average wealth.
