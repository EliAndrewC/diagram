Does a graveyard stand beside its temple or its shrine?

Sources: the GM's campaign notes for the merged orders and the village shrine's resident brother; danka-terauke-encyclopedia (the household as the registered unit, and the funerals and memorial rites the temple renders it), bunkotsu-jawiki (divided remains), kaf2-kinsei-bo (the Edo temple graveyard), kawazoe-2010-ryobosei (the shrine upstream, the burial ground never above the houses)

Why the graveyard is the hall's own yard. The state merged the Buddhist strand with Fortune worship, so ANY temple may host a graveyard, and a graveyard is a parish ground that sits inside a temple precinct, as Edo Japan's increasingly did. A village SHRINE hosts one too, which is the churchyard rule and the thing most likely to look wrong at first glance: real-world Shinto shuns death pollution (kegare), but this setting's country shrines are officially of the Buddhist-analog order even while they house Fortune deities, and the resident brother - the monk who keeps it - performs the funerary rites. The shrine IS the death-handling institution - the parish-temple logic, in which a household is registered to the temple that buries it - the registered unit being the household rather than the person, and what the temple renders it being the funerals and the continuing memorial rites for all its members (that arrangement is the Tokugawa danka registration, a state anti-Christian apparatus; this setting reaches the same outcome by another route) - so its burial ground is its churchyard rather than a plot held away. That unifies burial across the tiers: the graves lie by the monk-run building everywhere, the shrine in a village and the monastery or temple in a town or a city.

The exceptions, and what they are worth. A HILLTOP shrine keeps no graves: graves do not climb the sacred hill, and a prominent hill shrine is not the humble earth-god monk's funerary base. A survey of two-grave villages is on the same side: it puts the shrine at the upper end of the village and the burial ground below the houses, never above them. That a hill shrine in particular kept no graves is a judgment about the setting: no source says it either way. A ground may also be a village-style burial ground not attached to a hall at all. History attests that form beside the temple's, and a village's seed chooses between them (Does a village bury in its temple's yard, in a ground of its own, or in its fields?); it is also how a farming quarter inside a city wall gets a ground of its own. And divided remains (one person's cremated remains divided among two or more graves or ossuaries) are real and simply not drawn, because nothing of the practice is visible in this view.

The rule the map follows: in a VILLAGE whose seed puts the burial ground in the shrine's own yard, it lies within about 500 ft of the hall ; a shrine on a hill never takes it, and the village's ground then lies apart, downstream of the houses. In a TOWN or CITY a graveyard lies in its monastery's or temple's precinct - about 690 ft of the hall on a city sheet, the same drawn distance being about 230 ft on a town sheet - and at city scale that binds only a ground INSIDE the walls, since an extramural common ground belongs to the whole city. The hall and its torii arches themselves stay clear: the graves fill the yard AROUND them and never lie on them. Any WALLED settlement keeps a ground inside AND outside its walls, and the exterior common ground is at least 1.3 times the largest interior one, because there is room beyond the wall while the parish plots inside are hemmed in; an unwalled town's grounds are all parish grounds. A town, having no pauper ossuary to take the poor, carries proportionally more in its common ground than a city does and draws it larger.
