Were a town's and a city's burial grounds that size before modern times?

Sources: sohu-xuancheng-yizhong (a Qing county's charity graveyards, by road, in mu), feng-yizhong-jiangnan (the range of one Jiangnan county's charity graveyards), chinese-units-enwiki (the mu, to put them in acres), kaf2-kinsei-bo (an Edo burial area too small for its dead), kimura-1934-bochi-menseki (the modern factor for paths)

The maps draw a town's burial ground at 0.25 to 0.75 acre and a city's at 0.75 to 2 acres, split across its yards (How our maps draw village burial grounds). This question asks whether grounds that size are found before modern times - before 1868 in Japan, before 1912 in China.

China: measured grounds, several to a county. The county gazetteer of Xuancheng, as a local historian quotes it, lists the county's charity graveyards by the road out of town they stood on: two mu on Anle hill and one on Wangcheng ridge on the west road, three mu by the southern altar on the south road, one and a half mu on Jiezi ridge on the north road, and, bought in 1805, twenty-two mu more. In Baoshan, a Jiangnan county, a count of the Guangxu reign (1875-1908) found each charity graveyard running from 3 fen to 10 mu. At the mu of 614.4 m² of the 1915 table, Xuancheng's four road grounds come to about 0.15 to 0.45 acre each and some 1.1 acres together, its later purchase to about 3.3 acres, and Baoshan's grounds to about 0.05 to 1.5 acres each. A town's 0.25 to 0.75 acre, and a city's 0.75 to 2 acres split across several yards, sit inside those figures, and a county seat's dead lying in several grounds beside different roads out is the Xuancheng pattern. The limit is what these grounds were: charity graveyards for the unclaimed and the poor, not the whole of a town's dead, who were buried on family land (Where a village buries its dead: its own ground, the temple yard, the fields or the home plot); and neither source gives the population a ground served.

Japan: no area found. No source read gives the area of an Edo temple's or village's burial ground, nor the population it served. What the archaeology does show is that a ground was cramped: at the Obodo site in Sagamihara, a city southwest of Tokyo, an Edo temple's burial area held many grave pits dug over one another, because the area was limited and the dead were buried in the same place.

The factor for paths: modern only. The step from the plots to a whole ground - a factor of 1.5 to 2 for paths and buildings - comes from a 1934 paper in Japan's landscape-architecture journal on the common cemeteries of Japan's cities, its factor worked out from Tokyo's, and nothing earlier gives one.

The decision. The ladder stays, calibrated to the premodern figures: a town's ground and each of a city's yards within the 0.05 to 1.5 acres of the Qing charity graveyards. The 1934 factor for paths is modern, so the maps do not use it: a ground's size is its graves and the lanes the drawing gives them, not a multiple of its plots.

The rule the map follows: a town's burial ground is about 0.25 to 0.75 acre; a city's 0.75 to 2 acres is split across two or more yards, each of them within about 0.05 to 1.5 acres, beside different roads out where the city's plan allows. No factor for paths is applied to the plots. The sizes are calibrated to Qing charity graveyards; no premodern Japanese ground's area was found.
