Were graves weeded, and the ground around them kept clear, before modern times? The graves themselves, yes

Sources: dijing-jingwulue-wikisource (the grave weeded and topped up at Qingming in 1630s Beijing), dijing-jingwulue-enwiki (that book's date), qingming-enwiki (tomb-sweeping), ryobosei-jawiki (the burial place away from habitation), kotobank-nanukabon and ndl-crd-nanukabon (grave cleaning at the start of Bon), yakult-bon-kusakari (the grass cut round the graves before Bon); the equinox festivals, from the campaign notes

What the research found. Tending a grave by weeding it is attested before modern times in China. A description of Beijing and its customs, its preface dated 1635, counts among the families at their graves on Qingming day "those who bow, those who pour libations, those who wail, and those who clear the weeds from the grave and add earth to it". The festival is kept so still: the young and the old alike kneel before the ancestors' tombstones, burn incense and paper, sweep the tombs and offer food. In Japan the burying grave of the two-grave custom is often in mountain forest and the like, away from human habitation; the seventh day of the seventh month, which opens Bon, is when, region by region, the graves are cleaned, the graves and, in one modern popular account, the paths to them, and in some places the grass round the graves is cut before Bon so that the spirits can pass easily. Those are records of custom as it was observed in modern times; no page read dates a Bon grave cleaning before 1868. In this setting families weed and clean their ancestors' graves at the spring and autumn equinox festivals. What no source read attests is a cleared band of ground around the graves: the weeding is of the grave itself, and how wide any collar ran is in no source read. So: clear AT the graves, wild BETWEEN them.

The decision (the GM's rule of 2026-09-28, that anything attested only in modern times is eliminated). The premodern form is the grave itself weeded and topped up with earth (accurate); a band of cleared ground around a graveyard rests only on modern custom, so the maps do not draw one.

The rule the map follows: a grave plot keeps the loose scatter (the scrub of the commons, the reeds of a marsh) off its own ground, the graves themselves, and no wider collar is drawn around it ; the scrub runs up to the plot's edge. The grove, the buildings and every placement rule ignore it.
