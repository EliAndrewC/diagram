How big was the country monk's dwelling, and did it share the hall's roof? A farmhouse in form, and once attested under one roof

Sources: kuri-jawiki (the ordinary temple's kuri like a house), kawasaki-chonenji-kuri, kawasaki-chonenji-hondo, bunka-tokuunji-kuri and ohtawara-daioji (measured kuri and halls), sakai-kaieji (the one under the hall's roof), xietang-tudimiao-zhwiki (China)

Every designated kuri that could be read is a rich temple's - "9 ken along the ridge and 5 ken across the beams", with "the head priest's living room ... a twelve-mat room", beside a hall of "7 ken" by "7 ken"; the largest, "12 ken along the ridge by 8 ken", says of its own form that it resembles "the honmune-zukuri farmhouse characteristic of this region". At an ordinary temple, the encyclopedia says, the kuri often doubles as the temple's office, and many are buildings much like ordinary houses. No small kuri was measured, so the map draws the monk's dwelling at the farmhouse's own footprint, 46 by 28 ft: accurate in form, a guess in size. The form the GM asked for - the hall that is also the home - is attested once and called rare: at Kaie-ji, a Zen temple in the port city of Sakai, the "main hall and kuri" are "one building: ridge span 25.2 meters, beam span 8.0 meters on the left side, 13.0 meters on the right side", and "a main hall and kuri in one building is unusual for temple architecture of the beginning of the Edo period". That is 83 ft long by 26 to 43 ft deep, some 200 to 330 m²; the hall of a temple "protected as the family temple of the Ōzeki clan, who were the domain's lords", for contrast, is "25.5 meters" by "16.3 meters". China's earth-god temples span a range of sizes, up to a hall: a Song-dynasty foundation at Xietang, "first built in the Southern Song", keeps a surviving main hall "three bays wide and one bay deep, 10 meters long and 7 meters wide" - large only against the few-square-meter shrines under "Who kept a Chinese village temple?", a comparison the page itself does not make.
