How many monasteries does a county town keep, and who lives in one?

Sources: the GM's campaign notes (a preceptor per Order, serving in a town monastery; two patron Fortunes a clan); ranzan-temple-register (nineteen temples in the old villages of one Saitama district), amagasaki-teramachi (a castle town's twenty temples in 1635), miyazu-teramachi (a small castle town's twelve), hongwu-emperor-enwiki (the Ming cap of one monastery a county), ranzan-kinsenji (a village temple's priest's house and monks' dormitory), ranzan-koshoji (the Ranzan area's largest temple precinct, with two dormitories), eijuji-shinshiro (a Sōtō temple's halls)

What the setting says. Each town has a preceptor per Order, and town preceptors serve in town monasteries in county towns; each clan cultivates two patron Fortunes, and an Order is the network of one Fortune's temples, monasteries and shrines across a domain. A county town of some 900 to 1,500 inhabitants therefore keeps a monastery for each of its clan's two patron Fortunes, and in one of the campaign's own towns the monastery stands just outside the town.

What the history says about the count. Real places of that size kept more. In the Ranzan area of Hiki district, Saitama, the prefecture's temple register lists nineteen temples in fourteen of the old villages, two apiece in five of them - nearly every village kept a temple of its own, and that a Japanese town of 1,200 would have kept several is this page's inference from it. Castle towns gathered theirs into a temple quarter, the teramachi: Amagasaki's plan of 1635 shows twenty temples, the small castle town of Miyazu gathers twelve in one part of the town, and the larger cities' counts are under "Many modest temples per walled city is the historical norm". China went the other way by law: the Ming founder limited each county to one monastery of at most two monks - a cap, whose enforcement is on no page read. The setting's two monasteries sit between the Ming cap and the Japanese counts.

Who lives in one. In the Hiki register a temple's dwelling is its kuri, which the transcription's editors gloss as the building where the head priest and his family live; even a village temple there could keep a monks' dormitory, glossed as the lodging of monks in training, and the largest precinct of the area's nineteen lists two dormitories, a front and a back. A Sōtō Zen temple in Aichi sets its kuri and study on one side of its forecourt and its dormitory and meditation hall on the other, the halls the Sōtō school required of its ranked temples that held the training retreat, a rank the page does not give for this one. The household, then, lived inside the precinct: a head priest's family in the kuri, monks in training in a dormitory. How many lived in one - priest, family, disciples and servants counted - is on no page read.

The decision. A town draws its two monasteries, one for each patron Fortune, as the setting has it - a deviation from a Japanese town of that size, which kept more, and more than the Ming law allowed a whole county. Inside each is implied the preceptor, the monks under them and, where the Order's monks marry, a householder's family, housed as the kuri and the dormitory house them (accurate in kind; how many is a guess).
