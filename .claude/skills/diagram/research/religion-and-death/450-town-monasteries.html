Town monasteries

In the setting our maps are drawn for, a town monastery is where an Order's clergy live and serve in a county town. Each town has a preceptor for each Order, and the town preceptors serve in town monasteries; each clan cultivates two Fortunes as its patrons, and an Order is the network of one Fortune's temples, monasteries and shrines across a domain. Real places of a county town's size, some 900 to 1,500 inhabitants, kept more temples than that. Nearly every village of one Saitama district kept a temple of its own, and castle towns gathered a dozen or twenty into a quarter of their own. Ming China went the other way by law, allowing each county one monastery.

A Japanese temple of that kind stood in a precinct of its own, in one district's register from about 10,000 to 69,000 sq ft, the middle one about 16,000, round a main hall some 24 to 63 ft across the front. Its head priest's family lived in the precinct, in the kuri, and monks in training in a dormitory. The register was made after Japan began to modernize in 1868, but an Edo village's record of two of the same temples puts their land of the same order.

  • A Saitama district's old villages kept nineteen temples among them.
    In the Ranzan area of Hiki district, Saitama, the prefecture's temple register lists nineteen temples in fourteen of the old villages, two apiece in five of them. Nearly every village kept a temple of its own, and that a Japanese town of 1,200 inhabitants would have kept several is this page's inference from it.
  • Castle towns gathered their temples into a temple quarter (teramachi).
    Amagasaki's plan of 1635 shows twenty temples in its quarter - the quarter's count, not every temple in the town. The small castle town of Miyazu gathers twelve in one part of the town. The counts for larger cities are at Many modest temples per walled city is the historical norm.
  • The Ming dynasty's founder limited each county to one monastery of at most two monks.
    The law was a cap, and how far it was enforced is on no page we read.

The size of such a temple is known from the same Saitama register, which gives each temple's precinct in tsubo and its main hall's frontage in ken.

  • How big was a temple's precinct?
    The register's nineteen temples run from 285 tsubo to 1,944 tsubo, about 10,000 to 69,000 sq ft, with a median of 462 tsubo, about 16,400 sq ft - the area of a square some 128 ft on a side. These are the temples of a rural district's villages; that a county town's temple was not smaller is this page's inference.
  • How wide was a temple's main hall?
    The register's main halls run from 4 to 10 and a half ken across the front, about 24 to 63 ft, with a median of 7 ken, about 42 ft.
  • When were the register's figures taken?
    The prefecture made the register in 1963, and its entries, with their land classes and values in yen, read as the Meiji era's - this page's inference - so they may not be an Edo precinct. Why a register can understate or overstate one is at Village shrines: where they stand, and how big their grounds are.
  • A Zen temple's main hall of 1697, in Aichi, is about 54 by 47 ft.
    The main hall of Eijuji, a Sōtō temple, wider than the register's median, is 6 ken by 7 ken, 16.57 m (~54 ft) by 14.29 m (~47 ft), and was built in 1697.

Beside its main hall and its kuri, the register lists what else each temple held.

  • A temple might keep a bell tower, a storehouse and several gates.
    The register lists a bell tower 1 ken square and an earthen storehouse at one temple, and at the largest a treasure house, a main gate, a middle gate, an outer gate and a side gate.
  • The Aichi temple sets its gate at the south end of the precinct's axis.
    Its site is long from north to south.
  • A temple's graveyard was not always inside its precinct.
    One temple's register entry lists its graveyard, 4 se 10 bu, about 130 tsubo (~4,600 sq ft), among the land it held outside the precinct, at another named place in its village.
  • No page we read says whether a temple's precinct was walled, fenced or hedged.
    The register lists gates and no boundary, and a register of buildings need not list one.

The household lived inside the precinct.

  • A temple's head priest and family lived in its kuri.
    In the Hiki register a temple's dwelling is its kuri, which the transcription's editors gloss as the building where the head priest and his family live. Even a village temple there could keep a monks' dormitory, glossed as the lodging of monks in training.
  • The largest precinct of the nineteen lists two dormitories.
    They are a front dormitory and a back one.
  • A Sōtō training temple set its dwelling on one side of its forecourt and its dormitory on the other.
    Eijuji, in Aichi, sets its kuri and study on the east side of its forecourt and its dormitory and meditation hall on the west. These were halls the Sōtō school required of its ranked temples that held the training retreat, and the page calls the layout the traditional one of the school's core temples.
  • No page we read says how many lived in a temple.
    Neither the register nor the training temple's page counts the priest, family, disciples and servants.

Where the temples stood in a town took two forms, a Japanese one and a Chinese one.

  • A castle town's temple quarter stood apart from its streets of shops.
    Amagasaki's lies in the town's northwest corner, north of the samurai quarter, and is thought to have been set apart from the townsmen's quarter to weaken the temples and to use their great buildings in the castle's defense. Miyazu's twelve lie in the town's southwest part, a quarter thought to have been planned with the castle town itself.
  • A Chinese county seat kept its temples inside its walls, placed by its plan.
    At Pingyao the Daoist temple stands in the east and the Buddhist temple in the west, symmetrical about the main street.

The register's figures were taken after 1868. For two of its temples, both in the village of Senjudō, an Edo record survives.

  • Was a temple's land that size before 1868?
    Roughly, by one Edo village's own record. The account of itself that Senjudō drafted in 1843 names its two temples, the Zen temple Senjuin and the Nichiren temple Kōshōji, and the land each held exempt from tax for a hall or holy place in its care: 2 tan 4 se 22 bu, partly paddy and mostly upland, for the Senju Kannon hall in Senjuin's care, and 2 tan 3 se 4 bu of upland for the place of the thirty guardian deities in Kōshōji's care. That is about 742 and 694 tsubo, some 26,400 and 24,700 sq ft. The register gives the same two temples precincts of 813 and 453 tsubo.
  • An exempt holding is not a precinct, but the two are of one order.
    A holding counts fields, and Senjuin's later precinct is larger than its holding, so it bounds an Edo precinct only roughly. Both temples surrendered their exempt land after the Restoration. The register's median of 462 tsubo lies below both Edo holdings and within the two temples' later range. No page we read measures an Edo temple's precinct as a precinct.
  • Were the main halls that size before 1868?
    The one main hall of known Edo date we read, the Aichi temple's of 1697, is inside the band of 24 to 63 ft. The register's halls are those standing after 1868, and not all are Edo buildings. Senjuin's halls burned in 1876 and were rebuilt, so its hall of 7 ken, the register's median width, is a Meiji one. Kōshōji's hall of 4 ken, the band's narrowest, is most likely the one rebuilt after a fire of the 1740s - this page's inference from the register's history.
  • The Kannon hall in Senjuin's care was 3 ken square, about 18 ft, in 1843.
    The village's record lists it apart from the temple.
