Did a village shrine's basin stand in the open before modern times? The basin did stand there; whether it had a roof is not known

Sources: kawasaki-nagao-chozubachi (a parish's dated basin), liga-temizuya (the rite old, the roofed pavilion new), yakyu-inari-temizuya (a dated pavilion over a dated basin), ubusuna-jinja-ameblo (shrines with no pavilion today)

The maps draw a plain stone basin beside the approach of a village shrine, with no pavilion over it (under "What else stood in a village shrine's precinct?"). This question asks whether that form is found before 1868.

What the research found. The basin is: the whole body of a shrine's parishioners gave it a basin in 1828. Its roof is another matter. The rite of rinsing is very old, but the roofed pavilion over a basin is much newer. The one pavilion found carved with a date before 1868 is Yakyu Inari Shrine's, in Saitama: a pavilion of 1836 over a basin of 1826, both given by a donor living in Edo, at a shrine whose following reached across the Kantō. A shrine with a basin and no pavilion is described only today: many shrines have none, a shrine writer observes, unstaffed shrines being especially many in the provinces. No page read places a village shrine's basin in the open, or under a roof, before 1868.

The decision (the GM's rule of 2026-09-28, that anything attested only in modern times is eliminated). The basin stays: a parish gave its shrine one before modern times (accurate). Its unroofed state is seen only today, and the one pavilion found carved with a date before 1868 stands at a shrine a whole region visited, so neither form is attested at a village shrine before modern times. Whether the maps keep the plain basin or draw a pavilion over it is for the GM; until they rule, the basin is drawn plain, its roof a guess.
