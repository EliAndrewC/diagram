Temples of the state cult: the City God and Confucius (chenghuang miao, wenmiao)

A Chinese seat of local government kept, beside its yamen, the places of the state cult: open-air altars to the gods of soil and grain, to wind, clouds, thunder, rain, mountains and rivers, and to the unquiet dead, and two temples. One was the City God's, the guardian of the walled seat, where the highest official of the place led the sacrifices at set times; the other was Confucius's, the wenmiao, built as one complex with the official school. The temples were large compounds: a prefecture's City God temple covers about an acre, and a county's Confucian temple and school together nearly three.

The setting our maps are drawn for has none of this. Its canon names no City God, no Confucian temple and no state altar; what it puts at every seat is the Ministry of Rites' own ladder of temples: at least one preceptor, serving in a town monastery, in every county town; at least one provincial abbot, in a subordinate temple, in every provincial city; at least two grand abbots in every domain capital.   • Every seat of local government had a City God temple.
    A study of the temple fair in north China under the Qing puts it plainly: wherever a local government office stood, there was without exception a City God temple.
  • The Ming founder ranked the City Gods at four levels.
    The first Ming emperor gave the City Gods titles at the capital, the prefecture, the department and the county, each rank with its own noble title and vestments.
  • Every county school was to have a Confucian temple, by a decree of 630.
    The Tang dynasty decreed a Confucian temple for the schools of every province and county, and the temples spread through China.
  • A Confucian temple and its official school were built as one complex.
    The temple stood in front of the school, or beside it on either hand.
  • Shanghai's Confucian temple and school run on two axes side by side.
    Rebuilt in 1855 inside the county seat's west gate, the complex puts the temple's gates, main hall and rear shrine on a western axis, and the school's gate, ceremonial gate, lecture hall and library pavilion on an eastern one.

Where the altars and the City God temple stood in a seat is recorded in the gazetteers of Yanzhou prefecture, in Shandong.

  • Where in a county seat did the state altars stand?
    Each toward its own side of the seat, several outside a gate. At Juye the altar of soil and grain stood outside the west gate, the altar of wind, clouds, thunder, rain, mountains and rivers outside the south gate, and the altar of the unquiet dead north of the seat. Yuncheng repeats the pattern, its altar of the unquiet dead outside the north gate. It is a tendency, not a rule: Shouzhang put its altar of the unquiet dead east of the seat, and Dong'e its altar of soil and grain to the southwest.
  • In Yanzhou's county seats, the City God temple stood beside the yamen.
    Northwest of it at Juye and west of it at Yuncheng, and west of it at Shouzhang.

The sizes below are each from one temple.

  • How big was a City God temple?
    About 4,700 m² (~50,600 sq ft) for the prefecture of Ningbo's, nearly all of it built over, about 4,200 m² (~45,200 sq ft). The county seat of Pingyao's covers over 7,300 m² (~78,600 sq ft), but as three temples on one site.
  • Ningbo's City God temple runs on one axis from a screen wall to a rear stage.
    South to north: screen wall, gate, ceremonial gate, stage, main hall and rear stage, with side halls and wing rooms to either hand.
  • How big was a Confucian temple's main hall?
    Some 260 to 374 m² (~2,800-4,000 sq ft). Shanghai county's covers about 260 m² (~2,800 sq ft), three bays wide and 14 m (~46 ft) tall. The hall of the prefectural school at Shaozhou is 374 m² (~4,000 sq ft) inside, five bays by three and 16.4 m (~54 ft) tall.
  • How much ground did a Confucian temple and its school take?
    Shanghai's, rebuilt on its present site, took 17.3 mu, about 11,500 m² (~124,000 sq ft) at the modern mu of 667 m² (~7,180 sq ft); the conversion is ours.
  • A prefectural school-temple held a run of gates, halls and shrines.
    The school at Shaozhou once held a stone balustrade, a ceremonial gateway, a semicircular pool, a second gate, a lecture hall, two side galleries, the main hall, a rear hall, a library pavilion and two shrines, one to famous officials and one to local worthies.
