Cremation grounds and bone mounds (kasoba)

A cremation ground (kasoba) was where the dead were burned before their bones were buried. Before modern times nearly every one was an open field with no building: the body was laid on stacked firewood in open country away from the houses, or, where a ground was used again and again, in a shallow stone-framed trench beside a burial ground. Cremation was the custom of some regions of Japan, not all: it spread among commoners with the Shin sect, and where it did, a settlement often built and ran its own ground.

Such grounds were small. The one size on record from before modern times is a city's: Edo's crematory at Yoyogi, some 180 ft square. Cremation leaves little to bury, so the bones of many unclaimed dead could lie under a single low mound, while a domain's founder might lie in a mausoleum of halls and gates of their own.

  • Cremation spread among commoners with the Shin sect.
    As the Shin sect spread, cremation became common among ordinary commoners, centered on its followers, and open-air cremation grounds were set up in many places.
  • A folklorist counts cremation as the custom of only five prefectures before it became universal after the Second World War.
    Masuda Katsumi, as a local encyclopedia page quotes him, confines it to the Shin areas of the three Hokuriku prefectures and to Hiroshima and Yamaguchi.
  • Settlements built and ran cremation grounds of their own.
    In regions of the Jodo sect, cremation grounds that commoners set up themselves, as the shared facility of a settlement or of a devotional society, stand out. When the Meiji ban on cremation was lifted in 1875, many towns and villages had only simple grounds attached to a temple or owned by a settlement or a person.
  • A hamlet that burned its own dead last used its ground in 1977.
    The hamlet called its cremation ground the sanmai, and a memorial it raised in 2016 records its last use in 1977; the hamlets around it burned their dead with their own hands until some 40 or 50 years before 2017.

What was burned on, and how much ground it took, is recorded for the Edo period and for Japan's early modern decades.

  • Nearly every cremation ground was open-air until the end of the Edo period.
    Burning in the open, with no building, furnace or other permanent equipment, was called field burning, and until the end of the Edo period the great majority of cremation grounds were field-burning grounds.
  • The fire bed was a stack of firewood, or a stone-framed trench a little larger than a coffin.
    In open country away from where people lived, firewood was stacked and the body laid on it. Beside a burial ground, a permanent fire bed was a shallow trench about one size larger than a coffin, framed with stone or earthenware.
  • Around the middle of the Edo period some grounds roofed their fire bed.
    One form set a simple roof on four posts standing several shaku from the bed on leveled ground; in snow country, a hut of about four to six tatami, with a tiled roof on walls of stacked cut stone, held a channeled fire bed dug at its center. The source gives these forms a period, not a year.
  • The early permanent grounds in the countryside were plain.
    Outside the cities, the first cremation grounds with a permanent hearth had only a simple hearth burning wood or straw, or a hearth attached to the settlement's graveyard.
  • Edo's crematory at Yoyogi covered 900 tsubo, the one size on record from before modern times.
    A shogunate gazetteer, as a crematory company's history quotes it, gives the ground 900 tsubo, some 180 ft square, with three houses of the men who worked it.
  • The recorded sizes of village grounds are all modern.
    Three sites in the Tama district west of Tokyo are recorded with a size: a village cremation ground of 10.5 tsubo (34.65 m², ~373 sq ft) built in 1922, a three-village union's ground of 42 tsubo set up in 1947, and Hachioji's of 600 tsubo (1,980 m², ~21,313 sq ft), about 145 ft square, set up in 1910, probably as a private facility, and taken over by the city in 1920. No page we read gives a village's or a town's ground a size before 1868.

Where a village's cremation ground stood is recorded in two ways: with its graves, or apart from the houses.

  • Some cremation grounds were kept together with the graves.
    A Fukui town history reads the word sanmai - the hall at a graveyard, or the graveyard itself - as showing that relatives and fellow parishioners once kept their burial and cremation place together with the graves. The permanent fire bed lay beside a burial ground, and the early rural hearths were often attached to the settlement's graveyard.
  • Where did a ground kept apart from the houses lie?
    The places recorded are burial grounds, not cremation grounds. In one Wakasa hamlet the sanmai where the dead were buried stood at the hamlet's edge, while the graves the living visited stood in its temples. In the two-grave villages of Wakasa the burial ground is often called the sanmai, and in a modern survey of 22 two-grave villages, 11 of them in Wakasa and the rest in the Shiwaku islands and Shima, it lay on average about 370 m (~1,214 ft) from the middle of the houses. No page we read puts a cremation ground on its own at a settlement's edge, or says how far from the houses one lay. On which side of the houses a burial ground lay is at Village burial grounds (bochi).
  • Did six stone jizo stand at a cremation ground?
    Only one undated community page says so: a Takamatsu settlement's, which says the word sanmai meant the place of a cremation ground and that six jizo were always set at one. The six jizo at a graveyard's entrance, attested before modern times, are at Village burial grounds (bochi).
  • The dead were burned away from the houses.
    The pyre stood in open country away from where people lived. No page we read gives a distance between a cremation ground and houses before modern times.
  • Japan's first set distance from houses came with the modern burial rules of 1884.
    The model standard issued with the burial regulations of 1884 put a cremation ground at least 120 ken, about 216 m (~709 ft), from dwellings and from crowded places, on ground that was not upwind, walled, with a chimney and a device against the foul smoke. Edo's largest cremation ground, at Kozukappara, was closed in 1887 because it failed the same 120-ken clause in that year's revised regulations.

In Edo the cremation grounds began inside the city, in its temples.

  • By about 1650 almost every temple in Edo's Asakusa and Shitaya had a cremation ground in its precinct.
    Edo also burned its dead in the precincts of the sect temples or at cremation temples (kaso-dera) tied to them, and eighteen such cremation grounds clustered at Senju.
  • In 1667 Edo moved its temple cremation grounds out, for their smoke.
    The smoke of the grounds scattered through Asakusa and Shitaya reached the shogun on a visit to a grave, and some twenty-odd were moved together to ground by the Kozukappara execution ground, which stayed Edo's largest crematory for 220 years.
  • In 1873, early in the modern era, Tokyo's police proposed moving the crematories for their stench.
    Their proposal named the cremation temples of Senju and two temples' grounds, and gave as its reason that the smoke spread in all directions and the severe stench injured people's health. Neither recorded removal was made for fear of fire.
  • How far can a pyre's fire reach?
    Only present-day figures were found. A present-day fire code, the IFC, keeps a bonfire 50 ft from a structure or anything that burns. In wildfires, only a burning firebrand starts a new fire more than 60 m (~197 ft) from the main front, and brands carried that far are lofted by the convection column of a large fire. No rule before modern times sets a distance between a pyre and anything that burns.

Cremation leaves little to bury, and what it leaves could be gathered.

  • An adult's cremated remains weigh about 2.4 kg (~5 lb).
    That is roughly 3.5% of the body's mass, and cremation makes very economical use of cemetery space.
  • At a Shinagawa temple in 1708, the bones of those who died in the local jail were gathered under a mound.
    The dead of the holding jail had been buried at the temple, more than seven hundred between 1691 and 1765; in 1708 a local man had their bones gathered, a burial mound raised over them and a Kannon image set on top. Stupas for the unconnected dead were raised at the same temple in 1865 and 1873. How large a city's ground for the unclaimed dead was is at How large is a pauper burial ground, and how many does a seat keep?
  • How large was a mound over the dead?
    No page we read gives a pauper bone mound a size. The one mound measured is a monument: Kyoto's ear mound is 30 ken across (about 55 m (~180 ft) at 1.82 m (~6 ft) to the ken; the page's own conversion says 49 m (~161 ft)) and 4 ken (about 7.2 m (~24 ft)) high, under a 5-ken stone pagoda, by a regional encyclopedia site that names no source for the figures.
  • No page we read describes a mound at Edo's great execution ground.
    More than two hundred thousand were put to death at Kozukappara, on a plot about 108 m (~354 ft) across the front and a little over 54 m (~177 ft) deep, yet the fullest account of the ground describes no mound of bones.

A ruling clan's dead lay very differently: in a mausoleum, a precinct of halls and gates.

  • Sendai's Zuihoden, the mausoleum of the domain's founder, faces the castle across the river.
    It is the mausoleum of Date Masamune, founder of the Sendai domain, and stands on a hill facing the site of the castle's honmaru across a bend of the Hirose River. It was made up of a main hall, a worship hall, a Chinese gate, an offering hall and a further gate.
