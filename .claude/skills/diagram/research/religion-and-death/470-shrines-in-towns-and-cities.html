Shrines in towns and cities

Above the village shrine (sonsha) stood the district shrine, and in a district's seat such as the town of Sano the district shrine was the town's own; Edo kept a principal shrine for the whole city. Rules of 1871, made as Japan began to modernize, fixed about one district shrine for each household-register district, in the rule's own example some twenty villages. The one whose buildings we found kept halls of a village shrine's size; what set it apart was its gate, its two stone torii and its many lesser shrines. Smaller shrines stood in a town's wards and among its temples.

In China the counterpart was the City God temple, kept at every seat of government under the Qing.

  • In 1871, after Japan began to modernize, a new rule fixed about one district shrine (gosha) for each household-register district.
    Say twenty villages of about a thousand households, with five shrines among them: the chief of the five became the district shrine, and the other four its village shrines.
  • Under the Meiji government's rules of 1897, a shrine needed at least 500 tsubo of ground and 1,000 parishioner households to be raised to a district shrine.
    That is about 17,800 sq ft, against 300 tsubo (~10,700 sq ft) for a village shrine and 600 (~21,300 sq ft) for a prefectural one, and the households against 200 and 2,000. These are floors for promotion, not averages.
  • How big was a real district shrine's ground?
    One in Hiki district, Saitama, raised to that rank in 1927, has a precinct of 3,373 tsubo, about 120,000 sq ft; the village shrines in the same register run to a median of 403 tsubo (Village shrines).
  • Were a town shrine's halls larger than a village shrine's?
    Not in the one town whose shrine's buildings we found. Sano, the seat of its district's office, whose two ranked shrines counted over 7,600 parishioners, kept the district shrine Asahimori. A gazetteer of 1902 gives its sanctuary as 1 and a half ken by 2 and a half, about 9 by 15 ft, and its worship hall as 3 and a half ken by 3, about 21 by 18 ft. A village shrine's sanctuary runs most often to 1 or 3 ken (Inside a village shrine's precinct).
  • What set Sano's district shrine apart from a village shrine?
    What stood around its halls: the same gazetteer gives it a Chinese-style gate, two stone torii and ten lesser shrines in its ground.

A town also held smaller shrines, in its wards and among its temples.

    Suwa shrine's hall, rebuilt at the end of the Meiji era, joins its worship hall, offering hall and sanctuary in one composite building. The quarter's temples are at How many temples a city keeps, and its temple quarter (teramachi)
  • The other shrine of Kanazawa's temple quarter took a slice of a temple's precinct for its ground.
    It once stood inside the temple, was made independent when shrines and temples were separated at the start of the Meiji era, and after a fire in 1871 took the west side of that temple's precinct for its ground the next year.
  • How much ground did a small shrine in a town keep?
    In the town of Kanzaki a shrine in one of its wards once stood on a lot of about 100 tsubo (some 3,600 sq ft), lost that ground to river works, and now stands on 36 m² (~386 sq ft). The district's small shrines stand today on some 250 to 525 m² (~2,600-5,700 sq ft), and a register of 1459 gives one 2 se 11 bu, some 71 tsubo. Most of these, in this project's reading, are a hamlet's shrines rather than a town's.
  • How big were a small shrine's halls?
    Among the small shrines of the town of Kanzaki, a sanctuary 1 ken square and a worship hall 3 by 2 ken (some 18 by 12 ft), or a single hall of 1 tsubo.

A great city kept one shrine for the whole town.

  • Edo kept one principal shrine for the whole town (sochinju), the Sanno shrine.
    It was the peak of Edo's shrines. It was enshrined in 1478, when Edo castle was built, on the hill of the old Edo residence.
  • Edo's principal shrine kept to raised ground as it moved.
    It was moved outward as the castle grew, and each new site kept the slightly raised ground of the one before.
  • Did Edo's shrines stand on high ground?
    Many did at first, and they drifted over time from high ground - plateau tops, their edges, hilltops - toward flat ground in the town and along its back streets, though many stood on flat ground from the first.

A province, too, kept a shrine of its own near its capital.

  • A province kept a shrine near its capital that gathered all the province's deities (soja).
    Its rites for the province's deities were performed there all at once.
  • Bitchu province's soja stands in the center of the city that grew up around it.
    The city grew from the shrine's gate town and a post town.

In China the principal shrine of a seat of government was its City God temple (chenghuangmiao).

  • Under the Qing, every seat of local government had a City God temple.
    Wherever a local government office was set up, there was one.
  • How big was a county seat's City God temple?
    They ranged widely. Licheng's is one courtyard, 36.23 m (~119 ft) by 52.65 m (~173 ft), 1,892 m² (~20,400 sq ft), with a gate and a main hall on its axis. Pingyao's, three temples on one site, covers over 7,300 m² (~78,600 sq ft). Sanyuan's, with three gates, four archways, five towers and six courtyards on its axis, covers 13,390 m² (~144,100 sq ft).
  • Did a higher-ranking city keep a larger City God temple?
    Not by rule. The prefectural city of Zhangde's covers 6,773 m² (~72,900 sq ft), less than the City God temples of two county seats, Pingyao and Sanyuan.
  • At Pingyao the City God temple stands in the east of the city, the county office in the west.
    The two stand on either side of South Street, the city's central axis.
