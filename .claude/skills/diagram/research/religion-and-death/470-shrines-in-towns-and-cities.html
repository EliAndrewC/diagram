Shrines in towns and cities

Above the village shrine, a Japanese town kept a larger shrine for its district, and a great city a principal shrine for the whole town. The rules of 1871 fixed about one district shrine for each household-register district of some twenty villages. The one whose buildings are recorded kept halls of a village shrine's size; what set it apart was its ground, its gate and its many lesser shrines. Smaller shrines stood in a town's wards and temple quarters, on a few thousand square feet.

In China the counterpart was the City God temple, kept at every seat of government under the Qing. The village shrine's own ground and halls are at Village shrines: where they stand, and how big their grounds are and Inside a village shrine's precinct.

  • In 1871, after Japan began to modernize, a new rule fixed about one district shrine (gosha) for each household-register district.
    Say twenty villages of about a thousand households, with five shrines among them: the chief of the five became the district shrine, and the other four its village shrines.
  • Rules of 1897 set a district shrine's ground at 500 tsubo or more.
    That is about 17,800 sq ft, against 300 tsubo (~10,700 sq ft) for a village shrine and 600 (~21,300 sq ft) for a prefectural one, with parishioners of at least 1,000 households, against 200 and 2,000. These are floors for promotion, not averages.
  • How big was a real district shrine's ground?
    One in Hiki district, Saitama, raised to that rank in 1927, has a precinct of 3,373 tsubo, about 120,000 sq ft; the village shrines in the same register run to a median of 403 tsubo (Village shrines).
  • Were a town shrine's halls larger than a village shrine's?
    Not in the one town whose shrine's buildings we found. Sano, the seat of its district's office, whose two ranked shrines counted over 7,600 parishioners, kept the district shrine Asahimori. A gazetteer of 1902 gives its sanctuary as 1 and a half ken by 2 and a half, about 9 by 15 ft, and its worship hall as 3 and a half ken by 3, about 21 by 18 ft, with a Chinese-style gate, two stone torii and ten lesser shrines in its ground. A village shrine's sanctuary runs most often to 1 or 3 ken (Inside a village shrine's precinct).

A town also held smaller shrines, in its wards and among its temples.

  • In Kanazawa's temple quarter one shrine stands on a lot of its own with a joined hall, a shrine office and a small Inari shrine.
    Suwa shrine's hall, rebuilt at the end of the Meiji era, joins its worship hall, offering hall and sanctuary under one roof. The quarter's temples are at How big is a temple in a temple quarter?
  • Another shrine of the quarter took a slice of a temple's precinct.
    It once stood inside the temple, was made independent when shrines and temples were separated at the start of the Meiji era, and after a fire in 1871 took the west side of that temple's precinct for its ground the next year.
  • How much ground did a small shrine in a town keep?
    In the town of Kanzaki a shrine in one of its wards once held about 100 tsubo (some 3,600 sq ft) and has lost all but 36 m² (~386 sq ft) to river works. The district's small shrines stand today on some 250 to 525 m² (~2,600-5,700 sq ft), and a register of 1459 gives one 2 se 11 bu, some 71 tsubo. Most of these, in this project's reading, are a hamlet's shrines rather than a town's.
  • How big were a small shrine's halls?
    Among the Kanzaki shrines, a sanctuary 1 ken square before a worship hall 3 by 2 ken (some 18 by 12 ft), or a single hall of 1 tsubo.
  • How much ground did a ward's shrine in a castle town keep before 1868?
    No page we read gives it, nor the size of a small shrine in a Chinese city's lanes.

A great city kept one shrine for the whole town.

  • Edo kept one principal shrine for the whole town (sochinju), the Sanno shrine.
    It was the peak of Edo's shrines. It was enshrined in 1478 on the hill where Edo castle was built.
  • Edo's principal shrine kept to raised ground as it moved.
    It was moved outward as the castle grew, and each new site kept the slightly raised ground of the one before.
  • Did Edo's shrines stand on high ground?
    Many did at first, and they drifted over time from high ground - plateau tops, their edges, hilltops - toward flat ground in the town and along its back streets, though many stood on flat ground from the first.
  • A province kept a shrine near its capital that gathered all the province's deities (soja).
    Its rites for the province's deities were performed there all at once.
  • Bitchu province's soja stands in the center of the city that grew up around it.
    The city grew from the shrine's gate town and a post town.

In China the principal shrine of a seat of government was its City God temple (chenghuangmiao).

  • Under the Qing, every seat of local government had a City God temple.
    Wherever a local government office was set up, there was one.
  • How big was a county seat's City God temple?
    They ranged widely. Licheng's is one courtyard, 36.23 m (~119 ft) by 52.65 m (~173 ft), 1,892 m² (~20,400 sq ft), with a gate and a main hall on its axis. Pingyao's, three temples on one site, covers over 7,300 m² (~78,600 sq ft). Sanyuan's, with three gates, four archways, five towers and six courtyards on its axis, covers 13,390 m² (~144,100 sq ft).
  • Did a higher-ranking city keep a larger City God temple?
    Not by rule. The prefectural city of Zhangde's covers 6,773 m² (~72,900 sq ft), less than two of the county seats' above.
  • At Pingyao the City God temple stands in the east of the city, the county office in the west.
    The two face each other across the main street, the city's central axis.
