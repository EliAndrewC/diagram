How big is a country shrine, and what stands in its precinct? Larger than a farmhouse, because it contains one

Sources: nagarezukuri-jawiki and nagare-zukuri-enwiki (the one-bay sanctuary), ehime-pref-honden-56 (a village honden and haiden, measured), saitama-kannonji-kannondo, nara-byakugoji-hondo and chikusei-saishoji-yakushido (measured halls), homemate-shichido-garan (the minimal temple), kawasaki-chonenji-kuri, kawasaki-chonenji-hondo, sakai-kaieji, ohtawara-daioji and bunka-tokuunji-kuri (the priest's dwelling, and the one under the hall's roof), xietang-tudimiao-zhwiki and gantang-temple-thepaper (China), jinja-jawiki and jaanus-shamusho and jaanus-haiden (the parts of a precinct, and their axis), jochi-jawiki, shuinchi-jawiki, danka-seido-jawiki, lijia-zhwiki and mianning-village-temples (the land, the income and the records), gltjp-zenkokuji and darumamuseum-bishamonten (the dedication's furniture)

What the research found - the buildings. The sanctuary is tiny. The flowing-roof honden "is the most common form of shrine honden in the whole country",162 it is a one-bay building "if the bay spacing along the ridge (the front) is one ken",163 and "the most common sizes are 1 and 3 ken";164 the honden of a rural Hachiman shrine in the Ehime highlands, built 1789, listed as a cultural property and therefore measured, comes to "1.98 m along the ridge and 1.82 m across the beams",165166 about 6 ft square. The villagers' hall is the precinct's largest building, and on measured village examples it runs from about 20 ft square to 35 ft: the same shrine's worship hall, "built in Kansei 2 (1790)",160 is "9.78 m along the ridge and 10.62 m across the beams",167 a thatched three-bay Kannon hall of 1778 in Saitama is "6.540 meters" on each side, "three bays square",168169 and a Nara temple's main hall of the "early 17th century"161 "5 ken" by "5 ken";170 the smallest hall read, a subsidiary hall rebuilt in "Jōkyō 2 (1685)" part-way along a temple's approach,199 is "3.83 m" square.171 A minimal temple had no more than that: "small temples with only a 'main hall' and a 'bell tower' are by far the most common",172 which is why the bell is a knob and the office building is not on the sheet at all.

The dwelling, and the one roof. Every designated kuri that could be read is a rich temple's - "9 ken along the ridge and 5 ken across the beams",173 with "the head priest's living room ... a twelve-mat room",174 beside a hall of "7 ken" by "7 ken";175 the largest, "12 ken along the ridge by 8 ken",179 says of its own form that it resembles "the honmune-zukuri farmhouse characteristic of this region".180 No small kuri was measured,193 so the map draws the monk's dwelling at the farmhouse's own footprint, 46 by 28 ft: accurate in form, a guess in size. The form the GM asked for - the hall that is also the home - is attested once205 and called rare: at Kaie-ji, a Zen temple in the port city of Sakai, the "main hall and kuri" are "one building: ridge span 25.2 meters, beam span 8.0 meters on the left side, 13.0 meters on the right side",176 and "a main hall and kuri in one building is unusual for temple architecture of the beginning of the Edo period".177 That is 83 ft long by 26 to 43 ft deep, some 200 to 330 m²; the hall of a temple "protected as the family temple of the Ōzeki clan, who were the domain's lords",200 for contrast, is "25.5 meters" by "16.3 meters".178 China's earth-god temples span the same range from a hut to a hall: a Song foundation at Xietang keeps a surviving main hall "three bays wide and one bay deep, 10 meters long and 7 meters wide" - large only against the few-square-meter shrines above, a comparison the page itself does not make -181 and one Zhejiang village temple's compound - its villagers "began gathering to plan the temple's reconstruction" in 1999 and the new hall "was finally completed in 2012"201 - "covers about 2.5 mu",182 the only precinct area any readable source gives; no Japanese one could be found.192

What stands in the precinct. "Beside the approach stand the temizuya, for 'purifying the body'",183 and the arch marks the sacred ground; at a small shrine no office, its "business ... frequently carried on at the private home of the administrator";184 the grove around it and the burial ground beside it are the village rule already recorded on this page. The plot the monk lives on is exempt land, jochi - "the precincts of temples and shrines and fields and homesteads holding a document of tax exemption"185 - and the sealed grants, the shuinchi, ran down to "a mere 1 koku";186 the income is the parish's, each household paying "fuse" to the temple it "entrusts all of its funerary and memorial rites to".187 In China the temple's land came "partly bought and partly donated, to serve as a permanent resource for the deity's incense and lamps",191 while the population was the lijia's business, "allocating corvée labor and collecting tax by head-count",190 and nothing read puts a village temple in the registration business; whether Ming or Qing temple land was exempt could not be read.195 The three-bay village hall that search summaries repeat as the standard could not be found on a page and is not used.194

What the dedication adds. What a Fortune puts at the entrance of its shrine was read for one of them, Bishamon. There the guardian pair is tigers: the deity "is flanked by two stone tigers at the temple entrance. Unlike the more common guardian lion-dogs (komainu), these tiger statues are a rare sight"206 - rare against the lion-dogs of other shrines, not at his own - and the general form is the same, "Stone tigers like Komainu in front of Bishamon temples".207 The tiger goes on to the votive plaques and charms,209 which no sheet draws. One page describes a named city temple and the other states the form without a village example; nothing readable describes the precinct of a village shrine of Bishamon.208 So on the sheet the dedication is named at the sanctuary and, where the wealth knob draws guardian figures at all, a Bishamon shrine's are tigers in place of lion-dogs - the convention attested at named temples, its reach down to a village a guess.

So: is it bigger than a farmhouse? Yes, because it contains one. The hall alone is farmhouse-sized or smaller; the monk's dwelling IS a farmhouse in form; under one roof the two together make a building of 200 to 330 m², two to three farmhouses, and on the sheet the sanctuary, the arch, the well, the garden and the privy stand around it inside the fence, with the grove and the graves outside it.

The rule the sheet follows: the sanctuary about 6 ft square, one bay, at the back of the precinct on the approach axis - the worship hall "usually placed on the same axis as the offering hall ... and the main sanctuary"202 (accurate); the villagers' hall 18 to 38 ft on a side (accurate as a band from four measured halls), and under one roof with the dwelling a building of 2,100 to 3,600 sq ft (accurate as a band from one attested example - a city temple's, not a village's, and the only one - disclosed as such); the dwelling, where it stands apart, at the farmhouse's 46 by 28 ft (form accurate, size a guess); an arch over the approach where it crosses the fence, a well or basin beside the approach, a fence (the tamagaki among a shrine's listed fittings204) or hedge and never a wall - a convention that keeps a shrine's boundary from reading as a compound's -, the grove around and the burial ground beside, a kitchen garden and a privy at the dwelling, fire-water at the wooden buildings; a bell tower only where the knob says so; no office building. The precinct's own size is a guess bounded by what it must contain.
