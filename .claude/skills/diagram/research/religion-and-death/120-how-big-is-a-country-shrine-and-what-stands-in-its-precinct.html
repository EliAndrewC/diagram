How big is a country shrine, and what stands in its precinct? Larger than a farmhouse, because it contains one

Sources: nagarezukuri-jawiki, nagare-zukuri-enwiki and kotobank-nagarezukuri (the one-bay sanctuary, and how common its style is), ehime-pref-honden-56 (a village honden and haiden, measured), saitama-kannonji-kannondo, nara-byakugoji-hondo and chikusei-saishoji-yakushido (measured halls), homemate-shichido-garan (the minimal temple), jaanus-haiden (the axis of the halls)

What the research found - the buildings. The sanctuary is tiny. The flowing-roof honden "is the most common form of shrine honden in the whole country", more than half of the sanctuaries designated as national treasures or important cultural properties, it is a one-bay building "if the bay spacing along the ridge (the front) is one ken", and "the most common sizes are 1 and 3 ken"; the honden of a rural Hachiman shrine in the Ehime highlands, built 1789, listed as a cultural property and therefore measured, comes to "1.98 m along the ridge and 1.82 m across the beams", about 6 ft square. The villagers' hall is the precinct's largest building, and on measured village examples it runs from about 20 ft square to 35 ft: the same shrine's worship hall, "built in Kansei 2 (1790)", is "9.78 m along the ridge and 10.62 m across the beams", a thatched three-bay Kannon hall of 1778 in Saitama is "6.540 meters" on each side, "three bays square", and a Nara temple's main hall of the "early 17th century" "5 ken" by "5 ken"; the smallest hall read, a subsidiary hall rebuilt in "Jōkyō 2 (1685)" part-way along a temple's approach, is "3.83 m" square. A minimal temple had no more than that: "small temples with only a 'main hall' and a 'bell tower' are by far the most common", which is why the bell is a knob and the office building is not on the sheet at all; whether a village's shrine-temple had its bell, no page says. The three-bay village hall that search summaries repeat as the standard could not be found on a page and is not used.

The dwelling, and the one roof. The monk's dwelling is a farmhouse in form, drawn at the farmhouse's 46 by 28 ft, and the hall and the dwelling under one roof are attested once, at Kaie-ji in Sakai, 83 ft long by 26 to 43 ft deep, some 200 to 330 m² - the evidence under "How big was the country monk's dwelling, and did it share the hall's roof?".

The precinct around it - its fence, its area, what else stands in it and whose land it is - is under the four questions after this one.

So: is it bigger than a farmhouse? Yes, because it contains one. The hall alone is farmhouse-sized or smaller; the monk's dwelling IS a farmhouse in form; under one roof the two together make a building of 200 to 330 m², two to three farmhouses.

The rule the sheet follows: the sanctuary about 6 ft square, one bay, at the back of the precinct on the approach axis - the worship hall "usually placed on the same axis as the offering hall ... and the main sanctuary" (accurate); the villagers' hall about 20 to 35 ft on a side (accurate as a band: the Saitama hall's 6.54 m sets the small end, the Ehime worship hall's 10.62 m the large; the 3.83 m subsidiary hall is not a villagers' hall), and under one roof with the dwelling a building of 2,100 to 3,600 sq ft (accurate as a band from one attested example - a city temple's, not a village's, and the only one - disclosed as such); the dwelling, where it stands apart, at the farmhouse's 46 by 28 ft (form accurate, size a guess); the arches over the approach, the first where it enters the shrine's ground (their spacing under "Torii spacing"); no fence, wall or hedge around the precinct (under "Was a village shrine walled or fenced?"); the precinct itself the grove, its area inside the register band (under "How large was a village shrine's precinct, and how much of it was built on?"); the sacred tree, the basin beside the approach and the wealth knob's gifts (under "What else stood in a village shrine's precinct?"); the burial ground beside, a kitchen garden and a privy at the dwelling, fire-water at the wooden buildings; a bell tower only where the knob says so; no office building.
