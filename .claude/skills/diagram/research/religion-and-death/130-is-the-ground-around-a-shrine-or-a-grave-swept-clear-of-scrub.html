Is the ground around a shrine or a grave swept clear of scrub?

Sources: cssn-citang-panyu (the pool and plaza before a Cantonese ancestral hall), weilongwu-zhwiki (the drying floor and the fengshui pond before a Hakka hall), sando-jawiki (the purifying gravel of the approach), chinju-no-mori-jawiki (the kept grove around a shrine), kotobank-ujiko (who cleaned a village shrine); the GM's question of 2026-07-19

What the research found. That the ground immediately around a sacred or a funerary feature was kept clear of wild scrub while the waste around it stayed scrubby, a tended verge, is this page's reading; no page read says so. That a shrine's keidai, its precinct, was a swept raked-gravel or flagged surface is general reading; no page giving the precinct's surface was found. Who kept it is attested, in the form folklore records in modern Japan: a village shrine's own households chose representatives, sometimes in rotation for a fixed term, who answered for its cleaning, the care of its priest and its festival. No page read gives that cleaning, or a swept ground around a shrine's hall or its arches (its torii gates), before modern times. The approach is attested as well. In the Chinese case a Cantonese ancestral hall fronts a body of water and an open plaza, where the clansmen assemble and hold their ceremonies, an arrangement read both as fengshui and as drainage and fire protection. Grain drying is the first use the record gives that ground: in front of a Hakka hall-and-wing house lies a rectangular drying floor, the heping, for drying grain and other crops and for the gatherings of the new year, the festivals, the weddings and the funerals, with the half-moon fengshui pond before it holding water against drought and for putting out fire. The market and the opera on that plaza, and the same arrangement before a village earth-god shrine, remain this project's own extensions and stand on no page read. On the Japanese side the tamajari gravel laid along the sando, the approach, is there to purify the ground it covers - the custom behind a clear approach - while the claim that the path and the ground under each arch were swept the whole length of it is again general reading. Graves are the other case, and they are under "Were graves weeded, and the ground around them kept clear, before modern times?": the grave itself was weeded before modern times, and a cleared band around it rests only on modern custom.

The one nuance that shaped the design. A Japanese shrine's setting is a deliberately PRESERVED grove (chinju no mori) - a forest kept and maintained around the sanctuary, the approach and the place of worship, so that the precinct sits inside kept trees rather than in cleared ground. So nothing drawn clears the grove, which is a drawn feature in its own right: a shrine's back grove stands untouched right up to the precinct, which is this page's reading of the preserved grove above rather than a measured fact.

The decision (the GM's rule of 2026-09-28, that anything attested only in modern times is eliminated). The swept ground around a shrine's hall and its arches rests on general reading and on custom recorded in modern times with no date, so the maps do not draw a swept collar there; the precinct is marked by its grove, its approach and its arch.

The rule the map follows: no swept collar is drawn around a shrine's hall or its arches ; loose scatter (the scrub of the commons, the reeds of a marsh) keeps off only the buildings' and the approach's own ground, and the grove, the buildings and every placement rule are unchanged. The ground around a grave plot is under "Were graves weeded, and the ground around them kept clear, before modern times?".
