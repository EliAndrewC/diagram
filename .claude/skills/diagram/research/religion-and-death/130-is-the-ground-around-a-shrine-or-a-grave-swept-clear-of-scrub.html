Is the ground around a shrine or a grave swept clear of scrub?

Sources: cssn-citang-panyu (the pool and plaza before a Cantonese ancestral hall), weilongwu-zhwiki (the drying floor and the fengshui pond before a Hakka hall), sando-jawiki (the purifying gravel of the approach), qingming-enwiki (tomb-sweeping and the groundskeeping at a grave), ryobosei-jawiki (the burial place away from habitation), chinju-no-mori-jawiki (the kept grove around a shrine); the GM's question of 2026-07-19

What the research found. The ground immediately around a sacred or a funerary feature was kept clear of wild scrub while the waste around it stayed scrubby - a tended verge, not a big bald radius. That a shrine's keidai, its precinct, was a swept raked-gravel or flagged surface is general reading; no page giving the precinct's surface or its upkeep was found. What is attested is the approach. In the Chinese case a Cantonese ancestral hall fronts a body of water and an open plaza, where the clansmen assemble and hold their ceremonies, an arrangement read both as fengshui and as drainage and fire protection. Grain drying is the first use the record gives that ground: in front of a Hakka hall-and-wing house lies a rectangular drying floor, the heping, for drying grain and other crops and for the gatherings of the new year, the festivals, the weddings and the funerals, with the half-moon fengshui pond before it holding water against drought and for putting out fire. The market and the opera on that plaza, and the same arrangement before a village earth-god shrine, remain this project's own extensions and stand on no page read. On the Japanese side the tamajari gravel laid along the sando, the approach, is there to purify the ground it covers - the custom behind a clear approach - while the claim that the path and the ground under each arch were swept the whole length of it is again general reading. Graves are the partial case: a burial place is often in mountain forest away from human habitation, yet grave tending keeps the tomb itself clear - at Qingming the young and the old alike kneel before the ancestors' tombstones, burn incense and paper, sweep the tombs and offer food - so the weeds come off the mound and a modest collar while the waste a short way out stays scrubby. How wide that collar ran is in no source read: the figure below is this project's own. So: clear AT the graves, wild BETWEEN them.

The one nuance that shaped the design. A Japanese shrine's setting is a deliberately PRESERVED grove (chinju no mori) - a forest kept and maintained around the sanctuary, the approach and the place of worship, so that the swept precinct sits inside kept trees rather than in cleared ground. So the swept collar holds off only the loose scrub and reed scatter. It never holds off the grove, which is a drawn feature in its own right, and it never governs where a building may stand: a shrine's back grove stands untouched right up to the swept precinct, exactly as in reality.

The rule the map follows: on a village sheet (2 ft per pixel) a small swept collar reaches about 115 ft beyond the hall , about 60 ft around each arch and its footing, and about 60 ft around a grave plot . It is deliberately modest - "a little bit of clearance" - and the scrub commons is unchanged a stride out. Loose scatter (the scrub of the commons, the reeds of a marsh) keeps off that ground; the grove, the buildings and every placement rule ignore it.
