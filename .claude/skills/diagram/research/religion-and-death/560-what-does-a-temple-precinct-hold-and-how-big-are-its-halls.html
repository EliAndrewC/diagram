What does a temple precinct hold, and how big are its halls?

Sources: garan-jawiki (the seven halls, old and Zen), eiheiji-garan-mlit (a Zen head temple's seven halls and corridors), hondo-jawiki (the main hall), kuri-jawiki (the kuri at a large and an ordinary temple), jorokuji-hondo-bunka (a measured main hall), matsui-2014-edo-temples (an average city temple's precinct), chongming-fosi (a county seat's temple), zhihuasi-zhwiki (a walled city's temple and its axis), dajuesi-zhwiki (a temple's axis with its towers)

What the research found - the full plan. A Kamakura-period text counts a full Japanese temple as seven buildings: the golden hall, the pagoda, the lecture hall, the bell tower, the sutra house, the monks' quarters and the refectory; the Zen sects count instead the Buddha hall, the main gate, the monks' hall, the kuri, the dharma hall, the bathhouse and the privy. The name was used loosely, too, of any great temple with many buildings. Which buildings count differed by sect and era, and at Eiheiji the seven are joined by covered corridors and stairs.

What the research found - an ordinary temple. A temple's central building is its main hall, which houses its principal image. The kuri, where the monks live and often their kitchen too, is a building of its own at a large temple, but at an ordinary one it often doubles as the temple office. One measured early-Edo main hall, Jorokuji's in Tokushima, built in 1629, is 18.7 by 11.8 m (61 by 39 ft), one story under a hip-and-gable tiled roof. An Edo temple of about 1,000 tsubo (some 35,600 sq ft, a square about 190 ft on a side) is called one of average size.

What the research found - China. In the county seat of Chongming, the Hanshan temple, founded in the late Ming north of the east gate, had thirty-odd rooms with 700 m² of floor (some 7,500 sq ft) on a site of 3,300 m² (some 35,500 sq ft), about 5 mu - halls, a guest hall, dormitories, a kitchen, a refectory, a storeroom, a retreat room, an incense pavilion, a store for coffins and a gate. The two Chinese temples below set their buildings one behind another on a single axis. The Zhihua temple in Beijing runs from its gate through an inner gate, its main hall, a two-story pavilion and two more halls, a plan the page calls built after the seven-hall system; the temple as it stands now is 278.8 by 44.5 m (915 by 146 ft), about 12,400 m². The Dajue temple outside Beijing sets, from its gate up, stele pavilions, a pond with a bridge, a bell tower and a drum tower, the hall of the Heavenly Kings and the main hall, on a site of 6,000 m² in all. The ordinary Japanese temple and the Chinese county-seat temple, measured independently, come to the same ground, about 3,300 m².

What was not found. No readable page gives an ordinary temple's lecture hall, kuri or gate in feet, or says how many city temples kept a lecture hall or a cloister. Whether a graveyard stands in a temple's precinct is "Does a graveyard stand beside its temple or its shrine?"; its bell tower and pagoda are "Does a city temple keep its own bell tower and a pagoda?".

The decision it drove. An ordinary city or town temple is drawn at the size history gives it - accurate - and the full seven-hall plan belongs only to a great temple. The city's two great complexes are drawn at about 73,000 sq ft each, twice the ordinary precinct, which is the deliberate liberty "City temple size" records.

The rule the map follows: an ordinary temple precinct, in a county town or among a city's lesser temples, is about 35,000 sq ft, a square some 190 ft on a side; it holds a gate, a main hall of about 60 by 40 ft, a kuri that is also the temple office, and a bell tower. The lecture hall, the sutra house, the monks' hall and the corridors belong only to a great temple. The main hall's size is a guess at an ordinary temple from the one measured hall, which was built as its temple's abbot's quarters and later became its main hall.
