Wayside shrines and stone figures (dosojin, jizo)

Beside the roads of a Japanese village stood small stone figures of the deities who kept its thresholds. The dosojin, the deity of the way, was set at a village's entrance and exit, a mountain pass, a crossroads or the foot of a bridge, and the stone jizo, a Buddhist figure that merged with it, stands at the same places and in graveyards. The stones run from ones that sit on a palm to standing figures nearly 2 m (~7 ft) tall, and one city in Nagano counts more than 600 of them. In one region the settlements also built small open wayside halls beside their roads, about one to every 20 to 30 households.

Where a village's roads meet its houses is at Where does a village begin, and what marks it?, and the great old tree kept beside a boundary shrine at Did a village keep a great old tree at its entrance?

  • Most dosojin stand at a settlement's entrance.
    In Matsumoto, whose city holds more than 600 of the stones, most stand at a settlement's entrance, the village boundary.
  • Stones stood at a settlement's entrance before 1868.
    A paired-figure dosojin in Ueda, 49 cm (~19 in) high, set up at the entrance of a settlement on an old road running along an irrigation channel, carries the year 1695 cut into it, though the city's page calls a stone inscribed with a date of that era very rare in the prefecture.
  • In one Matsumoto district each of three settlements has its own dosojin.
    The three settlements of the Hanami district each have one.
  • At a settlement's entrance the stones can stand in a group.
    At one settlement in Matsumoto two dosojin stand in a row at the entrance, lined up with a koshin tower and a memorial tower for the nenbutsu, the chanted name of the Buddha.
  • A jizo could stand at a crossroads.
    One Fukuoka town's jizo stands at a crossroads, moved there from a household's grounds.
  • A small shrine could keep a well.
    A dragon-god shrine stands near a named well in northern Tohoku, still kept with a festival.
  • A Fukuoka village's jizo was held to have been angered when rubbish fouled the village's great well.
    The well was cleaned; the page does not say how near the well the figure stands.
  • How big were the stones?
    They run from a stone small enough to sit on a palm to a standing figure nearly 2 m (~7 ft) tall. The Ueda pair of 1695 is 49 cm (~19 in) high.

In one province the settlements built a hall as well as a stone.

  • In the Fukuyama domain the settlements built small open wayside halls (tsujido), about one for every 20 to 30 households.
    They stood along the roads between villages, at passes and in busy places, each kept by its settlement, and none was built far from habitation.
  • One district of Fukuyama had 17 wayside halls in 1700, in the Edo period.
    That is the Kumano-cho district, in the middle of the period.
  • The wayside halls were a regional form.
    A gazetteer of 1809 calls them rare in other provinces and common in the province of Bingo alone, some 600 of them in the Fukuyama domain.
  • How big was a wayside hall?
    Most of those standing today are four corner posts under a roof, with at most one wall and a board floor of about two mats, some 6 ft square. An account of 1862 puts such halls on Shodoshima at 2 to 3 ken, about 12 to 18 ft, on a side.

In towns, a street shrine did the work the village's stones did.

  • In the Kinki region, a town kept a jizo for each town block.
    Especially around Kyoto, a jizo image has been enshrined for each block, with a festival for it centered on children.
  • A block with no jizo of its own borrowed one from a temple for its festival.
    Or it used a Buddhist painting.
  • From the Kanto eastward, it is said, the dosojin did the street jizo's work.
    That is one explanation offered for why the jizo's festival did not spread to eastern Japan.
  • In Edo, Inari shrines were one of the three things met everywhere in the town.
    The saying ran "Iseya, Inari, and dog droppings".

China's counterpart of the village's guardian was the earth god, kept at an altar or a small temple.

  • The Ming statutes ordered one altar to the gods of soil and grain for every hundred rural households.
    So the article reporting them quotes the Wanli edition of the collected statutes.
  • How many earth-god temples did a Chinese village keep?
    A count from the gazetteers of the Yangzi delta found about one earth-god temple to every ten-odd villages on the low ground, and one to about 40 of the much smaller villages on the slightly higher ground to the east. The historian reporting it doubts the count as too low, since a gazetteer could not record every temple of its area.
