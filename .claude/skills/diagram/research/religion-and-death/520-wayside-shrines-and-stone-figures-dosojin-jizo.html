Wayside shrines and stone figures (dosojin, jizo)

Beside the roads of a Japanese village stood small stone figures of the deities who kept its thresholds. The dosojin, the deity of the way, was set at a village's entrance and exit, a mountain pass, a crossroads or the foot of a bridge, and the stone jizo, a Buddhist figure that merged with it, stands at the same places and in graveyards. The stones run from ones that sit on a palm to standing figures about as tall as a person, and one city in Nagano counts more than 600 of them. In one region the settlements also built small open wayside halls beside their roads, about one to every 20 to 30 households.

What marks where a village begins is covered in Where does a village begin, and what marks it?, and the great old tree kept beside a boundary shrine in Did a village keep a great old tree at its entrance?

  • In Matsumoto, most dosojin stand at a settlement's entrance.
    The city's own account of its stones puts most of them at a settlement's entrance, the village boundary.
  • A dosojin set up at a settlement's entrance in 1695, in the Edo period, survives in Ueda.
    It is a pair of figures, a male and a female god holding hands, set up at the entrance of a settlement on an old road along an irrigation channel, with the year cut into it. The city's page calls a dosojin inscribed with a date of that era extremely rare in the prefecture.
  • In one Matsumoto district each of three settlements has its own dosojin.
    The city's account names a dosojin in each of the upper, middle and lower settlements of the Hanami district.
  • At a settlement's entrance the stones can stand in a group.
    At one settlement in Matsumoto two dosojin stand in a row at the entrance, lined up with a koshin tower and a memorial tower for the nenbutsu, the chanted name of the Buddha.
  • A jizo could stand at a crossroads.
    One Fukuoka town's jizo stands at a crossroads, moved there from a household's grounds.
  • A small shrine could stand beside a well.
    A dragon-god shrine stands near a named well in northern Tohoku, still kept with a festival.
  • A Fukuoka village's jizo was tied to the village's great well.
    When rubbish thrown into the well turned its water purple, the jizo was taken to have been angered, and the well was cleaned. The page does not say how near the well the figure stands.
  • How big were the stones?
    They run from a stone small enough to sit on a palm to a standing figure nearly 2 m (~7 ft) tall. The Ueda pair of 1695 is 49 cm (~19 in) high.

In the Fukuyama domain, a lord's territory in the province of Bingo, the settlements built small open wayside halls (tsujido) as well as stones.

  • How many wayside halls did a Fukuyama settlement build?
    About one for every 20 to 30 households, each hall kept by its settlement.
  • The wayside halls stood beside the roads, near the houses.
    They stood along the roads between villages, at passes and in busy places, and, it is said, unlike shrines they were not built far from habitation.
  • One district of Fukuyama had 17 wayside halls in 1700, in the Edo period.
    The article on the domain's halls gives that count for the Kumano-cho district, in the middle of the period.
  • The wayside halls were a regional form.
    A gazetteer of 1809 calls them rare in other provinces and common in the province of Bingo alone, some 600 of them in the Fukuyama domain.
  • How big was a wayside hall?
    Most of those standing today are four corner posts under a roof, with at most one wall and a board floor of about two mats, some 6 ft square. An account of 1862 puts such halls on the island of Shodoshima, in the Inland Sea, at 2 to 3 ken, about 12 to 18 ft, on a side.

In towns, a street shrine did the work the village's stones did.

  • In the Kinki region, a town kept a jizo for each town block.
    Especially around Kyoto, a jizo image has been enshrined for each block, with a festival for it centered on children.
  • A block with no jizo of its own borrowed one from a temple for its festival.
    Failing that, the block used a Buddhist painting in its place.
  • Did the dosojin do the street jizo's work east of the Kanto?
    One explanation offered for why the jizo's festival never spread to eastern Japan is that from the Kanto eastward the dosojin filled the street jizo's role.
  • Inari shrines were so common in Edo that a saying counted them among the three things met everywhere in the town.
    The saying ran "Iseya, Inari, and dog droppings".

China's counterpart of the village's guardian was the earth god, kept at an altar or a small temple.

  • The Ming statutes ordered one altar to the gods of soil and grain for every hundred rural households.
    An article on the temples of the delta's market towns quotes the order from the Wanli edition of the Ming collected statutes.
  • How many earth-god temples did a Chinese village keep?
    A count from the gazetteers of the Yangzi delta found about one earth-god temple to every ten-odd villages on the low ground, and one to about 40 villages on the slightly higher ground to the east. The historian reporting it doubts the count as too low, since a gazetteer could not record every temple of its area.
