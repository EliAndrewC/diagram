A Fukui record of the funeral custom puts the sanmai, the cremation and burial place, at the edge of the hamlet while the visiting graves stand in two temples inside it, and the Japanese graveyard vocabulary keeps a category for exactly that ground: the graveyard a village settlement's own inhabitants hold in common and manage themselves. The form is the usual one - most often a graveyard held in common by each settlement, its ground sometimes divided into plots by household or by age and sometimes under no rule at all - and a national folklore survey counted the settlements keeping the two-grave custom prefecture by prefecture: 60 in Shiga, 59 in Nara, 45 in Mie, 42 in Kyoto, 39 each in Hyogo and Fukui, 22 in Shizuoka, few elsewhere. Those counts are of the two-grave custom rather than of village graveyards in general, they lean heavily to the Kinki region, and they count settlements confirmed at survey time in the twentieth century, so they are a floor rather than a rate. Whether a twelve- to fifteen-household hamlet kept one, and the grandfathering that leaves them standing today, are in no page read. The two-grave regions (ryobosei) formalize the split: the body goes to a burial place mostly of earth burial, often in mountain forest away from habitation, while the visiting grave - a stone pagoda holding neither body nor bone, raised for grave-visiting at Bon and for the memorial of the ancestors - commonly stands inside a temple's precinct. Ming and Qing China is the deliberate contrast - family graves sited by feng shui and scattered through the farmland, with communal ground only as pauper charity; a farm survey of the 1920s (buck-1930-farm-economy) found graves on 2.6 per cent of the farm land of its five level-land localities, and on 9.1 per cent of the fertile land of one of them, where, with no hill land to take them, they stood anywhere in a field whatever the trouble to the plow.
