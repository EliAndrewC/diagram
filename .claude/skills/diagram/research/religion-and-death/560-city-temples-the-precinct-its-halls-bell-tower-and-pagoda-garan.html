City temples: the precinct, its halls, bell tower and pagoda (garan)

A Buddhist temple in a town or city stood in a walled precinct of its own: a gate, a main hall for its principal image, and a kuri where its monks lived. Most temples were small. An Edo-period temple of about 1,000 tsubo (~35,600 sq ft) was called one of average size, Edo Japan is thought to have had some 90,000 temples, and an average Chinese temple of the late thirteenth century kept about five clergy. A great temple was another thing. Its full plan, the garan, counted seven halls, with its gates and principal halls set on one line and a bell tower, a sutra house and sometimes a pagoda beside them. The greatest stood on hundreds of acres with dozens of sub-temples and kept thousands of monks.

The full plan and what an ordinary temple kept of it:

  • A full Japanese temple counted seven buildings.
    A Kamakura-period text names them: the golden hall, the pagoda, the lecture hall, the bell tower, the sutra house, the monks' quarters and the refectory. The Zen sects count instead the Buddha hall, the main gate, the monks' hall, the kuri, the dharma hall, the bathhouse and the privy.
  • Which buildings made the seven differed by sect and era.
    The name was used loosely, too, of any great temple with many buildings. At Eiheiji, a Zen head temple, the seven are joined by covered corridors and stairs.
  • An ordinary temple's heart was its main hall, and its kuri often doubled as its office.
    The main hall houses the temple's principal image. The kuri, where the monks live and sometimes their kitchen too, is a building of its own at a large temple, but at an ordinary one it often doubles as the temple office.
  • How big was an ordinary temple's main hall?
    Only one measured early-Edo main hall was found: Jorokuji's in Tokushima, built in 1629, 18.7 m (~61 ft) by 11.8 m (~39 ft), one story under a hip-and-gable tiled roof. It was built as its temple's abbot's quarters and later became the main hall.
  • How much ground did an ordinary temple take?
    About 1,000 tsubo, some 3,300 m² (~35,600 sq ft), a square about 190 ft on a side: a Tokyo temple of that size is called one of average size in the Edo period. A Chinese county seat's temple, measured independently, comes to the same ground (the next bullet).
  • A Chinese county seat's temple stood on about 3,300 m² (~35,500 sq ft).
    In the county seat of Chongming, the Hanshan temple, founded in the late Ming north of the east gate, had thirty-odd rooms with 700 m² (~7,500 sq ft) of floor on a site of 3,300 m², about 5 mu. It held halls, a guest hall, dormitories, a kitchen, a refectory, a storeroom, a retreat room, an incense pavilion, a store for coffins and a gate.
  • How big were an ordinary temple's other buildings?
    No page we read gives an ordinary temple's lecture hall, kuri or gate in feet, or says how many city temples kept a lecture hall or a cloister.

A temple's clergy are counted in two ways in the sources: an average across a realm's temples, and the count of a famous house.

  • How many priests did an ordinary Edo temple keep?
    No page we read gives it as a number; one or two is this project's GUESS. Edo Japan is thought to have had some 90,000 temples, a figure its writer gives as one view among several.
  • A temple's clergy were its head priest, a retired head priest and disciples.
    Many were younger sons of farming or samurai households, ordained in their early teens, who moved among their sect's temples before rising to head one.
  • Most of the Jodo sect's Edo-period temples were young.
    By one study, of the 6,000 temples the sect had in 1696, those founded from the sixteenth century on made up nine-tenths.
  • How many clergy did a Chinese temple keep?
    About five on average: a count late in the reign of Khubilai Khan, the Yuan founder, found 42,318 temples and 213,148 monks and nuns across the realm. How many a single small hereditary temple kept is on no page we read.
  • The famous great houses kept hundreds to thousands of monks.
    Tiantong went from under two hundred monks to twelve hundred under one Southern Song abbot. Jingshan passed 1,700 under Dahui and built a Thousand-Monk Pavilion in 1140 to hold them. Daxiangguo at its height held several thousand, and Shaolin over 2,000 by the Tang-Song period.
  • A great Japanese city temple was a complex of small temples rather than one house.
    Sensoji's more than a hundred temples within its precinct were cut to thirty-three, twelve of scholar-monks and twenty-one of ritual monks. The same paper sorts Edo temples into three kinds: the single temple, the complex of sub-temples, and the sub-temple within one.
  • The Ming founder capped each county at one monastery of at most two monks.
    It was a legal cap, not a census, and reading from it that most temples were small is this record's own inference.
  • How many monks did a Ming city monastery keep?
    No page we read gives a count for any Ming city monastery. The nearest Ming figure is a frontier one: a Tibetan Buddhist monastery founded by imperial order in 1442 at Hezhou and given fifty-five official monks.
  • What share of the population were clergy?
    About half of one percent in Song China: records of 1221 counted 400,000 monks and 61,000 nuns, about 0.46% of some 100 million inhabitants.

The great temples of Japan and China, of every sect read, share one plan: gates and principal halls on a single line, the other buildings to its sides.

  • Japan's greatest temples stood on hundreds of acres.
    At its Edo-period height Zojoji, a shogun's family temple in Edo, held a precinct of 250,000 tsubo (~200 acres) with forty-eight sub-temples and a hundred-odd dormitories, and is said to have kept three thousand monks in training. Today its halls, bell tower and sutra house stand on 16,000 tsubo (~570,000 sq ft). Kan'ei-ji at Ueno held over 305,000 tsubo (~250 acres) with 36 sub-temples at its height.
  • Zojoji's approach runs about 156 ken (~930 ft) from its outer gate to its main hall.
    About 108 ken (~640 ft) from the outer gate to the main gate and about 48 ken (~290 ft) on to the main hall, numbers the temple ties to the 108 passions and the 48 vows. Its sutra house is 9.5 ken (~56 ft) square.
  • How big was a great temple's main hall?
    About 150 ft across and 115 to 140 ft deep. Kan'ei-ji's of 1698 was 45.5 m (~149 ft) by 42 m (~138 ft) and 32 m (~105 ft) tall, with a square cloister before it entered by a gate on the front, and the abbot's quarters behind it. Chion-in's in Kyoto is 44.8 m (~147 ft) by 34.5 m (~113 ft), eleven bays by nine.
  • On a hillside a great temple climbed in terraces.
    Chion-in climbs its hill in three: the main gate and the sub-temples below, the central halls in the middle, Honen's mausoleum above. Its gate, 24 m (~79 ft) tall, is the largest two-story main gate of any temple standing in Japan.
  • A lord's temple in a provincial castle town followed the same plan.
    Zuiryuji in Takaoka, built to mourn the lord who built Takaoka's castle, sets its main gate, Buddha hall and dharma hall in one straight line, with cloisters to left and right and the other halls placed symmetrically, a plan said to follow a great Chinese monastery. Its plan names an outer gate, the main gate, the Buddha hall, the dharma hall, a bath, a privy, the great kuri, the monks' hall and a tea hall.
  • Zuiryuji's bell tower stands on its cloister, and its lords' tombs outside it.
    The bell tower stands on the north cloister between the tea hall and the kuri; five stone mausolea of the Maeda and Oda houses stand outside the southwest cloister; and its dharma hall is a six-room plan of 186 tsubo (~6,600 sq ft) of floor.
  • How large was a great temple in a provincial castle town?
    No page we read gives its precinct, or the size of an abbot's quarters or of a sub-temple's lot.

Chinese temples set their buildings one behind another on a single axis in the same way, and the greatest of them were larger still.

  • Beijing's Zhihua temple runs from its gate through five halls on one line.
    From the gate come an inner gate, the main hall, a two-story pavilion and two more halls, a plan the page calls built after the seven-hall system. The temple as it stands is 278.8 m (~915 ft) by 44.5 m (~146 ft), about 12,400 m² (~133,500 sq ft).
  • The Dajue temple outside Beijing sets its bell and drum towers on its axis.
    From its gate up come stele pavilions, a pond with a bridge, a bell tower and a drum tower, the hall of the Heavenly Kings and the main hall, on a site of 6,000 m² (~64,600 sq ft) in all.
  • Quanzhou's Kaiyuan temple, in a prefectural city, stands on 78,000 m² (~19 acres).
    On its axis come a screen wall, the main gate that is also the hall of the Heavenly Kings, a worship pavilion, the main hall, an ordination platform and the sutra library, with a pagoda, a shrine to its patrons and a chapel to the east and a second pagoda and further halls to the west. Its main hall is nine bays by six, 1,338 m² (~14,400 sq ft) of floor and 20 m (~66 ft) tall.
  • Kaifeng's Daxiangguo, a capital's great temple, was a market as well.
    At its Northern Song height it ran 64 sub-temples on 540 mu (~90 acres, if the mu is the modern one) with several thousand monks, and a Song writer says its court and side galleries held ten thousand people trading.

Two tall buildings stood in some precincts: the bell tower and the pagoda.

  • The bell tower (shoro) was one of the seven halls of the old full plan.
    It is the building in a temple where its great bell hangs. In the Nara period it faced the sutra house across the plan; from the Heian period its place grew less fixed; and from the Muromachi period some temples joined it to the gate as a bell gate.
  • How big was a temple bell tower?
    About one bay to 23 ft square. One early-Muromachi bell tower, Ryosenji's in Nara, is a single bay square under a hip-and-gable roof, its lower story boarded in and flared outward like a skirt. The Zhihua temple's drum tower, of the same form as its bell tower, is 7.1 m (~23 ft) square.
  • A Chinese city temple could keep its own bell and drum towers.
    In Beijing's Dongcheng district the Zhihua temple has a two-story bell tower east of its inner gate and a drum tower of the same form west of it. Its bell, cast in 1444, is 1.05 m (~3 ft) across the mouth and 1.60 m (~5 ft) tall. These are the temple's own, not the city's civic tower (The bell-and-drum tower - one per walled seat).
  • In several Japanese towns the town's time bell hung at a temple.
    At Tenryuji in Shinjuku, at Sensoji in Asakusa, at Nionji in Kaizu.
  • In the 740s Emperor Shomu ordered a seven-story pagoda built in every province.
    Each province was to have a monastery and a nunnery as well.
  • How tall were pagodas?
    Three-story pagodas run from about 19 to 34 m (~63-112 ft): Kohfukuji's in Nara stands 19.1 m (~63 ft), its first story three bays square and 4.8 m (~16 ft) across, Hokkiji's about 24 m (~79 ft) and Yakushiji's about 34 m (~112 ft). Five-story pagodas run from 16.1 m (~53 ft) at Muroji, the lowest standing in the open, to 54.8 m (~180 ft) at Toji in Kyoto, the tallest before the modern era.
  • Did ordinary temples keep a bell tower or a pagoda?
    No page we read says how many ordinary city temples kept either. Every measured pagoda we read stands at a great or famous temple.
  • In southern China a town might raise a pagoda that was not Buddhist at all.
    A "literary peak" pagoda was built to pray for the town's literary fortune, a fengshui building with nothing to do with Buddhism, most often to the town's southeast.
