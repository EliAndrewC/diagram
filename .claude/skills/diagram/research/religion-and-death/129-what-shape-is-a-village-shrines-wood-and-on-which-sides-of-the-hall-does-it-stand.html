What shape is a village shrine's wood, and on which sides of the hall does it stand?

Sources: short-2012-sacred-groves (where a shrine's trees stand), fujita-2007-shaso-slopes (the forms on sloping ground, surveyed), chinju-no-mori-jawiki (the wood in the paddy plain), rots-2015-chinju-no-mori (the outline as pictured, and as it is), jochi-jawiki (the precinct as surveyed land)

What the research found - which sides. A naturalist writing of Japan's shrines and temples today puts their big trees in two places: single trees planted in front of the buildings or along the approach, and a grove behind the main building and around its sides, in many cases the remnant of the forest that grew there before - a general statement whose named examples are great shrines, so that it holds at a village shrine is our reading. The one survey read that sorts groves by where they stand, of the 27 wooded shrines on slopes inside Tokyo's Yamanote loop, ties the form to where the hall sits. Where the hall stands at the foot of a slope, the grove is on the slope behind it - behind only, or behind and at the sides. Where the hall stands at the top of a slope and the approach climbs to it, the grove is behind and at the sides, or at the sides only. On a hill-shaped rise, a form the survey found few of, the grove stands all around the hall; and where the hall stands at the top of a gentle slope, a shrine may keep no more than a row of trees along its approach, with buildings or other land beyond it. The survey sorts the groves a modern city has left: its shares do not come out as whole counts of its 27 shrines, many of the city's groves burned in the war and were replanted, and their grounds have shrunk and been built on, so any of its forms may be in part what the city left - the paper names only one form for its weakest, built-up shrines, a row of trees along the approach. It tells which forms occur, not the rates of an Edo countryside. None of its forms is a grove in front of the hall alone. The wood is defined as one kept so as to surround the hall, the approach and the place of worship, and in the flat rice country a shrine's wood with paddy all around it is still a common sight. So a village shrine was not always wooded on every side. It was wooded all around on a rise or, by the definition and the common sight, in the paddy plain; on a slope it was wooded behind the hall, at its sides, or both, with the approach running up through open ground.

The outline. No source read gives a grove's plan. The picture modern writers hold up, since the ecologists of the late 1970s, is "a clearly demarcated, broccoli-shaped patch" of natural broad-leaved forest - "broccoli-shaped" is the canopy seen from the side, and only "clearly demarcated" speaks to the plan - and the same article says at once that many real shrine woods are conifers planted in the premodern or modern period. The precinct itself came to be surveyed land: shrine precincts were among the grounds exempted from the annual tax, and though at first left out of the land survey, they came in time to be surveyed and entered in the survey register, the kenchicho, as exempt. No source read says the wood's edge ran along that surveyed line, and a remnant of old forest has the edge its trees give it. One picture shows an edge: the photograph over the paddy-plain caption, taken at ground level, shows the wood's crowns ragged against the sky but its foot running level along the edge of the fields that surround it, as straight as a view from the ground can show, the torii at that edge and the approach a grass strip across the field. Where a wood meets fields, then, its foot may be the fields' straight line; the photograph is modern and shows one side. How Edo village maps drew a shrine's trees was not found.

The rule the map follows. The grove's form is a knob, because the sources attest four distinct forms and tie each to the ground (accurate as forms; behind and at the sides is also the naturalist's general description, while behind only and at the sides only rest on the city survey alone, with its caveat): behind, behind and at the sides, at the sides, and all around. The ground names the candidates: a hall at the foot of its slope takes behind, or behind and at the sides; a hall at the top of its slope, with the approach climbing to it, takes behind and at the sides, or at the sides; a hall on a rise, or on flat paddy ground, is wooded all around; a hall midway on an even slope, with no break of slope at it, is in neither of the survey's classes, and takes the forms of both - behind, behind and at the sides, or at the sides (a guess). Between two or three candidates a settlement rolls from its seed with equal weights (a guess: the only counts are the city survey's, of an unstated base). Where the wood meets scrub or slope its edge is drawn irregular, the edge of its own crowns, never a ruled line (a guess, from the remnant stand; ruled edges belong to what is surveyed or built, as under "Why does that swept ground have a ragged edge?"). Where a paddy-plain wood meets its fields, whether its foot follows the fields' straight edge, as the photograph shows, is put to the GM before that form is drawn. How far a flank reaches along the approach, and how many trees stand in it, are calibrated liberty. The precinct's size is as given under "How large was a village shrine's precinct, and how much of it was built on?": the wood stands on part of it, and the rest is open ground. Single trees may stand along the approach, as the sacred tree does. At Hoshigaoka the hall stands midway on the back-slope - the ground rises behind it toward the village and falls before it down the approach, with no break of slope at it - so its candidates are behind, behind and at the sides, or at the sides, and the roll gave behind and at the sides: the wood stands behind the hall, where the well is at its back edge, and down both its flanks to ragged tips beside the forecourt; the lower approach runs up through open ground, and the sacred tree stands alone beside it. The wood meets no paddy there - its ground is the scrub of the back-slope.
