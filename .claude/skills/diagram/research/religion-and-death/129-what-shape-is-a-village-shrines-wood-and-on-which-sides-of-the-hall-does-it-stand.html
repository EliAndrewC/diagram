What shape is a village shrine's wood, and on which sides of the hall does it stand?

Sources: short-2012-sacred-groves (where a shrine's trees stand), fujita-2007-shaso-slopes (the forms on sloping ground, surveyed), chinju-no-mori-jawiki (the wood in the paddy plain), rots-2015-chinju-no-mori (the outline as pictured, and as it is), jochi-jawiki (the precinct as surveyed land)

What the research found - which sides. A shrine's big trees stand in two ways: single trees planted in front of the buildings or along the approach, and a grove behind the main building and around its sides, often the remnant of the forest that grew there before. The one survey that sorts groves by where they stand, of the shrines on slopes inside Tokyo's Yamanote loop, ties the form to where the hall sits. Where the hall stands at the foot of a slope, the grove is on the slope behind it - behind only, or behind and at the sides. Where the hall stands at the top of a slope and the approach climbs to it, the grove is behind and at the sides, or at the sides only, and at the sides only is the commonest single form of the survey. On a hill-shaped rise, a form the survey found few of, the grove stands all around the hall; and on the gentlest ground a shrine may keep no more than a row of trees along its approach. None of the forms is a grove in front of the hall alone. In the flat rice country the wood stands on every side: a shrine's wood with paddy all around it is still a common sight there. So a village shrine was not always wooded on every side. It was on a hill or in the paddy plain; on a slope it was wooded behind, at the sides, or both, with the approach running up through open ground or between the flanks. The survey is of a modern city's 27 wooded slope shrines, and its shares are of an unstated base, so it gives which forms occur and which is commonest, not the rates of an Edo countryside.

The outline. No source read gives a grove's plan. The picture the literature holds up is "a clearly demarcated, broccoli-shaped patch" of natural broad-leaved forest - and the same article says at once that many real shrine woods are conifers planted in the premodern or modern period. The precinct itself was surveyed land: shrine precincts were among the grounds exempted from the annual tax, and the exemption was entered in the survey. No source read says the wood's edge ran along that surveyed line, and a remnant of old forest has the edge its trees give it. How Edo village maps drew a shrine's trees was not found.

The rule the map follows. The grove's form is a knob, because the sources attest four distinct forms and tie each to the ground (accurate as forms): behind, behind and at the sides, at the sides, and all around. The ground names the candidates: a hall at the foot of its slope takes behind, or behind and at the sides; a hall at the top of its slope, with the approach climbing to it, takes behind and at the sides, or at the sides; a hall on a rise, or on flat paddy ground, is wooded all around. Among the candidates a settlement rolls from its seed with equal weights (a guess: the only counts are the city survey's, of an unstated base). The wood's edge is drawn irregular, the edge of its own crowns, never a ruled line (a guess, from the remnant stand; ruled edges belong to what is surveyed or built, as under "Why does that swept ground have a ragged edge?"). How far a flank reaches along the approach, and how many trees stand in it, are calibrated liberty. The precinct's size is unchanged, under "How large was a village shrine's precinct, and how much of it was built on?": the wood stands on part of it, and the rest is open ground. Single trees may stand along the approach, as the sacred tree does. At Hoshigaoka the approach climbs from the south to the hall, which stands at the top of its slope, and the roll gave at the sides: the wood stands on both flanks of the precinct, the ground behind the hall, where the well is, and the lower approach are open, and the sacred tree stands alone beside the approach.
