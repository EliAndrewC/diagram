Village burial grounds (bochi)

A village's burial ground was common ground set apart for its dead, and no page we read describes a Japanese commoner's ground laid out to a surveyed plan. Its graves were small - most of those excavated at one Edo-period temple graveyard are under 1.4 m (~5 ft) long - and an Edo village lost some 20 to 30 of every thousand inhabitants in an ordinary year.

Where the two-grave custom was kept, the body went to a burial ground away from the houses, and the stone the family visited stood at a temple or a hall in the village. A modern survey of such villages found the burial grounds on average about 370 m (~1,214 ft) from the houses, below them on the village's stream and never above. By the Edo period, six stone jizo stood in a row at a graveyard's entrance. Which ground a village's dead lay in - its own, a temple's yard, the fields or the home plot - is at Where a village buries its dead: its own ground, the temple yard, the fields or the home plot.

  • How much ground did a grave take?
    Excavated Edo graves run from about 2 ft to about 8 ft long, most under about 5 ft. At the Jisho-in site in Shinjuku, once a temple's grounds, jar burials in wooden chambers run 85 cm (~3 ft) to 1.4 m (~5 ft) along their longer axis, and the jar burials and wooden coffins of another category 65 cm (~2 ft) to 2.3 m (~8 ft), most of them under 1.4 m (~5 ft). The paper finds its wooden-coffin graves the same as the graves of farmers found in villages.
  • How closely were graves packed?
    About one to every 4 m² (~43 sq ft), in the one excavation that gives a count. A 40 m² (~431 sq ft) trench at the site of a former temple in Higashi-Komagata, in Tokyo's Sumida ward, held the traces of ten graves, one box coffin and nine barrel coffins. At another temple site in the Honjo district, graves were found stacked layer on layer, a great many made in one narrow ground. The 40 m² (~431 sq ft) is the trench, not the whole ground, and these are bodies in coffins, not urns.

China measured its charity graveyards (yizhong), the grounds where the unclaimed and the poor were buried, by field area and coffin count.

  • A Qing charity graveyard buried 573 coffins in 4 mu of field.
    After Lake Tai burst its banks in 1670 and the floating dead numbered upward of ten thousand, an endowment bought 4 mu of polder field outside Zhenze's north gate and buried 573 coffins there. Taking the mu at its standard 666.7 m² (~7,176 sq ft), which the gazetteer does not state, that is about 4.7 m² (~51 sq ft) a grave. One county's charity graveyards, Baoshan's in a count of the Guangxu era, ranged from 3 fen to 10 mu.
  • Was a charity graveyard packed more tightly than a family grave?
    This project thinks so: it was a ground for the drowned, while a family grave was sited by feng shui. The comparison is a GUESS; no page we read makes it.

How fast a village's ground filled depends on how many of its inhabitants died each year.

  • How many died in an Edo village each year?
    About 20 to 30 in a thousand in an ordinary year, more in a famine decade. Three farming villages of the Nihonmatsu domain, followed in their registers from the early 1700s to 1870, died at about 30 in a thousand a year in the decade of the Tenmei famine and 29 in that of the Tenpo famine, and at about 18 in the decades after them.
  • The birth rates and life expectancy of Edo villages point the same way.
    Birth rates estimated from the same kind of register run from 18.4 to 31.9 in a thousand across seven villages between 1671 and 1871, while the country's surveyed population stayed nearly flat from 1721 to 1846, so deaths ran close to births. One Mino village's registers give a life expectancy at birth of about 37 years for 1773 to 1869, its infant deaths assumed rather than counted, and a population that neither grows nor shrinks loses one over its mean length of life each year - about 27 in a thousand.
  • How long before a grave was dug again?
    No page we read gives a term of years for a premodern ground. A 1934 planner held that where cremated remains are interred in a family urn plot, they stay for good, so such a plot is never cleared.
  • How did a modern planner, in 1934, size a city cemetery where the dead were cremated?
    By households, not by burials. A paper in Japan's landscape-architecture journal quotes a North American rule - a plot's area, times the burials a year, times the years before a grave is reused, times a factor for paths and buildings - and replaces it, for cities that inter a household's urns together, with one family plot for each registered household, times the same factor. It advises a plot of 1 tsubo, about 36 sq ft, and a factor of 1.5 to 2. A 1-tsubo plot easily holds the remains of 20 to 30 dead.
  • How much ground did Tokyo's modern cemeteries give each burial?
    About half a tsubo, some 18 sq ft, on average. The same paper's table puts the ground per interment in Tokyo's common cemeteries at the start of 1932 at 0.514 tsubo on average. Their plots ran from 0.5 tsubo a user in one cemetery to 2.61 in another, 2.075 on average, and the factor for paths and buildings in the older five averaged 1.78.
  • No page we read gives a premodern Japanese burial ground's area against the inhabitants it served.
    The 1934 paper sizes modern city cemeteries, and the survey of two-grave villages measures distances, not areas; the only premodern grounds read with an area and a count are China's charity graveyards. Whether a town's and a city's grounds reached the sizes our maps give them is at Were a town's and a city's burial grounds that size before modern times?

Where the dead had two graves, a modern survey could measure how far each lay from the houses.

  • In the two-grave custom, the body and the grave stone lay in different grounds.
    The survey defines it as a grave system in which the burial ground and the ground of the stones are separate. The Heibonsha encyclopedia sets the burial grave on riverbeds, in the hills and on beaches, and the visiting grave in the grounds of the temples and halls within the village, and dates the custom to the early Edo period, from its tie to the spread of grave stones. Mypedia puts the burial grave away from the houses, unmarked and visited only until the end of the 49 days of mourning, and the visiting grave within a house plot or a temple's grounds.
  • How far from the houses did the two graves lie?
    In 22 two-grave settlements measured today, the visiting graves lay on average about 130 m (~427 ft) from the middle of the houses, 18 of them within 200 m (~656 ft). The burial grounds lay on average about 370 m (~1,214 ft) away, and in some villages about 900 m (~2,953 ft).
  • The surveyor reads the burial ground's distance as a compromise.
    The village wanted its dead as far off as it could, and stopped where carrying them grew too hard.
  • The survey measured villages as they stand today.
    Its settlements are in three regions - the Shiwaku islands of the Inland Sea, Shima and Wakasa - and many of them small fishing villages on the coast. It chose villages that still keep the shape they had when their two graves were made, untouched by large postwar development, while granting that when the custom formed cannot be clearly dated. It gives averages, not a village-by-village table.
  • Were a village's two graves always far apart?
    Often not. Across Japan the two are often quite close - side by side across a single road, the visiting grave on a small raised terrace above the burial ground, or on one piece of ground. The article that says so gives no date or figure.
  • A Fukui folklore record puts the burial ground at the hamlet's edge.
    At Takenami, a 1966 volume on the folk customs of Wakasa records, the burial ground stands at the edge of the hamlet and the visiting graves inside its two temples.
  • No page we read gives a distance from a village's houses to its burial ground before modern times.
    The one older rule found names none: the eighth-century Yoro code forbade burial near the capital and beside roads. Searches in Japanese, Chinese and English found no premodern figure.
  • No page we read says how far a village that buried in one ground kept it from its houses.
    The two-grave survey measures only two-grave settlements.
  • A national rule of the Meiji period, in 1884, set new graveyards about 60 ken from houses.
    A Home Ministry instruction of 1884 put a new graveyard about 60 ken, some 360 ft, or more from houses, off national and prefectural roads, railways and great rivers, on high, dry ground that does no harm to drinking water. It governs new graveyards, in the Meiji period.

Along a village's stream, the same survey found an order of up and down.

  • In today's two-grave villages, the burial ground lies below the houses on the stream, never above them.
    The survey finds the watercourse giving the village an upper and a lower end, with the burial ground below the houses or, mainly in fishing villages on the coast, across the water from them. Its model of such a village puts the shrine at the upper end and the visiting graves with the temple.
  • Did the downstream side keep the water clean?
    Perhaps. The surveyor suggests, hedging, that where a farming village had room, putting the burial ground downstream may also have been meant to prevent a hygiene problem.
  • Downstream is not the same as far from water.
    Where the burial ground lies across a stream from the houses, mainly in fishing villages, the water is its boundary, and the burial grounds by the sea stand on unstable ground that tsunami wash away.
  • China's state pauper grounds stood on high, open, barren ground.
    Paupers' burial grounds were generally sited on high, open, barren ground, fenced on all four sides. The Song dynasty began them in 1104, and the Song and Yuan gazetteers of several Zhejiang counties put them one to ten li outside the county towns, some where they "chose high, barren ground".
  • Japan's older burial grounds often lay by the water.
    In 871 the Daijokan kept the lower reaches of Kyoto's Kamo River from being turned to farmland because they were "the burial ground of the common people and a place for grazing", though most commoners of that age were laid out in the open rather than buried, so it shows where the dead were taken more than where graves were dug. The burial grave of the two-grave villages was set on riverbeds, in the hills and on beaches.
  • The only distance from water we found is a modern one.
    One Japanese city today requires a graveyard to stand 20 m (~66 ft) or more from a river or a lake. No source before modern times puts a figure on how far from water a grave must lie.

How a burial ground was laid out differed between Japan and China.

  • Kyoto's great burial grounds had no set bounds.
    Toribeno in the east, Adashino in the west and Rendaino in the north are called the capital's three great burial grounds, and the extent of Toribeno has no clear definition. Adashino was a place of exposure burial, and in the Edo period a cremation ground.
  • Osaka gathered its graveyards to the city's rim.
    The graveyards scattered through the city were gathered, for military reasons, into grounds on its outer rim, and one of them yielded more than 1,500 skeletons and 350 ossuaries to excavation.
  • Were Japan's commoner burial grounds laid out to a surveyed plan?
    No page we read describes one that was. The nearest is a village burial ground divided into sections by household or by age.
  • In southern China, family graves stood on the highest hills, far from water.
    Feng shui put the graves on the highest hills far from water and on otherwise worthless land.
  • China's pauper grounds were fenced, their graves numbered, and kept by monks.
    Cai Jing proposed them in 1104, to bury the bones of the unclaimed and the destitute on government land, and the Yuan and the Ming kept them. A fence ran round each, each grave was numbered by the Thousand Character Classic with the dead's name, home and date, and Buddhist monks managed them.
  • No page we read says whether a pauper ground's graves stood in rows, or had ditches between them.
    The pages that give the fence, the numbering and the monks say nothing of rows or of ditches.

The stone figures at a burial ground's entrance are dated too.

  • Six stone jizo stood at a graveyard's entrance before modern times.
    The six jizo stand in a row at the entrance of a temple or a graveyard, and stone ones are said to appear at temples and graveyards from the end of the Muromachi period. Two sets at a temple gate in Hiratsuka carry inscriptions of 1753 and 1797. A city museum's exhibition of Edo-period gravestones names the six jizo at the entrances of graveyards and temples as a representative stone monument of the graveyard.
  • A set of six in a Kiso valley graveyard is dated to the Edo period, 1678.
    The set in the Shinden graveyard at Agematsu is cataloged as Edo-period work of 1678, the oldest jizo in the town. The catalog dates the figures, not where they stood.
  • Did six jizo stand at a cremation ground?
    One undated page says so: a community page on an old cremation ground in a Takamatsu district says six jizo were always set at a cremation ground, used "formerly". No page dated before modern times puts them at a cremation ground standing on its own.
