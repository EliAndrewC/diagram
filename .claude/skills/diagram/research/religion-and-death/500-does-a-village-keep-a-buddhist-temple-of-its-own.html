Does a village keep a Buddhist temple of its own?

Sources: the GM's campaign notes for the country monk of each village district, serving in its shrine; tamamuro-2009-terauke (villages with no temple, households choosing one by nearness, halls raised to temples), ndl-crd-fukudanka (a village's houses divided among temples outside it), kasuga-mura-no-tera (a village of about 70 households with three temples), kofukuroman-sanmai (two temples inside one hamlet), onga-choshi-jiin (village temples' parishes, and a whole village becoming one temple's parish), koshoji-jiin-zoka (a meeting hall becoming a temple)

What the history shows: three arrangements, side by side. When the Tokugawa state made every household register with a Buddhist temple in the seventeenth century, not every village had one: not all of Japan's villages, numbering close to 65,000, had a temple that could take parishioners, and many of the new parishioners chose a temple by nearness rather than by doctrine. So one arrangement was the temple of a neighbor, and a folklorist who looked at temple ties across the country judged it the commonest: a village's houses were not all registered at one temple inside it, and several temples outside the village dividing its houses between them seems safe to call the most common form, especially early in the period. The second arrangement was a temple of the village's own, often made from something it already had: the Buddhist sects raised halls dedicated to a local deity, or to Amida, Shakyamuni or Fudo, to the status of temples, halls that religionists from the village had served season by season; in the Shin sect, the meeting hall of the faithful became a temple as the farming households round it became its parishioners; and in one Fukuoka village a hall-hermitage became the temple of the whole village in 1688, a villager helping to pay for its founding the next year. The third arrangement was several temples in one village: medieval Sugaura on Lake Biwa, with only about 70 households, had three temples and twelve hermitages and cloisters in the fifteenth century, and a Fukui hamlet kept its visiting graves in the two temples inside it. A temple register of one Saitama district, read under How many monasteries does a county town keep, and who lives in one?, lists nineteen temples in fourteen old villages, two apiece in five of them.

How big a parish was. The village temples of one Fukuoka town counted between 24 and 216 parish households each - 35, 41 and 88 at three of them - so a temple's parish was of the order of one village's households.

What is not known. How many villages kept no temple, one or several, and whether that differed by region, is on no page read.

The setting. The campaign notes give each village district one country monk, who serves in the village's shrine and keeps the district's records of births and deaths; a district is its village and the hamlets round it. That shrine is the setting's parish temple (Does a graveyard stand beside its temple or its shrine?), and the setting has no second, Buddhist, temple for it to stand beside. Of the three historical arrangements, the setting's is the first two at once: a village has its own hall, and its hamlets are served from the village's, as a village without a temple was served by its neighbor's.

The rule the map follows: a village draws its shrine and no separate temple; a hamlet draws neither and is served by its village's shrine. This follows the setting's canon, and it is a deliberate deviation from the history above, in which a village might keep no temple, one, or several beside its shrine.
