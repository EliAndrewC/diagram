How large is a pauper burial ground, and how many does a seat keep?

Sources: songshi-178-wikisource (the ground ordered for each county of the capital district), mu-land-enwiki and chinese-units-enwiki (the units, to put it in acres), chnmus-louzeyuan-bricks (the ground per burial, and the excavated cemeteries), gongyishibao-louzeyuan (the fence, the trees and the layout), louzeyuan-zhwiki (the siting, and the keeper's house), sohu-xuancheng-yizhong (one county's several small charity grounds)

A town or city keeps one pauper ground, the record finds (Which capital features grow faster than the city, and which stay one to a seat?); this question asks how big it was and whether "one" held.

A field, not a mound. The Song pauper cemetery was a large piece of land. Before it had its name, the emperor Shenzong ordered each county of the capital district to measure out three to five qing of barren government land where the coffins of the poor, left lodged at monasteries, could be laid to rest, with a monk in charge; in 1104 Cai Jing made this the louzeyuan, with a register, and graves three chi deep. A mu was already 240 square paces in the third century and the Northern Song foot 0.308 m, with a pace of five feet; taking a qing as a hundred mu, as the later tables do, three to five qing is about 42 to 70 acres, a square some 1,350 to 1,750 ft on a side - on this page's arithmetic, which carries the later hundred-mu qing back to the Song.

What was in it. The edicts gave each burial eight chi of ground and two square bricks, nine for one whose kin wished it buried there - about 8 to 9 ft. The Kuaiji gazetteer has the ground surrounded by a wall or palisade against intruders and those grazing livestock, and shaded with trees suited to its soil and easy to grow; Xu Du's notebook has the graves in groups of thirty with ditches, and a corner of the ground set aside for a Buddhist temple. It stood on high, open, barren ground, fenced all round, with a house for the monk who kept it. The ground as dug bears this out: nearly a thousand such graves at nineteen sites, the largest cemetery 849 graves near Sanmenxia, the dead laid in shallow pits in neat rows.

Or several small grounds. A later county shows the other form. Xuancheng's gazetteer lists its charity graves as four separate plots on three roads out of the city - two mu, one, three, one and a half - with twenty-two mu more bought later; at the Qing mu of 614.4 square meters such a plot is some 80 to 140 ft on a side.

What is not known. No page read gives the area of a provincial seat's Song pauper ground, the three-to-five-qing order being for the capital district, nor the size of a Japanese mound for the unclaimed dead.

The decision. Two Chinese forms are attested, so a seat drawn in the Chinese form rolls between them rather than choosing: one great fenced ground of numbered rows with a keeper's house and trees, or several small charity grounds on the roads out of the seat. The Japanese-form pauper mound the maps draw by default keeps its one-to-a-seat rule, and its size stays a guess.

The rule the map follows: a seat drawn in the Chinese form draws, by its seed, either ONE pauper ground outside the walls - fenced, planted, laid in rows of graves about 8 ft apart, with a keeper's house at one side - or two to four small charity grounds, each about 80 to 140 ft on a side, beside different roads out. The large ground is drawn a few hundred feet on a side, not at the capital district's 42-70 acres: that size is a guess, calibrated to the sheet. A seat in the default Japanese form draws one pauper mound, 10-30 ft across - a guess.
