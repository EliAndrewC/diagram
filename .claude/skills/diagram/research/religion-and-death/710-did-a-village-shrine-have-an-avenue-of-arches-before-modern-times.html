Did a village shrine have an avenue of arches before modern times? No page shows one

Sources: fushimi-inari-senbon-map (the donated-gate custom arising in the Edo period), fushimi-inari-kanban (the same custom dated to around the late Edo period), ndl-crd-senbon-torii (Fushimi's rows raised from the Edo into the Meiji period), kyoto-senbon-meiji-blog (an early-Meiji drawing of Fushimi's precinct with six gates, as a book reports it), kato-kawataka-torii and kudamatsu-stone-torii (single early stone arches), sanuki-kokubunji-jawiki and shimotsuke-kokubunji-jawiki (a great precinct's size)

What the research found. At Fushimi Inari the custom of donating arches is premodern. The shrine says the devotion of giving arches along one of its approaches "arose already in the Edo period", and, more narrowly, that the donations "are said" to have begun around the late Edo period; the national library's reference service puts the first of its rows "from the Edo into the Meiji period". How thickly even Fushimi's arches stood before modern times is less sure: a book by Kyoto teachers, as a sightseeing blog reports it, says the shrine's own drawing of its precinct in the first years of Meiji shows only six arches, with their bark on, and not one vermilion - a report at second hand of a drawing that may leave out the mountain paths.

At an ordinary shrine the dated arches are single gates. A single stone arch at a country shrine is thought to date from as early as the late Muromachi or early Edo. One city's inventory counts 64 stone arches and lists its dated ones up to the Kansei era (1789-1801) year by year, the earliest, of 1679, raised by the parishioners on the front approach of Kudamatsu Shrine at the solicitation of Zōhen, priest of the temple Washizu-ji and restorer of Myōkenzan, and a later one, of 1701, a single sponsor's gift, each arch listed on its own. No page read shows a village shrine, or any ordinary shrine, with a row or an avenue of arches before 1868, and the present-day rows the spacing question measures, at Yutoku and Manzo Inari, are dated by no page read. A great precinct, by contrast, has ground to spare for a long approach: Sanuki's Nara-period provincial temple, its kokubunji, stood on 240 by 220 m, and Shimotsuke's ran 457 by 413 m - two Buddhist temples, whose sizes show the scale of a great precinct, not where any arch of theirs stood.

The decision (the GM's rule of 2026-09-28, that anything attested only in modern times is eliminated). A donated row of arches is attested before modern times only at a great Inari site, and the record already draws such a site as a designated special case (under "Torii spacing"). An avenue of arches at a village shrine, or at any ordinary shrine, is attested only in modern times, so the maps do not draw one: an ordinary shrine's approach is marked by a single arch where it enters the precinct, the form dated by inscription from 1679 and thought older still (accurate), except where the GM has given a particular shrine its arches, as at Hoshigaoka. Where arches are drawn, their spacing is under "Torii spacing".
