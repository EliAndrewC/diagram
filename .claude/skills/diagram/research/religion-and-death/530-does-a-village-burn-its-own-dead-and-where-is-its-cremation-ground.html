Does a village burn its own dead, and where is its cremation ground?

Sources: the GM's campaign notes for cremation; kasoba-jawiki (cremation spreading with the Shin sect, and the settlement's own ground), tiikijiten-sanmai (a hamlet burning its own dead at its own ground, and where cremation was the custom), kofukuroman-sanmai (the cremation place held with the graves, or at the hamlet's edge), kawazoe-2010-ryobosei (the ground called sanmai, and how far it lay), higashiueta-sanmai (the six jizo at a cremation ground)

Where Japan cremated, settlements commonly burned their own. Cremation spread among commoners with the Shin sect, and open cremation grounds were set up in many places; in regions of the Jōdo sect the cremation grounds commoners built for themselves, as the shared facility of a settlement or of a devotional society, stand out. Until the end of the Edo period nearly every cremation ground was an open one, with no building, and when the Meiji ban on cremation was lifted in 1875 many towns and villages had only simple grounds attached to a temple or owned by a settlement or a person. Before cremation became universal after the Second World War it was the custom only in the Shin regions of Hokuriku and in Hiroshima and Yamaguchi, and around one hamlet there the hamlets burned their dead with their own hands: one hamlet's ground, which it called the sanmai, was last used in 1977.

Two seats for the ground. A Fukui town history reads the word sanmai - the hall at a graveyard, or the graveyard itself - as showing that relatives and fellow parishioners once kept the burial and cremation place together with the graves. The early rural cremation grounds with a permanent hearth were as plain: a simple hearth, or one attached to the settlement's graveyard. Elsewhere a sanmai where the dead were buried stood apart, at the edge of the hamlet, while the graves that were visited stood in its temples; in the two-grave villages of Wakasa the ground where the dead were laid is often called the sanmai, and in a survey of 22 two-grave villages, 11 of them in Wakasa and the rest in the Shiwaku islands and Shima, it lay on average about 370 m, some 1,200 ft, from the middle of the houses. What stood at it. One Takamatsu settlement's cremation ground kept six jizo, which the page that describes it says were always set at a cremation ground. The fire bed and the cleared ground are sized under How large is a cremation ground, and what was its fire bed?

What is not known. Whether a cremation ground ever stood on its own at a settlement's edge, rather than with its graves, how far a cremation ground held apart lay from the houses - the 370 m is the distance of a ground where bodies were buried, not burned - and whether a village kept any mound or house for gathered bones, are in no page read.

The setting. The campaign notes have the whole empire cremate, and put the setting's cremators among the burakumin, who live in towns rather than villages. The history above has cremating villages burn their own dead; that the setting's villagers do the same, with their country monk officiating, is this page's reading, and the notes neither say it nor rule it out.

The rule the map follows: a VILLAGE draws a cremation ground of its own, and its seed chooses where: beside the village's burial ground, wherever that lies, or on its own at the village's edge, downstream of the houses and beyond the last of them. Either way its fire bed stays off the approach to the shrine, and six small stone jizo stand at it. The ground beside the burial ground is attested; the seat on its own at the edge is a guess, borrowed from the sanmai where a hamlet buried its dead, and the odds of the choice are a guess: even. How far out a ground on its own lies is a guess: within about 650 ft of the middle of the houses , the reach of the village's burial ground. A village draws no bone mound. A hamlet draws no cremation ground; its dead are burned at its village's.
