The five sizes of settlement: hamlet, village, town, provincial city and capital

The setting these maps are drawn for divides each domain by office: into provinces, each under a governor who lives in its provincial city; provinces into counties, each under a magistrate who lives in its town; and counties into village districts, each under a peasant headman who lives in its main village, with small farming hamlets outlying. The maps draw these as five sizes of place, from a hamlet of 50 to 100 inhabitants to a daimyo's capital of about 12,000, and each size keeps institutions the one below it lacks. Every figure is counted in households of about five.

These sizes are the setting's own canon, from its population tables and budget notes, not a historical finding. The history they simplify is that of Japan's villages in the Edo period, before 1868, where a small branch hamlet answered to a parent village for its taxes and its officials, though some branch hamlets kept officials of their own.

  • A hamlet is 10 to 20 households, 50 to 100 inhabitants.
    That is the setting's figure. A hamlet usually works one to three small fields. Below ten households the place is an outlying farmstead or two; above twenty it is a small village.
  • A hamlet has no headman, no shrine and no tax-free plot of its own.
    It falls under the headman of the village district it belongs to, who lives in the main village and not in the hamlet. Whether a hamlet in history kept its own shrine is at Village shrines: where they stand, and how big their grounds are, and where its dead lie at Where a village buries its dead: its own ground, the temple yard, the fields or the home plot.
  • A village is 40 to 100 households, 200 to 500 inhabitants.
    That is the setting's figure, around 350 inhabitants in the ordinary case. A village has a headman, a shrine and two or three tax-free plots.
  • A village district takes its main village's name.
    The setting's place names follow one pattern across its levels, and one of its pairs is a village and its district, read here as the district sharing its village's name. So the district a hamlet belongs to is named for the village its lanes lead to.
  • The shared name is a deliberate departure from how real places were named.
    The setting's notes say so themselves: real capitals are not named for their countries, but keeping the names the same spares a reader many extra names.
  • A town is a county seat of about 1,200 inhabitants in about 238 households.
    That is the setting's town line. About 156 of its households farm; the other 82 are about 24 merchant, 29 laborer, 13 servant, 12 burakumin and 4 samurai households. Who they are is set out at Who lives in a town, and in how many houses?
  • A town's figure counts the farmers of the fields around it.
    The setting counts a town's inhabitants in and around it, so even the ordinary town is about two-thirds farmers.
  • A town is the smallest place with samurai in it.
    Villages and hamlets are peasant-only. The samurai of a county live in its town, where the state is present as the county magistrate in a walled manor rather than as a peasant headman. A samurai family may keep a country estate near farmland, but the estate is never part of the village or hamlet, and never under its headman.
  • A provincial city is a governor's seat of 2,000 to 4,000 inhabitants inside its walls.
    That is the setting's provincial-city line, about 3,000 inhabitants in 600 households in the ordinary case.
  • A provincial city counts no farmers.
    The farmers who work a city's land live in the villages and hamlets around it and are counted there. Where a city has fields inside its wall, the city's own households work them beside their other work, as some townsmen of small castle towns farmed alongside their trade, and they stay in their own castes (Who works the fields inside a city's wall, and where do they live?).
  • A provincial city's households are about 40% laborer, 20% servant, 25% merchant, 10% samurai and 5% burakumin.
    That is about 240 laborer, 120 servant, 150 merchant, 60 samurai and 30 burakumin households, by the setting's budget tables.
  • A provincial city's samurai are its provincial government.
    They are the governor, the six provincial ministers and their deputies, the clerks under them, and rank-and-file bushi on administrative and military duty. How many of a city's samurai live inside its wall is at How many of a city's samurai live inside its wall?
  • A small Imperial post in each provincial city answers to the Imperial Magistrate, not the governor.
    A handful of yoriki from other clans audit the tariff collection there, since a daimyo's own samurai cannot be trusted to audit the daimyo, and they report to the Imperial Magistrate at the domain capital.
  • A domain capital is the daimyo's castle-town seat of about 12,360 inhabitants.
    The setting's table gives about 12,400; the capital keeps everything a provincial city has, and overrides it where the two differ.
  • A household is about five, over two or three generations.
    That is the setting's median, and every population figure above is counted from it.

Japan's villages before 1868 were tied together by tax and office in a way the setting's ladder simplifies.

  • In the Edo period, a branch hamlet (edago) hung from a parent village (hongo).
    A settlement that split off from an older village was called its edago, and the village it came from the hongo; the parent usually gave the branch a share of its assessed yield. The branch was subordinate to the parent in its administration: its land register and tax notice stood in the parent's name, it could pay its land tax only through the parent, and it needed the parent's consent to petition its lord.
  • Why did a branch hamlet usually have no headman of its own?
    On this project's reading, because there was no separate tax unit for one to administer: the tax ran through the parent village. The English Wikipedia article on the village headman gives one to each village, but does not say whether it means a settlement or the administrative village that could take in several.
  • Some branch hamlets did keep their own officials.
    Some had a shoya and kumigashira of their own and were treated on a par with the parent village. So the setting's rule that a hamlet has no headman is contradicted as history: it is the setting's deliberate simplification, stated twice in its notes, once in the hamlet's definition and once in a direct answer whose caveat is about how much ground one headman oversees, not about hamlets having one.
  • How common was each form of settlement?
    No page we read gives a national count or share of any form. A study of Kishu (the old province of Kii, today Wakayama) found an administrative village made of one settlement mostly on the plain, and of several mostly in the mountains, more of them the deeper the valley. The encyclopedias call the clustered village the general form in Japan and the scattered village a regional one, and put no number on either (Were most villages clustered or scattered, and how common was each form?). The setting's own table counts about 1,296 hamlets to 216 villages in a domain, with about 40% of its inhabitants in a hamlet.
  • Was wet rice the main use of the land?
    No source we read gives the share of paddy against dry field. The kokudaka, the assessed yield in rice, looks like such a figure and is not: it estimated the whole yield of the land, with other crops converted to their value in rice, so it covers dry fields too.
  • How large was a household in history?
    No page we could read gives a historical figure. The entry on the demographer Hayami Akira says he studied household size from the annual household listings and found a long-run trend toward smaller households, but gives no figure; the one study we found of a single Tohoku village's household size would not open. The household of five is the setting's own.
