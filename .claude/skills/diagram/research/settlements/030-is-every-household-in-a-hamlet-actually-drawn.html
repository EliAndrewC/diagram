Is every household in a hamlet actually drawn?

Sources: l7r-median-domain (five to a household, over two or three generations); the GM's own wording of the village floor, "~70 households ... at least 50 houses"; okada-bitchu-2004 (household sizes and make-up in three regions' registers of the 1860s and 1870); nawata-sangiin-2006 (households halving from the seventeenth century to the nineteenth, and the stem family's rise); kotobank-inkyo (the retirement house, and where it was kept); kotobank-inkyoya (the early house plot with its dependents' huts, counted as one hearth); this project's measurement of the seventeen shipped settlement maps

Essentially yes, at hamlet and village scale. These are to-scale maps, so a declared household is expected to have a roof on the sheet: the placer is held to between 0.85 and 1.05 occupied farmhouses for every household declared, and a hamlet that declares fifteen and seats twelve is a failure to be fixed rather than a map to be shipped. Measured across the shipped maps, sixteen of seventeen sit at exactly 1.00 - one house per household - and the seventeenth at 0.94.

The GM's figure is a floor, not a ratio: about seventy households want at least fifty houses drawn, which is a minimum on what a sheet may show, not a statement that three households in ten share a roof with another. Abandoned houses do not count toward the households: they are former homes.

How many lived in one farmhouse. In the setting a household averages five, across two or three generations. That sits inside what the village registers of the period show. The shumon aratame cho, the yearly registers of every household and its members, put the average for twenty-two villages of Bitchu province in 1870 at a little over six, for fifty-six villages of Echizen at under six, and for two villages near Nihonmatsu at six and a half; the commonest size in Bitchu was five, with a few households of up to twenty-four. Earlier the households were larger: in the Suwa region the average halved, from about eight in the seventeenth century to about four in the nineteenth.

Extended families genuinely did share a roof, and the sharing was inside the household, not between two of them. The early large households held dependent farmers and collateral kin under one powerful farmer; as those split off into small farms of their own, the stem family - parents living with one married heir and the heir's children - became the ordinary household. How ordinary varied by region. In the three regions' registers the stem family was over half of the households near Nihonmatsu but under a fifth in Bitchu, where a quarter were joint households, two or more married couples of the same generation under one head.

One family, two roofs: the retirement house. In much of Japan the old couple did not stay under the heir's roof. On inkyo, retirement, they moved out to a small retirement house of their own, and most retirement houses stood inside the family's house plot, with an entrance of their own; where the custom was kept thoroughly, every house had one. Moving out was the main form of retirement, and the two households often kept separate meals and purses - one family living as two households. Where it was done followed a line across the country: the custom was thin from the northeast along the Sea of Japan coast, where the large family held together under one roof, and strong from the Pacific coast through the Inland Sea, above all in Kyushu and Shikoku. Another encyclopedia draws the same line, separate house, meals and property on the Pacific side west of Kanto and in Kyushu, one house, one table and one purse on the Sea of Japan side and in the northeast.

So a farm family took one of two forms, and both are attested: one roof, the generations together in the farmhouse, or a farmhouse and a retirement house in the same yard. That is a choice between forms, not a degree, so it is rolled per settlement.

A third form belongs to an earlier time. In the land surveys of the early Edo period, a registered house plot often held, inside its enclosure, the main house together with huts, gate-houses and retirement houses in which dependent small farmers lived under the master's family, and a register of houses and labor counted the whole plot as one hearth even where those families kept their own livelihood. Those dependents are the ones who later split off into households of their own, and the villages on these maps are drawn as the settled villages that came after: a household on the sheet is a farm family with its own farmhouse, and no plot is drawn as a master's yard full of dependents' huts.

Where the two numbers do differ, the map's own card says so rather than leaving a reader to divide: one sheet drawing 66 farmhouses for 70 households names both, because "66 farmhouses, population ~350" otherwise invites the reader to divide and get 5.3 to a house.

The rule the map follows: a hamlet or village map seats between 0.85 and 1.05 occupied farmhouses per declared household, and aims at one apiece. When the placer runs short it widens the ground it draws candidate seats from rather than re-rolling the map, so the houses already seated stay where they are. Abandoned houses are not counted against the household total, and a map whose drawn houses and declared households differ states both. Each settlement rolls one of two family forms from its seed: the generations under one roof, or a farmhouse with a small retirement house in the same yard, entered separately. A retirement house belongs to its farmhouse's household - it is a second roof of the same family, not a household of its own - so it is neither counted toward the households nor against the band. How many of a settlement's homesteads keep one where the custom is kept is a guess, and so is how the two forms are weighted against each other in the roll.
