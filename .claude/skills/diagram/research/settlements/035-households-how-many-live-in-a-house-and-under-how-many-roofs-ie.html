Households: how many live in a house, and under how many roofs (ie)

A household (ie) in the setting these maps are drawn for averages five, across two or three generations. The village registers of the last years of the Edo period, which ended in 1868, and of the first years after it hold a little under five inhabitants to a household, and the households of the seventeenth century were larger. Over the period the ordinary household became the stem family: parents living with one married heir and the heir's children.

A household did not always keep to one roof. In much of the country the old couple, once they had handed the farm to their heir, moved out to a retirement house of their own on the family's house plot. Elsewhere the generations stayed together in the farmhouse.

  • How large were households in the village registers?
    The shumon aratame cho were the yearly registers of every household and its members. Read, as this project reads them, as the size of household the average inhabitant lived in, they put the average for twenty-two villages of Bitchu province in 1870 at a little over six, for fifty-six villages of Echizen at under six, and for two villages near Nihonmatsu at six and a half. The commonest size in Bitchu was five, with a few households of up to twenty-four.
  • Why do the village registers' averages run above the setting's five to a household?
    In this project's reading, they are counted over inhabitants: they say how large a household the average inhabitant lived in, which weighs the big households more heavily. Divided by households, the same Bitchu registers hold 6,737 people, servants aside, in 1,431 households, under five to a household.
  • In the Suwa region, households halved in size over the Edo period.
    The average fell from about eight in the seventeenth century to about four in the nineteenth.
  • Early in the Edo period, one house plot could hold several families counted as one hearth.
    In the land surveys of the early Edo period, a registered house plot often held, inside its enclosure, the main house together with huts, gate-houses and retirement houses - here the names of buildings, not a sign of retired parents - in which dependent small farmers lived under the master's family. A register of houses and labor counted the whole plot as one hearth, even where those families kept their own livelihood.
  • As the dependent farmers of the early large households split off into households of their own, the stem family became the ordinary household.
    The early large households held dependent farmers and collateral kin under one powerful farmer. As those dependents became small farmers, they formed new households of their own, and the stem family took the place of the large joint family.
  • How common was the stem family?
    It varied by region. Counted over inhabitants, over half of those near Nihonmatsu lived in a stem family but under a fifth in Bitchu, where a quarter lived in joint households, two or more married couples of the same generation under one head. Counted by household, Bitchu's households were about one in seven stem and one in seven joint.
  • In much of Japan the retired couple moved out to a retirement house in the family's own yard.
    On inkyo, retirement, the old couple left the heir's roof for a house of their own. Most retirement houses stood inside the family's house plot, with an entrance of their own, and where the custom was kept thoroughly, every house had one.
  • A retired couple often kept their own meals and purse.
    Moving out was the main form of retirement, and the two households often kept separate meals and purses - one family living as two households.
  • The retirement-house custom was strong on the Pacific side and in the southwest, and thin in the northeast and along the Sea of Japan.
    It was thin from the northeast along the Sea of Japan coast, where the large family held together under one roof, and strong from the Pacific coast through the Inland Sea, above all in Kyushu and Shikoku. Another encyclopedia draws the same line: separate house, meals and property are the common type on the Pacific side west of Kanto and in Kyushu, one house, one table and one purse on the Sea of Japan side and in the northeast.
