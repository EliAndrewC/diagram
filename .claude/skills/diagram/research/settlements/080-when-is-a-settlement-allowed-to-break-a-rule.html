When is a settlement allowed to break a rule?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-27 on the walled town's history; this project's own experience of building the first city and town maps

Some settlements are not ordinary, and a rule that is right for every planned seat can be wrong for one of them. A map may be excused a rule in writing, with the reason stated at length - never by quietly relaxing the rule for everybody.

The worked case is a walled town that was never planned as one (the GM, 2026-07-27). It stood unwalled, changed hands during a war between two Great Clans, and was walled in haste afterwards: the rampart was thrown around the town that was already there, so unlike a planned seat its enclosure was never sized to a population budget at all. Two consequences follow, and both cost it dwellings a planned town of the same footprint would have housed - a chrysanthemum field stands inside the walls for symbolic reasons, and there is no burakumin quarter, excluded for logistical ones. So its housing cannot come out to its declared population, and it is excused that arithmetic on exactly that ground and no other.

Reach for a waiver only when the settlement's premise conflicts with the rule - not when a map is merely hard to tune. The distinction is worth being strict about, because from the outside the two look identical. Another city also could not meet its declared population at first, but its oddity - eight temple precincts scattered through it by trade - turned out to be something the space budget could price honestly, so the answer there was a bigger wall, not a waiver. Fix the model first; waive only when the map is telling you something true about the place.

And the process lesson, which is the more valuable half: lock the rules in against ordinary settlements first. The first city and the first walled town were both drawn early and both are atypical, so for a long time the defaults were being bent to fit the exceptions. Build the normal cases until the rules are settled, then let the unusual ones earn their waivers.

The rule the map follows: a settlement that cannot meet a rule because of its own history is excused that one rule, with a written reason that names the history and what it cost; the excuse lapses when it stops excusing anything. A settlement that merely comes out awkward is re-planned instead - most often by growing its wall. New rules are calibrated against ordinary settlements, never against the unusual ones.
