How many lived in one farmhouse, and under how many roofs?

Sources: l7r-median-domain (five to a household, over two or three generations); okada-bitchu-2004 (household sizes and make-up in three regions' registers of the 1860s and 1870); nawata-sangiin-2006 (households halving from the seventeenth century to the nineteenth, and the stem family's rise); kotobank-inkyo (the retirement house, and where it was kept)

How many lived in one farmhouse. In the setting a household averages five, across two or three generations. That sits inside what the village registers of the period show. The shumon aratame cho, the yearly registers of every household and its members, put the average for twenty-two villages of Bitchu province in 1870 at a little over six, for fifty-six villages of Echizen at under six, and for two villages near Nihonmatsu at six and a half; the commonest size in Bitchu was five, with a few households of up to twenty-four. Those figures are counted over inhabitants: they say how large a household the average inhabitant lived in, which weighs the big households more heavily. Divided by households, the same Bitchu registers hold 6,737 people, servants aside, in 1,431 households, under five to a household. Earlier the households were larger: in the Suwa region the average halved, from about eight in the seventeenth century to about four in the nineteenth.

Extended families genuinely did share a roof, and the sharing was inside the household, not between two of them. The early large households held dependent farmers and collateral kin under one powerful farmer; as those split off into small farms of their own, the stem family - parents living with one married heir and the heir's children - became the ordinary household. How ordinary varied by region. Counted over inhabitants, over half of those near Nihonmatsu lived in a stem family but under a fifth in Bitchu, where a quarter lived in joint households, two or more married couples of the same generation under one head. Counted by household, which is what a map draws, Bitchu's households were about one in seven stem and one in seven joint.

One family, two roofs: the retirement house. In much of Japan the old couple did not stay under the heir's roof. On inkyo, retirement, they moved out to a small retirement house of their own, and most retirement houses stood inside the family's house plot, with an entrance of their own; where the custom was kept thoroughly, every house had one. Moving out was the main form of retirement, and the two households often kept separate meals and purses - one family living as two households. Where it was done followed a line across the country: the custom was thin from the northeast along the Sea of Japan coast, where the large family held together under one roof, and strong from the Pacific coast through the Inland Sea, above all in Kyushu and Shikoku. Another encyclopedia draws the same line: separate house, meals and property are the common type on the Pacific side west of Kanto and in Kyushu, one house, one table and one purse on the Sea of Japan side and in the northeast.

So a farm family took one of two forms, and both are attested: one roof, the generations together in the farmhouse, or a farmhouse and a retirement house in the same yard. That is a choice between forms, not a degree, so it is rolled per settlement.

The rule the map follows: Each settlement rolls one of two family forms from its seed: the generations under one roof, or a farmhouse with a small retirement house in the same yard, with its own entrance. A retirement house belongs to its farmhouse's household - it is a second roof of the same family, not a household of its own - so it is neither counted toward the households nor against the band of occupied farmhouses (Is every household in a hamlet actually drawn?). How many of a settlement's homesteads keep one where the custom is kept is a guess, and so is how the two forms are weighted against each other in the roll.
