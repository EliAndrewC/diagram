Ways: the research behind the roads, lanes, bridges and planks

The findings behind the roads, lanes, bridges and planks on these maps, the decisions they drove, and - where no generator draws the feature yet - the rules a map follows.

How far past the bank does a bridge land?

Sources: ritter-timber-bridges (what a timber beam's bearing seat measures, and what an abutment holds), nrcs-ts14q-abutments (scour under a small bridge's abutment); the GM's ruling of 2026-08-09; this project's measurement of the drawn decks. Neither gives a distance from the water's edge to the abutment

A bridge does not stop at the water's edge. Its girder bears on an abutment sill set back from the channel edge, because the current undercutting the bank must never reach the bearing: a small bridge stands on a shallow strip footing, too shallow to be sunk below the scour, so the bank is armored and the footing kept clear of it instead.12 Behind the sill the abutment is doing its other work, holding the roadway embankment in place.13 What does NOT account for the landing is the seat the girder bears on: a timber beam's bearing length is measured in inches - 10 to 24 of them under highway loading, on spans up to 94 ft1 - so a foot or two of timber is the whole of it, and the seat cannot be what carries a deck feet past the water. The maps draw 10 ft of deck past the water on each side. That distance is a GUESS: no page a reader can open gives a landing distance for a bridge of this size,14 and what the record can offer for it is the scour setback and the embankment behind the sill, not the seat.

The GM ruled on 2026-08-09 that every corner of a deck stands on dry ground, and the reason is obliquity. Where a way crosses a watercourse at an angle, the water under the deck is wider than the channel by the sine of that angle, and the deck's own width pushes its outer corners further out again before they clear the bank - which is how the capital's east deck over the diagonal river came to have 0.0 ft of landing at its worst corner, the deck the GM saw. The crossing is solved for that angle exactly, and the test is applied to the deck's four real corners rather than to the ends of its centerline, because a corner is what runs out of bank first.

The rule the map follows: every corner of a carried deck stands on dry ground, with about 10 ft of deck past the water on each bank. A standalone footplank is the exception and keeps a deliberately short landing: its ends simply rest on the bank, and giving it a bridge's landing turns it back into a jetty. Both figures are real feet at every tier, converted to the sheet's own scale when drawn.

What vehicle used a village lane, and where could the lane run?

Sources: lowtech-chinese-wheelbarrow (what the Chinese rural network was built for, and who used it), daihachiguruma-jawiki (the Edo hand cart and the three places it was allowed), stone-routes-enwiki (the width of a purpose-built ox-cart route), toyama-1988-road-undevelopment (why the Japanese countryside had no carts, and what the highway prohibition was for), ctie-michi-nazenaze (the width of the Five Highways, and the wider figure near Edo), nippon-com-gokaido (a second, wider account of that width); the GM's rulings of 2026-07-21, 2026-08-12 and 2026-08-27. None of these gives a width for an ordinary village lane

China leads here, as it does everywhere in this setting's geography, with Japan corroborating. The Chinese countryside was deliberately built for the one-wheeled barrow rather than for the cart: its rural network was "narrow paths, scarcely wide enough for foot passengers and wheelbarrows", with rough unpaved cart tracks predominating only on the eastern plains, which is not the rice-growing south our settlements are modeled on.2 Those paths were made for the traffic that used them - pedestrians, porters with carrying poles, pushers of wheelbarrows, and men carrying litters.3 So the vehicle to picture on a village lane is the wheelbarrow and the shoulder-pole porter, and the track is one barrow or one packhorse wide.

Japan says the same thing from the other side. Up to the end of the Edo period the horse-drawn carriage did not exist there at all: inhabitants went on foot or on horseback, and goods moved by human power or on a horse's back.4 Carts were confined to the wide level streets inside the cities, and on the highways their use was forbidden to the very end of the shogunate - to keep the post-horse system alive, the fear being that more vehicles would mean fewer horses.15 Road wear is NOT the reason: the slow light carts a person could push damaged the surface little, and what is named instead for a country that never built a carriage road is its terrain and climate and the kind of horse it kept.16 Heavy freight went by boat. The one wheeled way that was built for carts sets a ceiling rather than a norm: the Edo hand cart (大八車, daihachiguruma) was confined by law to Edo, Owari and Sunpu,5 and the purpose-built ox-cart stone route was 2.7 m wide - 9 shaku, about 9 ft - with a 90 cm track laid for the beast itself.6 The trunk highways were wider again: about 5.4 to 7.2 m (3 to 4 ken) as the ordinary width, with mountain roads expressly left out of that figure, and 9 m (5 ken) where the road ran close to Edo.7 A second account puts the ordinary width at 4 ken (7.2 m) rising in places to 7 or 8 ken (12.6 to 14.4 m), so the two readable accounts bracket the width rather than agree on it.17 What a mountain road narrowed to, and the 2 ken often given for the Hakone barrier, are on no page that was read.18 Paving followed the same split: China had many narrow stone-slab causeways, especially in the wet rice south where a paved way keeps porters and barrows above the mud, while Japan paved only its highways and only on steep passes (this too rests on general reading; no source is cited).8 In neither country was an ordinary village lane a wide two-lane road.

What the record does not give is a number. A search pass of 2026-08-27, made at the GM's request - "I just want to make sure that we did not set it arbitrarily" - read twenty-three pages and found no numeric width for an ordinary village lane or farm path in any language. A figure that circulates as "a rural way is one ken wide" traces to a page that does not say it, and neither the Japanese article on rural ways nor a prefectural authority's own list of questions about them gives a width. So the widths on these maps - 3 ft for a footpath, 5 ft for the cluster's spine and its field spur, 6 ft for the track out to the wider world, at the hamlet's scale of one foot to the pixel - are a map drawing convention, chosen inside the bounds the record does give (wider than nothing, narrower than the 9 ft cart road) and neither corroborated nor contradicted by a measured lane. A survey of a surviving hamlet lane would settle it.

Where a lane may run was ruled on the map. A trodden path never crosses row crops: historically it runs on the baulk between plots or along the edge of the hem, and the GM ruled so on 2026-07-21 after looking at a village's west field spur. It may touch a plot's boundary, since paths hug field margins by design. A limitation was accepted with it: where a staircase of dry plots blocks the way to the paddy and no clear baulk exists, the field track simply stops at the hem's edge, because past it farmers walk the plot baulks. The alternative - routing a detour around the foot of the staircase - was drawn and measured, and it fought the homestead packing on every seed of that map, while the hem-edge stop left the known-clean packing untouched.

The other ruling concerns wet ground. The GM, on 2026-08-12: "there's supposed to be a rule that paths don't pass through marshland". None of these maps draws a causeway, so a track does not cross the reed toe - but it does not have to turn away over the settlement's back either, because the wet toe is only as wide as the ground the fan waters (see the water page), leaving dry footslope at both of its lateral ends.

Two further questions about lanes are answered on the homesteads page: whether every farmhouse is reached by a lane, and in what form, and how a village lane bends.

The rule the map follows: a village lane is a narrow single track of packed earth with a soft worn shoulder and no marking down its middle, never a wide two-lane road. At the hamlet's scale the map draws a footpath 3 ft wide, the spine and the field spur 5 ft, and the connecting track 6 ft; those three widths are a map drawing convention, not a measured figure. A lane is a corridor nothing is built on - farmsteads front it, and no corner of a house stands on its tread. It runs on the baulk between plots or along the hem's edge and never across row crops, and it stops at the flooded paddy, since from there the way into the fields is on foot along the bunds. An important inter-village or pilgrimage path may be a narrow stone causeway; a modest settlement's own lanes are earth.

The rule the map follows: the track that links the settlement to the wider world continues out of the cluster and runs off the edge of the sheet. It never stops in mid-landscape, which reads as a path to nowhere, and it carries no label, because only an Imperial road is labeled. It keeps off wet ground: it leaves downslope past the end of the wet toe, on the flank the cluster already sits on, and turns along the contour only where the fan reaches the frame and leaves no dry ground to pass by. The field-ward spur and the road-ward connector are the two ends of one through-route. The settlement's own inner lanes are exempt from the off-the-edge rule: they legitimately end in the cluster or at the paddy.

What is a plank bridge, and what is it for?

Sources: itabashi-kotobank (what an itabashi is), dobashi-jawiki (how an earthen deck is built); the GM's rulings of 2026-07-22; this project's census of the planks on the shipped maps

The small crossing drawn over a farm ditch is an itabashi (板橋, ita plank + hashi bridge): a bridge made of boards,9 a planked timber deck with the seams running across it, which is what the glyph draws. It is not a dobashi (土橋), an earthen deck - logs laid side by side with no gaps, then earth thrown over them and trodden down until it fills the hollows and the surface is level10 - and the two are different objects rather than two names for one. The name matters because the glyph has to keep its meaning when a reader clicks it.

What such a plank is FOR is the whole of where it may be drawn. It is the board field-workers lay to cross an irrigation ditch while walking the paddy bunds - so no path leads to one, because the way to it is the bund, and it crosses its ditch square rather than obliquely (this rests on general reading; no source is cited). It is laid only over water one cannot stride across; the width at which a household bothers to build one, and the GM's ruling behind that width, are on the water page. And because it exists so that someone can cross TO something, both of its banks must land on ground worth crossing to - cultivated ground, wet or dry, the settlement itself, or a walked dike - and never on reed marsh, on scrub commons, or off the edge of the map.

That last rule came from a census rather than from a single bad plank. Asked about one useless crossing on 2026-07-22, this project counted them across the whole pool and found the defect everywhere: the drainage toes of the water-first maps and the diagonal edge-drains along every field's outer boundary all carried planks stepping straight into swamp or scrub. The rule clears them pool-wide, while every field-to-field, dry-to-wet and settlement-to-field crossing stands. A ditch running along a field's outer margin with nothing on its far side now carries no plank at all: nothing to cross to needs no crossing.

Its size is a ruling, not a finding. The GM cut both the width and the length on 2026-07-22: a footplank is a single-file crossing about 4 ft wide over a ditch of roughly 8 ft, with a short abutment at each end, and a board any wider or longer reads as a jetty. It is kept both narrower and shorter than a cart lane on purpose, because the deck that carries a way is as wide as the way it carries, and a footplank that reached that width would read as a road bridge.

The rule the map follows: a standalone plank crosses its ditch square, at about the middle of a long stretch, and a very long stretch takes further planks at intervals along it; both of its ends land on cultivated ground, on the settlement, or on a walked dike, and a plank that can find no such spot is not drawn. No path leads to it. A ditch stretch whose far bank is marsh or scrub for its whole length carries none.

The rule the map follows: a plank never sits on a farmhouse. A bridge is allowed to lie on the water it spans - that is what it is for - but its deck is tested against every house on the map, and a plank near the settlement's edge slides along its own ditch, staying over the water, until it clears them.

Where does a village's freight go? Onto the water - but not onto a canal

Sources: chinaknowledge-caoyun (the tribute-grain transport and the canal that carried it); the Japanese half rests on general reading and is not cited; the GM's setting notes, which are canon for this setting rather than evidence about history

In Ming China bulk goods - above all the tribute rice - moved by canal and river boat, and the Grand Canal was the spine of it: eight provinces were ordered in 1472 to send four million shi of rice a year up it to the terminal outside Beijing, and sea carriage was given up in its favor.11 Japan's heavy freight likewise went by water (this rests on general reading; no source is cited). Land carriage was for what a barrow or a packhorse could take.

The setting departs from the Chinese model here, deliberately and by the GM's own canon. Artificial transport canals are not an Empire-wide feature: they belong to the Lion, who embrace the Chinese precedent, while the other clans rely on natural water or on land and simply have no need of them. Crane lands are coastal and never far from the ocean; the Dragon have the Drowned Merchant River; the Unicorn have the Firefly River valley and are in any case horse-focused; Scorpion holdings are smaller. So for a settlement in this setting the rule inverts: where there is navigable natural water, that water is the main freight route and the settlement gets a boat landing as its primary link to the world - but an artificial transport canal is never the default.

One more piece of canon shapes what a road on these maps means. The Imperial trunk network is unusually well kept: tens of thousands of miles of Imperial road with thousands of staffed waystations, maintained by corvee - the peasantry owes ten to twenty days a year, called for most often to repair the roads after the spring thaws and the autumn storms - and road tolls were outlawed by an Emperor. That is the trunk network, though, and none of it reaches down to a settlement's own lanes: those, and the local connector, stay humble tracks. An Imperial road, where a map shows one, may read as a maintained road - earth or gravel, and wider - plainly distinct from the village track.

The rule the map follows: a settlement standing on navigable natural water - a river or the coast - gets a boat landing or jetty as its main freight link. A transport canal is drawn only for a map in Lion lands or when the GM asks for one; it is never added because a settlement needs a way to move goods. A settlement with no navigable water beside it moves its freight by its land track, and that track is legitimately its main connector.

Is the bridge where the road actually crosses the water?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-27; this project's measurement of every deck on the two drawn provincial cities

Yes. The GM, looking at a city map on 2026-07-27: "the bridge by the cargo basin is NOT aligned with the road, which results in not only the bridge being crooked but the road just running over the water". A deck that stands near its crossing but at the wrong angle is precisely what a reader sees as a road running through the water past a crooked plank, so nearness alone is not enough: the deck has to lie on the crossing and run along the way.

Why it had happened is worth keeping, because it is the shape of the failure rather than one map's mistake. The ring road was not counted as a way that carries traffic and the cargo canal was not counted as a watercourse, so the automatic pass could not see that crossing at all, and both cities had their deck placed by hand at design coordinates. Hand-placed coordinates then drift: when the first city's ring road was re-derived from its own budget the deck stayed where it had been typed, ending about 51 ft and 39 degrees off its crossing, and the second city's about 45 ft and 24 degrees off.

The tolerance now applied comes from measuring the difference. Every deck the engine solves lands within about a yard and a degree of its crossing - rounding, and nothing else - so asking for roughly 24 ft and 8 degrees separates a solved deck from an eyeballed one by two orders of magnitude, with no honest deck anywhere near the line.

One adjacency that looks like a drawing error is not. Where a cargo canal enters a walled city through a water gate, the ring road necessarily crosses it a few paces inside the arch, because the ring runs at a fixed inset from the rampart - so the deck and the water gate share ground, which is the Suzhou pattern, where the wall road bridges each canal at its water gate (this rests on general reading; no source is cited). It is recorded as a deliberate mount, and shortening the deck does not separate the two.

The rule the map follows: wherever a way - a road, a ring road, a street or a lane - crosses a watercourse, a railed timber deck carries it over, drawn above both the water and the roadbed and oriented along the way. Every such crossing is bridged: a way does not simply run through open water. A way that merely runs alongside water needs no bridge, and a buried conduit recorded only for the water's topology is not a crossing at all, since there is no seam on the ground. Each deck lies on its crossing, within a few paces of it, and runs along its way within 8 degrees; a deck with no crossing beneath it does not belong on the map. Standalone footplanks are outside this rule - no way carries them, and they cross their ditch square by construction.
