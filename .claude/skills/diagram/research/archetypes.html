Field archetypes: the research behind the overlay, polder and dike-pond rules

The research behind the field archetypes - the crop overlays, the polder and the dike-pond: what was found, the decision it drove, and any deliberate departure from literal reality.

Why rape (油菜) was tried and removed

Sources: aburana-jawiki (autumn sowing, March-April cutting; rape into the harvested paddy), rice-rape-calendar-summary (the rape-harvest and rice-transplanting windows3). The rice months and the Jiangnan rotation calendar were not found on any page read, and the sowing window "late Sept-mid Oct" is unsourced

RAPE (油菜, Japanese aburana, Chinese youcai - the oilseed) never stands beside rice: rice and rape are the two halves of one rotation in the SAME plot (rice May-Oct (the rice harvest month is unsourced - the footnoted source gives transplanting and rape-harvest dates but no harvest month);3 rape sown into the drained stubble2 Oct-Nov, flowering Mar-Apr),1 so they are never both standing - mixing them at any fraction depicts two seasons at once, and the real spring picture is yellow rape against BARE stubble, not against green rice. Showing rape would mean showing a whole different season of the same field, which these maps do not do.

Overlay extent - a calibrated liberty, disclosed

Sources: the lotus area share (a few percent to 10-15%) was found nowhere on 2026-08-28 (zh.wikipedia 莲藕 silent; a provincial share of aquatic-vegetable OUTPUT is the nearest figure) - the interpolation stands as a guess and is labeled one; the liberty is the GM's (2026-07-19)

A CALIBRATED LIBERTY is recorded here (GM 2026-07-19): the wet low band an overlay may cover is taken as the bottom two levels of the valley floor rather than only the single hem along the drain. How wide a valley bottom's wet backswamp ran is unrecorded, and the researched extent for a lotus-growing village (a few percent to ~10-15% of field area) is itself interpolated from a national average that includes regions growing none. The single-hem reading put lotus at ~2% - inside the range, but so sparse that it stopped making villages look different from one another, which is the whole reason the overlay exists. We chose the upper part of a plausible range for a stated non-historical reason (legibility) and disclosed it.

The three overlay values - mulberry_fishpond, lotus, tea_fringe

Sources: fortune-1843

  • The mulberry fishpond (桑基魚塘, sangji yutang) - dug out of 低洼易有洪患之处, the low flood-prone hollows, as a flood adaptation that drained the hollow while raising the dike.4 A scatter of dike-ponds among rice was the system's NORMAL state. Shunde county was ~4.6% dike-pond in 1581 (Longshan, its earliest pond district, 18% - 8,124 of 44,947 mu);79 a 1980s survey of the heartland found rice at 25% against the fishponds' own 35%;78 and at Lake Tai mulberry sat on the pond (tang) banks with rice remaining the polder's main crop permanently.5 Gazetteers found total absence of rice remarkable enough to record.6 The wall-to-wall landscape is the rare end state of a ~300-year plot-by-plot process7 (挖塘培基, wa tang pei ji - dig one low plot into a pond, pile the spoil into a dike;8 a one-household, one-dry-season job), which is what the whole-landscape mulberry-dike-fishpond ARCHETYPE is for. The archetype and the overlay are two SCALES of one system, not duplicates. The archetype opts out of the topographic filter, since at that scale the ponds really had engulfed the ordinary ground too. Negative result worth keeping: the outer-delta 沙田 shatian were equally low and equally wet and stayed in rice - low ground is necessary but not sufficient; the dike-pond zone also had smallholder tenure inside an established polder and water access to Canton.
  • The lotus field (藕田, outian) - models deep-water lotus (深水藕, shenshui ou, 30-50cm, tolerating ~1m) against paddy rice's ~5-9cm optimum, so it physically cannot sit on high ground. Shallow-water lotus (浅水藕, qianshui ou, 10-20cm) in ordinary paddy is equally real but is deliberately NOT modeled: it is an economic choice rotating with rice (稻藕轮作, dao ou lunzuo, the rice-lotus rotation), driven by a choice these maps do not model, and drawing it would be visually indistinguishable from uniform random scatter. Honest limitation: the agronomy is solid but pre-modern village-scale practice is not directly sourced - the clearest conversions in the record (Kasumigaura 1970s, contemporary Vietnam) are modern and policy-driven. Extent where present is a few percent up to maybe 10-15% of field area; that range is interpolation, not a sourced figure.
  • The tea fringe (茶, cha) - tea took the lower-to-mid FERTILE hillside, never the low lands and never the barren upper slope (Robert Fortune, eyewitness, 1843: "always situated on the lower and most fertile sides of the hills, and never on the low lands";9 the upper slopes were forest, sweet potato and peanut (unsourced - Fortune's tea passage says nothing about the slope above it, and his sweet-potato/earth-nut sentences describe the coastal plain and inland ground near Amoy, not the upper slope)). The boundary rule states itself - the line is the highest irrigation ditch. Form is smallholder: recognizable rows in acre-scale patches, one small garden to a cottager,10 dotted across a hillside, not one contiguous block. Two anachronisms to avoid: neatly contour-terraced tea is post-1949, a state program; and bund-margin tea (畦畔茶, keihan-cha) is a Japanese practice with no Chinese equivalent.

Polder parcels were a private-tenure patchwork

Sources: cssn-jiangnan-weitian (the tangpu canal grid at five-to-ten li), sdlib-shunde-jitang (ponds dug and dikes piled plot by plot)

The research upheld the instinct: the surveyed rectilinear chessboard of the Taihu tangpu 塘浦 system was the CANAL grid at kilometer scale,11 while the parcels inside it were a private-tenure patchwork - mid-Qing Jiangnan farms averaged ~10 mu scattered over several parcels; Buck's pre-mechanization survey (1929-33) found a mean parcel of ~1 mu, rectangular only where it fronted a straight ditch (unsourced - the archive.org item is borrow-only, and a fetch returns only its catalog title, which says nothing about parcel size or shape); dike-ponds accreted household-by-household (挖塘培基, wa tang pei ji, dig the pond and pile the dike13), so 66 identical ponds contradict the map's own backstory. Uniform machine-sized rectangles are a 20th-century consolidation look (Japan's 1963 hojo seibi 30m x 100m standard; PRC consolidation campaigns).

The perimeter dike followed the natural water edge

Sources: sdlib-shunde-jitang (the dike as pond spoil, planted), miragenews-polders (fish-scale polders named for their aerial outlines; organic forms replaced by standardized rectangles after 1949)

What the research found: a wei-tian 圩田 / dike-pond dike was dredged pond-mud heaped and packed (the 挖塘培基 dig-and-pile cycle that also formed the ponds),14 trapezoidal in section, PLANTED with mulberry/willow/crops that bind the soil,15 walked and lived on, and constantly breached-and-repaired16 - so it read as a mottled, vegetated green-brown band of VARYING width, not a uniform stroke. Crucially, the plan form splits: the surveyed interior ditch/paddy grid was genuinely rectilinear, but the OUTER perimeter dike followed the natural water edge (lake / creek / marsh) in gentle curves and non-square bends - the "fish-scale polder" (鱼鳞圩, yulin wei) form named for exactly those irregular overlapping outlines.17 The dead-straight right-angled rectangle is a post-1949 industrial shape (heritage scholars: "the organic forms of polders were replaced by standardized rectangular patterns"),18 so a dead-straight rectangular polder would be the one genuinely anachronistic thing on the map.

Why dikes were planted, and what the row spacing rests on

Sources: willow-palisade

Why dikes were planted at all: a bare bank of heaped dredge-mud is a liability - rain gullies it, wave wash gnaws the waterside toe, and a slump becomes a breach - so planting the dike was the standard remedy wherever these earthworks existed: the roots bind the packed mud. The species split by role: WILLOW on the water face (thrives with wet feet; its root mat armors the bank against wave wash; pollarded for withies - willow-fascine bank protection is an ancient Chinese technique, in use by the last century BC), and in sericulture districts MULBERRY on the crest/inner face20 - the dike was elevated, well-drained, annually re-fertilized by the dredge cycle,21 i.e. prime planting ground in a landscape that is otherwise water (Lake Tai mulberry sat on the pond banks). A dike that looks bare and cleanly engineered is the modern-concrete-era tell. What the numbers rest on: dike planting was ROW planting along the alignment, not scatter. The closest attested in-row figure is the Qing Willow Palisade statute - willow whips planted every 5 chi (~1.67 m ~ 5.5 ft) in rows on an embankment19 - corroborated by modern willow-fascine practice descending from the same mechanics (rows 1-1.5 m apart on erosive soil, 1.5-2 m on cohesive). A polder-dike-specific statute was NOT found; the Willow Palisade figure is an ANALOG from a border embankment, recorded here as such, not as polder canon.

Why dike willows do NOT replace the village windbreak

Sources: usu-windbreak (shelter up to 30 H - the entry's 10-20 H is inside it), yashikirin-jawiki (igune on the north and west; species スギ・マツ・ヒノキ・ケヤキ)

Why the dike willows do NOT replace the village windbreak (GM 2026-07-24, asked and answered): a windbreak's sheltered zone runs ~10-20x barrier HEIGHT,22 and the homestead belt is full-height dense planting for exactly that reason, while dike willows are POLLARDED low because the withies are the harvest - a single porous row at rooftop height shelters almost nothing at the houses' distance. Worse, willow and mulberry are DECIDUOUS:23 bare sticks during the NW winter monsoon, the one season shelter matters, which is why homestead windbreaks ran dense, multi-row and conifer-dominated (the igune of the flat Sendai plain, on the north and west against the winter wind,24 are sugi, pine and hinoki with keyaki25 - the same reason; that they persisted amid abundant field-edge trees is unsourced). And an east-dike village sits downwind of the whole pond sheet - open water is the smoothest fetch there is, so the wind reaches the dike at full strength. Utility rows on the dike and a dense grove at the homestead are DIFFERENT plants doing DIFFERENT jobs; real polder landscapes carried both.

The ring canal runs on the INNER toe - 一河围田

Sources: cssn-jiangnan-weitian (「channels within, sluice gates without」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「中有河渠，外有门闸」) - channels inside the dike, gates through it, opened in drought and shut in flood), weinei-he-summary (the general dike-and-canal chessboard pattern80). The 一河围田 shorthand, the inside-toe placement and the crescent 月样 / bow 弓样 forms are unsourced - not found on any page read (zh.wikipedia 圩田 silent; the weinei-he page carries none of the crescent/bow forms or gated-ends language)

What the research found: the polder's trunk distribution+collection canal rings the block on the INSIDE toe of the perimeter dike, on the FIELD side (Jiangnan shorthand 一河围田, yi he wei tian, "one river surrounds the field"; cross-section outside->in: wild water -> dike -> inner ring canal 圩内河 (weineihe) -> field ditches 浜 (bang) -> paddies). Outside the dike is the wild lake/creek the dike holds back, so NO irrigation channel runs out there; water crosses the dike ONLY through gated sluices (斗门 doumen / 水閘 shuizha / 涵洞 handong - gate, lock and culvert) cut at discrete points26 (the inlet + outfall), never as an open cut over the dike body. And the trunk line is organized-but-organic: long runs that read straight-ish but GENTLY WAVY (a surveyed dug canal wavers with terrain and repair; crescent (月样, yueyang) / bow (弓样, gongyang) trunk forms are named options in the literature (unsourced - not found on any page read)), with rounded corners rather than hard 90-degree turns - the finer laterals are visibly crookeder.

Wet-rice hydrology has no crossings to draw

Sources: fusekoshi-jawiki (the inverted siphon under a watercourse, with historical examples), suirokyo-jawiki (the aqueduct bridge)

Why any crossing is wrong here: wet-rice hydrology has no crossings to draw - a ditch either feeds another or is fed by it, and where two courses genuinely had to pass at different levels the builders put in27 an aqueduct28 or an inverted siphon, a distinct structure these maps do not draw.

Grid vs mosaic - the arrangement differed by system

Sources: cssn-jiangnan-weitian (the tangpu lattice at five-to-seven and seven-to-ten li). The Pearl-delta "mosaic-like ... boundary blurred" description has no publicly readable source81, and neither has the dikes' erosion from ~20 m to under 4 m (the 1967-2016 growth of the ponds at the dikes' expense rests on a page that could not be read,82 and the 20 m / 4 m magnitude is not on that record either) - both are GUESSES

What the research found: the answer is BOTH, and it splits by system. The individual ponds genuinely WERE rectangular oblongs29 - hand-dug from marsh (挖塘培基: dig the pond, pile the spoil into the dike),30 the recurring descriptor a "chessboard" of "rectangular-shaped dikes... with water in between" - because you dig a pond in whatever packs the space and straight dikes are easiest to pile, walk, and plant mulberry on. So all-rectangular ponds are correct; this is the rare farm landscape that does NOT grow organically around the terrain, because the farmers MADE the terrain. BUT the arrangement differed by system. The lower-Yangtze 圩田 (wei-tian) was a SURVEYED rectilinear grid - the Song-era 塘浦 tangpu lattice, deliberately engineered31 - so a clean grid is historically right for it. The Pearl-delta 桑基魚塘 (dike-pond) instead accreted household-by-household into a MOSAIC; the landscape-ecology literature describes the historical delta not as a uniform chessboard but as "mosaic-like constructed ponds with meandering natural river systems, [with] the boundary between constructed and natural blurred" - rectangles, yes, but of varied sizes at varied local orientations fitted around winding interior creeks (the perfectly-uniform grid is closer to the MODERN consolidated look, which the same sources note eroded the dikes considerably - ponds grew while dikes shrank 1967-201682 (the specific ~20 m to under 4 m magnitude is unsourced)).

The 6:4 water-to-dike ratio, and coppiced mulberry

Sources: gd-gazetteer-sangji (the water-to-dike split 水基比, shui ji bi, 「a split of three to seven up to four to six」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「三七至四六开」)),83 fao-ac241e (6:4; 1:1 where the worms alone feed the fish),34 isis-dykepond (carrying Ruddle & Zhong's figures - the monograph itself has no publicly readable copy84 - for the 0.4-0.6 ha ponds and 6-10 m dikes),37 cssn-sangji-yutang (「six parts dike, four parts pond」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「基六塘四」)), kuwa-jawiki (mulberry kept as a low shrub). The coppiced-bush density (one bush per 10-20 sq ft, crowns 4-6 ft) and the bushes' height are unsourced - a GUESS

The classic dike-pond prescription was an explicit area ratio of 6 parts water to 4 parts dike32 (some districts 7:3);33 measured on the Kuwabata map, water is 76% of each parcel (bank 24%) and 50% of the whole block once the shared 22 ft dikes and canal corridors count - bracketing 6:4. The band is not marginal ground: each pond's ~6,300 sq ft (~0.14 ac) bank was the SILK side of the loop (leaf36 -> silkworm -> frass -> fish -> dredged pond mud -> dike fertility), and the ratio existed because too much water starves the silkworms and too much dike starves the fish.35

Silkworm mulberry was not grown as trees: it was COPPICED into low bushes38 (crowns ~4-6 ft) so the leaf could be stripped several times a year, planted in dense rows on the dike at roughly one bush per 10-20 sq ft - i.e. 300-600 bushes per pond, tens of thousands across the map. At 1 px = 1 ft a crown is a 2-4 px dot and adjacent crowns nearly touch, so drawing the actual trees at honest density IS a packed dot band; individually distinguishable tree glyphs would mean 3-5x crown inflation, forbidden on to-scale maps. No berries either: leaf-stripped coppice barely fruits, and a mulberry fruit is sub-pixel at this scale - crown color carries the read.

The ORDER of the ratio is contested: the one page read writes the classic prescription as 「six parts dike, four parts pond」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「基六塘四」) - six parts DIKE to four parts POND39, while a people.cn page seen only in a search summary has 「six parts as pond, four parts as dike」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「六分为塘，四分为基」) - six water to four dike, the reading Kuwabata is drawn to at 76% water per parcel. The literature carries both orders and a 7:3 variant, so this may be a regional split rather than an error.

GM ruling (2026-08-28) - a DEVIATION, a disclosed regional reading: Kuwabata keeps its 6 water : 4 dike parcels. The record carries the prescription in BOTH orders - 「six parts dike, four parts pond」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「基六塘四」) on the page read (cssn-sangji-yutang), 六分为塘、四分为基 in the people.cn summary - and a 7:3 variant; the map follows the water-heavy reading, and says so on every pond and bank of Kuwabata: "a regional reading; the classic prescription is also recorded as six parts dike to four parts pond".

A dike-pond is fed and drained through sluice gates

Sources: fao-x6708e (the sluice-gate definition), cssn-sangyuanwei (the polder's 窦, dou, its sluice),42 cssn-jiangnan-weitian (gates through the dike, worked by season - the polder case).43 The inlet-high / outlet-low plumbing comes from Ruddle & Zhong (Cambridge 1988), which has no publicly readable copy - unsourced

What the research found: a 桑基魚塘 pond is NOT a sealed basin - each connects to the creek/canal network through a SLUICE GATE, "a protected opening in the pond dike that can be easily closed with wooden boards to regulate water level"40 and, by pulling the boards, drain the pond at harvest.41 And a pond on a slope is plumbed INLET-HIGH, OUTLET-LOW so water flows downhill through it (the whole dike-pond net runs in series from a high intake to a low outfall). So the channels do not "irrigate" the ponds paddy-style; they are the conveyance-and-drainage network the ponds exchange water with.

Polder siting - full enclosure, fluctuating water, and where the village sits

Sources: fei-xiaotong

(1) Does the dike really surround the paddies completely? Yes - full enclosure IS the defining feature, not a drawing convenience. A weitian is wetland "enclosed with dykes to be hydrologically separated from the surrounding fluctuating water and then drained"44 (the character 圩 means the enclosing embankment); Shen Kuo (Northern Song, Illustrated Records of Wanchun Polder) is credited with describing "dikes and dams... built along the rivers to enclose the farmland, which is called a polder" (unsourced -the zh.wikipedia 万春圩 page carries no such quotation and no sentence about enclosure; it records only the polder's history - built by the Qin family in the late Tang, rebuilt by 14,000 laborers in 90 days, with Shen Kuo possibly taking part and writing the 万春圩图说). The enclosed floor sits at or below the surrounding FLOOD stage, so on a flat lake plain there is no safe side to leave open - any gap re-floods the whole block at the next high water, which is why water crosses only at the two gated sluices. The scale is attested too: fragmented small polders were each "cultivated by a group of 10-20 households" behind dikes ~1.5 m high (unsourced -the quotation and the dike height are on no registry page read, Fei's text included) - Enokida's 16 households are exactly this unit. (2) Should there be marsh/wetland on all sides then? The outside is "fluctuating water," but that means neither open lake on every side nor water year-round. Attested surroundings: the lake/creek network the polder was dug from; reed marsh and mudflat where reclamation had not yet reached; NEIGHBOR polders across a shared creek (the mature Taihu landscape was "islet-like" fish-scale polders packed into the water net);45 and - at the district margin - the natural shore, because reclamation advanced FROM the shore ("mudflats along rivers and lakes were transformed" (unsourced -the quotation is on no registry page read)). The water also FLUCTUATES seasonally: at low stage much of the outside is exposed mudflat and reed, at flood it is sheet water - the dike is built against the flood stage, not the average day. Both pool polders are drawn as the landward-margin case: wild header pond NW (the source), reed marsh at the low south outfall side, village on naturally dry ground east. GM ruling (2026-07-24): both polders carry a reed fringe along the west flank, so the map shows that the west dike holds water back. (3) Should the farmhouses be INSIDE the dike? Both siting patterns are attested, split by what dry ground exists. Where the polder abuts the natural shore, the village sits on the landward dry ground at the dike's shoulder - the way both maps are drawn, and the natural one for a margin polder (nobody lives on a flood-fighting earthwork when real dry ground is a few steps east). In the DEEP-water landscape with no natural dry ground, settlement used the polder's own raised earth instead: linear villages ON the dikes (canal-dike settlement "taking advantage of the elevated typology" is attested from the 8th century on (unsourced -neither the quotation nor the settlement date is on a registry page read; the one page read that names the eighth century dates the tangpu polders themselves to it, not villages on their dikes)) or houses inside the polder along its interior streams (Fei Xiaotong's Kaixiangong / the Xichang polder layout).46 So Enokida and Kuwabata are correct AS margin polders - but an "islet" polder map, water-ringed on all sides, would REQUIRE dike-top or interior housing. GM ruling (2026-07-24): dike-top housing is attested and is available as a variation the GM has not yet taken up; the GM chose NOT to convert any existing settlement. One more confirmation from the same research: the polder community IS its dike-maintenance community ("sustaining a polder necessitated a close-knit community," often one extended family, which named its polder) (unsourced -the quotation is on no registry page read) - so a polder village always sits hard against its own block, which is what the map's cluster-hugging placement already enforces.

What stands on a dike-pond hamlet that a paddy hamlet lacks - the audit

Sources: fei-1939, fao-ac264e, isis-dykepond (carrying Ruddle & Zhong's Pearl-delta figures), cssn-sangyuanwei, cssn-sangji-yutang, dili360-2005-sangji

What the question was. The GM (2026-08-27), looking at the rice hamlet's outhouses and the rest: "this is a different type of map ... I could imagine there being map features which would exist in Kuwabata but not in Inashiro." The question was what PHYSICAL features stood on a silk-and-fish hamlet's ground - farmsteads, dikes, water's edge - that a rice hamlet's did not, and which rice features it should lack.

What the research found. The one primary ethnography of a silk village that could be read in full is Fei Hsiao-tung's Peasant Life in China (1939), on Kaihsienkung south of Lake Tai - a rice-AND-silk village on a stream net,47 not a converted dike-pond block, so it answers the SILK half of the loop directly and the FISH half only by the Pearl-delta secondary literature.

  • Silkworms were reared INSIDE the dwelling, not in a separate building. "Silkworms are raised in rectangular and shallow containers about 1.5 by 1 metre in size. They are put on the shelves of a stand. Each stand has eight containers. Each house has enough room for five stands."48 And during the two-week peak "all the rooms except the kitchen may be used for sheltering silkworms. All the members of the household will crowd in one bed-room."49 The front room "is used for working, such as raising silkworms, manufacturing silk, threshing rice"50; the old reeling machine ("a furnace to boil water, a wheel ... a rotating axle connected to a plate for treading") stood in the house too.51 So a sericulture hamlet has no silkworm house and no reeling shed: the record puts both indoors. (Communal rearing halls (蚕房, canfang) are a 1950s collective form - the Shunde examples found are dated 1958 - and are not premodern.)
  • Boats, and a village laid out along its water. "Nearly every household possesses one or more boats except those who are not engaged in agricultural or fishing work."52 "The importance of the boat in communication means that the houses must be near the water and consequently determines the plan of the village. Villages grow up along the streams."53 "Every village has its own agent boat which serves as the buying and selling agent of the villagers in the town market."54 The Pearl-delta proverb of the Sangyuanwei polder says the same of silk: 「a boat of silk goes out, a boat of silver comes back」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「一船蚕丝出，一船白银归」)55 - a boat of silk out, a boat of silver back. A cash-crop hamlet that sells silk and fish and buys grain in is a hamlet that LIVES by water transport. Kuwabata as drawn has a header reservoir and a ring canal and no navigable water at all. It was put to the GM as a candidate (a creek along the landward flank with boats and a landing) and DECLINED (2026-08-28): "would it necessarily be the case that any settlement of this sort would have one. I wouldn't think so" - Kaihsienkung's water is that village's geography, not the archetype's; the hamlet's connector lane leads to the market, or to whatever water carries its goods there, and the map presumes rather than draws it.
  • Manure PITS, not heaps. "Human manure, being the most important fertilizer in the farm, is preserved in the pits made of earthenware, half buried in the ground at the back of the building. Along the southern bank of the Stream A, the public road is lined up with these manure pits."56 Two attested forms for the same fixture: the rice hamlet (Inashiro) draws a heap, and a roadside pit would be a distinct thing to draw.
  • Mulberry on the raised margin, houses beside it, vegetables beneath it. "Along the margin of each yu, ten to thirty metres of land is left for plantation of mulberry trees and a wider space for house building. This land on the margin is higher. It also serves as a dyke for the farm."57 "Limited space under the mulberry trees for growing vegetables."58 Kuwabata draws exactly this: the planted perimeter dike, the village on the dry flank.
  • Sheep huts (Fei: "little huts for the sheep and sometimes also for storage", "the sheep hut has become a common appendage of a house") are a 1920s-30s Lake Tai innovation ("in the past ten years"59) - so none stands on a premodern map.
  • Pigs and ducks ON the pond dikes - the Pearl-delta loop. Ruddle & Zhong (1988)84 as carried by the i-sis summary: "Pigs, chickens and ducks are reared on the dykes, to provide manure to fertilise the fishponds"60; the modern FAO/NACA manual: "The simple pig shed constructed on the pond dyke or over the water surface" lets the excreta flow to the pond;61 fish-cum-duck ponds fence a dry run on the dike and a wet run in a corner of the water.62 The premodern prevalence is not in anything read; the specific proverb 蔗榨糖后的蔗渣喂猪 was not found on any page fetched, but isis-dykepond records sugar-cane leaves fed to fish and pigs and refinery waste returned to the dyke pond as feed,85 and cssn-sangji-yutang records farmers building frames over the pond to raise pigs,86 which puts pigs in the traditional loop. A pig sty on a pond dike and a duck pen at a pond corner are both attested forms, then, though how common they were before the modern period is unquantified; neither is drawn.
  • Fish fry (鱼花, yuhua) as a trade, and nursery ponds. Jiujiang township (Nanhai) was the delta's fry center from the Ming (Miles 2003, which has no publicly readable copy: the township's rise 1395-1657 on fry collected from the Xijiang)87; the Sangyuanwei proverb 「the men trade fish fry, the women feed the silkworms」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「男贩鱼花，妇女喂爱蚕」) - men trade fry, women feed the worms63. Fry are reared in small ponds before stocking the grow-out ponds, so a few of a block's smallest parcels would be its fry ponds.
  • Other dike crops: sugar cane, fruit, banana (蔗基 / 果基 / 蕉基, zheji / guoji / jiaoji). The 1980s survey carried by i-sis: sugar-cane dikes 18% of the district against mulberry 1264%; 中国国家地理 2005 on the lost landscape: 蕉林蔗海 (jiaolin zhehai, banana groves and a sea of cane65); gd-gazetteer-sangji, the gazetteer office: dikes planted 桑、蔗、蕉 (mulberry, cane, banana).83 Attested alternative FORMS of the dike planting, none of them a plant on any map yet; Kuwabata itself is the mulberry case by name.
  • The dike-pond's own sluices - already drawn ('A dike-pond is fed and drained through sluice gates', above).
  • Pond-side huts (塘头屋, tangtouwu / a watchman's hut): searched twice in Chinese and English, nothing attested.
  • A silk-goddess shrine (蚕花娘娘 Canhua Niangniang, 蚕神庙 canshen miao): the Huzhou-Jiaxing festival temples are VILLAGE and town institutions (the Qingming 轧蚕花 gatherings, ya canhua, the crowded silkworm-flower fair); a hamlet has no shrine by the tier's own definition, so a shrine of this kind belongs on a village map.
  • What a no-rice hamlet should LACK. Fei's village threshed rice "in the open space in front of the house, or in the front room"66 - the yard's threshing use is a RICE use. Kuwabata draws a threshing yard on every farmstead as the rice hamlet does; with no rice within its bounds the yard is at best a work yard (cocoons are sorted and leaves handled somewhere). The straw rick is already not drawn. The in-field pocket pond, the dry hem and the comb deliveries are absent by archetype already.
  • Holdings. Not settled: nothing read gives a silk-and-fish household's ground, so the ground per household (the paddy hamlet's 1.3 ac) stays a labeled GUESS on this archetype.

Departures and honesty. The gazetteer office frames 桑、蔗、蕉 (mulberry, cane, banana) as a SUCCESSION of dike-pond types (the mulberry dike 桑基 giving way to fruit, cane and vegetable dikes, 果基、蔗基、菜基, over the region's history), not one dike carrying all three - so a hamlet would be of one dike type rather than mixing crops on a dike. Fei is Lake Tai, 1936; the Pearl-delta reads are 1980s surveys and a 2006 summary of the 1988 monograph, which itself could not be fetched (403 on every host) - every Ruddle & Zhong number here rests on the summary, not the monograph. No single source describes a WHOLLY converted hamlet's farmsteads; the list is assembled across the two systems and says so per item.

What a settlement IS, and what the place card may say about it

The interactive page's title card describes the settlement itself, so it makes claims the feature writeups never had to: what kind of place this is, how many live here, and whether that kind is ordinary. The research came back mostly NEGATIVE - which is the finding.

Branch hamlets under a parent village are a real and common form, and the mechanism is attested. Edo Japan called the parent settlement the hongo and the newly founded one the edago: ja.wikipedia 枝郷: 「Whereas the original village was called the hongo (本郷; also motogo 元郷, oyago 親郷, oyamura 親村, honmura 本村), the new village was called the edago (枝郷; also edamura 枝村, komura 子村)」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「もとの村を本郷（本郷、元郷、親郷、親村、本村）とよぶのに対し、新村を枝郷（枝村、子村）と称した」) and 「In many cases the village assessed yield (murataka) was apportioned from the hongo's shoya and nanushi」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「多くの場合、本郷の庄屋・名主から村高が分け与えられていた」).70 The National Diet Library's reference service gives the fiscal half: 「In the assessment and collection of the nengu land tax, the cadastral register and the tax-rate notice stood in the parent village's name, and the small village could make its tax payment only through the parent village」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「年貢の賦課徴収では検地帳・年貢免定は親村の名義で、小村の年貢納入は 親村を通してしかできませんでした」) - the land registers and the tax quota stood in the PARENT village's name,71 and the branch could pay only through it. That is the reason a branch hamlet has no headman of its own standing: there is no separate tax unit for one to administer.

But the record ranks NOTHING by frequency, so neither does the map. No page read gives a national count or share of any settlement form; a J-STAGE study of Kishu shows only that multi-hamlet administrative villages were commoner in mountains than on plains.67 The GM asked whether a rice-farming hamlet is "the most common type of hamlet that exists or whatever" - and nothing answers it, at either scope. Decision: the card makes no historical ranking. It states the SETTING's arithmetic, which is decisive for these maps and is the GM's own: the setting notes' median-domain table gives ~1,296 hamlets to ~216 villages and ~40% of a domain's inhabitants living in one.72 It ranks no kind of hamlet at all.

Wet-rice dominance: not found, and kokudaka is a trap. Every source that would give the paddy-versus-dry-field land share either did not address it or could not be fetched. Kokudaka looks like evidence and is not - en.wikipedia: "The amount of taxation was not based on the actual quantity of rice harvested, but was an estimate based on the total economic yield of the land in question, with the value of other crops and produce converted to their equivalent value in terms of rice."73 A fiscal convention covering dry field too. Decision: the card says this hamlet farms rice because the map draws rice, and claims no dominance.

"A hamlet has no headman of its own" is CANON, and a deliberate deviation from history. The same 枝郷 entry records the exceptions: 「There were also edago in which a shoya, a kumigashira and the like were installed and which received treatment on a par with the hongo」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「中には庄屋や組頭などが置かれ、本郷並の扱いを受けていた枝郷も存在した」) - some branch hamlets had their own shoya and kumigashira74 and were treated on a par with the parent. So the flat rule is contradicted as history. It is not a defect in this project's rule, because that rule was never a historical finding: the setting notes state it twice, in the hamlet definition ("overseen by a village headsman who lives in the main village and not in the hamlet") and in the direct answer ("Do hamlets have their own village headsman? No, but with a caveat"75 - the caveat being about the headman's workload, not about hamlets having one). Rokugan is deliberately simpler here. Decision: the card states the setting's rule AND discloses the simplification, in the basis paragraph. The "deliberate deviation" class covers exactly this - a departure the reader is told about rather than one they discover.

Five to a household is canon, and only weakly supported as history. The setting notes: "The median household size is generally assumed to be 5", and "Most hamlets have a population of 50-100 (i.e. 10-20 households)".76 The nearest historical figure found was Kinoshita 1995, which has no publicly readable copy: one Tohoku village whose mean household size "rose from about five to six persons" over 1760-187088 - one village, and a trend rather than a constant. Decision: the card multiplies by the setting's five, and the historical figure is recorded here as support rather than as the basis.

The shrine and the burial ground: nothing found either way. No page read said whether a branch hamlet had its own chinju or graveyard. Neither claim is reopened here: the burial ground rests on the district-catchment finding of the religion-and-death research, which is sourced in its own right, and the shrine on the GM's tier rule.

A district takes its main village's name, which is what lets one name serve for both the district a hamlet belongs to and the village its lanes lead to. The setting notes, "Place Names": "a village and its district".77
