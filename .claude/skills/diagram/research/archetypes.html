Field archetypes: the research behind the overlay, polder and dike-pond rules

The findings behind the field archetypes on these maps - the crop overlays, the polder and the dike-pond - the decisions they drove, and, where no generator draws the feature yet, the rules a map follows.

Why is there no yellow rape flower beside the rice?

Sources: aburana-jawiki, aburana-zhwiki (autumn sowing, March-April cutting; rape into the harvested paddy), rice-rape-calendar-summary (the rape-harvest and rice-transplanting windows3). The rice months and the Jiangnan rotation calendar were not found on any page read, and the sowing window "late Sept-mid Oct" is unsourced

RAPE (油菜, Japanese aburana, Chinese youcai - the oilseed) never stands beside rice: rice and rape are the two halves of one rotation in the SAME plot (rice May-Oct (the rice harvest month is unsourced - the footnoted source gives transplanting and rape-harvest dates but no harvest month);3 rape sown into the drained stubble2 Oct-Nov, flowering Mar-Apr),1 so they are never both standing - mixing them at any fraction depicts two seasons at once, and the real spring picture is yellow rape against BARE stubble, not against green rice. Showing rape would mean showing a whole different season of the same field, which these maps do not do.

Overlay extent - a calibrated liberty, disclosed

Sources: the lotus area share (a few percent to 10-15%) was found nowhere on 2026-08-28 (zh.wikipedia 莲藕 silent; a provincial share of aquatic-vegetable OUTPUT is the nearest figure) - the interpolation stands as a guess and is labeled one; the liberty is the GM's (2026-07-19)

A CALIBRATED LIBERTY is recorded here (GM 2026-07-19): the wet low band an overlay may cover is taken as the bottom two levels of the valley floor rather than only the single hem along the drain. How wide a valley bottom's wet backswamp ran is unrecorded, and the researched extent for a lotus-growing village, a few percent to about 10-15% of field area, is itself interpolated from a national average that includes regions growing none.118 The single-hem reading put lotus at ~2% - inside the range, but so sparse that it stopped making villages look different from one another, which is the whole reason the overlay exists. We chose the upper part of a plausible range for a stated non-historical reason (legibility) and disclosed it.

Two terms decide how far an overlay spreads, and they are independent: the GROUND decides which plots are eligible, the ECONOMY decides how many of the eligible plots convert. A pond, a lotus bed and a hill tea garden each need their own ground (below), so the plots an overlay may take are the lowest, wettest ground of the field - or, for tea, the fertile lower hillside - and a map with none of that ground draws no overlay at all rather than scattering one across ordinary rice. The second term is then a share of that ELIGIBLE ground, never of the whole field: as a share of the whole field it was inert, because the eligible ground is always the smaller of the two, so every settlement converted all of its low ground and the economic term decided nothing. What supports a share well short of everything is the county reading below - Shunde about 4.6% dike-pond in 1581, its earliest pond district at 18% - which is a district deep in ponds inside a county still mostly in rice.

What could not be checked: conversion spread plot by plot from one household to its neighbors, so the converted plots should sit in patches rather than evenly scattered, and the maps place them that way. The patchiness cannot be demonstrated on the map itself: the eligible ground is always a thin continuous strip along the wet bottom, and every subset of a strip counts as clustered by any nearest-neighbor-against-span measure - an even random scatter of the same number scored exactly as well. So the patchwork is asserted from the history rather than shown by the drawing, and it becomes checkable only if an archetype is ever added whose eligible ground is a genuinely two-dimensional area.

The three overlays a village may carry - the mulberry fishpond, the lotus field and the tea fringe

Sources: fortune-1843

  • The mulberry fishpond (桑基魚塘, sangji yutang) - dug out of 低洼易有洪患之处, the low flood-prone hollows, as a flood adaptation that drained the hollow while raising the dike.4 A scatter of dike-ponds among rice was the system's NORMAL state. Shunde county was ~4.6% dike-pond in 1581 (our arithmetic - the pond figure is on a readable page, the county's cultivated total that makes the denominator is not; Longshan, its earliest pond district, 18% - 8,124 of 44,947 mu);79 a 1980s survey of the heartland found rice at 25% against the fishponds' own 35%;78 and at Lake Tai mulberry sat on the pond (tang) banks with rice remaining the polder's main crop permanently.5 Gazetteers found total absence of rice remarkable enough to record.6 The wall-to-wall landscape is the rare end state of a ~300-year plot-by-plot process7 (挖塘培基, wa tang pei ji - dig one low plot into a pond, pile the spoil into a dike;8 a one-household, one-dry-season job), which is what the whole-landscape mulberry-dike-fishpond ARCHETYPE is for. At that scale the ponds really had engulfed the ordinary ground too, so the whole-landscape form is not held to the low-ground rule. Negative result: the outer-delta 沙田 shatian were equally low and equally wet and stayed in rice - low ground is necessary but not sufficient; the dike-pond zone also had smallholder tenure inside an established polder and water access to Canton.
  • The lotus field (藕田, outian) - models deep-water lotus (深水藕, shenshui ou, 30-50cm, tolerating ~1m) against paddy rice's ~5-9cm optimum, so it physically cannot sit on high ground. Shallow-water lotus (浅水藕, qianshui ou, 10-20cm) in ordinary paddy is equally real but is deliberately NOT modeled: it is an economic choice rotating with rice (稻藕轮作, dao ou lunzuo, the rice-lotus rotation), driven by a choice these maps do not model, and drawing it would be visually indistinguishable from uniform random scatter. Honest limitation: the agronomy is solid but pre-modern village-scale practice is not directly sourced - the clearest conversions in the record (Kasumigaura 1970s, contemporary Vietnam) are modern and policy-driven. Extent where present is a few percent up to maybe 10-15% of field area; that range is interpolation, not a sourced figure.
  • The tea fringe (茶, cha) - tea took the lower-to-mid FERTILE hillside, never the low lands and never the barren upper slope (Robert Fortune, eyewitness, 1843: "always situated on the lower and most fertile sides of the hills, and never on the low lands";9 the upper slopes were forest, sweet potato and peanut111). The boundary rule states itself - the line is the highest irrigation ditch. Form is smallholder: recognizable rows in acre-scale patches, one small garden to a cottager,10 dotted across a hillside, not one contiguous block. Two anachronisms to avoid: neatly contour-terraced tea is post-1949, a state program; and bund-margin tea (畦畔茶, keihan-cha) is a Japanese practice with no Chinese equivalent.

What does rice land look like where there is no valley floor to spread across?

Sources: this project's general reading of the terraced rice landscapes of south China and mainland Southeast Asia (Yuanyang 元陽, Longsheng 龍勝); no page is cited for it

Wet rice needs a level basin that holds water, and the valley-bottom fan most of these maps draw is simply the cheapest way to get one. Where the ground will not give it, two other forms do. On a hillside the basin is cut INTO the slope and held by a retaining bank, and the stepped hill terrace is the standard answer wherever valley-bottom paddy is impossible - it is the defining alternative to the valley fan, not a rarity (this rests on general reading; no source is cited).89 Where a stream valley is too narrow to spread across, the basin runs WITH the valley instead: a chain of small fields following the floor down, the brook threading between them (this too rests on general reading; no source is cited).90 In both, the leveled cell stays the size it is everywhere else - a basin has to be flooded to an even depth by hand, which is what caps it - so a terrace step and a valley floor alike read as a ROW or a CHAIN of small paddies rather than as one long sheet.

The rule the map follows: on hill ground, rice is drawn as stacked contour terrace bands stepping down the slope from the catchment above, a supply channel running down one flank and cascading from step to step into a drain at the foot. A map that draws terraces carries at least eight retaining banks across the slope, and every step is divided along the contour into cells of the leveled-basin size the record gives, so a step reads as a row of small paddies rather than one continuous band. Hill ground is what unlocks the form; a map that declares no ground of its own is drawn as the ordinary valley fan.

The rule the map follows: in a confined valley, rice is drawn as one long narrow field meandering down the valley floor with the brook down its center, stepped down the fall by cross banks and split across its width into cells of the same leveled-basin size, so it reads as a chain of small paddies rather than a single sheet; the field's outline stays markedly longer than it is wide. Narrow-valley ground is what unlocks the form.

Polder parcels were a private-tenure patchwork

Sources: cssn-jiangnan-weitian (the tangpu canal grid at five-to-ten li), sdlib-shunde-jitang (ponds dug and dikes piled plot by plot)

The research upheld the instinct: the surveyed rectilinear chessboard of the Taihu tangpu 塘浦 system was the CANAL grid at kilometer scale,11 while the parcels inside it were a private-tenure patchwork - mid-Qing Jiangnan farms averaged ~10 mu scattered over several parcels; Buck's pre-mechanization survey (1929-33) found a mean parcel of ~1 mu, rectangular only where it fronted a straight ditch;112 dike-ponds accreted household-by-household (挖塘培基, wa tang pei ji, dig the pond and pile the dike13), so 66 identical ponds contradict the map's own backstory. Uniform machine-sized rectangles are a 20th-century consolidation look (Japan's 1963 hojo seibi 30m x 100m standard; PRC consolidation campaigns).

What the maps draw, and against what. The parcels are drawn at TRUE size, with no legibility inflation: a rice polder runs a module of about 110 ft, so a drawn parcel averages roughly one mu - a whole bay about 1.9, a half about 0.9, a third about 0.6 - and a dike-pond block runs a module of about 160 ft with a merge-heavy mix, so most ponds come out about 160 by 320 ft of parcel holding about 4 mu of water - a little over a quarter of a hectare - behind a planted collar about 7 ft wide (measured on the Kuwabata map, a median of 2.0 m). From one pond's water to the next is about 44 ft (13.3 m), but that span holds two collars with a canal down the middle of it, not a single bank. The traditional figures those are set against - ponds of 0.4-0.6 ha behind dikes of 6-10 m110 - reach the record only at second hand, through a summary of a monograph that has no publicly readable copy, so they are carried as a comparison rather than as a measured target. The drawn pond sits inside the pond band; the drawn bank does not sit inside the dike band at either end, being a collar of 2.0 m where the band starts at 6, and 13.3 m from one pond's water to the next where the band stops at 10 - because that span is two collars and a canal rather than one dike.

What lies between two parcels, and how wide is it?

Sources: the GM's question of 2026-07-24 and this project's decision on it; no page is cited for the ridge's width

A polder's parcels are drawn as separate shapes, so the space between two of them IS the bund - and the two directions are not the same thing. Between parcels stacked one below another down the fall there is nothing but the walking bund: a ridge of piled earth with the footpath along its top, about three feet all told (a ridge of some 20-50 cm plus the path; this rests on general reading, no source is cited).91 Along the module lines the other way a delivery ditch runs with its spoil banks either side, and that corridor is about eight feet. The distinction matters because a wide gap is a PROMISE: a reader who sees eight feet of ground between two parcels is being told that water runs there. When a bay was split lengthwise into strips of a single holding, the cut between two strips of the same farm was taking the full ditch corridor - wider than any plain walking bund, and a promise of water that is not there. The decision (2026-07-24): a gap is only as wide as what runs in it. A split within a bay gets the same plain walking bund a row edge gets, and only the module lines, where a ditch is actually drawn, keep the corridor width. The dike-pond block is unaffected: its gaps are the same in both directions already.

Why is a hand-piled bund never straight - and never square at the corners?

Sources: the GM's questions and ruling of 2026-07-25; this project's reasoning about how piled earth behaves. No page is cited for the mechanics below

A farmer piling a rectangular basin out of puddled mud intends four straight sides and four right angles and gets neither, for two separate physical reasons worth drawing separately (this rests on general reading; no source is cited).92 Corners round. A right angle in soft earth is the thinnest, least supported point of the whole ridge - it slumps under its own weight and under every rain - and it is the one spot on the bund that every foot, hoof and carrying pole cuts across instead of walking out to the point; the spring re-plastering restores the ridge, not the geometry, so a corner converges on a walked-and-slumped curve a few feet across. Edges wander. A run is paced and eyeballed between two corners, and re-cut a little differently every time a holding is split, sold or re-plastered, so a straight hundred-foot bund bows by a foot or two: straight in intent and to the eye at a distance, never to the ruler. The argument covers every earthwork the farmers piled, the dug ponds and the planted banks included, not only the ones holding fish.

How the earth moves depends on whether the bund is SHARED, and the two cases are opposite (the GM's ruling, 2026-07-25). Where the parcels are separate shapes with a real gap between them - a polder, a pond block - the rounding is SUBTRACTIVE: each parcel gives up its corners, and the bund, being the space between, simply widens there. Where a field is carved instead of laid out, the bund IS the shared line and the basins must tile without a gap, so shrinking each cell would tear holes in the field. There the correct operation is the opposite one: leave the carve untouched and ADD material, piling earth into the crossings, so that a junction stops being two hairlines meeting at a point and becomes a lumpy node of bund - and the four basin corners read rounded because the earth has taken them. That is the truest part of the whole rule, because a bund junction really is the most worked point in a field: four basins push water at it, it carries the crossing foot traffic, it is where someone stands to open and close the water, and it is the first thing to slump and be re-piled (this rests on general reading; no source is cited).93 The node is drawn at true size - an ordinary walking bund is about a foot and a half across and a junction node widens to four to six feet - floored at the pen width so it never vanishes and never inflated past the node itself, since a legibility-sized dot here would be a fake fifteen-foot earthwork at every crossing. A polder's parcels are held apart and share no corner at all, so nothing is piled at a crossing there.

The regularity was in the LATTICE, not in the corners (the GM, 2026-07-25). The first pass of rounding read as "a circle at each intersection, which actually makes it look less natural", and the GM's own second note outranked the first: "maybe a big part of the solution is to make the lines less straight generally - even human-made bunds won't be perfectly parallel to one another in the way that these are depicted." That is the root of it. Every wander the maps had was COHERENT - a polder's lattice is warped as one piece on purpose, so that each field edge stays parallel to its own section of dike, and a valley fan's rows were constant-fall contours - so every interior line kept exactly its neighbors' shape and the spacing between two bunds never changed anywhere. Fragmented tenure does not look like that (above): a bund is laid by eye between two points, by a different household in a different decade, so neighboring lines drift, converge and open out again, and a strip is wider at one end than at the other. Four decisions came out of it. (1) Each row line and each column line of a polder gets its OWN gentle profile, amplitude and phase, up to about a tenth of the module, while still meeting its neighbors at the shared nodes - so the lattice stays consistent, parcels stay four-sided, and the ditches that follow the column lines meander with them; each profile fades to nothing at both ends and is suppressed on the four boundary lines, so the block's edge, its dike and its ring canal do not move. (2) In a valley fan a point now drifts in the direction of FALL as well as along its contour, by an amount that depends on where it sits along the row and on which row it is, so no two rows carry the same shape; that drift fades to zero at a sector's first and last row, because those two are pinned by water rather than free - the head row sits on the supply canal and takes water through cuts in its own bund, and the closing rank sits on the drain. (3) A corner's rounding is drawn from a wide, long-tailed spread with a floor that leaves some corners all but square, because corners really do differ that much - the one on the walked side wears to a broad sweep while the one behind a neighbor's bund keeps its angle for years - and the arc itself is roughened, since a mathematically clean curve is the other half of what reads as stamped. (4) The earth at a shared crossing is no longer one disc but a separate fillet in each quadrant, one per basin corner meeting there, each with its own reach, with roughly a quarter of quadrants left bare - so a crossing can be piled heavily on one side and untouched on the other.

The perimeter dike followed the natural water edge

Sources: sdlib-shunde-jitang (the dike as pond spoil, planted), miragenews-polders (fish-scale polders named for their aerial outlines; organic forms replaced by standardized rectangles after 1949)

What the research found: a wei-tian 圩田 / dike-pond dike was dredged pond-mud heaped and packed (the 挖塘培基 dig-and-pile cycle that also formed the ponds),14 trapezoidal in section, PLANTED with mulberry/willow/crops that bind the soil,15 walked and lived on, and constantly breached-and-repaired16 - so it read as a mottled, vegetated green-brown band of VARYING width, not a uniform stroke. Crucially, the plan form splits: the surveyed interior ditch/paddy grid was genuinely rectilinear, but the OUTER perimeter dike followed the natural water edge (lake / creek / marsh) in gentle curves and non-square bends - the "fish-scale polder" (鱼鳞圩, yulin wei) form named for exactly those irregular overlapping outlines.17 The dead-straight right-angled rectangle is a post-1949 industrial shape (heritage scholars: "the organic forms of polders were replaced by standardised rectangular patterns"),18 so a dead-straight rectangular polder would be the one genuinely anachronistic thing on the map.

Why dikes were planted, and what the row spacing rests on

Sources: willow-palisade

Why dikes were planted at all: a bare bank of heaped dredge-mud is a liability - rain gullies it, wave wash gnaws the waterside toe, and a slump becomes a breach - so planting the dike was the standard remedy wherever these earthworks existed: the roots bind the packed mud. The species split by role: WILLOW on the water face (thrives with wet feet; its root mat armors the bank against wave wash; pollarded for withies - willow-fascine bank protection is an ancient Chinese technique, in use by the last century BC), and in sericulture districts MULBERRY on the crest/inner face20 - the dike was elevated, well-drained, annually re-fertilized by the dredge cycle,21 i.e. prime planting ground in a landscape that is otherwise water (Lake Tai mulberry sat on the pond banks). A dike that looks bare and cleanly engineered is the modern-concrete-era tell. What the numbers rest on: dike planting was ROW planting along the alignment, not scatter. The closest attested in-row figure is the Qing Willow Palisade statute - willow stakes - three of them at every 5 chi (~1.67 m ~ 5.5 ft), each four cun (~13 cm) thick and six chi (~2 m) long - planted in rows on an embankment19 - corroborated by modern willow-fascine practice descending from the same mechanics (rows 1-1.5 m apart on erosive soil, 1.5-2 m on cohesive). A polder-dike-specific statute was NOT found; the Willow Palisade figure is an ANALOG from a border embankment, recorded here as such, not as polder canon.

Why dike willows do NOT replace the village windbreak

Sources: usu-windbreak (shelter up to 30 H - the entry's 10-20 H is inside it), yashikirin-jawiki (igune on the north and west; species スギ・マツ・ヒノキ・ケヤキ)

Why the dike willows do NOT replace the village windbreak (GM 2026-07-24, asked and answered): a windbreak's sheltered zone runs ~10-20x barrier HEIGHT,22 and the homestead belt is full-height dense planting for exactly that reason, while dike willows are POLLARDED low because the withies are the harvest - a single porous row at rooftop height shelters almost nothing at the houses' distance. Worse, willow and mulberry are DECIDUOUS:23 bare sticks during the NW winter monsoon, the one season shelter matters, which is why homestead windbreaks ran dense, multi-row and conifer-dominated (the igune of the flat Sendai plain, on the north and west against the winter wind,24 are sugi, pine and hinoki with keyaki25 - the same reason; that they persisted amid abundant field-edge trees is unsourced). And an east-dike village sits downwind of the whole pond sheet - open water is the smoothest fetch there is, so the wind reaches the dike at full strength. Utility rows on the dike and a dense grove at the homestead are DIFFERENT plants doing DIFFERENT jobs; real polder landscapes carried both.

The ring canal runs on the INNER toe - 一河围田

Sources: cssn-jiangnan-weitian (「channels within, sluice gates without」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「中有河渠，外有门闸」) - channels inside the dike, gates through it, opened in drought and shut in flood), weinei-he-summary (the general dike-and-canal chessboard pattern80). The 一河围田 shorthand, the inside-toe placement and the crescent 月样 / bow 弓样 forms are unsourced - not found on any page read (zh.wikipedia 圩田 silent; the weinei-he page carries none of the crescent/bow forms or gated-ends language)

What the research found: the polder's trunk distribution+collection canal rings the block on the INSIDE toe of the perimeter dike, on the FIELD side (Jiangnan shorthand 一河围田, yi he wei tian, "one river surrounds the field"; cross-section outside->in: wild water -> dike -> inner ring canal 圩内河 (weineihe) -> field ditches 浜 (bang) -> paddies). Outside the dike is the wild lake/creek the dike holds back, so NO irrigation channel runs out there; water crosses the dike ONLY through gated sluices (斗门 doumen / 水閘 shuizha / 涵洞 handong - gate, lock and culvert) cut at discrete points26 (the inlet + outfall), never as an open cut over the dike body. And the trunk line is organized-but-organic: long runs that read straight-ish but GENTLY WAVY (a surveyed dug canal wavers with terrain and repair; crescent (月样, yueyang) / bow (弓样, gongyang) trunk forms are named options in the literature113), with rounded corners rather than hard 90-degree turns - the finer laterals are visibly crookeder.

Wet-rice hydrology has no crossings to draw

Sources: fusekoshi-jawiki (the inverted siphon under a watercourse, with historical examples), suirokyo-jawiki (the aqueduct bridge)

Why any crossing is wrong here: wet-rice hydrology has no crossings to draw - a ditch either feeds another or is fed by it, and where two courses genuinely had to pass at different levels the builders put in27 an aqueduct28 or an inverted siphon, a distinct structure these maps do not draw.

How a junction is told from a crossing - and why it is measured ACROSS the other channel, never along the ditch (2026-07-24). The GM, looking at the rice polder: "many of the irrigated channels intersect rather than joining". Two things had to be settled. The first is where a ditch's tip is put. The field's lattice is bent, so a tip solved on the straight line between two points of the trunk lands a few feet off the curve the trunk is actually drawn along - two to five feet, which is precisely the range in which a near-perpendicular ditch pokes a visible tail out the far side and the pair reads as a four-way crossroads instead of a T. Tips are therefore projected onto the trunk's own drawn line, which is exact whatever the bending does; the rice polder's went from those few feet to a fraction of an inch. The second is how the result is judged, and that is the part worth keeping. At every meeting of two drawn channels, take the nearer end of each and ask whether it sits inside the OTHER channel's drawn width: if a tip is buried, the meeting is a junction and the overrun hides under the ink; if neither is, the stub shows. The measure runs across the other channel and never along the ditch's own line, and that is the whole trick - a delivery ditch taking off at a shallow angle overruns the meeting by four to eight feet along its own line and still never leaves the trunk's width, which is why a shallow offtake reads as a clean Y on Honda, Ikegami, Nagahara and Hoshizora alike, while the same overrun on a right-angled T pokes straight out. .

Grid vs mosaic - the arrangement differed by system

Sources: cssn-jiangnan-weitian (the tangpu lattice at five-to-seven and seven-to-ten li). The Pearl-delta reads as a mosaic of CONSTRUCTED ponds threaded by meandering natural rivers, the made and the natural hard to tell apart.81 The dikes' erosion from 20 m to under 4 m as the ponds grew is chi-2024-dike-pond-commons's,82 measured in one Shunde village over the decades since the reform and opening-up

What the research found: the answer is BOTH, and it splits by system. The individual ponds genuinely WERE rectangular oblongs29 - hand-dug from marsh (挖塘培基: dig the pond, pile the spoil into the dike),30 the recurring descriptor a "chessboard" of "rectangular-shaped dikes... with water in between" - because you dig a pond in whatever packs the space and straight dikes are easiest to pile, walk, and plant mulberry on. So all-rectangular ponds are correct; this is the rare farm landscape that does NOT grow organically around the terrain, because the farmers MADE the terrain. BUT the arrangement differed by system. The lower-Yangtze 圩田 (wei-tian) was a SURVEYED rectilinear grid - the Song-era 塘浦 tangpu lattice, deliberately engineered31 - so a clean grid is historically right for it. The Pearl-delta 桑基魚塘 (dike-pond) instead accreted household-by-household into a MOSAIC; the landscape-ecology literature describes the historical delta not as a uniform chessboard but as "mosaic-like constructed ponds with meandering natural river systems, [with] the boundary between constructed and natural blurred" - rectangles, yes, but of varied sizes at varied local orientations fitted around winding interior creeks (the perfectly-uniform grid is closer to the MODERN consolidated look, which eroded the dikes considerably: as the delta commercialized the water bodies expanded and arable dikes that used to be 20 m wide were eroded to less than 4 m, the fish farming itself narrowing the dike82 - a study of one Shunde village over the decades since the reform and opening-up, not a measurement across the whole delta).

So the arrangement is ROLLED per settlement, not chosen once. Both readings are supportable and two polders that look different multiply the variety a reader can see, so the arrangement is a knob each map rolls from its own seed: the surveyed grid at one end; at the other, the interior lattice displaced by a smooth correlated drift that fades to nothing at the pinned perimeter, so neighboring nodes move together, the lines stay continuous but BEND, the interior ditches become meandering creeks and the parcels skew to trapezoids while the individual ponds stay four-sided - which is what the record describes, the mosaic being in the ARRANGEMENT and never in the ponds being triangular. The pool's rice polder keeps the surveyed grid it is named for; its dike-pond block runs the mosaic and declares it.

The variety this buys is meant to count structurally: the pond layout is one of the axes that decide whether two settlements read as genuinely distinct places, so a grid polder and a mosaic polder of the same water direction are different by construction.

The 6:4 water-to-dike ratio, and coppiced mulberry

Sources: gd-gazetteer-sangji (the water-to-dike split 水基比, shui ji bi, 「a split of three to seven up to four to six」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「三七至四六开」)),83 fao-ac241e (6:4; 1:1 where the worms alone feed the fish),34 isis-dykepond (the 0.4-0.6 ha ponds and 6-10 m dikes),37 chi-2024-dike-pond-commons (the dikes eroded from 20 m to under 4 m as the ponds grew),82 cssn-sangji-yutang (「six parts dike, four parts pond」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「基六塘四」)), kuwa-jawiki (mulberry kept as a low shrub). The coppiced-bush density (one bush per 10-20 sq ft, crowns 4-6 ft) and the bushes' height are unsourced - a GUESS, sdlib-shunde-jitang

The classic dike-pond prescription was an explicit area ratio of 6 parts water to 4 parts dike32 (some districts 7:3);33 measured on the Kuwabata map, water is 80% of the parcels and the planted banks 20%, falling to 50% of the whole block once the canal corridors between them count - so the drawing falls either side of the prescribed six parts water: water-heavy inside a parcel, under it across the block. The band is not marginal ground: each pond's ~5,600 sq ft (~0.13 ac) bank was the SILK side of the loop (leaf36 -> silkworm -> frass -> fish -> dredged pond mud -> dike fertility), and the ratio existed because too much water starves the silkworms and too much dike starves the fish.35

Silkworm mulberry was not grown as trees: it was COPPICED into low bushes38 (crowns ~4-6 ft) so the leaf could be stripped several times a year, planted in dense rows on the dike at roughly one bush per 10-20 sq ft - i.e. 300-600 bushes per pond, tens of thousands across the map. A crown is two to four feet across and adjacent crowns nearly touch, so drawing the actual bushes at honest density IS a packed band of dots; individually distinguishable tree glyphs would mean three to five times the true crown size, which a to-scale map does not do. No berries either: leaf-stripped coppice barely fruits, and a mulberry fruit is sub-pixel at this scale - crown color carries the read.

What the drawn rows fall short of, and by how much. At the guessed density above - one bush per 10-20 sq ft, crowns four to six feet, three to five feet apart in the row - real adjacent crowns TOUCH, and a row drawn at that density honestly is a fused continuous hedge line, which on the map reads as a solid green band rather than as bushes. The GM asked for the touching-crown read without the fusion: bushes close enough to run together in stretches, with bank earth showing through the notches, rather than a chain of separate dots or one unbroken stripe. Closing the in-row step from six feet to four and a half gets that read, at about one bush per 23 sq ft (10,613 crowns over some 243,000 sq ft of bank on the dike-pond map), with the crowns themselves honest at four and a half to seven feet against the four to six of the guess. It is still short of the low end of that guessed band, and the reason is that only two rows fit the width of a bank legibly: the remaining gap is the price of the crowns reading as separate plants at all, and it is disclosed here rather than closed.

A bush stands on the bank, so its trunk never stands in the canal. The planting is coppiced BUSHES on the dike, not canopy trees arching over water, so a crown whose CENTER fell over open water would be a bush growing in a dug channel - impossible. A crown's edge may overhang, though, and the channels are drawn beneath the canopies, so an overhanging leaf edge covers part of a channel while a channel never slices across a crown: the canal runs between the bushes at ground level, which is what it does on the ground. A rule that kept the whole crown circle clear of the water would pull the canopies artificially back from the bank edge; it is the crown's CENTER that must stand on the bank, not the whole circle. Grazing the edge passes deliberately (2026-07-24): the canal genuinely runs along the toe of the dike, where earth meets water, and a bent ditch grazes a parcel edge by a foot and a half or so; that is the toe line clipping the rim of the bank, not a canal cut through the dike, and a rule that fired on it would be wrong about the place. Widening each bank by five feet to cover the drawn parcel line put banks over the wavering ditches in 72 places, up to six and a half feet deep, so the bank stays on the true parcel line and that line is covered another way.

The ORDER of the ratio is contested: the one page read writes the classic prescription as 「six parts dike, four parts pond」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「基六塘四」) - six parts DIKE to four parts POND39, while a people.cn page seen only in a search summary has 「six parts as pond, four parts as dike」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「六分为塘，四分为基」) - six water to four dike, the reading Kuwabata is drawn to at 80% water across the parcels and a 78% median parcel. The literature carries both orders and a 7:3 variant, so this may be a regional split rather than an error.

GM ruling (2026-08-28) - a DEVIATION, a disclosed regional reading: Kuwabata keeps its 6 water : 4 dike parcels. The record carries the prescription in BOTH orders - 「six parts dike, four parts pond」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「基六塘四」) on the page read (cssn-sangji-yutang), 六分为塘、四分为基 in the people.cn summary - and a 7:3 variant; the map follows the water-heavy reading, and says so on every pond and bank of Kuwabata: "a regional reading; the classic prescription is also recorded as six parts dike to four parts pond".

The bank is a ring, not a disk under the pond. A crop dike is drawn as the BAND between its outer edge and the water's own outline, with the pond showing through the middle - the same shape the perimeter dike has, and for the same reason. This is a map drawing convention, and the GM ruled it on 2026-09-12 after hovering a dike on the interactive page: it lit "not only the Mulberry Dyke itself, but the fish ponds Inside each Mulberry dike, which is confusing", and they asked for "basically the same behavior that we give to the perimeter dyke". Priced and declined: a stylesheet fix, which cannot cut a hole in a filled shape; drawing the pond above the lit feature, at a measured cost of a fifth of a second on every hover; and a second outline element for the outer edge, which doubles a dike's ink for a fringe nobody sees.

A dike-pond is fed and drained through sluice gates

Sources: fao-x6708e (the sluice-gate definition), cssn-sangyuanwei (the polder's 窦, dou, its sluice),42 cssn-jiangnan-weitian (gates through the dike, worked by season - the polder case).43 The inlet-high / outlet-low plumbing comes from Ruddle & Zhong (Cambridge 1988), which has no publicly readable copy - unsourced

What the research found: a 桑基魚塘 pond is NOT a sealed basin - each connects to the creek/canal network through a SLUICE GATE, "a protected opening in the pond dike that can be easily closed with wooden boards to regulate water level"40 and, by pulling the boards, drain the pond at harvest.41 And a pond on a slope is plumbed INLET-HIGH, OUTLET-LOW so water flows downhill through it (the whole dike-pond net runs in series from a high intake to a low outfall). So the channels do not "irrigate" the ponds paddy-style; they are the conveyance-and-drainage network the ponds exchange water with.

Polder siting - full enclosure, fluctuating water, and where the village sits

Sources: fei-xiaotong

(1) Does the dike really surround the paddies completely? Yes - full enclosure IS the defining feature, not a drawing convenience. A weitian is wetland "enclosed with dykes to be hydrologically separated from the surrounding fluctuating water and then drained"44 (the character 圩 means the enclosing embankment); Shen Kuo (Northern Song, Illustrated Records of Wanchun Polder) is credited with describing "dikes and dams... built along the rivers to enclose the farmland, which is called a polder" (unsourced -the zh.wikipedia 万春圩 page carries no such quotation and no sentence about enclosure; it records only the polder's history - built by the Qin family in the late Tang, rebuilt by 14,000 laborers in 90 days, with Shen Kuo possibly taking part and writing the 万春圩图说). The enclosed floor sits at or below the surrounding FLOOD stage, so on a flat lake plain there is no safe side to leave open - any gap re-floods the whole block at the next high water, which is why water crosses only at the two gated sluices. The scale is attested too: fragmented small polders were each "cultivated by a group of 10-20 households" behind dikes ~1.5 m high114 - Enokida's 16 households are exactly this unit. (2) Should there be marsh/wetland on all sides then? The outside is "fluctuating water," but that means neither open lake on every side nor water year-round. Attested surroundings: the lake/creek network the polder was dug from; reed marsh and mudflat where reclamation had not yet reached; NEIGHBOR polders across a shared creek (the mature Taihu landscape was "islet-like" fish-scale polders packed into the water net);45 and - at the district margin - the natural shore, because reclamation advanced FROM the shore ("mudflats along rivers and lakes were transformed"115). The water also FLUCTUATES seasonally: at low stage much of the outside is exposed mudflat and reed, at flood it is sheet water - the dike is built against the flood stage, not the average day. Both pool polders are drawn as the landward-margin case: wild header pond NW (the source), reed marsh at the low south outfall side, village on naturally dry ground east. GM ruling (2026-07-24): both polders carry a reed fringe along the west flank, so the map shows that the west dike holds water back. (3) Should the farmhouses be INSIDE the dike? Both siting patterns are attested, split by what dry ground exists. Where the polder abuts the natural shore, the village sits on the landward dry ground at the dike's shoulder - the way both maps are drawn, and the natural one for a margin polder (nobody lives on a flood-fighting earthwork when real dry ground is a few steps east). In the DEEP-water landscape with no natural dry ground, settlement used the polder's own raised earth instead: linear villages ON the dikes (canal-dike settlement "taking advantage of the elevated typology" is attested from the 8th century on116) or houses inside the polder along its interior streams (Fei Xiaotong's Kaixiangong / the Xichang polder layout).46 So Enokida and Kuwabata are correct AS margin polders - but an "islet" polder map, water-ringed on all sides, would REQUIRE dike-top or interior housing. GM ruling (2026-07-24): dike-top housing is attested and is available as a variation the GM has not yet taken up; the GM chose NOT to convert any existing settlement. One more confirmation from the same research: the polder community IS its dike-maintenance community ("sustaining a polder necessitated a close-knit community," often one extended family, which named its polder)117 - so a polder village always sits hard against its own block, so a polder village always sits hard against its own block.

What stands on a dike-pond hamlet that a paddy hamlet lacks?

Sources: fei-1939, fao-ac264e, isis-dykepond (carrying Ruddle & Zhong's Pearl-delta figures), cssn-sangyuanwei, cssn-sangji-yutang, fao-ac264e-ch10, hudson-munoz-2023, dili360-2005-sangji

What the question was. The GM (2026-08-27), looking at the rice hamlet's outhouses and the rest: "this is a different type of map ... I could imagine there being map features which would exist in Kuwabata but not in Inashiro." The question was what PHYSICAL features stood on a silk-and-fish hamlet's ground - farmsteads, dikes, water's edge - that a rice hamlet's did not, and which rice features it should lack.

What the research found. The one primary ethnography of a silk village that could be read in full is Fei Hsiao-tung's Peasant Life in China (1939), on Kaihsienkung south of Lake Tai - a rice-AND-silk village on a stream net,47 not a converted dike-pond block, so it answers the SILK half of the loop directly and the FISH half only by the Pearl-delta secondary literature.

  • Silkworms were reared INSIDE the dwelling, not in a separate building. "Silkworms are raised in rectangular and shallow containers about [1.5] by [1] metre in size. They are put on the shelves of a stand. Each stand has eight containers. Each house has enough room for five stands."48 And during the two-week peak "all the rooms except the kitchen may be used for sheltering silkworms. All the members of the household will crowd in one bed-room."49 The front room "is used for working, such as raising silkworms, manufacturing silk, threshing rice"50; the old reeling machine ("a furnace to boil water, a wheel ... a rotating axle connected to a plate for treading") stood in the house too.51 So a sericulture hamlet has no silkworm house and no reeling shed: the record puts both indoors. (Communal rearing halls (蚕房, canfang) are a 1950s collective form - the Shunde examples found are dated 1958 - and are not premodern.)
  • Boats, and a village laid out along its water. "Nearly every household possesses one or more boats except those who are not engaged in agricultural or fishing work."52 "The importance of the boat in communication means that the houses must be near the water and consequently determines the plan of the village. Villages grow up along the streams."53 "Every village has its own agent boat which serves as the buying and selling agent of the villagers in the town market."54 The Pearl-delta proverb of the Sangyuanwei polder says the same of silk: 「a boat of silk goes out, a boat of silver comes back」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「一船蚕丝出，一船白银归」)55 - a boat of silk out, a boat of silver back. A cash-crop hamlet that sells silk and fish and buys grain in is a hamlet that LIVES by water transport. Kuwabata as drawn has a header reservoir and a ring canal and no navigable water at all. It was put to the GM as a candidate (a creek along the landward flank with boats and a landing) and DECLINED (2026-08-28): "would it necessarily be the case that any settlement of this sort would have one. I wouldn't think so" - Kaihsienkung's water is that village's geography, not the archetype's; the hamlet's connector lane leads to the market, or to whatever water carries its goods there, and the map presumes rather than draws it.
  • Manure PITS, not heaps. "Human manure, being the most important fertilizer in the farm, is preserved in the pits made of earthenware, half buried in the ground at the back of the building. Along the southern bank of the Stream A, the public road is lined up with these manure pits."56 Two attested forms for the same fixture: the rice hamlet (Inashiro) draws a heap, and a roadside pit would be a distinct thing to draw.
  • Mulberry on the raised margin, houses beside it, vegetables beneath it. "Along the margin of each yu, ten to thirty metres of land is left for plantation of mulberry trees and a wider space for house building. This land on the margin is higher. It also serves as a dyke for the farm."57 - the yu being the polder itself, the diked block the fields lie inside. "Limited space under the mulberry trees for growing vegetables."58 Kuwabata draws exactly this: the planted perimeter dike, the village on the dry flank.
  • Sheep huts (Fei: "little huts for the sheep and sometimes also for storage", "the sheep hut has become a common appendage of a house") are a 1920s-30s Lake Tai innovation ("in the past ten years"59) - so none stands on a premodern map.
  • Pigs and ducks ON the pond dikes - the Pearl-delta loop. Ruddle & Zhong (1988) as carried by the i-sis summary: "Pigs, chickens and ducks are reared on the dykes, to provide manure to fertilise the fishponds"60; the modern FAO/NACA manual: "The simple pig shed constructed on the pond dyke or over the water surface" lets the excreta flow to the pond;61 fish-cum-duck ponds fence a dry run on the dike and a wet run in a corner of the water.62 The premodern prevalence is not in anything read; the specific proverb about cane pressings fed to pigs (蔗榨糖后的蔗渣喂猪, zhe zha tang hou de zhezha wei zhu) was not found on any page fetched, but isis-dykepond records sugar-cane leaves fed to fish and pigs and refinery waste returned to the dyke pond as feed,85 and cssn-sangji-yutang records farmers building frames over the pond to raise pigs,86 which puts pigs in the traditional loop. A pig sty on a pond dike and a duck pen at a pond corner are both attested forms, then, though how common they were before the modern period is unquantified. Both are drawn. A dike that carries a shed is a WIDER dike, which is the one constructional number the record gives: "The width of the dikes between fish ponds and inflow and outflow canals should be kept within 5 m; the width of the dikes for pigsties, cow sheds piping, or traffic should range from 5 to 10 m".94 Only the floor of that range bears on a map like this one: its ceiling is set by cart traffic and pipework a hand-piled mulberry dike never carried. And measured against the drawing, the map does NOT meet it. The ground between one pond's water and the next runs about 13 m, but that is not one bank - a canal runs down the middle of it, and the planted collar a sty actually stands on is 2 to 5 m wide. So the strip under the shed is narrower than the 5 m a shed-carrying dike is told to have. The mismatch is recorded rather than drawn away: that figure is a design requirement for farms built in the 1980s, not a measurement of any old dike, and nothing read gives the width of a premodern one. A reader should know the drawn collar is snug by a modern standard and that no older standard was found to judge it by. And the pig on the dike is a CHINESE form, which the map should say rather than leave implied. The Japanese record does not carry it: the standard account - that pigs were introduced early, never took hold, and that religious proscription limited meat until the nineteenth century - is named by its most recent scholarly treatment as an assumption under review rather than a finding,95 and where mainland Japanese pigs WERE kept the reading is pannage, loose in forest, with Okinawa the stated exception: "With the exception of Okinawa, Japan seems to have followed the pannage system".96 Japanese pigs WERE kept, and the same survey records them in the early modern towns - "Sites with significant numbers of Sus bones (both wild and domesticated) include the Satsuma domain residence in Edo, Osaka castle, Hakata, and Nagasaki harbour".109 What has no Japanese counterpart is the FORM - a pen standing on a fish-pond dike. (No source: nothing in the material read - that survey, the integrated-farming reports, and Japanese carp-culture pages searched 2026-09-12 - links a Japanese pig to a pond at all.) A dike-pond hamlet is a Chinese-derived form throughout, so its pond stock is Chinese too, and a hamlet of any other archetype has no business with either.
  • Fish fry (鱼花, yuhua) as a trade, and nursery ponds. Jiujiang township (Nanhai) was the delta's fry center from the Ming (Miles 2003, which has no publicly readable copy: the township's rise 1395-1657 on fry collected from the Xijiang);87 Qu Dajun's seventeenth-century observation of Jiujiang village in Nanhai, in his Guangdong xinyu, 「the men trade fish fry, the women feed the silkworms」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「男贩鱼花，妇女喂爱蚕」) - men trade fry, women feed the worms63. Fry are reared in small ponds before stocking the grow-out ponds, so a few of a block's smallest parcels would be its fry ponds.
  • Other dike crops: sugar cane, fruit, banana (蔗基 / 果基 / 蕉基, zheji / guoji / jiaoji). The 1980s survey carried by i-sis: sugar-cane dikes 18% of the district against mulberry 1264%; 中国国家地理 2005 on the lost landscape: 蕉林蔗海 (jiaolin zhehai, banana groves and a sea of cane65); gd-gazetteer-sangji, the gazetteer office, which frames the types as a SUCCESSION - the mulberry dike giving way to fruit, cane and vegetable dikes over the region's history - rather than as three plantings side by side.83 Attested alternative FORMS of the dike planting. Each is drawn: a hamlet rolls one crop and plants every dike with it, cane, banana or fruit, and Kuwabata is the mulberry case.
  • The dike-pond's own sluices - already drawn ('A dike-pond is fed and drained through sluice gates', above).
  • Pond-side huts (塘头屋, tangtouwu / a watchman's hut): searched twice in Chinese and English, nothing attested.
  • A silk-goddess shrine (蚕花娘娘 Canhua Niangniang, 蚕神庙 canshen miao): the Huzhou-Jiaxing festival temples are VILLAGE and town institutions (the Qingming 轧蚕花 gatherings, ya canhua, the crowded silkworm-flower fair); a hamlet has no shrine by the tier's own definition, so a shrine of this kind belongs on a village map.
  • What a no-rice hamlet should LACK. Fei's village threshed rice "in the open space in front of the house, or in the front room"66 - the yard's threshing use is a RICE use. Kuwabata draws a threshing yard on every farmstead as the rice hamlet does; with no rice within its bounds the yard is at best a work yard (cocoons are sorted and leaves handled somewhere). The straw rick is already not drawn. The in-field pocket pond, the dry hem and the comb deliveries are absent by archetype already.
  • Holdings. Not settled: nothing read gives a silk-and-fish household's ground, so the ground per household (the paddy hamlet's 1.3 ac) stays a labeled GUESS on this archetype.

Departures and honesty. The gazetteer office frames 桑、蔗、蕉 (mulberry, cane, banana) as a SUCCESSION of dike-pond types (the mulberry dike 桑基 giving way to fruit, cane and vegetable dikes, 果基、蔗基、菜基, over the region's history), not one dike carrying all three - so a hamlet would be of one dike type rather than mixing crops on a dike. Fei is Lake Tai, 1936; the Pearl-delta reads are 1980s surveys and a 2006 summary of the 1988 monograph, which itself could not be fetched (403 on every host) - every Ruddle & Zhong number here rests on the summary, not the monograph. No single source describes a WHOLLY converted hamlet's farmsteads; the list is assembled across the two systems and says so per item.

Does a pig sty have to stand back from the water, or from the pond's sluice?

Sources: fao-ac264e-ch9, fao-ac264e, fao-y1187e, qimin-yaoshu-yangzhu, fao-ac257e, fao-x6708e, isis-dykepond

No, and standing at the water is the whole point of the fixture. A pig shed on a fish-pond dike is not a shed that happens to be near water - it is put there so the waste goes in: "Pigsties are usually built on the pond dike of an integrated fish farm so that the pig excrement can be directly flushed into the pond",97 and a pond the size of the ones drawn here is exactly the case where that is done directly rather than through a pit: "If the area of a fish pond is less than 8 mu, a pigsty can be set up on the pond dyke and pig wastes will flow directly into the pond".98 A dike-pond hamlet's ponds are about 4 mu of water each as drawn, inside that band - though they are small ponds even so, below the 0.4 to 0.6 hectares reported for the delta at second hand,108 because a hamlet's ponds are small, not because of anything in this question.

The manure is feed, not fouling. It fertilizes the water rather than poisoning it: "this technique of feeding animal manures to fish fertilizes the water, producing plankton which is a good natural fish food",99 by three routes at once - "stimulating phytoplankton production; and acting as substrate for bacterial production (detritus) and as feed for zooplankton".100 So the fish the household cultivates are, in part, eating what the pigs produce. Where the reasons for a pen's position are given at all, they are the labor of carrying waste and the comfort of the animals - "On the pond dike: Pens close to the pond to reduce labour cost of loading waste", "Over the pond: Pens are cooler and more humid"101 - and no page read gives contamination as a reason.

The oldest instruction runs the other way. The Qimin Yaoshu of about 540 AD gives exactly one rule for where a pig pen goes, and it is the opposite of a cleanliness rule: 「The pen is not disliked for being small - a small pen makes them fatten fast. The place is not disliked for being filthy - mud and muck let them escape the heat.」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「圈不厭小。〈圈小則肥疾。〉處不厭穢。〈泥污得避暑。〉」)102 It is northern China a thousand years before these settlements and it says nothing of fish ponds, so it does not settle how a pig and a pond stood together - but it does show that the instinct to hold a pen back from filth and water is not one the record supplies.

What CAN go wrong is a rate, and a place within the pond. Too much manure tells on the fish, and the symptom is visible from the bank: "Fish surfacing increases (dissolved oxygen content decreases) when pig manure sinks to the bottom of the pond or when too much manure flows into the pond".103 Every handle the record gives for that is a quantity or a date rather than a distance - more than thirty pigs is too many for the direct-flow shed, and none applied after mid-October.104 The one source that speaks to WHERE the waste enters keeps the shed where it is and moves the manure instead: "manure distribution techniques generally need to be employed to avoid the development of anaerobic conditions in the pond adjacent to the livestock quarters",105 the failure it is curing having been watched - "It was observed that the fish lost their apetite, presumably due to lack of dissolved oxygen because of anaerobic conditions at the manuring - feeding sites".106 So there is a dead patch in the water under the shed, and it is managed with buckets and channels rather than by moving the shed. Nothing of that is drawn: a bucket round is not a map feature, and a tinted patch would assert a condition no source ties to a premodern pond.

On the sluice itself, the record is silent. (No source: searched 2026-09-12 across the integrated-farming reports of the United Nations' Food and Agriculture Organization - chapters 7 to 10 of Integrated Fish Farming in China, the training manual, the agriculture-aquaculture primer, the consultancy report and the training series - the Institute of Science in Society's dike-pond summary, and Chinese and Japanese material including the Qimin Yaoshu and the Nogyo Zensho, the 1697 Japanese farming encyclopedia. Nothing relates a shed's or a pen's position on the dike to the pond's inlet, outlet, sluice or drainage gate, for it or against it. The sources place the sheds only in relation to the pond itself, and describe the canals with no building on them at all.) So the map claims nothing about it, and the reason a sty is not drawn standing on its pond's sluice is not a sanitary one.

What the map does, and why. A sty or a pen is held about 6 ft clear of the short culvert that feeds or drains its pond. That is a CONSTRUCTIONAL clearance, not a sanitary one: nobody builds a shed over the opening they have to reach in order to work it, a sluice being "a protected opening in the pond dike that can be easily closed with wooden boards to regulate water level",107 and 6 ft is about the room one person needs to stand there and draw a board. The 6 ft itself is a GUESS - the record gives dike WIDTHS and no spacing along a dike at all - and it is small enough to cost the household nothing: the sty simply stands a few paces further round the same bank.

What a settlement IS, and what the place card may say about it

The interactive page's title card describes the settlement itself, so it makes claims the feature writeups never had to: what kind of place this is, how many live here, and whether that kind is ordinary. The research came back mostly NEGATIVE - which is the finding.

Branch hamlets under a parent village are a real and common form, and the mechanism is attested. Edo Japan called the parent settlement the hongo and the newly founded one the edago: ja.wikipedia 枝郷: 「Whereas the original village was called the hongo (本郷; also motogo 元郷, oyago 親郷, oyamura 親村, honmura 本村), the new village was called the edago (枝郷; also edamura 枝村, komura 子村)」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「もとの村を本郷（本郷、元郷、親郷、親村、本村）とよぶのに対し、新村を枝郷（枝村、子村）と称した」) and 「In many cases the village assessed yield (murataka) was apportioned from the hongo's shoya and nanushi」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「多くの場合、本郷の庄屋・名主から村高が分け与えられていた」).70 The National Diet Library's reference service gives the fiscal half: 「In the assessment and collection of the nengu land tax, the cadastral register and the tax-rate notice stood in the parent village's name, and the small village could make its tax payment only through the parent village」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「年貢の賦課徴収では検地帳・年貢免定は親村の名義で、小村の年貢納入は 親村を通してしかできませんでした」) - the land registers and the tax quota stood in the PARENT village's name,71 and the branch could pay only through it. That is the reason a branch hamlet has no headman of its own standing: there is no separate tax unit for one to administer.

But the record ranks NOTHING by frequency, so neither does the map. No page read gives a national count or share of any settlement form; a J-STAGE study of Kishu shows only that multi-hamlet administrative villages were commoner in mountains than on plains.67 The GM asked whether a rice-farming hamlet is "the most common type of hamlet that exists or whatever" - and nothing answers it, at either scope. Decision: the card makes no historical ranking. It states the SETTING's arithmetic, which is decisive for these maps and is the GM's own: the setting notes' median-domain table gives ~1,296 hamlets to ~216 villages and ~40% of a domain's inhabitants living in one.72 It ranks no kind of hamlet at all.

Wet-rice dominance: not found, and kokudaka is a trap. Every source that would give the paddy-versus-dry-field land share either did not address it or could not be fetched. Kokudaka looks like evidence and is not - en.wikipedia: "The amount of taxation was not based on the actual quantity of rice harvested, but was an estimate based on the total economic yield of the land in question, with the value of other crops and produce converted to their equivalent value in terms of rice."73 A fiscal convention covering dry field too. Decision: the card says this hamlet farms rice because the map draws rice, and claims no dominance.

"A hamlet has no headman of its own" is CANON, and a deliberate deviation from history. The same 枝郷 entry records the exceptions: 「There were also edago in which a shoya, a kumigashira and the like were installed and which received treatment on a par with the hongo」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「中には庄屋や組頭などが置かれ、本郷並の扱いを受けていた枝郷も存在した」) - some branch hamlets had their own shoya and kumigashira74 and were treated on a par with the parent. So the flat rule is contradicted as history. It is not a defect in this project's rule, because that rule was never a historical finding: the setting notes state it twice, in the hamlet definition ("overseen by a village headsman who lives in the main village and not in the hamlet") and in the direct answer ("Do hamlets have their own village headsman? No, but with a caveat"75 - the caveat being about the headman's workload, not about hamlets having one). Rokugan is deliberately simpler here. Decision: the card states the setting's rule AND discloses the simplification, in the basis paragraph. The "deliberate deviation" class covers exactly this - a departure the reader is told about rather than one they discover.

Five to a household is canon, and only weakly supported as history. The setting notes: "The median household size is generally assumed to be 5", and "Most hamlets have a population of 50-100 (i.e. 10-20 households)".76 The nearest historical figure found was Kinoshita 1995, which has no publicly readable copy: one Tohoku village whose mean household size "rose from about five to six persons" over 1760-187088 - one village, and a trend rather than a constant. Decision: the card multiplies by the setting's five, and the historical figure is recorded here as support rather than as the basis.

The shrine and the burial ground: nothing found either way. No page read said whether a branch hamlet had its own chinju or graveyard. Neither claim is reopened here: the burial ground rests on the district-catchment finding of the religion-and-death research (how much ground a village burial ground needs, and whose dead lie in it), which is sourced in its own right, and the shrine on the GM's rule for what a hamlet has.

A district takes its main village's name, which is what lets one name serve for both the district a hamlet belongs to and the village its lanes lead to. The setting notes, "Place Names": "a village and its district".77
