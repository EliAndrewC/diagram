The justice works - why a county seat executes, and why the ground is outside

Sources: caishikou-enwiki, qiushen-zhwiki; suzugamori-jawiki, kozukappara-jawiki (the two highway-entrance grounds). The 棄市 term and the third Edo ground (Ōwada) were not confirmed in the pages read; the county-jail reading comes from the setting's own budget notes, which are canon rather than evidence, dosojin-jawiki, teramachi-jawiki, cangue-enwiki, rekilabo-shokeijo, tsuihokei-jawiki, senju-shuku-jawiki, eta-jawiki, tenmacho-jawiki

  • The China/Japan split, and how it resolves. This is one of the few places the two grounding traditions genuinely disagree. Japan pushed executions OUT of the settlement, to where they would be seen - Edo's execution grounds (two are confirmed, Suzugamori and Kozukappara; a third, Ōwada, is unconfirmed)77 all sat at highway entrances outside the center,7 where everyone entering the city passed them, and a village's own authority ran to banishment.147 China executed in the marketplace (棄市 qishi, "abandon in the market"; Qing Beijing's Caishikou was the vegetable market at a crossroads outside Xuanwumen),8 and although a county magistrate could not CONFIRM a death sentence - capital cases climbed to the Board of Punishments and the emperor's autumn check-marking (秋審 qiushen9) - the confirmed sentence came back DOWN and was carried out at the county seat where the crime happened,109 because local deterrence was the point. So: China supplies the jurisdiction (the seat executes), Japan supplies the siting (past the built edge). That is the answer to the question every town on these maps is drawn from: what would a Chinese county seat administered by samurai do?
  • It explains a line of the setting's canon. The setting's own county budget funds "prisoner food, jail upkeep, court materials" at county level - a jail with no execution line - while "ceremonial executions" appear only in the domain and Imperial budgets. Read through the Chinese chain that is precisely right: the county jail is a holding pen for the condemned while the warrant travels, not a punishment in itself. (The building plans record the same shape: Edo detention was a holding function, not imprisonment-as-punishment.)193
  • The governing variables. For the execution ground: the road and the direction pollution runs - NOT population and not the settlement's geometric edge. The ground exists to be seen by travelers arriving, so it hugs the busiest way out; kegare puts it on the outcast side - at the edge and by the water, where the quarter itself stood; downwind and downstream is this page's reading - which is why the ground is measured against the burakumin quarter (in the setting's canon, burakumin perform every execution that is not a samurai's).192 For the punishment ground: foot traffic.
  • Where the sizes come from. The hard anchor is Suzugamori: 74 x 16.2 m (~243 x 53 ft, about a third of an acre),10 serving Edo - a city of one million - for 220 years.11 Execution grounds are SMALL; the deterrent is the sight of the posts from the highway, not acreage. The tier ladder scales down from that by execution VOLUME, which is also what sets each tier's character: at ~1-3 executions per 100,000 per year, a county of ~7,000-8,000 inhabitants reaches the formal channel about once every 5-10 years (bandit sweeps add batches), a province sees on the order of one a year, a domain capital a handful.146 Hence the county ground is drawn weedy with EMPTY post sockets and no hoarding: a busy scaffold at county scale would assert something false about how often the Empire kills.
  • The beating is not here. The bamboo (笞 / 杖) was a COURT act administered inside the yamen courtyard before the magistrate's bench - which in our maps is the magistracy compound - while the 枷 jia (cangue) was the public part, worn for weeks or months - as long as several months in severe cases - under the magistrate's seal, for passers-by to see.145 So the street feature DISPLAYS; it does not try. It also draws no notice board, because the crime text rides on the cangue and the settlement already has a kosatsuba.
  • A deliberate departure from literal reality, disclosed. The 400 ft clearance between the execution ground and the burial ground is a map-legibility floor, not a historical measurement. In reality the executed went into a pit AT the ground (Kozukappara's burials were haphazard enough that a memorial hall was founded beside it in 1667 for that reason)12 while the community's dead went to temple graveyards at the town's outer edge, in the temple quarter that castle towns gathered there - a different ground from the execution ground.144 Our maps are a few thousand feet across and cannot hold that, so the separation is compressed and the RELATIVE ordering kept honest instead: the execution ground is always the further-out, more polluted of the two. Analogy to this record's other pollution separations (the cremation ground and tanning yard demand 120 ft clear of dwellings, and two POLLUTED grounds need more separation than polluted-from-clean does) gives about 150 ft, and at 225 ft Nagahara's ground still read, in the rendered map, as part of the burial cluster beside it. 400 ft is derived from the picture rather than by analogy - at the coarsest scale we draw it is the distance at which the two are unmistakably two places.
  • Two rules, and why they are shaped as they are. (1) The ground stands ~120 real ft from a drawn ROAD but ~400 ft from a GATE: a road is a line the ground can hug (Suzugamori sat on the Tokaido),13 while a gate is a point standing in for the road that leaves it, and Edo's grounds were miles out along their highways.191 (2) The outcast-side rule tests DIRECTION only: radius is an artifact of where the wall happens to be, while the claim the research actually supports (downwind, downstream, the outcast side) is directional.
  • The boundary stone earns its place. 道祖神 dosojin / sae no kami stand at village boundaries and crossroads, and the etymology is the point: sae means "to block", and the deity's job is to stop intruders and calamity from ENTERING.143 That converts "outside the settlement" from a vague spatial claim into a stated ritual boundary - the ground is not merely far from the houses, it is on the far side of the stone that keeps pollution out - which is why the stone stands outside the wall. A dosojin inside the rampart bounds nothing.

The pages read call Suzugamori and Kozukappara Edo's14 「the two great execution grounds」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「二大刑場」); the third (Ōwada) was not confirmed. The siting finding - past the built edge, on the highway - rests on the two.

The stone stands nearer the houses than the ground does, and a radial ordering could not say so (GM 2026-07-27). The ordering is measured to the settlement's EDGE and not to its center: a settlement is not a disc, so a ground tucked in beside a quarter on a different bearing can lie inside the stone's line for most of the town: at Ubame, 86 of 118 dwellings stood nearer the killing ground than the stone that is supposed to bound it.

The rule the map follows: a seat of justice draws two grounds sited by opposite logics, and a settlement that is not a seat of justice draws neither. The PUNISHMENT ground - a cangue frame, a flogging post and a kneeling stone on about 30 by 12 ft of tamped earth - stands inside the settlement at the market or the magistracy frontage, within about 60 ft of a street and never in the roadbed, inside the rampart where there is one, and carries no notice board of its own because the crime rides on the cangue. The EXECUTION ground stands outside: past the boundary stone, within about 120 ft of a drawn road but about 400 ft out from a gate (a road is a line the ground can hug; a gate is a point standing in for the road that leaves it), 400 ft clear of the burial ground, off the farmland, and on the same side of town as the outcast quarter - a direction, never a radius. It is sized by tier: about 60 by 60 ft at a county seat, drawn weedy and unfenced with empty post sockets; about 100 by 60 ft at a provincial city, screened on three sides with the road side open; 150 to 250 by 50 to 80 ft at a capital. Its road side faces the road. The BOUNDARY stone stands on the road out, between the settlement and the ground: past the rampart where there is one, and 60 ft clear of the nearest dwelling's wall where there is not - not the 120 ft the ground owes, because that is a pollution separation and a boundary stone pollutes nothing; it marks where clean ground ends and all it must not do is stand among the houses. And the stone stands nearer the settlement's edge than the ground does. Every distance here is wall to wall, never center to center. The tier sizes are calibrated down from the one measured ground by execution volume rather than taken from a record, and the 60 ft around the stone is this project's own figure.
