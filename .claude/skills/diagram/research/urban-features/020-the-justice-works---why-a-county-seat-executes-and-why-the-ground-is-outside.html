The justice works - why a county seat executes, and why the ground is outside

Sources: caishikou-enwiki, qiushen-zhwiki; suzugamori-jawiki, kozukappara-jawiki (the two highway-entrance grounds). The 棄市 term and the third Edo ground (Ōwada) were not confirmed in the pages read; the county-jail reading comes from the setting's own budget notes, which are canon rather than evidence, rekilabo-shokeijo, tsuihokei-jawiki, tenmacho-jawiki

  • The China/Japan split, and how it resolves. This is one of the few places the two grounding traditions genuinely disagree. Japan pushed executions OUT of the settlement, to where they would be seen - Edo's execution grounds (two are confirmed, Suzugamori and Kozukappara; a third, Ōwada, is unconfirmed) all sat at highway entrances outside the center, where everyone entering the city passed them, and a village's own authority ran to banishment. China executed in the marketplace (棄市 qishi, "abandon in the market"; Qing Beijing's Caishikou was the vegetable market at a crossroads outside Xuanwumen), and although a county magistrate could not CONFIRM a death sentence - capital cases climbed to the Board of Punishments and the emperor's autumn check-marking (秋審 qiushen) - the confirmed sentence came back DOWN and was carried out at the county seat where the crime happened, because local deterrence was the point. So: China supplies the jurisdiction (the seat executes), Japan supplies the siting (past the built edge). That is the answer to the question every town on these maps is drawn from: what would a Chinese county seat administered by samurai do?
  • It explains a line of the setting's canon. The setting's own county budget funds "prisoner food, jail upkeep, court materials" at county level - a jail with no execution line - while "ceremonial executions" appear only in the domain and Imperial budgets. Read through the Chinese chain that is precisely right: the county jail is a holding pen for the condemned while the warrant travels, not a punishment in itself. (The building plans record the same shape: Edo detention was a holding function, not imprisonment-as-punishment.)

The pages read call Suzugamori and Kozukappara Edo's 「the two great execution grounds」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「二大刑場」); the third (Ōwada) was not confirmed. The siting finding - past the built edge, on the highway - rests on the two.

Three questions follow from this one. Where does the execution ground stand is which road, which side of town, and how far from the graves and the boundary stone. How big is an execution ground is its size at each tier and why a county seat's is drawn empty. What stands on the punishment ground in town is the display ground inside the settlement.

The rule the map follows: a seat of justice draws two grounds sited by opposite logics, and a settlement that is not a seat of justice draws neither: a PUNISHMENT ground inside the settlement, where the traffic is, and an EXECUTION ground outside it, past the built edge on a road out. Where each stands, and how big the execution ground is at each tier, are the three questions above.
