Village granaries (gogura)

A village of the Edo period kept a storehouse, the gogura, for its tax rice and for grain laid up against a bad harvest. After the harvest the tax rice went into it before it was shipped on to the lord's stores, and from the middle of the period it held reserve grain as well. It stood among the village's houses, mostly on dry-field ground.

  • Not every village had a storehouse of its own.
    Several villages might share one, or the headman's own storehouse served instead.
  • The shogunate ordered villages to build their own storehouses in 1789.
    At first village officials' private storehouses were often used, until grain was found going out of them improperly; the shogunate then ordered storehouses built at the village's expense. A Yabu city page dates the order to 1790.
  • The village officials kept the storehouse and entered every deposit and release in books.
    So says the dictionary's entry.
  • The Kurobane domain built grain stores in its villages in 1769, graded by each village's population.
    They were graded upper, middle or lower, held barnyard and foxtail millet, and were run mainly by the village officials. They were themselves called gogura: on the ground one more village storehouse, not a second kind of building.
  • How much did a household lay up in a Kurobane store?
    About 2 sho of barnyard millet a year, as far as the city's page can say ("it seems").
  • The relief granary on the Chinese model (shasō) was first set up in Japan, it is said, in the Aizu domain in 1655.
    At harvest farmers put in grain in proportion to their holdings; it was stored district by district against famine and lent out to farmers in ordinary years.
  • How many storehouses did a village keep?
    One, where the village built its own, but the unit varied. At Hataya in Katashina (Gunma) one was built for each hamlet section on the intendant's order, holding 10 koku; 96 once stood on the island of Sado; and in the Nikko shrine estate villagers built 8 together.
  • How big was a village storehouse?
    The measured village storehouses at The granary and the tax rice (gokura) run from about 21 by 21 ft to about 37 by 20 ft; that section also takes up whether the floor stood on posts. The surviving one at Yamaji is smaller than any of those: a two-story tiled building 340 cm (~11 ft) across the front, 290 cm (~10 ft) deep and 500 cm (~16 ft) high to the roof.
  • A manual for rural officials of 1794 sets the storehouse among the village's houses.
    It is mostly built on dry-field ground, and on a house plot where a vacant one is to be had.
  • At Yamaji the surviving storehouse turns its door to the old road.
    A single storehouse stands there, its door to the old prefectural road.

China drew the same line between a village's own grain store and the state's granary at the seat.

  • Under the Sui dynasty a charity granary was set up in each village community.
    Its grain was levied by law according to wealth.
  • The later village relief granary (shecang) was run by the villagers themselves.
    It stood mostly in the rural community, and its grain was given by well-off or ordinary households, stored in ordinary times and given out in disaster.
  • The ever-normal granary (changpingcang), the state's store, stood mostly in the county seat.
    It steadied the grain price and served for relief, and the county magistrate ran it directly.
