Notice boards (kosatsuba)

A notice board (kosatsuba) was the wooden frame on which the shogunate and the domains posted standing law, so that it would be known to everyone. The boards carried such things as the ban on Christians, the rules on coin, on arson and on packhorse charges, and they stood where traffic was heaviest: at a town's central crossroads, at its entrances, at the foot of bridges and at the road barriers.

The boards measured at two post towns on the Tokaido highway were roofed frames about 10 ft high and 16 to 18 ft along the road, on a stone base inside a fence. In a village, the headman who copied out the lord's circulars had to be able to read and write, so one reader was enough for a village to follow what its board said.

  • Did every town and village keep a board?
    The shogunate ordered boards set at the entrances and centers of towns and villages, and told the domains to do the same, and in one district of Musashi, now the town of Ogose, each of seventeen villages had a board at a key point. That every town and village kept one is this page's reading of these two; no page we read says so in words.
  • The boards carried standing law.
    The most important of the ofuregaki circulars were posted on the board, which the shogunate meant to make its orders known to the whole population. The five great boards of the early Edo period covered miscellaneous matters, Christians, poisons, packhorse fees and arson, and many others were issued beside them; one of the boards issued in 1711 forbade gamblers outright.
  • The boards stood where traffic was heavy.
    The shogunate set its boards at points of heavy passage, at barriers and ports, at the foot of large bridges, and at the entrances and centers of towns and villages.
  • Edo had six great boards and 35 more.
    The six stood at the south end of Nihonbashi, outside Tokiwabashi, inside Asakusabashi and Sujikaibashi, at the Takanawa great gate and outside Hanzomon - bridge ends and gates.
  • A post town's board stood in the station itself.
    Ogose's stood in its lower town, at the southern entrance of the post town. No page we read puts a post town's board at its transport office.
  • A board could not be moved at will.
    The boards at post stations also served as the points from which road distances between stations were measured, so they could not be moved without the lord's permission.
  • Where did a farming village put its board?
    The pages we read name the village's center or its entrance, and the ground before the gates of the village officials' houses, as well as bridge ends. A shrine precinct, or the place where the villagers assembled, is this page's GUESS at other likely spots; no page we read names either.
  • Did a board ever stand set back from the way?
    Every site the pages we read name is on a way - a verge, the front of a gate, the foot of a bridge - and never a plot of open ground beside it. That none stood set back is this page's reading, since no page says so in words.
  • The people who placed a board chose the seat that more passers-by would see.
    Nakatsugawa's board on the Nakasendo highway stood not where its replica stands today but some 20 m (~66 ft) up the slope, at the crossroads with the Hida road, so that more people would see it. Boards were most often set where people passed, at the village center or at a crossroads of main highways.
  • Traffic was not the only reason for a seat.
    Iwadare, a village divided between two lords, set its board at about the same distance from each half's headman's house.

A board's notices reached the villagers through the few who could read them.

  • The headman copied out the circulars and passed them on, and had to read and write.
    A headman (shoya) entered the lord's circulars in a ledger and sent the original on to the next village; since almost every official document needed his signature and seal, he needed at least reading, writing and the abacus. That a hamlet too small for its own headman had a senior farmer answering to the village headman do the same is this page's GUESS.
  • Were the notices read aloud?
    This page's GUESS is that officials also read them aloud, but no page we read says so.
  • How often did an intendant come to a village?
    The shogunate's intendants (daikan) around the Kanto lived in Edo and went out to their territory only for a land survey, a harvest inspection, a tour of inspection or a serious incident. The page gives the occasions, not a count; perhaps twice a year is this page's GUESS. Between visits, the board was the state's standing word in the village.

The boards themselves were far larger than a sign on a post.

  • How big was a notice board?
    Only post-town boards were measured on any page we read. The notices themselves were boards about 30 to 40 cm (~12-16 in) high, hung high enough to be read over the heads of a crowd. A survey of the Tokaido post towns gives frames of about 10 to 11 ft high, 16 to 18 ft along the road and 5 to 6 ft deep at Kanagawa-juku and Totsuka-juku.
  • A board stood on a stone base, inside a fence, under a roof.
    The reconstructed board at Kanagawa has a stone base, a fence round its top, the notices in two tiers inside it, and a roof against wind and rain.
  • A board's site was closed in.
    It was surrounded by a palisade of crossed stakes, or by a stone wall or a bank of turf, and strictly kept.
  • Were village boards as large as a post town's?
    A village's board could be built the same way: the surviving board of Iwadare, a village now in Shiojiri, Nagano, stands on a stone base, large, with sound timbers and a sound roof. But no page we read measures a village board, and none says which of the enclosures was the usual one.
  • No page we read says whether a board stood under a tree.
    Nor does any say that the ground round a board was cleared of trees.

A magistrate's office had business to announce too, and the practice differed between Japan and China.

  • Did a Japanese magistrate's office post a board at its own gate?
    In the Edo period the boards hung before the gates of village officials' houses, as well as at bridge ends. No page we read puts a board at the gate of a Japanese intendant's office or a magistracy.
  • A Chinese county office posted edicts and its own notices on the walls at its gate.
    A modern popular history of China's government offices says the splayed walls flanking the gate carried the emperor's edicts, which the county's inhabitants could read there every month, and beside them the county magistrate's own notices and bans - among them a notice to arrest fleeing murderers and escaped criminals - with lists of tax arrears, rulings on complaints, verdicts and examination results. Its dates are thin: the edicts it quotes are of 1519, but that they hung on the gate walls is its author's own framing, and its verdicts and lists are of county offices of old with no date given.
  • Japan's courts posted laws at their gates only after the boards were abolished.
    Laws came to be posted before the gates of courts and prefectural offices in the Meiji period, after the notice-board system ended.
