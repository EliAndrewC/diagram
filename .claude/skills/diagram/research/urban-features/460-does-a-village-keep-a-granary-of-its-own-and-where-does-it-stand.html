Does a village keep a granary of its own, and where does it stand?

Sources: kotobank-gogura (what the village storehouse held, who kept it, that some villages shared one, and where it stood), yabu-yamaji-gogura (a surviving one standing alone, its door to the road), nasushiobara-kazugamuro-gogura (a domain's village storehouses graded by population), okayama-u-gogura-list (one per hamlet section, and storehouses built jointly), kotobank-shaso (the relief granary in Japan, and the Chinese village granary's start), shecang-zhwiki (the Chinese village relief granary), changpingcang-zhwiki (the Chinese county seat's granary)

The village storehouse. The gogura was the storehouse an Edo-period village kept for its tax rice, and for grain laid up against a bad harvest. Not every village had one: several villages might share one, or the headman's own storehouse served instead. After the harvest the tax rice went into each village's gogura before it was shipped on to the lord's stores; from the mid-Edo period it also held reserve grain; at first village officials' private storehouses were often used, until grain was found going out of them improperly and the shogunate ordered villages to build their own, at the village's expense, in 1789. The village officials kept it and entered every deposit and release in books. (A Yabu city page dates the order to 1790.)

The relief granaries. Some domains built reserve stores in their villages on their own account: the Kurobane domain built them in its villages in 1769, graded upper, middle or lower by the village's population, laying up, it seems, about 2 sho of barnyard millet a household each year. The shasō was the relief granary on the Chinese model: at harvest farmers put in grain in proportion to their holdings, it was stored district by district against famine and lent out to farmers in ordinary years, and it is said to have been first set up in the Aizu domain in 1655. The Kurobane stores were themselves called gogura: on the ground one more village storehouse, not a second kind of building.

How many. One to a village where the village built its own, but the unit varied: at Hataya in Katashina (Gunma) one was built for each hamlet section on the intendant's order, holding 10 koku; on Sado 96 once stood; and in the Nikko shrine estate villagers built 8 together. How the storehouses were sized is the buildings question on grain storehouses, whose measured village storehouses run from about 21 by 21 ft to about 37 by 20 ft. The surviving one at Yamaji is smaller than any of those: a two-story tiled building about 11 ft across the front and 9.5 ft deep.

Where it stands. A manual for rural officials of 1794 puts it plainly: the gogura is attached within the village's houses, mostly built on dry-field ground, and on a house plot where a vacant one is to be had. The one at Yamaji stands alone, a single building, with its door facing the old road.

China. Under the Sui dynasty the charity granary was set up in each village community (she). The later village relief granary, the shecang, was mostly set in the rural community and mostly run by its inhabitants from grain the well-off or ordinary households gave. The ever-normal granary, the state's store for steadying the grain price and for relief, was mostly in the county seat, run by the county magistrate. The pattern is the same as Japan's: the village's grain store is a village building, and the big state granary is the seat's.

What it means for the map. Three forms are attested, so which one a village has is a knob, rolled per village from the map's seed: its own gogura; a gogura shared with neighbors, which stands in one of them; or the headman's storehouse doing the work, which draws as part of their compound. How often each comes up is a guess. A village's own gogura is one storehouse standing on its own among the houses, on dry-field ground rather than paddy, with its door to the lane. Whether its floor stands on posts is the buildings record's question on raised granary floors.

The rule the map follows: each village rolls its granary form: its own storehouse (about half the villages, a guess), a storehouse shared with a neighboring village and drawn in only one of them (about a quarter, a guess), or none apart from the headman's storehouse (about a quarter, a guess). An own storehouse is one building between about 11 by 9.5 ft (the surviving Yamaji gogura) and about 37 by 20 ft (the buildings record's largest measured village storehouse), sized along that range from the seed (calibrated liberty), standing free among the houses on dry ground, door to the lane; a hamlet draws none of its own (a guess).
