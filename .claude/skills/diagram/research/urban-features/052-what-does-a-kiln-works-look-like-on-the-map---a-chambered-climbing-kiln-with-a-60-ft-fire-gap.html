Why the works is one kiln rather than a tile kiln, and why its potters live at it - a firing that is stoked round the clock for days among the reasons - is Kiln works - a kiln is not a tile kiln, and the potters live at it. This question is the kiln's drawn shape, its fire gap and its sizes.

  • THE FORM. A low earthen mound is a CHARCOAL kiln's shape, and would be indistinguishable from Ubame's charcoal district. A tile or pottery kiln in this culture is a long chambered tunnel built into a slope: firebox at the bottom, chambers climbing, chimney at the top. The chambers grow larger toward the top of the kiln. The chambered silhouette also carries the siting fact a mound would hide - the works wants rising ground. (No separate slope band is drawn behind the kiln: at city scale it reads as a stray tab off the kiln's shoulder rather than as ground, and the chambered form says it already.)
  • THE 60 FT FIRE GAP, and why it is not a new number. It joins the existing separation ladder at the rung the refining forge already occupies. A firing is a very large fire, but an ATTENDED one - being stoked in shifts is the whole reason it runs the way it does - so it sits with the live worked fire at 60 ft and not with the UNATTENDED self-heating charcoal stack at 30 ft, nor with the 120 ft nuisance figures, which defend against smell carried on air rather than against ignition. Duration does the work here that the forced blast does for the forge. No page read says how far a real kiln stood from the houses round it, so the 60 ft is this project's rung on its own ladder, not a finding; what the record does show is that the works' own workshop stood right beside the kiln - at Motoyashiki the floor of a disused kiln became the workshop of the new one next to it. The gap is measured from the KILN BODY rather than from the works' ground, because the works' own cottages stand inside that ground - a bounding-rect test could only ever measure the gap to somebody else's house.
  • How long a kiln is. Longer than the map draws it. The smallest excavated climbing kiln read, a lord's private kiln at Kokura of a stoke mouth and four chambers, is 16.6 m (about 54 ft) long; the oldest in Mino, at Motoyashiki, is about 24 m (79 ft) long and on average 2.2 m (7 ft) wide, with fourteen chambers above its firebox. The porcelain villages built far larger kilns later - Arita's grew past 100 m and were shared by several kiln houses, and Mikawachi fired kilns of 120 m and 100 m from the late 17th and early 18th centuries into the 20th. A shared kiln of that length serves a whole potters' village, a different thing from one works with its own kiln. The map's 46 ft kiln is shorter than any kiln read; one works' kiln should run about 55 to 80 ft up its slope and about 7 ft wide, its chambers widening as they climb.
