Clan borders and their markers

Under the Tokugawa, in the Edo period, the domains of Japan were already marking off their lands with agreed, linear borders, and the evidence is their boundary disputes, their boundary markers and their maps. A border was a line two domains had argued over and then agreed.

The best-known example is the border between the domains of Morioka (the Nanbu clan's) and Sendai (the Date clan's), settled in 1642 after half a century of dispute and marked with a line of more than a hundred earth mounds along about 130 km (~426,509 ft). Separately, the shogunate had every province draw a map of its lands, with the district boundaries drawn on it.

  • Linear borders were not foreign to early modern Japan.
    A study of the period's boundary disputes, boundary markers and map-making finds the domains well into building a territorial order of demarcated borders, each excluding the other, and frontiers that had been broad, fuzzy zones becoming clearly drawn lines. Its author adds that this was not true everywhere: some areas stayed much less territorial than others.
  • Morioka and Sendai agreed their border in 1642, early in the Edo period, after more than fifty years of dispute.
    The two domains agreed to build more than 100 mounds along 130 km (~426,509 ft) of the line, so that it could not be confused again. The line ran from Mount Komagatake in the Ou Mountains in the west to the Pacific in the east.
  • The Morioka-Sendai mounds grew smaller and closer together over time.
    They were large and far apart at first, then smaller and more densely spaced, as the border changed from a broad frontier to a line; in 1688 a further series of smaller mounds was built to mark it more closely.
  • The English Wikipedia article calls such boundary markers common under the Tokugawa.
    It describes the Morioka-Sendai mounds as earth mounds built early in the Edo period, and says markers of the kind were common under the shogunate.
  • The shogunate had every province draw a map of its lands (kuniezu).
    The provincial maps were made through the Edo period at the shogunate's order.
  • The Genroku revision of the provincial maps drew the district boundaries clearly.
    The revision's basic policy drew the district boundaries vividly and wrote each village's assessed yield inside its shape, and any place in dispute, or at odds with the older maps, was put to the intendants and lords and settled before the map was made up. The boundaries the entry names are those between districts, not between domains.
