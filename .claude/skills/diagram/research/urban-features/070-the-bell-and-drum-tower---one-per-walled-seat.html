The bell-and-drum tower - one per walled seat

Sources: pingyao-shilou-zhwiki (133.4 m2, three bays square), dongjing-menghualu-rujia (军巡铺 every 300 paces, 望火楼 with a hundred soldiers). What the research found: the "morning bell, evening drum" institution (dawn gate-opening, the dusk gate-closing that began the street curfew, the five night watches, alarm and ceremony) stood beside the yamen, the Confucian temple and the City God temple in a Ming county seat (that the bell and drum governed the gates and began a street curfew, and that the set was standard in walled northern seats, is this page's reading; the attested pieces are the towers' timekeeping, the five watches, and the City God temple ranked down to the county from 1369). A county seat's tower is a drum tower, a combined bell-and-drum tower, or a market tower straddling the main street; the paired gulou and zhonglou facing each other is the fuller form (Beijing, Xi'an), and Pingyao, a wealthy county seat, has exactly one (the ~60 ft Market Tower on South Street, the only tall building in the old city). County-tier form: Pingyao's Market Tower - the wealthy-county showpiece - is ATTESTED at 133.4 m^2 plan, ~38 ft square; these towers dominated by HEIGHT (50-60 ft), not plan, and Kawagoe's famous time-bell tower served a town of tens of thousands from a base that is small rather than broad. The larger square examples at Dingbian and Xingcheng (whose platform is 21 m square) are the tower-over-street-arch type - garrison and prefecture fabric, not a 3,000-person seat. The drawn band is therefore 30-45 ft: ~36 ft at city tier, ~30 ft at town tier - one rowhouse width, which is exactly how they read in plan. Siting: the central crossroads, where Xingcheng's and Dai county's towers stand at the exact center; the qiaolou - the official compound's own drum tower - is the recognized variant, not drawn here. Critically, FIRE WATCH WAS A SEPARATE INSTITUTION in both reference cultures - Song Kaifeng put a patrol post every three hundred paces in each ward and a wanghuolou fire-watch tower on high ground with a hundred soldiers quartered under it, and in Edo the licensed toki no kane time bell and the hinomi-yagura fire towers were separate institutions, though elsewhere a fire tower's hansho could carry the time signal too - so on these maps the fire towers do not cover the timekeeping/curfew niche. The decision it drove: every walled seat (provincial city or walled town) draws exactly ONE combined bell-and-drum tower at its main street crossing (a corner of the crossing, not straddling the street), ~36 ft at city tier and ~30 ft at town tier, visibly heavier-built than the skeletal fire towers; the capital tier may take the full paired gulou/zhonglou when it exists (and the street-arch platform type, 50-66 ft, is available as a garrison-city variant). An UNWALLED town is exempt: it has no gates to close, and its time signal is the monastery's bell - the Edo pattern, where all but one of the city's own time bells hung at temples - implied within the precinct, no glyph (Hoshizora). The tower follows the WALL, not the population. The caption reads "drum/bell" over "tower" (GM 2026-07-24): the drawn building is genuinely the combined zhonggulou - both instruments in one pavilion, and both are drawn - so the label says so. Audibility - one central tower covers the whole seat with enormous margin (GM sanity check 2026-07-24). The geometry: Tango's farthest in-wall resident stands ~1,640 ft (500 m) from its tower and the farthest drawn gate-suburb structure ~790 m; Nagahara ~435 m / ~920 m. The attested anchors dwarf those distances: Edo's Kokucho time bell BILLED ~400 blocks of households "within earshot" (300 at first; the bell fee ran 4 mon per ken of street frontage - an effective fee radius of ~1.2-1.4 km in a far denser, noisier city, and a conservative figure, since only admitted hearers were billed); the Ueno and Asakusa bells were among the city's other time bells; Beijing's single drum tower timed the watches for an inner city ~6.5 x 5.3 km. Acoustically, a large bell or great drum runs ~110-130 dB near the tower; at a 500-730 m city edge that arrives ~40 dB above a premodern NIGHT noise floor (~25-35 dBA, when the watches matter most) - loud, not merely detectable, everywhere inside the walls and suburbs, and the low frequencies diffract over one-story rooflines. One tower stops sufficing only past a ~2-3 km radius of daytime urban din (this page's estimate), so even the future ~12,000-pop capital tier (~1 mi across) needs no second tower on audibility grounds: the historical scaling answers are Edo's multiplied fee-district bells vs the China-first pattern of ONE monumental tower whose watches ward night-watchmen relay through the streets with clappers and gongs - the capital tier should take the latter (grander tower, implied watchmen, still exactly one).
