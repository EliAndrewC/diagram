Did a town bar its street at night, and who kept the watch?

Sources: kidoban-jawiki (the gates of the castle towns, their keepers and their huts, and Kyoto's gates), kidoban-kotobank (how many gates a small town set, and what a gate looked like), jishinban-jawiki (the ward watch house, its size and its seat), shukuba-jawiki (the post town's two gates), shukuba-mitsuke-kaido-walking (what the post town's entrance banks were), banta-kotobank (who the watchmen were), jinkennagano-burakushi (villages that invited outcastes in as guards), tsujiban-kotobank (a provincial castle town's night rounds)

How many watch houses and guard boxes a city kept is the next question; the fire tower beside the gate is the one before.

Yes: the gates were shut at night. Many castle towns besides Edo set up kido, ward gates, which were shut at night; each had two keepers, mostly old men who lived in the hut beside it, and in 1658 Edo told any ward without a gate to put up a temporary one. A large town set a gate to every block, a middle or small town one to several blocks; in Edo a gate was a pair of doors hung between posts about 2 ken (12 ft) apart, with a fence or board wall running from each post to the edge of the road and the keeper's hut beside it, shut from ten at night to six in the morning, after which the only way through was the small side door under the keeper's eye. The keeper sent anyone who passed on to the next gate with a signal of wooden clappers, and walked the night round against fire. Kyoto had set gates, called kuginuki, at the crossings at both ends of each ward since the Muromachi period; in the Edo period a board-floored hut about one tatami in size stood beside each, kept by two watchmen at night and one by day, whom the ward hired from lower-status people. A post town had a gate at each end, near its mitsuke, the ground between the two being the post town itself, sometimes with a lamp kept burning all night as well, and in most cases they were shut at night; the mitsuke was an earthen bank on either side of the entrance with a fence along its top.

The huts. The gate keeper's hut was fixed at 6 by 9 shaku (about 6 by 9 ft) with eaves 1 jo (about 10 ft) high. The ward's watch house, the jishinban, was nominally 9 shaku by 2 ken (about 9 by 12 ft) but in practice some 2 to 3 ken; it usually stood at the south corner of a crossing on the main east-west street, and the gate hut and the watch house both stood against the gates at the two ends of each ward's main street, the gate keeper at one and the watch at the other. In Osaka the watch house was not a building of its own but was built in line with the ordinary townhouses.

Who kept the watch. The banta, watchmen whom towns and villages hired for the night watch and to watch for fire and over the sluice gates, many of them of hinin status; in some villages they also guarded the hills, fields and sluices, ran errands and controlled vagrants; outside Edo many were hinin and were called ban-hinin, and in some regions they also dug graves, cremated and dealt with those who died on the road. Villages too, later in the period, invited outcastes in to be their guards. In the provincial castle town of Aizu-Wakamatsu the town itself kept guard boxes, the tsujiban, about two men to each, who at midnight and at two in the morning went round their group of blocks calling "take care", asked any wanderer where they lived and passed them on to the next box, at the cost of a group of several blocks.

What it means for the map. A county seat bars its main street at night. It draws a ward gate at each end where the road leaves the built core - the post town's form, and the castle town's at a small town's grain of one gate to several blocks - and, as a degree rolled per settlement, at most one more across the street between them. Beside each gate stands the keeper's hut, about 6 by 9 ft, and at one end the watch house, from about 9 by 12 ft up to 12 by 18 ft, a degree rolled per settlement; the source's "some 2 to 3 ken" does not say which side it measures, so reading the larger house as 2 by 3 ken is a guess. The earthen banks of a post town's mitsuke belong to a post town, not to a market seat. The watchmen are hired from the lowest status, as many of the banta outside Edo were; in the setting that is the hinin.
