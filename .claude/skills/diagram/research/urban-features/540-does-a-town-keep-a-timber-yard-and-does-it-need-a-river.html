Does a town keep a timber yard, and does it need a river?

Sources: kotobank-zaimokuya (where the timber trade grew up, and the retail dealer), zaimokuuri-jawiki (a city stream serving as a log pond)

The quarter on the water. The timber trade grew up where consumers and good water transport met - Kyoto's Horikawa, where timber merchants gathered from the twelfth century, and the timber guilds of Kizu and Kamakura's harbor from the thirteenth - and the quarters where timber merchants gathered were called kiba, timber yards. The water need not be a great river: the Horikawa is said to have been about 12 m (40 ft) wide and could serve as a log pond. How Edo's own timber quarter was gathered onto new waterways is in Trade works.

The dealer in town. The retail timber dealer is the other form. He kept a few sawyers to cut timber into the large and small sizes his customers wanted, and raftsmen to move the timber he had bought in; and retail timber dealers were found scattered in every region, the builders taking their work from them. So a town without a river still had a man who sold boards and beams; how big his yard was no page read says.

The rule the map follows: two forms, a knob rolled from the map's seed. A town or city with a river or a channel may keep its timber on the water - a yard on the bank with logs held in the water beside it, as a river port does under Trade works. Any town, river or not, may instead keep a retail timber dealer: a shop with a small yard of stacked sawn timber behind it, the yard and its size this record's guess. A landlocked town is therefore not without timber; it is without the log pond.
