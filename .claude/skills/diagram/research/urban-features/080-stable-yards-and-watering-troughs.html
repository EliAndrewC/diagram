Stable yards and watering troughs

A stable yard was the open ground beside a stable, at an inn or at a city gate, where wagon trains pulled in, the animals were unhitched and tied up, and the teamsters waited between stages. China set hitching posts at the gates of its great houses and temples, and the Qingming Shanghe Tu, a Chinese painted scroll of a city, shows wagons, donkeys and mules crowding a city gate. No source describes the ground of such a yard; beaten earth with no fence is this project's reading of the picture.

A horse drinks about ten gallons of water a day, and several times that under hard work, so a train of a few dozen animals arriving thirsty needs hundreds of gallons. The troughs of premodern stable yards that the record found held feed, not water, and the free-standing watering trough is a modern form. Watering was done by ration at set times, and a caravanserai watered its trains from a well in its own courtyard. How big a stable is is at How big is a stable, and what is its yard like?

  • The hitching post was a common fixture at Chinese gates.
    A Chinese encyclopedia calls the hitching post a kind of stele common in the traditional architecture of China and all East Asia, set on either side of the main gate of a house, temple, tomb or palace so that visitors could tie up their horses. The page describes posts at a gate, not the rails of a cart yard.
  • The Qingming scroll shows a city gate crowded with wagons and pack animals.
    The Qingming Shanghe Tu scroll shows goods and travelers moved by wheeled wagons and a large number of donkeys and mules, with only a few guards at the city gate. That the animals stand tethered, and that the ground has no fence and no grass, is this project's reading of the picture, not the cited page's words.
  • What was the ground of a gate yard like?
    Beaten earth, trampled hard, with no grass, open to the road and edged by the town wall and the buildings beside it, not fenced. This is a reconstruction: no page we read describes the ground or the edge of an ordinary cart yard at a city gate. That the animals were fed hay at mangers there rather than grazed, and that a rail fence belongs to an inn's private back yard or a walled caravanserai, are this project's reading.
  • The oldest Japanese horse trough held feed.
    The Japanese word for a horse trough is found in the Wamyosho, a dictionary of about 934, and means the container a horse's feed is put in, a fodder tub. The dictionary gives no size and no sign that it held water.
  • A sixth-century Chinese farm manual gives a stable of many stallions its own yard, with many troughs.
    The Qimin Yaoshu tells whoever keeps many stallions to make a separate yard and set out many troughs and stalls, with the chopped fodder, the grain and the beans each in its own place. Its troughs are for fodder; it gives no watering trough and no size.
  • Did a stable's trough stand under a shed?
    Probably. The same manual, to harden a horse for riding on journeys, sets its trough out on open ground away from the stable and not under a shed, even in snow. That this is an exception, and so that an ordinary trough stood under a shed, is this project's reading.
  • Horses were watered by ration, three times a day.
    The Qimin Yaoshu gives a horse a little water in the morning, a moderate measure at midday, and its fill in the evening.
  • The free-standing watering trough is a modern form, first set up in Japan in 1906.
    The first in Japan was a gift to the city of Tokyo from London's drinking-fountain and cattle-trough association, set up on the sidewalk in front of the city hall in November 1906; the association's surviving troughs in London are large ones of granite, and it took cattle troughs into its name in 1867. That this kind of trough was drunk from on both sides is this project's reading of the form.
  • Did a premodern stable yard have a watering trough beside its well?
    None was found. Every premodern trough we found is a fodder trough, and none is sized; the stone troughs at the old wells of a village in Dali were for washing vegetables and clothes.
  • How much does a horse drink?
    A modern horse of 450 kg (~992 lb) drinks 10 to 12 gallons a day, more in hot weather or on dry hay, and as much as four times that under hard work. The smaller horses of a premodern post road drank less, and a working ox about the same as a horse; both are this project's estimates, and no premodern figure was found beside the modern one.
  • How much water does a wagon train need at a halt?
    About 300 to 600 gallons for 20 to 40 head arriving thirsty, drunk in one or two long sessions. The total is this project's arithmetic from the modern drinking figures, and the sessions are its reading.
  • A water buffalo in a hot climate needs a wallow, not just a trough.
    Buffalo need wallows, rivers or splashing water to keep cool.
  • How fast does an ox drink?
    Modern feedlot cattle drink 1.1 to 3.7 gallons a minute, and spend 7 to 22 minutes a day at the trough. At that rate a working ox's session of 5 to 8 gallons, this project's estimate, is over in two to seven minutes. The rate is a modern one, taken as the animal's own.
  • How long did a draft team rest at a halt?
    An hour or so, and several hours in hot weather. On the American emigrant trails the oxen were unhitched at the midday halt to rest and graze; most trains rested only an hour or so, and in hot weather might stay several hours and travel on in the cool of the evening. This is the one readable account found of how long a draft team rested; no page we read gives a Chinese or Japanese post station's rest.
  • Caravanserais watered whole trains from a well or fountain in their own courtyard.
    These roadside inns of the Silk Road housed horses, camels and pack animals beside their travelers, and gave water for humans and animals from a fountain or a well in the courtyard.
  • A single trough in Victorian London could water 1,800 horses a day.
    The London trough association reported that one trough had served the wants of 1,800 horses in 24 hours.
  • How did an inn yard in a town water its animals?
    By relay: the animals were led to the water a few at a time, and water was drawn by bucket from a well into the troughs. This is this project's reading, from the caravanserai's courtyard well and from the bucket and the well, not a channel, being how a town drew its water. A trough beside a well is a GUESS: none was found.
