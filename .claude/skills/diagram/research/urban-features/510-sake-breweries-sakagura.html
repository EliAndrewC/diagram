Sake breweries (sakagura)

A sake brewery (sakagura) brewed sake from rice in a hall of fermenting vats, which at Itami stood north of its brewer's shop. Under the shogunate brewing was at first confined to the towns and licensed, and many brewers were wealthy families of local note, commonly pictured with a solid shop at the center of the town. The brewery buildings measured from the Edo period, before 1868, run about 50 to 60 ft long.

  • In this setting a county's specialized trades include its sake brewer.
    A county's specialized trades are named as the local clothier and the sake brewer, and the county magistrate's gifts come from wealthy artisans and tradesmen such as the sake brewer, the master smith and the dye-house owner. That matches the shogunate's law, which at first kept brewing in the towns.
  • Many brewers came from wealthy families of local note.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on brewers says many were in origin families of wealth and figures of local note.
  • Did a brewer keep a shop at the center of the town?
    That is the common picture of a brewer in the Japanese Wikipedia article on brewers: a large household with a solid shop at the town's center. The article names no period or place for it, and that a brewer's standing put the shop there is this project's reading.
  • Many brewers also lent money, and some kept a pawnshop.
    Brewers commonly lent money at high interest, and in the provinces not a few also kept a pawnshop. The pawnbroker's trade is at Pawnshops (shichiya).
  • At first the shogunate confined brewing to the towns and licensed every brewer.
    Brewing was allowed in the castle towns, post towns, ports and other country towns and forbidden to farmers, and a system of licenses meant no one could open a brewery at will.
  • From the early eighteenth century the villages were encouraged to brew.
    After rice prices fell in the early eighteenth century the shogunate dropped its ban on rural brewing and encouraged brewing in the villages.
  • Hachijo, a village of what is now Yashio city, had a sake-brewing merchant.
    The district's local history names one, without a date.
  • Did breweries stand even along lonely country roads?
    Old travel diaries suggest so, says the Japanese Wikipedia article on brewers, which neither names nor dates them.
  • How many brewers did a town keep?
    No page we read counts the brewers of a town of a given size. The setting's county has "the" sake brewer, one house.

The brewing itself was done in the vat hall, and three brewery buildings of the Edo period have been measured.

  • A brewery hall of the early eighteenth century in Itami measures about 52 by 49 ft.
    The brewer built it around 1715 north of the shop to expand his brewing: a two-story hall 15.7 m (~52 ft) by 14.8 m (~49 ft), with eaves on three sides.
  • A fermenting store of the late Edo period in Tochigi measures about 60 by 30 ft.
    The Sugita brewery's fermenting store, built between 1830 and 1868 and moved about 1883-1897, is a two-story earthen storehouse 18.2 m (~60 ft) by 9.14 m (~30 ft).
  • A brewery's sake store north of Kanazawa castle is about 62 ft long, with a floor of about 1,400 sq ft.
    The Yachiya brewery's store, built between 1751 and 1829, is a long, narrow two-story earthen storehouse about 19 m (~62 ft) long with a building area of 132 square meters (~1,421 sq ft). By this project's arithmetic that leaves about 7 m (~23 ft) of width.
  • Were brewery halls of about 100 ft built only after Japan began to modernize in 1868?
    A fermenting store in Wakayama of 16 by 6 ken, about 96-103 by 36-39 ft depending on the length of the ken, is of the Meiji era, after 1868, and no Edo-period hall that long was found.
  • Was the vat hall the largest commercial building in a small town?
    Often, we GUESS: by this page's arithmetic an Edo-period hall has one to two times the floor of a shophouse (Shops and trades in towns and villages).
  • What else stood in a brewer's compound?
    At Itami the hall stood north of the brewer's shop. At Kanazawa the sake store stands north of a tool store in the middle of the brewer's long plot, and is thought to be of about the same age as the main house. That the hall usually stood behind the shop is this project's reading. That the compound also held a koji room, a rice-steaming stove, a well and a rice store is a GUESS from what brewing needs: no page we read lists the rooms and yards of a brewer's compound.
  • No page we read gives the height of the vats or the ground a brewery took.
    The brewery records and articles we read give no vat height, no size for the koji room and no area for the whole compound.
