Caste geography and status zoning

Sources: kichinyado-jawiki (the flophouse), abele-2018-kawata (the outcast cluster inside the village territory, and the riverbank name behind it); that kawata communities were relocated into castle towns (Matsumoto, Takada) is this page's reading, on no page read; burakumin-enwiki, kidoban-jawiki, jokamachi-jawiki, lifang-zhwiki, edo-hantei-jawiki

Castes lived apart. Burakumin cluster at the margin, at a town's edge or outside a city. The maps also put at least one burakumin neighborhood INSIDE a walled city, and the reason offered for it - that a city under siege cannot do without their labor - is this record's own reasoning rather than something read. The commoner blocks are closed by kido ward-gates shut at night - each town block's own gates, with a watchman and a wicket - and the samurai quarter lies beyond them (status separation and night security; a small provincial city uses kido, not the continuous walled wards of a Tang-style capital, which ended with the Song). The cheap flophouse (kichin-yado) belongs in a humble laborer/agrarian quarter, not beside the temples, merchants, or samurai. Great houses kept roomier walled estates in the outskirts - Edo's daimyo lower residences were villas and fire refuges away from the castle - while the working residence stood nearest the seat.

The seam is measured wall to wall, and the collar is reserved before the farm rings run (the GM's audit of 2026-07-27). Two things decide it together, and either alone loses the seam. A gap measured center to center at 40 ft counts a house as clear while its wall is not; and a surrounding farm ring laid AFTER the quarter is packed closes in on a collar nothing has reserved. What that costs is eight farmhouses standing inside the seam on the audited map, one of them 5 ft from a hut - and a quarter that reads as continuous with the farms around it is not a segregated quarter at all.

The rule the map follows: at town scale a burakumin quarter stands off every other dwelling by 60 ft of open ground, wall to wall, and that collar is reserved before anything else is allowed to grow into it. The figure has to distinguish a separate quarter from a merely dense one: dwellings inside a quarter pack at 10 to 30 ft of each other on these maps, so the seam must be several times that to read as a gap rather than as a wide lane. It is deliberately well BELOW the 120 ft pollution standoff, because this is a zoning statement about who lives beside whom rather than a buffer against kegare. CITIES are exempt by design: a ward system zones quarters wall to wall, so a city's segregation is the ward rather than the gap, and the seams between adjacent quarters on the drawn cities run about 30 ft. The 60 ft is a calibration against the drawn maps, not a finding - the historical outcast hamlet was a separate residential cluster inside the village's own territory, clearly set apart from the peasants' plots but at no fixed distance out, and in one Kawachi case the peasants of the village lived nearer to two neighboring villages than to the kawata of their own; the older name for the status, kawaramono, "people of the riverbank", is attested, but the dissertation draws no siting from it - reading "across its stream" out of the name is this project's own step, and nothing read places such a settlement on or over a watercourse.
