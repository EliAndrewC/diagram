Caste geography and status zoning

Sources: naniwa-nitto-lookback (Osaka's Nagamachi, its kichin-yado and its poor back streets), kichinyado-jawiki (Edo's first flophouse and its block), gannin-kotobank (the mendicants of that block),abele-2018-kawata (the outcast cluster inside the village territory, and the riverbank name behind it); burakumin-enwiki, kidoban-jawiki, jokamachi-jawiki, lifang-zhwiki, edo-hantei-jawiki

Castes lived apart. Burakumin lived in segregated villages or in parts of towns, and one painted buraku stands outside a city's walls. The maps also put at least one burakumin neighborhood INSIDE a walled city, and the reason offered for it - that a city under siege cannot do without their labor - is this record's own reasoning rather than something read. The commoner blocks are closed by kido ward-gates shut at night - each town block's own gates, with a watchman and a wicket - and the samurai quarter lies beyond them (status separation and night security; a small provincial city uses kido, not the continuous walled wards of a Tang-style capital, which did not outlast the Song and Yuan). The cheap flophouse (kichin-yado) stands where the poor lodge, not beside the temples, merchants, or samurai, and in a city of the Edo period that was a humble laborers' quarter on the road in. In Osaka the street where the highway south to Sakai left the city, Nagamachi, was lined with inns and kichin-yado, and migrant laborers and the destitute gathered in its back streets, living in tenements rented by the day or unregistered; its first five blocks were renamed in 1792, it is said to shed the name of a poor quarter. Edo's first kichin-yado, by a twentieth-century researcher's account, opened in the Meiwa era (1764-1772) in Shitaya Yamazaki-cho, a block where the town magistrate's office found the gannin, mendicants who begged in the streets, living in 1842. So a city's flophouse in its poor quarter is an Edo-period form, which the Meiji years multiplied, and the maps keep it there. A town's stands on the road at its edge, as the kichin-yado stood at the edge of a post town (The market-day flophouse - who actually stays over); in Nagamachi the two sitings were one place, the poor quarter lying along the road in. Great houses kept garden estates in the outskirts - Edo's daimyo lower residences were villas and fire refuges away from the castle - while the working residence stood nearest the seat.

The seam is measured wall to wall, and the collar is held open before the farmland around the quarter is laid (the GM's audit of 2026-07-27). Both are needed. A gap measured between house centers counts a house as clear while its wall stands inside the seam; and farmland laid around a finished quarter closes in on a collar nothing has held open. Miss either and farmhouses stand inside the seam, within feet of the huts - and a quarter that reads as continuous with the farms around it is not a segregated quarter at all.

The rule the map follows: at town scale a burakumin quarter stands off every other dwelling by 60 ft of open ground, wall to wall, and that collar is reserved before anything else is allowed to grow into it. The figure has to distinguish a separate quarter from a merely dense one: dwellings inside a quarter pack at 10 to 30 ft of each other on these maps, so the seam must be several times that to read as a gap rather than as a wide lane. It is deliberately well BELOW the 120 ft pollution standoff, because this is a zoning statement about who lives beside whom rather than a buffer against kegare. CITIES are exempt by design: a ward system zones quarters wall to wall, so a city's segregation is the ward rather than the gap, and the seams between adjacent quarters on the drawn cities run about 30 ft. The 60 ft is a calibration against the drawn maps, not a finding - the historical outcast hamlet was a separate residential cluster inside the village's own territory, clearly set apart from the peasants' plots but at no fixed distance out, and in one case from Kawachi, a province of western Japan, the peasants of the village lived nearer to two neighboring villages than to the kawata of their own; the older name for the status, kawaramono, "people of the riverbank", is attested, but the dissertation draws no siting from it - reading "across its stream" out of the name is this project's own step, and nothing read places such a settlement on or over a watercourse.
