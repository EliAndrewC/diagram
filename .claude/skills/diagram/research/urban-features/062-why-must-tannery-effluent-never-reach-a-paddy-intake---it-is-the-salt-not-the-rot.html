Why must tannery effluent never reach a paddy intake - it is the salt, not the rot

Sources: tanning-leather-enwiki, himeji-shironameshi-jawiki, fao-29-salt-tolerance

Why a town has a tanning yard and why it stands on the water is Tanning yards - hides come from fallen draft stock, and water is the gate. This question is what its discharge does to the fields below it.

  • WHY tannery effluent must not reach a paddy intake - and which part of it actually does the harm (GM question 2026-07-25). Tanning in general is famous for urine and dung: hides were limed OR soaked in stale urine to loosen the hair, then "bated" by kneading them in dog or pigeon dung. But the method these maps commit to is Japanese shironameshi, which uses none of it - no lime, no tannin, no dung, no urine. The hides soak in the river for several days (river bacteria do the unhairing), the hair is knifed off, and the hide is finished with salt and rapeseed oil alone. So the discharge is three things: several days of controlled putrefaction, SALT, and an oil film. The salt is what a rice paddy cannot take. Rice is a moderately salt-sensitive crop - the standard threshold is 3.0 dS/m in the soil and 2.0 in the irrigation water - and it answers to the salinity of the FIELD WATER it stands in, so brine arriving down an irrigation ditch into a flooded paddy is the precise wrong input. The counterintuitive corner: the ORGANIC half of the effluent is arguably fertilizer (Rokugani farmers deliberately spread night soil), so putrefaction is the WEAK argument here. The rule stands on salt, the oil film, and the stench.

A yard on a field drain meets this rule by construction; why that is, and what it costs, is May a tanning yard sit on a field drain.

The rule the map follows: a tanning yard stands DOWNSTREAM of everything the settlement draws from, which is two things at once: the yard's own course must end off the map or in a drain that does, never in a pond, a field, an irrigation ditch or the moat, and no sluice, weir or channel head on that course may lie below the yard, compared by how far each stands along the course itself, since what settles it is which of the two lies further upstream.
