Firebreaks (hiyokechi)

The other answer to a city's fires was open ground where nothing could burn. After the Meireki fire of 1657, the shogunate set aside open ground in Edo to stop a fire spreading, the hiyokechi, and widened streets, the hirokoji, for the same purpose. The firebreaks were few - thirteen by the Kyoho era - and nothing permanent could be built on them, so many of the widened streets filled with stalls and shows.

  • After the Meireki fire, Edo kept open ground against fire.
    The shogunate set it aside in the wake of the fire of 1657, and in the broad sense it included the widened streets, the hirokoji, made for the same purpose.
  • Edo's firebreaks were a few open sites, thirteen by the Kyoho era.
    Five burned-over sites were set aside after the fire, and more were added, mainly to shield Edo Castle, until there were thirteen.
  • A firebreak street filled with stalls and shows, and many became pleasure quarters.
    No permanent building could stand on a hirokoji, but at busy bridgeheads and before popular temples and shrines they filled with movable stalls, markets, street performers and reed-screened show booths, so that many grew into pleasure quarters.
