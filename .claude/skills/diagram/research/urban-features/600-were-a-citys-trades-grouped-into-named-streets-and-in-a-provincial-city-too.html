Were a city's trades grouped into named streets, and in a provincial city too?

Sources: jokamachi-jawiki (the castle town's townspeople settled by trade), kajimachi-jawiki (the smiths' wards of provincial castle towns), mengliang-lu-13-wikisource (Hangzhou's trade rows and markets)

Yes, in a Japanese castle town, by order. The townspeople's ground lay outside the samurai quarters, and it was filled by moving in nearby villages and by moving merchants and artisans in by force, divided by trade; the ward names that survive - Gofuku-machi (drapers), Aburaya-machi (oil sellers), Daiku-machi (carpenters), Kaji-machi (smiths), Konya-machi (dyers) - are these wards. The townspeople's lots were smaller than the samurai's and stood tight along the highway, narrow at the front and deep behind.

And in provincial castle towns, not only the great cities. A Kaji-machi was often the ward of a castle town where, under that settlement by trade, many smiths lived, and the name survives in Hirosaki, Hachinohe, Kuwana and Nabari among many others. The dyers' ward, and what a capital makes of it, is its own question.

A Song capital did it by trade row and market. Hangzhou's trades stood in rows and markets, many named for where they stood: the fish row north of the city, the crab row east of it, the cloth row at Henghetou; the medicine market at Tan bridge, the flower market in Guan lane, the rice market north of the city. Yet along the main street, from the Hening gate to Guan bridge, there was not one house that did not trade - so the city had both named trade rows and a main street where every house traded. No page read describes the trade streets of a Chinese provincial city.

What it means for the map: a provincial city and a capital lay out part of their townspeople's ground as wards named for one trade each - the smiths', carpenters', dyers', drapers' and oil sellers' among them - each a block of the trade's shophouses, while the main street stays a street of mixed shops. This is accurate for the grouping and the names. How many trade wards a city holds, and which trades beyond the five named here, is a guess. A town below a city draws none: nothing read speaks of settlement by trade below the castle town.
