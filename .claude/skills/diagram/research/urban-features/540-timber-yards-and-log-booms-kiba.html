Timber yards and log booms (kiba)

Timber reached a Japanese town by water, and the trade settled where good water transport met the people who bought it. The quarters where timber merchants lived together were called kiba, timber yards, and beside them retail timber dealers were found in every region.

Logs came down a timber river loose, were caught at a rope stretched across the water and made up into rafts, and at the end of the river were held in slack water: rafts moored along a bank, or ponds dug off the river. A floating fence of logs round a pen of water - the log boom - is on record in East Asia only from modern times.

  • Timber merchants gathered where water transport met their customers.
    They gathered at the Horikawa in Kyoto from the twelfth century, and timber guilds stood at the Horikawa, at Kizu and at Kamakura's harbor from the thirteenth.
  • How wide a water did a timber quarter need?
    Not a great river. The Horikawa of Heian Kyoto is said to have been about 12 m (~40 ft) wide, and able to serve as a log pond.
  • Retail timber dealers were found in every region.
    A dealer kept sawyers to cut the large and small sizes his customers wanted, and raftsmen to move the timber he had bought in, and the carpenters and builders took their work from the dealers.
  • Did a town without a river have a timber dealer?
    In this project's reading, yes: the dealers the dictionary describes kept raftsmen, so they worked on the water, and "in every region" is read as taking in a town without a river too. How big a dealer's yard was, no page we read says.

Above the timber quarters lay the timber rivers themselves, where the logs were cut and sent down.

  • Loose logs ran the swift upper river, and rafts the lower.
    Floating separate logs was dangerous work, and rafting needs a wider stream. On the Kiso above Nishikori the rapids meant the logs were floated loose, and at Nishikori they were made up into rafts and carried downstream.
  • A catch rope (tsunaba) stopped the loose logs where rafting began.
    A tsunaba was a river harbor on the middle course of a timber river, where a strong holding rope was stretched across the water to stop the floated timber. The one at Nishikori lay where the Kiso leaves its gorge.
  • The Nishikori rope is on record from the fifteenth century.
    A pass of 1422 - a lord's written permit for goods to go through a toll barrier - sends a hundred rafts of timber "from Kawakami to the tsunaba", Kawakami being, in the dictionary's reading, the barrier at Nishikori.
  • The Kiso rafts went on to Nagoya and to Kuwana.
    Three men rode each raft, and they carried the timber to the Shiratori storage yard in Nagoya and to Kuwana on Ise Bay.

At the end of the river, a city held its timber in slack water: moored along a quiet bank, or in ponds off the river.

  • On the Qingshui river, in China, a Qing dynasty rule said where rafts might moor.
    At Guazhi a rule of 1797 carved on a boulder on the bank kept the water above a boundary marker for the upland sellers to moor their timber, and forbade the downstream buyers to moor their rafts there.
  • Nanjing's timber rafts moored along the banks of a side channel off the Yangtze.
    At Shangxinhe the rafts moored mostly along the banks at the mouth of the Jiajiang, and the banks grew into a timber market that became the town of Shangxinhe, said at its height to be "always sufficient in all four seasons".
  • Nagoya kept its timber in a pond beside the Horikawa canal.
    Tradition says a great pond was dug at Shiratori about 1610. The Kiso rafts went out to sea and up the Horikawa to it, and it was enlarged from 1651 until, by the end of the Edo period, it covered 23,900 tsubo (about 20 acres).
  • Edo's timber trade moved across the river for fear of fire.
    It settled in 1703 in what is now Koto ward, a district of canals where logs floated in ponds until they were sawn and sold, moved there over the fire risk in the older parts of the city. Keeping timber in water kept it safe from fire as well, in this project's reading. How the merchants were first gathered after the fire of 1641 is at Shops and trades in towns and villages.
  • Each lot at Kiba was a pond ringed by dry ground.
    On the 1858 map of Edo the lots look like a row of square islands. At the center of each, a modern guide explains, was a large pond that stored the timber under water, the dry ground lay only round the lot's edge, and the pond was joined to the canals by which, it is said, rafted timber came in and went out.

The log boom proper - a fence of floating logs anchored round a pen of water - belongs to modern times in East Asia, and its great examples are American.

  • The floating log fence is on record in East Asia only from modern times.
    The one Japanese account found of a boom of floats strung on a rope, run from the far bank at a sharp angle to draw the logs to the near bank, rests on modern forestry histories. It takes the former imperial Kiso forest as its example, with a rope of vine and steel cable, and gives the form no date of its own beyond the river floating of timber that lasted into the early 1950s. No earlier East Asian boom was found.
  • How large could a boom be?
    The Susquehanna Boom at Williamsport, Pennsylvania, the largest found, ran seven miles, its line of cribs crossing the river diagonally from bank to bank. It was strung from 352 cribs, man-made islands of stone and timber 22 ft high, and its 450 acres of enclosed river held up to 300 million board feet, nearly a million logs. How boats got past its crossing line the page does not say.
  • Where boats used the river, the boom works had to leave them a channel.
    On the St. Croix the works filled miles of river with piers, pilings and floating log fences, making log channels and holding pens beside a navigation channel the Army Corps maintained, and a hinged sheer boom swung open or closed between islands as the logs rose and fell. An act made the works illegal unless the Corps approved them, logs spilled from the logging channel into the navigation right-of-way, and by 1888 Wisconsin had ruled the Boom Company negligent for jams at its sorting gap.
  • The American booms were anchored to fixed ground.
    The Susquehanna's hung from stone-filled cribs and the St. Croix's from piers and pilings. The Hudson's Big Boom was of hewn timbers bolted four together raftwise, chained end to end over friction rollers, each end chained to a heavy abutment.
