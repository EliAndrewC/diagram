Where were village and town children taught to read, and was the school a building of its own?

Sources: terakoya-jawiki (what a terakoya was, who taught, Edo's count and the size of a school), nippon-com-terakoya (a master and one room), hannan-terakoya (a district's villages, one school each), ndl-crd-terakoya-count (the national counts and the survey's gaps), sishu-zhwiki (the Chinese lineage hall as a school), yixue-zhwiki (the Chinese county charity school)

The writing school. A terakoya was a private school where a writing master taught the children of commoners, townsfolk and farmers alike, the first steps of reading, writing and reckoning. It needed only a master and one room to teach in, and there were said to be about as many of them as there were villages in Japan. The name itself points to the temple: a Meiji-era report says the schools were called terakoya in the Kamigata region because many children studied at the nearby temple. But the masters were of every kind: in one small area of Bingo, the seven schools of the late Edo period, not all open at once, were kept by one village headman, two village officials, one doctor, two monks and one other. So the school sat wherever its master did: in the headman's house, a doctor's, an official's, or the temple (on the village temple, see whether a village keeps one and what stands in its precinct).

How many. In what is now Hannan city, the national survey records one terakoya each in four villages and two in a fifth at the end of the Edo period. Nationally the one survey behind every count, published as the Nihon kyoikushi shiryo, is read as 15,560 schools in one book and 16,560 in another retelling, and many regions were missed by it altogether. Edo alone had 400 to 500 large ones, and 1,000 to 1,300 counting the small; a school held anywhere from 10 to 100 pupils. How many a provincial city or a county town held no page read counts.

China. A family's ancestral hall held its rites in spring and autumn and in ordinary times served as a private school, a sishu, for the family's children. The Qing court in 1713 ordered provinces, prefectures and counties to set up many charity schools that gathered the orphaned and poor and hired teachers for them; their pupils were mostly six to eleven.

What it means for the map. A village or town school is not a building of its own: it is a room in a house or a temple that the map already draws. Where a village's school sits is a knob with the attested seats as its forms - the headman's house, the temple, another householder's house - rolled per village from the map's seed, and it changes what a building's description says, not what is drawn. A town or city draws no school buildings either: its many schools are rooms in houses. The domain school of a capital is a building, and is its own question.

The rule the map follows: no village, town or city draws a building for commoners' schooling. A village rolls from its seed whether it keeps a school at all (about three villages in four, a guess against "about as many schools as villages") and, if it does, whether it sits in the headman's house, the village temple or another house (the three attested seats; their weights a guess); the chosen building's description names it. A hamlet sends its children to the village (a guess).
