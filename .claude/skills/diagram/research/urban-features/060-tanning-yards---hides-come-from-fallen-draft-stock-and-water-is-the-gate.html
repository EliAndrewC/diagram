Tanning yards - hides come from fallen draft stock, and water is the gate

Sources: shiniuba-jawiki (danna-ba / kusaba), himeji-shironameshi-jawiki, northampton-tannery-1996 (how many pits the largest excavated tannery of a medieval town ran); the horse stream is the setting's own canon, mizuarasoi-jawiki, suiriken-jawiki, tanning-leather-enwiki, fao-29-salt-tolerance, kimon-jawiki, kozukappara-jawiki, yoshiwara-jawiki, kawaramono-jawiki, eta-jawiki, jokamachi-wiki-corpus, danzaemon-jawiki, chouara-tannery-enwiki

  • Why towns tan too, and this is not a city-only trade. The GM's opening guess was that most towns would have no tannery. The research says otherwise, because of where hides come from: NOT butchery but fallen draft stock. Under the Tokugawa status order the kawata held carcass rights over a defined service territory (danna-ba29) - when an ox or horse died it became that community's property automatically, with no compensation to the owner.30 So a county town's ~12 burakumin households (60 inhabitants at the town tier) work the whole county's dead animals, not just the town's. L7R canon supplies the same stream from the other end: the setting's canon has daimyo giving surplus horses to village headmen to distribute to farms as draft animals, and those horses "often slaughtered and eaten when the need for meat outweighs the capacity of the peasants to feed those horses."
  • Throughput is small, and scales with the territory served - which is what the pit count encodes. A county of ~6,800 inhabitants sheds on the order of a couple dozen carcasses a year, so the town yard is a seasonal works of ~4 pits, never an industry.126 A provincial city adds the daimyo's stable (several hundred horses per domain) and the armor/saddle/drum/bellows demand of its ~300 samurai, so it runs ~12.202 That ratio is why the city yard draws roughly 1.7x the town yard's area rather than 5x: pits, not prestige, set the size. For comparison, the largest excavated urban tannery at Northampton ran 36-37 pits31 - far past anything in this pool.

  • WATER is the real gate, not settlement size. Tanning is a water process: the Japanese shironameshi method stakes raw hides in the river for 「several days」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「数日間」)79 before the hair is knifed off, and a tannery stood at the settlement's edge, on its water - the trade was ranked noxious and kept out among the poor.125 The name the caste went by was kawaramono, "riverbed people."179 Hence the rule: a town or city with a burakumin quarter and a watercourse keeps a yard; one with no running water keeps none, whatever its size.

  • WHY tannery effluent must not reach a paddy intake - and which part of it actually does the harm (GM question 2026-07-25). Tanning in general is famous for urine and dung: hides were limed OR soaked in stale urine to loosen the hair, then "bated" by kneading them in dog or pigeon dung.123 But the method these maps commit to is Japanese shironameshi, which uses none of it - no lime, no tannin, no dung, no urine.32 The hides soak in the river for several days34 (river bacteria do the unhairing), the hair is knifed off, and the hide is finished with salt and rapeseed oil alone.178 So the discharge is three things: several days of controlled putrefaction, SALT, and an oil film. The salt is what a rice paddy cannot take. Rice is a moderately salt-sensitive crop - the standard threshold is 3.0 dS/m in the soil and 2.0 in the irrigation water - and it answers to the salinity of the FIELD WATER it stands in, so brine arriving down an irrigation ditch into a flooded paddy is the precise wrong input.124 The counterintuitive corner: the ORGANIC half of the effluent is arguably fertilizer (Rokugani farmers deliberately spread night soil), so putrefaction is the WEAK argument here. The rule stands on salt, the oil film, and the stench.

  • WHY A YARD MAY SIT ON A FIELD DRAIN AT ALL, AND WHY THAT IS THE CASTE'S NATURAL WATER (GM question 2026-08-08, of Hoshizora: "the tanning yard is next to a drainage ditch of a farm field - is that realistic as a source of water for a tannery?"). Hoshizora has no stream near its burakumin quarter (the valley watercourse runs across the far west), so its yard takes the northeast comb's drain. Three separate findings say that is right, and one says what it costs.

  • Water RIGHTS, not water quality, is the strongest argument - and it was the one nobody had written down. In Japanese irrigation communities (the Chinese record has not been read on this) an upstream tap was communal property held by custom, allocated by the village association and fought over in the courts. A kawata quarter would never be granted a headwater offtake (this page's inference from the caste's standing).122 The water an outcast community could actually hold is the water nobody upstream is fighting over: the tail of the system, below every paddy intake. So a yard on a field drain is not the map settling for second best when no stream is handy - it is the sociologically correct water for this caste, and it is the literal sense of kawaramono, "riverbed people": not people who happened to live by water, but people whose only claim was to the part of the watercourse nobody else wanted. Read that way, a ditch-fed yard is more attested than a yard on a good stream reach would be.
  • It is also the only siting that automatically satisfies the salt rule above. A drain is the one course on a map where every intake is upstream by construction, which is exactly what the two intake rules demand. The residual risk is real but off-map: paddy drainage is commonly RECAPTURED by lower fields in both traditions (a "drain" at one level is a "supply" at the next), so a ditch-fed yard is only honest where the drain reaches a genuine sink - off the frame, a marsh, or a drainage tameike - rather than another comb's head.121 Hoshizora's NE drain leaves the east edge of the map.
  • What it COSTS, which should not be elided: the method changes. A ditch-fed yard is not a cosmetic variant of a stream-fed one. Shironameshi stakes hides in a CURRENT for several days80 and lets river bacteria do the unhairing; a drain has no current, so a ditch-fed yard ponds its own soak behind a gated intake cut and is running pit-soaking, the standing-vat soak other tanning traditions used (Fez's stone vats are the case read), rather than the Japanese one these maps otherwise commit to.177 That is a downgrade, not an equivalence - it is why the glyph draws a sluice gate instead of staking frames, and it is the honest thing to say if a PC asks a Hoshizora tanner why his leather is not Ubame's.

  • And it makes the yard SEASONAL in a way a stream-fed yard is not. A paddy drain runs during the irrigation season and is near-dry from harvest to transplanting, with its heaviest flush at the pre-harvest drawdown.119 Carcasses arrive on the opposite schedule: draft stock dies most in late winter and early spring, when the fodder is gone and the animal is worn from the autumn hauling.120 The two do not have to be reconciled, because raw hides were salt-cured to keep until they could be worked - a wet-salted pack stands a month - and that curing is how the hide trade bridged the gap - but it means a ditch-fed yard is a summer works whose pits stand empty half the year, while its winter is spent salting hides down and doing the clean in-town leather craft.168 Good adventure-hook texture, and it is why the small pit count is not the only thing keeping this from reading as an industry.

  • THE YARD FOLLOWS ITS BANK'S BEARING - and a ditch counts as a bank (GM 2026-08-08: "if one is next to a stream and the stream is at a 30 degree angle then the tannery itself is at that same angle. That should apply to ditches as well"). The rule: the pit rank and the staking frames share ONE edge of water, so a yard left square to the map on a slanted bank puts one corner in the water and strands the far end of the rank inland. A yard at a confluence may legitimately front either course, but when a yard fronts more than one course it is aligned to the reach it WORKS, never to a short incidental tail beside it.

  • WHERE THE QUARTER SITS RELATIVE TO ITS OWN WORKS - kegare has a DIRECTION, not a radius (GM question 2026-07-27: "does a tanning yard need burakumin housing close by? Or would the tanners just sleep at the yard?").

  • Distance between quarter and yard is not the measure. Across the pool it runs from 90 ft (Hirameki) to 2,587 ft (Tango), and the far ones are not defects. The GM's ruling: a walled city keeps its burakumin INSIDE, because the caste's in-town duties require it, while the noxious phase of the trade goes out to the water - so distance between the two is the NORMAL arrangement, not a failure of it. Measuring the same maps by BEARING from the settlement core separates them cleanly where distance cannot: Hirameki 0.6 deg, Minami 17.2, Hoshizora 28.5, Nagahara 56.0, Ubame 100, Tango 127. The rule is a direction test.

  • Why an in-city quarter is right. Japanese castle towns mostly began with no circuit town wall at all (the sogamae that took in a whole town came later, and to some), so "inside or outside the walls" is barely a Japanese question - the kawata quarter sat at the town's EDGE, typically beside a river.165 Edo is the clearest case: Asakusa Shinmachi, the Danzaemon compound, sat well within the city as an enclosed precinct - fenced and screened by temples and shrines - holding the head's residences, storehouses, a shrine, a courtroom and a prison, with housing for the three or four hundred family members of its officials.166 China-first has fewer complications in this page's reading: no hereditary outcaste attached to the leather trades (China had debased statuses of its own, but not this one), tanners and butchers as ordinary artisans, and the noxious WATER trades in the extramural suburbs (guanxiang) below the water gate.167 Either way, quarter in-town and works downstream is the attested arrangement rather than a compromise.
  • The load-bearing detail: one caste, TWO work sites, and only one of them stinks. The soak/unhair/dry phase is riverbank work. Everything else the caste does - execution, constabulary and watch service, the actual leather CRAFT (sandals, and in this page's reading drum heads and armor lacing), lamp wicks - is clean, quiet, and done at home or in town.163 So the quarter follows the in-town duties and the yard follows the water, and a daily walk between them is not a defect. It is also why no satellite hut stands at the pits: the historical unit is a corporate community under a headman, not scattered outbuildings, and a lone outcast house away from its quarter is not a thing the record shows (this page's inference from the corporate form).164
  • No, the tanners did not sleep at the works, and no watchman is needed. Nothing attests tanners bedding at the pits, and the setting does not need it: under shironameshi the exposed phase is several days81 of hides staked IN the river, which no watchman covers anyway. The security is not a person but kegare plus monopoly - a heimen who lifts a hide has handled death-pollution and has nowhere to sell it, because the burakumin hold the carcass right (danna-ba) and are the only legal channel for leather.33 Worth keeping straight for adventure hooks: stealing from a tanning yard is easy and nearly pointless, which is a more interesting fact than a guard post would have been.
  • And the smell argument runs the OTHER way. The 120 ft standoff exists to keep the stench off OTHER castes' houses, which is exactly why burakumin dwellings are already exempt from it. Distance-from-nuisance is a privilege of the clean castes; the quarter is inside the polluted zone by definition. Walking half a mile to escape your own trade's smell is a comfort a kawata household did not have. The exemption is deliberate and was paid for: counting their houses as something to stay away from inverts the institution, and it bites - with them counted, Hoshizora has no viable site for its yard at all.
  • Why direction is the thing taboo actually systematizes. Kegare leaves a settlement ONE way. Edo's Asakusa, its Kozukappara execution ground and its relocated Yoshiwara all lay to the north and northeast, the kimon quarter (the alignment is this page's reading; the pages give the places, not the reason); Kyoto put its outcast communities on the riverbeds.162 So the quarter, the execution ground and the tanning yard share a bearing, and the daily carcass haul never crosses the rest of the settlement - real castle towns are read as keeping designated carcass routes off the main streets for precisely that reason (a rule on no page read). The CREMATION ground is the deliberate exclusion: it is monk-run and follows the temple/funerary complex. Tested across the pool, that association is real where it matters (Hoshizora 130 ft from its monastery, Ubame 360 ft) and absent elsewhere (Tango 2,606 ft), so cremation is excluded from the bearing rule WITHOUT a positive "follows the monastery" rule being asserted in its place - the evidence supports the exclusion, not the converse claim.
  • The 90 deg threshold, and why it is not a fresh number. It is the "same half of the compass" test the execution ground already uses, applied to the other burakumin-run works rather than a fresh tolerance. The pool's loosest accepted map (Nagahara, 83.7 deg) sits inside it and the two defects sit outside.

A long walk to work is not a defect (GM ruling 2026-07-24). Tango's quarter is in-wall at the northwest and its yard is outside the southeast rim; Hoshizora's crematory is across town from its quarter. Moving a quarter closer to its work would be the wrong fix: being made to walk is one of the indignities the caste is subject to even in a Rokugan kinder to its underclasses than the societies it draws on. There is deliberately no rule pairing a yard with its quarter by distance.

The two maps that waive the direction rule, and why each is allowed to. A rule measured in feet was considered and rejected: Nagahara's yard stands about 1,390 ft from its quarter and is correct, and a distance rule would have condemned it. What the direction rule forbids is the yard facing the opposite way out of town, which sends the tanners' daily carcass haul back through the whole settlement - the traffic real castle towns routed around with designated carcass ways.161 Two maps stand outside it for reasons the map can state. TANGO because the Emperor lies southeast, and that status claim takes the quarter to the opposite side from its own works. UBAME because its land falls southwest while its only tannable water is the valley stream in the east, so the fall of the ground and the water genuinely disagree in that hill valley. A waived map no longer holds the rule honest, which is why both are recorded as waivers rather than quietly accommodated by loosening it. The cremation ground is excluded from the rule on purpose: it is monk-run and follows the funerary complex, which need not be the outcast side at all - Hoshizora's stands 130 ft from its monastery and almost exactly opposite the quarter, and that is a correct map. And the rule abstains on a settlement with no dwellings outside the quarter, where the core lands on the quarter itself and no bearing exists.

The rule the map follows: a town or city with a burakumin quarter AND a watercourse draws a tanning yard; one with no running water draws none, whatever its size. The yard stands (a) ON the water, its ground abutting a bank within about 20 ft, because a yard set back on dry ground could not work; (b) on DRY ground even so - the footprint stops at the bank and only the staking frames reach into the shallows, so a yard whose ground reads as a platform over the water is always a defect and never a design, and the test is the yard's true rotated rectangle against each course's real half-width, since a fixed brook-width tolerance cannot see a corner swallowed by a 120 ft river or a ditch threading under the rectangle between its corners; (c) strictly OUTSIDE the walls, with the kiln - the workers may live in-wall, the work may not; (d) DOWNSTREAM of everything the settlement draws from, which is two things at once: the yard's own course must end off the map or in a drain that does, never in a pond, a field, an irrigation ditch or the moat, and no sluice, weir or channel head on that course may lie below the yard, compared by how far each stands along the course itself, since what settles it is which of the two lies further upstream; (e) NEVER overlapping a field outline, dry plot or flower field, since a yard drawn on a field asserts ground that is at once cropped and ruined for cropping, though abutting one is fine; (f) SQUARE TO ITS BANK within 15 degrees, the yard's long axis running with the watercourse rather than with the map, measured against any course whose bank lies inside that same 20 ft reach and aligned to the reach it WORKS rather than to a short incidental tail beside it; and (g) on the QUARTER'S SIDE of the settlement, within 90 degrees of the quarter's bearing from the core - the same half-of-the-compass test the execution ground takes. It keeps 120 ft from every dwelling, wall to wall, except the burakumin dwellings that are exempt by design. The 15 degree tolerance is a calibration: a correct yard sits a few degrees off whichever segment it is measured against - the three drawn yards at 2.1, 3.8 and 7.2 degrees - while the failure it separates is an axis-aligned yard on a diagonal bank, which measured 22.9. The 20 ft reach and the 90 degrees are this project's own figures, the second borrowed rather than freshly invented.
