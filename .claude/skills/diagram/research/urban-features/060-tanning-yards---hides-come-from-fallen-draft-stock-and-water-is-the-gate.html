Tanning yards - hides come from fallen draft stock, and water is the gate

Sources: shiniuba-jawiki (danna-ba / kusaba), himeji-shironameshi-jawiki, northampton-tannery-1996 (how many pits the largest excavated tannery of a medieval town ran); the horse stream is the setting's own canon, tanning-leather-enwiki, kawaramono-jawiki

  • Why towns tan too, and this is not a city-only trade. The reason is where hides come from: NOT butchery but fallen draft stock. Under the Tokugawa status order the kawata held carcass rights over a defined service territory (danna-ba) - when an ox or horse died it became that community's property automatically, with no compensation to the owner. So a county town's ~12 burakumin households (60 inhabitants at the town tier) work the whole county's dead animals, not just the town's. L7R canon supplies the same stream from the other end: the setting's canon has daimyo giving surplus horses to village headmen to distribute to farms as draft animals, and those horses "often slaughtered and eaten when the need for meat outweighs the capacity of the peasants to feed those horses."
  • Throughput is small, and scales with the territory served - which is what the pit count encodes. A county of ~6,800 inhabitants sheds on the order of a couple dozen carcasses a year, so the town yard is a seasonal works of ~4 pits, never an industry. A provincial city adds the daimyo's stable (several hundred horses per domain) and the armor/saddle/drum/bellows demand of its ~300 samurai, so it runs ~12. That ratio is why the city yard draws roughly 1.7x the town yard's area rather than 5x: pits, not prestige, set the size. For comparison, the largest excavated urban tannery at Northampton ran 36-37 pits - far past anything in this pool.

  • WATER is the real gate, not settlement size. Tanning is a water process: the Japanese shironameshi method steeps salted hides in the river for 「several days」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「数日間」) before the hair is knifed off, and a tannery stood at the settlement's edge - the trade was ranked noxious and kept out among the poor. The name the caste went by was kawaramono, "riverbed people," and one account of why they lived on the riverbeds is that leather working needs a great deal of water. Hence the rule: a town or city with a burakumin quarter and a watercourse keeps a yard; one with no running water keeps none, whatever its size.

  • THE YARD FOLLOWS ITS BANK'S BEARING - and a ditch counts as a bank (GM 2026-08-08: "if one is next to a stream and the stream is at a 30 degree angle then the tannery itself is at that same angle. That should apply to ditches as well"). The rule: the pit rank and the staking frames share ONE edge of water, so a yard left square to the map on a slanted bank puts one corner in the water and strands the far end of the rank inland. A yard at a confluence may legitimately front either course, but when a yard fronts more than one course it is aligned to the reach it WORKS, never to a short incidental tail beside it.

Four questions follow from this one. Why must tannery effluent never reach a paddy intake is what in the discharge harms a rice field, and so why the yard stands downstream of every intake. May a tanning yard sit on a field drain is the yard with no stream to hand, and what taking a ditch changes. Does a tanning yard need its quarter close by is where the tanners live and sleep, and why the yard keeps its distance from every house but theirs. Which way out of town does a tanning yard stand is the direction rule and the two maps that waive it.

The rule the map follows: a town or city with a burakumin quarter AND a watercourse draws a tanning yard; one with no running water draws none, whatever its size. The yard stands (a) ON the water, its ground abutting a bank within about 20 ft, because a yard set back on dry ground could not work; (b) on DRY ground even so - the footprint stops at the bank and only the staking frames reach into the shallows, so a yard whose ground reads as a platform over the water is always a defect and never a design; (c) strictly OUTSIDE the walls, with the kiln - the workers may live in-wall, the work may not; (d) NEVER overlapping a field outline, dry plot or flower field, since a yard drawn on a field asserts ground that is at once cropped and ruined for cropping, though abutting one is fine; and (e) SQUARE TO ITS BANK within 15 degrees, the yard's long axis running with the watercourse rather than with the map, measured against any course whose bank lies inside that same 20 ft reach and aligned to the reach it WORKS rather than to a short incidental tail beside it. The 15 degree tolerance is a calibration: a correct yard sits a few degrees off whichever segment it is measured against - the three drawn yards at 2.1, 3.8 and 7.2 degrees - while the failure it separates is an axis-aligned yard on a diagonal bank, which measured 22.9. The 20 ft reach is this project's own figure. Where the yard stands along its water, how far it keeps from houses, and which way out of town it faces are the rules of the four questions named above.
