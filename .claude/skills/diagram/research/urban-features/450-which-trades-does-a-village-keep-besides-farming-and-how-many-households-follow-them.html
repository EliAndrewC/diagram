Which trades does a village keep besides farming, and how many households follow them?

Sources: yashio-noma-yogyo (a district's villages surveyed for their trades in 1829), ehime-kenshi-mura-shokunin (villages with no craftsmen, and a village's craftsmen in 1701)

How many. The fullest count found is a survey of 1829 of nineteen villages in the Yashio district: of about 1,000 houses, about 250, a quarter, were in trade or a craft alongside farming. The share ran from 41 percent in Yanaginomiya down to 4 percent, a single house, in Kamibaba. Across the district there were 45 taverns in 16 villages, 18 pawnshops in 11, 13 barbers in 10, and one bathhouse, in Hachijo village. The largest village, Hachijo, a traffic node, had 66 of its 166 houses in a trade. Elsewhere a village could have almost nothing: in the Kyoho era many shogunal villages of one Iyo district recorded no craftsmen at all.

Which trades. The Yashio villages' craftsmen were paper makers, cotton beaters, carpenters (boat carpenters too), roofers, tile makers, smiths, coopers and woodcutters, and the village sake shop also sold food and sundries: it was the village's general store. Another village of the same district could hold a whole row of trades: Kawanoe's register of 1701 counts 11 dyers, 15 sake shops, 8 carpenters, 6 smiths, 4 coopers, 3 sawyers and 1 umbrella maker. The smith is his own question. Brewing sake to sell is licensed in this setting: every brewery above the household level holds an annual license, and the county town keeps "the sake brewer" among its notables. Whether a village's brewer was usually its headman, no page read says.

What it means for the map. A village's trades are not separate buildings: they were kept by farming households, so a trade is a farmhouse that also sells or makes something. How many of a village's houses keep one is calibrated liberty along the Yashio range, rolled per village from the map's seed, centered on a quarter and running from almost none to about four in ten. For a village of about 350 inhabitants that is, at the center, a dozen or more households: most commonly a sake shop that is also the village store, a tavern, a carpenter, a cooper and perhaps a smith, with a pawnshop or a barber only in a bigger or busier village. A remote village with none at all is the far end of the same range. How a trade shows on a house is a drawing convention, not a finding.

The rule the map follows: each village rolls the share of its houses that keep a trade, between about 1 in 25 and about 4 in 10 and most often about 1 in 4 (the Yashio survey; its use for a village anywhere is calibrated liberty); the trades are drawn from sake shop and store, tavern, carpenter, cooper and smith first, pawnshop and barber only where the share is high. A trade marks its house; it adds no building, and a sake brewery bigger than a household's needs the county town (the setting's licensing).
