How big is a roofed playhouse, and how many does a capital keep?

Sources: kanamaruza-jawiki (a surviving playhouse's measures), shibaigoya-jawiki (where roofed playhouses stood), edo-sanza-jawiki (Edo's count and its theater street), dotonbori-jawiki (Osaka's count), goulan-zhwiki (the Song halls)

How big. The oldest surviving playhouse in Japan, the Kanamaruza in the temple-gate town of Kotohira, measures 13 ken 2 shaku across its front by 24 ken 2 shaku deep at ground level, about 80 by 145 ft, with a revolving stage 2 ken (about 12 ft) across. It was raised in 1835 on the model of one of the theaters of Dotonbori in Osaka, by one account, so its measures stand for a city playhouse of its day as well as any read; no page read measures one of the city theaters themselves. A Song performance hall in a great city held audiences of several thousand, but no page read gives its measures either.

Only the great cities had one before the modern age. Roofed kabuki theaters with seating arose in the late Edo period, centered on Edo, Kyoto and Osaka; playhouses came to be built in every city only after the Meiji restoration, and in small and middling towns from the 1900s. Before the Kanamaruza, Kotohira put up a temporary hut for each run of plays, about three times a year.

How many a capital keeps. Edo had many playhouses at first; they were thinned to four licensed houses by the 1670s and to three from 1714. Osaka's five Dotonbori theaters began with five theater licenses recognized in 1653. They stood together: two of Edo's three faced the same street a stone's throw apart, and after the fire of 1841 the shogunate moved them, with the puppet theaters, to a site of over 10,000 tsubo (about 8 acres) at Saruwaka-cho in Asakusa, far outside the outer moat. The Song capital went further: one entertainment precinct could hold several halls and a large one more than fifty.

What it means for the map: a capital keeps three to five roofed playhouses, a count rolled per capital between Edo's three and Osaka's five, and draws them in one of two forms rolled per capital: the Japanese form, the playhouses side by side on one theater street of the entertainment district, each about 80 by 145 ft; or the Song form, the halls standing inside the entertainment precincts among their shops. A provincial city and a town keep no roofed playhouse - their plays are staged in a temporary hut or on a shrine's stage (where a town's stage stands).
