How many households does a burakumin quarter hold, and what stands in it besides the houses?

Sources: l7r-budgets (the setting's household counts), asakusa-danzaemon-1000ya (the houses of the Asakusa enclosure, its shrine and its bathhouse), danzaemon-jawiki (what else stood inside the enclosure), danzaemon-kotobank (a whole region's count), kotobank-hisabetsu-buraku (the quarters' tutelary deity and their Buddhist sect), kyoto-burakushi-nadamoto (how early and how many the quarters' temples were)

Where the quarter stands is the next question; whether a walled city keeps one inside its wall is the city fabric's.

How many households. The count is the setting's own: a county seat holds 12 burakumin households, a provincial city 30 and a capital 120. The history gives the size of one great quarter and of a whole region, but not of a small one. The enclosure at Asakusa in Edo, where the hereditary head of the outcastes of the Kanto lived, held 232 houses crowded together, 15 of them monkey-trainers'; and the head's whole jurisdiction - the eight Kanto provinces, Izu and parts of three more - counted, in a return of 1800, 5,664 eta households, 1,995 hinin households and 61 monkey-trainers' households. Nothing read counts the households of one provincial town's quarter, so the setting's 12 stands on the setting alone.

What stands in it. The Asakusa enclosure was ringed by temples, shrines and walls so that no one could see in, and held the head's official and private residences, storehouses and a shrine, with housing for three to four hundred members of his officials' families; his own lot was 740 tsubo, with a pond in it, and the eta bathed at a bathhouse he ran. That is the seat of a regional head, not a town's quarter. For the quarters in general two kinds of religious building are attested. Not a few, above all in eastern Japan, kept the god of Mount Haku (Hakusan) as their ujigami, and the Asakusa enclosure had its Hakusan shrine; and Jodo Shinshu was the most common sect among them, their temples founded in numbers from before the Edo period - in Yamashiro, Tanba and Harima, 39 between 1501 and 1572, 57 between 1573 and 1614 and 57 more between 1615 and 1670 - and a temple, as the historian who reports the count puts it, presupposes a community settled enough to keep it. Whether a quarter had a well of its own, and what its houses were like, nothing read says.

What it means for the map. The count stays the setting's: 12 dwellings in a county seat's quarter, 30 in a provincial city's, 120 in a capital's, packed close, as the Asakusa houses were. A city's quarter also draws one religious building of its own, and which one is a knob rolled per settlement, because both forms are attested: a small shrine to its tutelary god, or a small Jodo Shinshu temple hall. A county seat's twelve households draw the smaller of the two, a shrine; that so small a quarter kept even that is a guess, since the sources speak of the quarters in general and never of one so small. The dwellings keep the ordinary small-house glyph and the quarter draws its water where its neighbors do - both guesses, for want of anything read.
