Does the charcoal yard's 30 ft fire gap apply to charcoal kept in a plastered storehouse?

Sources: dozo-jawiki (what a storehouse held, and its walls and fire doors), kotobank-dozozukuri (the plastered storehouse as a fire-resistant construction, spread in Edo for fire prevention)

What the 30 ft is for. The charcoal yard's gap is clear ground between an open stack of charcoal, burning unattended, and the timber of its neighbors - about one flame-height from a fully involved stack; how it was derived is at "Charcoal yards".

What a plastered storehouse is for. The storehouse (kura) was named for what it held - rice, unhulled rice, miso, gunpowder, money, sutras - and its purpose was to protect them against fire, damp and theft. Its walls were often 300 mm or more thick, about a foot, and its outer doors might be faced with earth and plaster; one kind of door, the Osaka-do, was a sliding door shut when fire came. The plastered storehouse is one of Japanese architecture's fire-resistant constructions, and in Edo the same construction, used for townhouses, was at first restrained as a luxury, then spread by order for fire prevention after the Kyoho era (1716-36).

So. The 30 ft does for an open stack what the storehouse's own wall does for what it holds - it is the separation - and a storehouse kept gunpowder behind that wall. No page read says charcoal was kept in a plastered storehouse, or gives the spacing between a storehouse and its neighbors.

What the map draws. The 30 ft gap applies to open charcoal stacks and sheds, not to charcoal sealed in a plastered storehouse, which stands at the ordinary spacing of the buildings around it. That charcoal was kept in one at all, and that it needed no gap, is a guess resting on the storehouse's recorded purpose; the charcoal is cooled before it goes in, as the charcoal yard's cooling ground requires.
