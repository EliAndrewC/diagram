Was bulk charcoal weighed on a balance beam or a steelyard?

Sources: zhwiki-ganchen (the steelyard as China's traditional weighing tool), zjnews-cixi-steelyard (an old Chinese steelyard for heavy loads), osaka-keiryo-history (what the balance and the steelyard were each used for), kanazawa-saobakari (steelyards of the Edo period and their loads, and when the platform scale came)

China first. The steelyard (gancheng), also called the hanging scale, is China's traditional weighing tool, used above all in the markets. It was built for heavy loads too: a wooden steelyard more than 200 years old, collected from Shaoxing, is 2.2 m long and weighs up to 175 kg against a stone weight.

Balance and steelyard, then Japan. An account that names no country or period says the balance is precise but needs many weights, which make it heavy and awkward to move; it served valuables and gems, while the steelyard weighed food and daily goods, and was the easier of the two to cheat with. In Japan, steelyards were in use from the Edo period into the mid-20th century, in sizes that included one for 16 kan - four of the best charcoal bales, at the weights in "How big is a bale of rice or charcoal?" The platform scale that weighed rice bales came only in the Meiji period.

What the map draws. The charcoal yard's weighing floor weighs on a steelyard, not on a two-pan balance: a wooden beam with a large hook at one end for the bale and a weight hung on the other, moved along the beam until it balances. That is the historically accurate form. The frame or post it hangs from at the weighing floor is a guess. Why the charcoal must be weighed at all is at "Charcoal yards".
