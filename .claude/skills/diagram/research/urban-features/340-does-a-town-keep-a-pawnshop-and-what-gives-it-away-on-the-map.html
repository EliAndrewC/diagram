Does a town keep a pawnshop, and what gives it away on the map?

Sources: yashio-noma-yogyo (how many pawnshops a district of villages kept, and that most were a sideline), shichiya-kotobank (the storehouse the pledges needed), dangye-zhwiki (the Chinese pawnshop as a storied building)

Yes, and more than one. Pawnbroking reached well down the settlement ladder: in 1829 a district of nineteen villages in what is now Yashio city, some 5,672 inhabitants, had 18 pawnshops spread across 11 of the villages, and its market village of 856 had three of them. Most were not shops of their own - pawnbroking was often combined with another trade, so the pawnbroker was often a liquor retailer selling sundries or, in one case, the village's oil presser, taking pledges on the side. At that rate a county seat of about 1,200 has three or four.

The tell is the storehouse. In Japan the pawnbroker's defining building gave the trade its name: the medieval lenders on pledges were called "earthen storehouses" (dosō, also read dozō), because keeping the pledges called for large, solid storehouses. In China the pawnshop itself was built as a storied building, its upper floors the store for the pledges. Neither source gives the size of a pledge store or of the court it stands in.

What it means for the map: a county seat keeps three or four pawnbrokers and a city more, in proportion; most are an ordinary shop-house that also takes pledges, and what shows on the map is the pledge store. Its form is a knob rolled per settlement between the two attested forms: a Japanese pawnbroker's one to three fireproof storehouses standing in a walled court behind the shop, or a Chinese pawnshop drawn as a larger, storied building on the street with a smaller court. The storehouse is the ordinary storehouse glyph; the count of one to three and the court's size are a guess.
