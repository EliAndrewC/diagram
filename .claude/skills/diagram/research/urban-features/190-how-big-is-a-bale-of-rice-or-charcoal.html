How big is a bale of rice or charcoal?

Sources: tawara-jawiki (what a bale is and what went in one), tawara-unit-jawiki (how much a bale held, by time, place and grade), crd-momigura-tawara (the Edo bales' capacities, and no size),to-unit-jawiki (how big a to is),nisira-komedawara (the size of a 60 kg rice bale), edo-tokyo-sumidawara (the size of a museum's charcoal bale)

What a bale is. A tawara is straw woven into a cylinder, used to carry and store rice and other grains, salt, fish, charcoal and coal; in one charcoal district of Hokkaido the charcoal was shipped in square bales instead.

No standard size. A bale of rice held roughly 2 to 5 to (a to being a tenth of a koku) from the Sengoku to the Edo period, differing by time and place - the shogunate's bale held 3 to and 5 sho, Kaga domain's 5 to - and the bales themselves came in 4-to, 6-to and other sizes. The same measure was used for charcoal, salt and other goods shipped in bales. Charcoal was packed by grade: in the Iburi district of Hokkaido the best at 4 kan (about 15 kg), good charcoal at 10 kan, and a lower grade at 8 kan rolled in straw mats. The 4-to rice bale became the standard only later, and weighs 60 kg in today's rice trade.

How big. A 60 kg rice bale, as made today for display, is 75 cm long and 47 cm across - about 2.5 by 1.5 ft. A charcoal bale of the mid-20th century in the Edo-Tokyo Museum measures 63 by 39 by 32 cm - about 2 by 1.3 by 1 ft. Both are modern bales. The Edo bale is on record by what it held, not by its size: bales of 3 to 3 sho 3 go, of 4 to and of 5 to, which one a domain used differing by domain, until the Meiji period made the 4-to bale of 60 kg the rule - and the modern rice bale is that later 4-to standard. No bale of the Edo period, of rice or of charcoal, was found measured. On this record's guess, an Edo bale woven to the modern bale's proportions changes its length with the cube root of what it holds: the shogunate's 3 to 5 sho bale about 2.4 ft long and 1.5 ft across, a 5-to bale about 2.7 by 1.7 ft - within a tenth of the modern bale either way.

The rule the map follows: a rice bale is about 2.5 ft long and 1.5 ft across, the modern bale's size, which the Edo bales' capacities put within a tenth of theirs (a guess), and a charcoal bale about 2 by 1.3 ft, a 20th-century bale's size, no older one having been measured. A bale drawn at about 4 ft is a convention, drawn larger so it reads; its real length is about 2.5 ft.
