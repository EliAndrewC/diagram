How big is a bale of rice or charcoal?

Sources: tawara-jawiki (what a bale is and what went in one), tawara-unit-jawiki (how much a bale held, by time, place and grade), nisira-komedawara (the size of a 60 kg rice bale), edo-tokyo-sumidawara (the size of a museum's charcoal bale)

What a bale is. A tawara is straw woven into a cylinder, used to carry and store rice and other grains, salt, fish, charcoal and coal; in one charcoal district of Hokkaido the charcoal was shipped in square bales instead.

No standard size. A bale of rice held roughly 2 to 5 to from the Sengoku to the Edo period, differing by time and place - the shogunate's bale held 3 to and 5 sho, Kaga domain's 5 to - and the bales themselves came in 4-to, 6-to and other sizes. The same measure was used for charcoal, salt and other goods shipped in bales. Charcoal was packed by grade: in the Iburi district of Hokkaido the best at 4 kan (about 15 kg), good charcoal at 10 kan, and a lower grade at 8 kan rolled in straw mats. The 4-to rice bale became the standard only later, and weighs 60 kg in today's rice trade.

How big. A 60 kg rice bale, as made today for display, is 75 cm long and 47 cm across - about 2.5 by 1.5 ft. A charcoal bale of the mid-20th century in the Edo-Tokyo Museum measures 63 by 39 by 32 cm - about 2 by 1.3 by 1 ft. No page read measures a bale of the Edo period itself, and the modern rice bale is the later 4-to standard, so an older bale ran smaller or larger with what it held.

The rule the map follows: a rice bale is about 2.5 ft long and 1.5 ft across, and a charcoal bale about 2 by 1.3 ft, each varying with what it holds. A bale drawn at about 4 ft is a convention, drawn larger so it reads; its real length is about 2.5 ft.
