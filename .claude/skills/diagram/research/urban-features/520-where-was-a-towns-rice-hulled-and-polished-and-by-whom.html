Where was a town's rice hulled and polished, and by whom?

Sources: momisuriki-jawiki (hulling on the farm), kotobank-tsukigomeya (the rice polisher's trade), seimai-jawiki (the treadle mortar and the waterwheel)

Rice passed through two steps between the paddy and the pot, done in different places. Hulling, stripping the husk to leave brown rice, was, on this record's reading, the farm's: the clay hulling mill broke many grains, and because broken rice could not be shipped, the farm household ground it to flour and ate it itself. If so, the rice that reached a town, as tax or for sale, came already hulled, though no page read says so outright.

Polishing brown rice white was a trade of its own. In the early modern period specialists took it up, called tsukigome-ya, rice polishers, and they were the last link in the rice trade; in Edo in 1744 there were about 2,100 of them, working some 5,500 mortars, and from that year Edo forbade the villages to polish rice for sale in the city. Those mortars were most likely worked by foot, though the entry does not say which mortar they were: the treadle mortar, a pestle on a see-saw, came into use in the Edo period. The other form was the waterwheel. Hokusai drew one on the Onden river in Edo, where there were said to be several, and such a wheel worked pestles up and down to polish brown rice.

Two forms, then, and both attested: the polisher treading his mortars in his shop, and a mill with a waterwheel driving pestles on a stream. So the rice dealer of Trade works is a polisher, not a huller. How often a county town had a mill rather than only treadle shops no page read says.

The rule the map follows: every town's rice polisher is an ordinary shop in the generic rows, his mortars inside. Whether a town also has a waterwheel mill is a knob rolled from the map's seed, and only where a stream with some fall runs past the town's edge: the mill stands on the bank, a small shed with its wheel in the water, outside the built-up blocks - its siting and its size are this record's guess. Hulling draws nothing in town; it is done on the farm.
