Where does a town's burakumin quarter stand - at the edge where a road leaves, or on the riverbank?

Sources: kotobank-hisabetsu-buraku (the three kinds of ground the quarters were given, and why so many sit on poor ground), chori-jawiki (the Shinano castle towns' quarters at their entrances, and the name "apart from town"), joetsu-hisabetsu-history (Takada's quarter on the road out), jinkennagano-burakushi (the post towns, checkpoints and villages they were also settled in), kyoto-burakushi-nadamoto (Kyoto's oldest quarter, a riverbank village), asakusa-danzaemon-1000ya (the Edo head moved outward by stages)

How many households it holds is the question before; which way out of town its tanning yard and execution ground face is the tanning yard's; and how far the quarter stands off its neighbors is the caste geography's.

At the edge, where the road goes out, or on the river. When the lords of the warring-states era took the leather workers under their protection and control, the ground they housed them on was, as far as the record can tell, the edge of the castle town, a chokepoint on the roads, or a riverbank. The cases read bear that out. Matsumoto gathered its kawata at the entrances of the castle town in 1588 and set them to cleaning, to the police's menial work and to the executioner's work at the execution ground; Ueda did the same in 1649, and the other castle towns of Shinano placed theirs likewise. In Shinano, and in the castle town of Tsuruoka and the port of Sakata in the north, the quarter was called machi-hanare, "apart from town". Takada, laid out in 1614, set the people of its guard and cleaning duties in a block at the town's northwest edge, along the Kaga road. Others were settled at post towns and road checkpoints, or moved when an intendant's office was set up, and later in the period many were invited into villages, mainly as village guards. Kyoto's oldest quarter, Tenbu, was a village of the riverbank people. And the head of the Edo outcastes was moved outward by stages, from north of Nihonbashi to beside the Torigoe shrine and then to Shinmachi, beyond the Sanya canal. The ground was often poor: many quarters today sit on sites badly placed for flood, sun or water, a state of things said to owe much to the forced removals of the Edo period.

Inside the wall or outside it. Most Japanese castle towns had no circuit wall to be inside, as the tanning yard's quarter question records, and none of the cases above sets a quarter inside a town wall; the entrance, the road out and the riverbank are all at or beyond the town's edge. The walled city's intramural quarter is therefore the setting's, and the city fabric records it as a deviation.

What it means for the map. A county seat's quarter stands at the settlement's edge, and where on the edge is a knob rolled per settlement, because both forms are attested: on a road where it leaves the town, as at Matsumoto and Takada, or on the riverbank below the town, as the riverbank people's villages were. Either way it keeps its collar of open ground and shares its side of the town with the tanning yard and the execution ground, as the questions linked above require. Where the edge offers better and worse ground, the quarter takes the worse - low, wet or cut off - a degree rolled per settlement rather than a rule, and this record's reading of the poor ground above rather than anything a source states of one town.
