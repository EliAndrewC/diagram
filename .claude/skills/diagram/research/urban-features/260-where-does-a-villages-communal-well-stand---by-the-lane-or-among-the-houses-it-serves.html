Where does a village's communal well stand - by the lane, or among the houses it serves?

Sources: kotobank-tsurube-ido (wells shared by a village or a group within it), kotobank-tsujiido (the shared well at the roadside), seiyo-karihama-ido (a surveyed village's wells, one to a handful of houses, paved round)

Both, and the record attests the two as different forms. A well was often shared - by a whole village or by a group of households within it. One kind stood by the way: the shared well at the roadside had a name of its own, the "wayside well". That entry speaks of wayside wells in general and does not say that villages had them, so a village's well at the lane side is this page's reading of it, not a village attested with one. The other was a well shared by a small group of neighboring houses; that such a well stood among those houses, rather than at the lane, is this page's reading of the Karihama survey below, which counts the houses to a well but does not map where the wells stood. In Karihama, a village on the Ehime coast, the wells were dug at about one to every five to ten houses, some 150 in all; a dozen of them were used in common, and round many of the common ones the ground was paved with stones, making a space at the wellside - where, this page takes it, the neighbors met. Karihama was short of water and drank from its wells and springs until piped water came in 1952, and this page takes it that a village on a stream would have needed fewer wells than that - an inference, not a count anyone made (Wells - the research, and the deliberate liberty has the counts).

What was not found: no page read sets a village's well out on open common ground away from the houses, and none says how often a village well had a roof over it - the well house is defined, and one survives, but its frequency was not read. A well on a village's commons, away from its houses, is therefore a guess.

What it means for the map. Two forms are found - the well among the houses in a village, the well by the way in general - so the siting is a knob rolled per settlement from the map's seed: in one village the common wells stand at the lane side, on the verge where the lane passes the houses; in another they stand inside the clusters of houses that share them. Either way the rule that a well stands among the houses it serves still holds, and a common well gets a paved apron. How the well looks from above is What does a communal well look like from above?

The rule the map follows: each settlement rolls from its seed whether its communal wells take the LANE-SIDE form (the wellhead on a lane verge, clear of the tread, within the reach of the houses) or the CLUSTER form (the wellhead in the open ground among five to ten houses, the share Karihama's wells served, off every lane); a well still has a dwelling within about 95 ft . A communal well of either form carries a paved apron about 4 ft wider than its curb all round. The apron's width is a guess; the CLUSTER form is attested in a village; the LANE-SIDE form is attested for wayside wells in general, and its use in a village is a guess; a well out on open common ground is not drawn.
