Dyers, weavers and papermakers (kon'ya)

A dyer (kon'ya) dyed the cloth and thread that households still wove for themselves in the Edo period, before 1868. Dyers worked at home, to order: in the towns they lived together in a craftsmen's ward, and in the villages a few settled too. The dye itself stood in vats buried in the ground, and what took room was the water to rinse the cloth in and the sun to dry it.

Weaving and papermaking needed no premises of their own. Cloth was woven in houses, at home or by farm households working for a clothier in the slack season, and paper was made on farms in the off-season and dried on boards in the sun. A great city could have a weavers' quarter, as Kyoto had.

  • The setting's county counts a dye-house owner among its wealthy tradesmen.
    The owner of the dye house stands beside the sake brewer and the master smith among the notables of a county.
  • Households wove their own cloth and sent it to a dyer.
    Cloth for clothing was still woven at home in the early modern period, but the dyeing of the cloth and the thread was left to specialists such as the dyer.
  • Dyers lived together in a ward in the towns, and a few settled in the villages.
    In both they worked at home, taking orders.
  • The place name Kon'yamachi most often marks a castle town's dyers' ward.
    It names the part of a castle town where many dyers lived once the town settled its trades by occupation.
  • A dyer's indigo vats were buried in the ground, four in a row.
    In the early modern period the indigo jars were buried four in a row and warmed with fire pots to hasten the fermentation of the indigo.
  • At Yamatokoriyama the dyers rinsed their cloth in a channel fed from the castle moat.
    In the town's dyers' street a channel runs down the middle of the street, its water flowing out of the moat, and the dyed cloth and thread were rinsed in it.
  • In Edo's dyers' town the bolts of cloth were dried in rows in the sun.
    The bolts bleached out on fine days were as beautiful as banners, and the rows of them were, it seems, a famous sight in the day of the print artist Hiroshige.
  • Dyeing waited on the weather.
    Work ordered was often not finished by its day, and "the dyer's day after tomorrow" became a byword for a date that could not be relied on.
  • No page we read measures a town dyer's drying ground.
    Nor does any give the length of the bolts it dried.

Weaving was the dyer's neighbor trade, but it was done in houses, not in works.

  • The setting counts weavers among its crafts, and its county has a local clothier.
    Weavers, with every other worker in fiber, are one of the setting's crafts, and the local clothier is among the specialized trades of its farming county.
  • A clothier who wove for sale did it in their own house or put the work out to farm households.
    Put-out weaving was a rural industry, done by farm households as a side job in the slack farming season; weaving under the clothier's own management was a household industry. The dictionary entry gives no period for either.
  • In Kyoto weaving filled a quarter of its own, Nishijin.
    After the Onin war weaving revived on the old camp ground of the western army, which gave the quarter its name, and the weavers of the high loom (takabata, the tall loom for patterned silk) formed a guild.

Papermaking belonged to the farms.

  • Papermaking was a farm household's side job in the off-season.
    From the Edo period into modern times paper was made on a small scale as a side job of the farming off-season, as around Toyota in Aichi.
  • Paper was dried on boards in the sun.
    The pressed wet sheets were peeled off one at a time, pasted onto drying boards and dried in the sun, in about an hour on a fine day. The dictionary entry describes the craft as it was practiced into modern times.
