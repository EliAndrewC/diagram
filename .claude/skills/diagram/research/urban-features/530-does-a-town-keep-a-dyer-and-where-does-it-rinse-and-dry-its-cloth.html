Does a town keep a dyer, and where does it rinse and dry its cloth?

Sources: l7r-budgets (the setting's county dye-house owner), kotobank-konya (where dyers lived and how they worked), konyacho-jawiki (the dyers' ward of a castle town), konya-jawiki (dyeing and the weather), yk-kankou-konya (a dyers' street rinsing in a channel off the moat), nippon-com-kanda-konyacho (bolts drying in rows)

Whether. In the setting the dye-house owner is one of a county's wealthy tradesmen, beside the sake brewer and the master smith. Historically too: cloth was still woven at home in the early modern period, but the dyeing of the cloth and the thread was left to the dyer; in the towns the dyers lived together in a craftsmen's ward, in the villages a few settled too, and all of them worked at home, to order. The place name Kon'ya-machi most often marks such a ward: the part of a castle town where the dyers lived once the town settled its trades by occupation.

The works. The dyer worked at home, and his vats were sunk in its ground: in the early modern period four indigo jars were buried in a row and warmed with fire pots to hasten the fermentation. What needs ground is the water and the drying. In Yamatokoriyama's dyers' street a channel runs down the middle of the street, fed from the castle moat, and the dyed cloth and thread were rinsed in it. In Edo's dyers' town the bolts bleached out on fine days were as beautiful as banners, and the rows of them were, it seems, a famous sight in Hiroshige's day. Dyeing waited on the weather, which is why a dyer's promised day was a byword for a date that could not be trusted. How much ground a town dyer's drying took no page read measures.

The rule the map follows: a county town keeps a dyer - one or two houses whose vats are inside, with a drying ground behind or beside them and rinsing water within reach, a stream, a channel or a moat, as Trade works already requires of a city's dye works. How many dyers, and where the drying ground lies against the house, are this record's guess. A town is too small for a dyers' ward; a city's dyers may stand together in a street on a channel. A village dyer, where the map keeps one, is a house like the rest.
