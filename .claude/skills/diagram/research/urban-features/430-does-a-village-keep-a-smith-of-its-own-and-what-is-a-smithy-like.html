Does a village keep a smith of its own, and what is a smithy like?

Sources: ehime-kenshi-mura-shokunin (craftsmen barred from villages at first, then licensed in them; the itinerant craftsmen; villages with none), boso-no-mura-kajiya (the village smith's family workshop and one workshop's plan), nabunken-kajiya-kikiude (the order of a smithy's fittings), yashio-noma-yogyo (smiths among a district's village craftsmen)

Two forms of village. In the villages of Iyo the rule at first was that whatever a village made besides its crops was for its own use only: craftsmen were not to live there, and farm tools and pots were bought in the castle town or a country town. Later, carpenters, coopers and smiths were licensed in the villages too; they farmed as well and worked small, and tinkers and coopers who went round from village to village cut into their trade. In the Kyoho era (1716-36) many shogunal villages of one Iyo district recorded no craftsmen at all. Where a village did keep one, the smith was a nokaji, a maker of farm tools, fishing gear and household tools, once found all over Japan: a husband and wife, a parent and child, or a master and an apprentice, working to order for the farmers round about and for the local hardware dealer. In one district of nineteen villages, smiths were among the villages' craftsmen, and a survey of 1829 found about a quarter of its roughly 1,000 houses in trade or crafts beside farming. So both forms are attested: a village with a smith of its own, and a village with none that bought its iron in town and waited for the traveling tinker.

What the smithy is. The one village workshop with a published plan that this search found is the model at the Boso no Mura museum, built from a village smith's workshop that stood at Arami in Narita: a gabled, tiled building 3½ ken wide and 3 ken deep, about 21 by 18 ft, whose left half and right front are a beaten-earth floor holding the bellows and the hearth, with a boarded, ceiled room at the back right. The fittings stand in a row: from the smith's left, the bellows, the hearth, the anvil and the water tub, in the working smithies one researcher at the Nara national research institute visited, swordsmiths' and village smiths' alike. It is a house-sized building with its work floor inside, not an open shed. How far a village smithy stood from its neighbors, and where in the village it stood, no page read says.

What it means for the map. Whether a village keeps a smith is a knob, rolled per village from the map's seed: a village either has one smithy or has none. How often each comes up is a guess, since no page read counts the villages with a smith. A smithy draws as a building about 21 by 18 ft, standing among the houses, not a lean-to at the lane. The farrier's shed and apron stay where the farrier question puts them, at the relay town and the caravan yard. A town's and a city's smiths are the next question.

The rule the map follows: each village rolls from its seed whether it keeps a smith, about one village in two (a guess); one that does draws one smithy about 21 by 18 ft among its houses, fronting a lane, clear of its neighbors by the ordinary gap between houses (a guess, since no gap round a village forge was found). A hamlet keeps none (a guess: the smith's trade needs more customers than a handful of households).
