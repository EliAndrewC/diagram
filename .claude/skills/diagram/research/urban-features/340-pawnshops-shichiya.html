Pawnshops (shichiya)

A pawnshop lent money against pledges, goods left in its keeping, and it had to keep those pledges safe. In Japan the storehouse that held them so defined the trade that the medieval lenders were named for it; in China the pawnshop was itself a storied building for storing pledges. Pawnbroking reached well down the settlement ladder: nineteen villages near Edo kept 18 pawnshops among them in 1829, most as a sideline to another trade.

  • Nineteen villages near Edo kept 18 pawnshops among them.
    In 1829 the nineteen villages of what is now Yashio city, some 5,672 inhabitants, had 18 pawnshops spread across 11 of the villages.
  • The largest of those villages, a crossroads of 856, had three.
    Hachijo was the largest village of the district and a hub of traffic.
  • Pawnbroking was often a sideline to another trade.
    In Hachijo's list of 1836, a liquor retailer selling soy sauce, sandals and sundries took pledges, and so did the village's oil presser.

What set a pawnshop apart from other shops was the building where it kept its pledges, and Japan and China housed them differently.

  • In Japan the pawnbroker's storehouse gave the trade its name.
    The lenders were called at first kura, and in the Muromachi period dosō or dozō, "earthen storehouse", because keeping the pledges called for large, solid storehouses.
  • In China the pawnshop was itself a storied building for storing pledges.
    The Chinese Wikipedia article on the pawn trade says so, in a sentence that gives no period.
  • A Qing-dynasty pawnshop in a market town near Wuxi was a two-story building about 59 by 66 ft.
    At Qinxiang, a rural market town north of Wuxi, a pawnshop said to have been built about the mid-Qing stood on the middle of the old street. It was five bays wide and three courts deep, 18 m (~59 ft) wide and 20 m (~66 ft) deep, its street gable wall 8 m (~26 ft) high and its fire walls 5 m (~16 ft) high on the other three sides.
  • A pawnshop at Yaowan kept its silver vault and pledge store in its front court.
    The town's gazetteer records two pawnshops there in the early Qing. The Baofengcui pawnshop, on the town's West Street, ran back four courts: the front court held the silver vault and the pledge store, the rear court offices and living quarters.
