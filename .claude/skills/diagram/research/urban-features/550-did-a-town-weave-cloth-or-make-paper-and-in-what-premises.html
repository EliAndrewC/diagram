Did a town weave cloth or make paper, and in what premises?

Sources: l7r-castes and l7r-budgets (the setting's weavers and its county clothier), kotobank-konya (cloth woven at home), kotobank-debata (weaving put out to farm households), kotobank-nishijin (a city's weaving quarter), toyota-museum-washi (papermaking as a farm's side job), kotobank-washi (paper dried on boards)

Weaving. The setting counts weavers, and every other worker in fiber, among its crafts, and its agrarian county has a local clothier among its specialized trades. Where the weaving was done is the question for a map. Cloth for clothing was still woven at home in the early modern period, and only the dyeing went to a specialist. Where a clothier wove for sale, they wove in their own house or put the work out: put-out weaving was done as a rural industry, by farm households as a side job in the slack season, while the clothier's own weaving was a household industry under their management. Weaving filled a quarter of its own in a great city, as in Kyoto's Nishijin, where weaving revived on the old camp ground after the Onin war and the weavers of the high loom (takabata, the tall loom for patterned silk) formed a guild.

Paper. Papermaking went on from the Edo period into modern times as a side job of the farming off-season, on a small scale, as around Toyota in Aichi. Its one visible yard is the drying: the pressed wet sheets were peeled one at a time, pasted onto drying boards and dried in the sun, about an hour on a fine day. That the paper maker also needed clean running water is this record's expectation; no page read here says so.

The rule the map follows: a town draws no weaving works and no paper mill. Its weavers and its clothier live in ordinary houses and shops, and the looms are inside them. A city may keep a weavers' quarter, drawn as houses. Papermaking belongs to a village, and where a village map declares it, it draws as drying boards propped in the sun beside the farmhouses in the off-season.
