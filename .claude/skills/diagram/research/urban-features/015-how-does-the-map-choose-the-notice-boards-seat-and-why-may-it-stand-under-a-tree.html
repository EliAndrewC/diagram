How does the map choose the notice board's seat, and why may it stand under a tree?

Sources: nakatsugawa-kosatsuba (a board set at a crossroads so that more people would see it), mlit-tokaido-kosatsuba (boards at the village center and where main roads cross; the frame's size), shiojiri-iwadare-kosatsuba (a village board seated between its two headmen's houses), kotobank-kosatsu (the site's own enclosure); the GM's rulings of 2026-07-27, 2026-08-02 and 2026-08-29; and the siting they serve is sourced in The notice board (kosatsuba) - siting is a TRAFFIC decision

Why the board goes where the traffic is, and the rule the map follows, are in The notice board (kosatsuba) - siting is a TRAFFIC decision. This question is how the map finds that seat on the ground it has drawn.

What the history says about choosing a seat. The people who placed a board did weigh one seat against another for the traffic it would meet: Nakatsugawa's board on the Nakasendo stood not where its replica stands but some 20 m up the slope, at the crossroads with the Hida road, so that more people would see it. Boards were most often set where people passed - at the village center, or at a crossroads where main roads met. Traffic was not the only reason a seat was chosen: Iwadare, a village split between two lords, set its board at about the same distance from each half's headman's house. The map follows the traffic reason, which the record finds everywhere; the split village's reason is one this setting's maps do not draw.

Every board is sited by the map's own probe, never hand-picked (GM 2026-07-27: "fix both of them"). The reason is that a hand-picked seat loses the traffic the board exists for: on the two town maps the probe finds seats standing on 19 and 29 structures within 250 ft, against 3 and 10 for the seats a hand would choose.

The caption is part of the seat, or the board walks out of town (GM 2026-07-27, from "the notice board in Ubame does not look well-placed; I'd expect it to be in a higher-traffic area"). The drawn marker is small and fits almost anywhere; its caption does not, and the busiest frontage is exactly where there is least room for one. A siter that hunts for ground large enough to hold both therefore walks away from the traffic by construction, and a seat across the bridge from the town, 120 ft past the last shop, scores 8 structures within 250 ft against 23 on the high street. The stretch of road beyond the last house is still a road. So the probe scores the caption instead of hunting for ground: a seat whose caption fits outranks any seat whose caption does not, and among those the busiest wins; a board with nowhere to put a caption is still placed. Whether a feature stands on the traffic is a judgment call: there is no mathematical rule for "lots of foot traffic".

The board is the last thing placed, and it clears no ground at all (GM 2026-08-29). It is placed after every other feature on the map, the crop and the title included, and the reasoning is about the settlement rather than about the drawing: "where you put the notice board on the map does depend on what other features already exist ... the real humans that live in the society that decide where the notice board will go will look around at the things which already exist and then decide where to put the notice board. They may even decide to move a notice board which has already been placed." Every other feature either reserves ground or grows into it; the board does neither, so it is the one thing that should see the whole map before it chooses. Two consequences. The board clears no ground at all; the GM's ruling: "it would be very easy to put the notice board at the edge of the forest. We could even display it as being underneath the canopy because I think in many cases it would be ... humans would not need to clear any amount of space in order to put up a notice board at the side of a path." The one ground a real board claimed was its own: the site was closed in with a palisade, a stone wall or a turf bank, an enclosure hardly larger than the frame, and no page read says a board's site was cleared of trees or that a board stood under one.

A board under a canopy is fine, and its label is why (GM 2026-08-29): "As long as there is a label attached to it and the label is visible - which it should be, because labels are always on the topmost layer of the way the image is rendered - then someone will always be able to see it, even on the PNG version of a map. The fact that the label is present tells someone looking at the map where the notice board is." A crown cannot paint over a label: on all five scripted hamlets the caption is drawn above every tree crown. So "is the board findable under a tree" is settled - the label carries the read, the same convention that lets an oversized location marker stand for a board whose true frame - about 16 to 18 ft along the road and 5 to 6 ft deep on the post-town boards measured - is too small to draw at city grain.

The board stands on a main way (GM 2026-08-02, a probe hunting the busiest clear verge can land on a side lane one block south of the high street, 200 ft off the road the whole town walks: "The notice board should be along a main street/road on every map, never a side street... in order to be more noticed."). Every road is a main way by construction and so is a street the map declares as its gate-to-yamen avenue; where a map declares either, the probe samples only those verges, so a board on a side lane cannot arise. Two scope edges are deliberate. A map with no declared hierarchy - a lane web, a town whose streets are all unflagged - is exempt, because there is nothing "main" to stand on and the busiest-node scoring stands in for the judgment. And the punishment ground is not covered on purpose (GM 2026-08-02: "other map features like punishment grounds don't always need to be along a main road, but a notice board must be"): the ground is a display for locals who already know where justice is done, while the board must ambush the eye of everyone passing through.
