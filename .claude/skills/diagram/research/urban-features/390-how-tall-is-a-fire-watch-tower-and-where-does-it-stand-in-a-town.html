How tall is a fire-watch tower, and where does it stand in a town?

Sources: hinomi-yagura-kotobank (the first tower's height, and the simple ladder form), hinomi-yagura-tfd (the first tower's height, and the rules that kept the others lower and open), hinomi-yagura-bousaihaku (a second figure for the height, and the same rules), kidoban-jawiki (the tower beside the ward gate), jishinban-jawiki (the ladder on the watch-house roof)

Whether a town keeps a tower at all is the town's question; how a city spreads its towers is the city fabric's.

How tall. The published accounts give the first towers - those of the shogunate's standing fire brigade, from 1658, at its fire-brigade mansions - two heights: 3 jo, about 9 m (about 30 ft), which the Tokyo Fire Department's own history also gives as about 9 m, or about 5 jo, about 15 m (about 50 ft). Every other tower was held below them. The towers at the daimyo mansions and at the ward gates of the town had to be lower than the brigade's, were all lacquered black, and a townsmen's tower was not allowed to have its legs boarded in; only a daimyo of 80,000 koku or more might build one at all, and where the brigade's tower kept two watchmen always on it with a great drum and a bell, the daimyo's and the townsmen's towers kept no watchman, only a wooden clapper-board or a bell hung on them. The simple form was a fire-watch ladder, and many a ward's watch house carried one on its roof with a bell - a small-scale fire tower, in the account's own words.

Where it stands. The townsmen's tower - a ladder tower - stood beside the ward gate, the kido, which is why the gate's keeper also struck its bell when there was a fire. What the gate and its keeper's hut were is the night-watch question.

How big on the ground. Nothing read gives a tower's footprint.

What it means for the map. A town's tower is a townsmen's tower: open-legged, as the rule required, and lower than the 30 to 50 ft of the brigade's towers. The open braced frame the maps draw is therefore right in its open legs, and the glyph's size stays a convention, since no footprint was found. Where it stands is the part the history now settles: beside a ward gate at the end of a street. Siting it on whatever open seam the finished map has, as the maps do, is a map convention.
