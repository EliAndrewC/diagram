Writing schools (terakoya)

A terakoya was a private school of the Edo period where a writing master taught the children of commoners, townsfolk and farmers alike, the first steps of reading, writing and reckoning. It needed only a master and one room to teach in, and there were said to be about as many of them as there were villages in Japan. A school held anywhere from 10 to 100 pupils.

  • The name points to the temple.
    A Meiji-era report says the schools were called terakoya in the Kamigata region because many children studied at the nearby temple.
  • The masters were headmen, village officials, doctors and monks.
    In one area of Bingo province the seven schools of the late Edo period, not all open at once, were kept by one village headman, two village officials, one doctor, two monks and one other.
  • Where was a village's school held?
    Most likely wherever its master lived: in the headman's house, a doctor's, an official's, or the temple. That is an inference from who the masters were, a GUESS - no page we read says where a village's school was held. On the village temple, see whether a village keeps one and what stands in its precinct.
  • A village kept one school, or two.
    In what is now Hannan city, one terakoya each in four villages and two in a fifth are recorded for the end of the Edo period.
  • How many writing schools did Japan have?
    About 15,500 to 16,500 were counted, and the count missed many regions. The survey behind the national count, published as the Nihon kyoikushi shiryo, is read as 15,560 schools in one book and 16,560 in another retelling, and it missed many regions altogether.
  • Edo alone had 400 to 500 large schools, and 1,000 to 1,300 counting the small.
    So says the same survey.

In China, schooling for a family's children or a county's poor was likewise held in a building that stood for other purposes, or was set up by the state.

  • A Chinese family's ancestral hall served as its children's school.
    The hall held its rites in spring and autumn, and in ordinary times served as a private school (sishu) for the family's children.
  • The Qing court ordered charity schools for orphaned and poor children in 1712.
    In the 51st year of the Kangxi emperor (1712; the page gives 1713) it ordered provinces, prefectures and counties to set up many charity schools that gathered the orphaned and poor and hired teachers for them. Their pupils were mostly six to eleven.
