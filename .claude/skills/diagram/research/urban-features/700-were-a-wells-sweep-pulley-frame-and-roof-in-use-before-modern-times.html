Were a well's sweep, pulley frame and roof in use before modern times? Yes - in China from the Han, in Japan by 1753

Sources: wangzhen-nongshu-18 (the sweep, the windlass frame and the depth that chose between them, 1313), tiangong-shuili (the sweep and the windlass, 1637), shi-zhang-lulu (the pulley on a Han well frame), kotobank-kurumaido (the pulley well in Kyoto, 1753), zhou-2026-gugong-wells (the roofed well and the pulley on a beam, Ming and Qing)

What the map draws at a well - the curb, the sweep or the pulley frame, the roof - is What does a communal well look like from above?; most of what that question read describes wells as museums and folklorists recorded them in modern times. This question is whether the same forms stood over wells before the Meiji Restoration in Japan and before the end of the Qing in China.

The sweep and the windlass frame, and the depth that chose between them. Wang Zhen's farming treatise of 1313 names the two together, the sweep that rocks and the windlass that turns, and sets them by depth: the sweep's rope is short and draws shallow, the windlass serves shallow and deep. Its windlass stands on a frame set up over the well carrying an axle, and the Tiangong Kaiwu of 1637 still names the pair.

The pulley on a frame. Older still: historians of technology read a drum-shaped pulley on a well frame in a Western Han tomb mural, and many more pulleys on Han reliefs and pottery well models. In Japan the pulley well is attested by 1753, in a comic verse saying that every well in Kyoto was one; the pulley served deep water and the sweep shallower.

The roof. Every well in the Ming and Qing Forbidden City had its pavilion, and one of them carries a pulley on two beams under its roof, very likely of the Qing, though most palace wells were drawn by rope and bucket by hand. A palace is the richest case; how many town or village wells had a roof is not known.

What it means for the map. All three forms the map draws at a well are premodern, so none is taken away: the sweep and the pulley frame are attested in China from the Han to the Ming and in Japan by the eighteenth century, and the choice between them by the water's depth is Wang Zhen's own rule. The roofed well is premodern too, but only its form: the share of wells that had one remains a guess.
