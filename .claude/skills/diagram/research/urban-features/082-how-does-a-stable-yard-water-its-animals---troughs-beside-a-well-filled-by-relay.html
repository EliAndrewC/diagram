How does a stable yard water its animals - troughs beside a well, filled by relay

Sources: equine-nutrition-enwiki, water-buffalo-enwiki, caravanserai-enwiki, mdfcta-enwiki, unl-beefwatch-cattle-water (how fast cattle drink), blm-oregon-trail-wagons (how long an ox team rested at a halt)

What a stable yard is on the ground - beaten earth, hitching rails, no fence, and where its muck heaps stand - is Stable yards - beaten earth, hitching rails, and watering by relay. This question is how the yard waters its animals: how much they drink, where the troughs stand, and why two or three are enough.

What the research found: a modern 450 kg horse drinks 10-12 gal/day and up to four times that under hard work (a post road's smaller horses less - this page's reasoning), a working ox about the same (this page's estimate), and a water buffalo in a hot climate wants a WALLOW (or a river, or splashing water), not just a trough - so a 20-40 head wagon-train arriving thirsty needs ~300-600 gallons (our arithmetic), drunk in one or two big sessions (this page's reading). On this page's reading, historical practice watered BY RELAY at a fixed point: Silk Road caravanserais watered whole trains from a courtyard well or fountain; an inn yard drew by bucket from a well into troughs (the bucket and the wells, not channels, being the in-town norm - this page's reading); London's high-traffic granite street troughs show the throughput, one busy trough serving 1,800 horses in a day by turnover (fed, on this page's reading, from the mains, which a bucket-filled yard basin is not), and the trough dimensions these maps draw (~14 ft long, ~2 ft of edge per drinking head, both sides usable) are our own. On this page's reading, animals are LED to the water in relays - you water at the well, not at the hitching rail. The decision: the map draws a WATERING POINT - 2 troughs for a plain stables yard, 3 for a caravan-scale ground, ~14 ft long each - clustered BESIDE the nearest well: offset from the wellhead by only its visual radius + half a trough (~bucket-pour distance, GM 2026-07-23 - "otherwise you'd have to carry the water a long way"), on the yard side, even when the well stands at or just past the yard rim. A yard with NO reachable well - or every flank of the near wellhead blocked - DIGS ITS OWN courtyard well and clusters the troughs beside that (GM 2026-07-23). The historical form supports the dig: the Silk Road caravanserai watered whole trains from its OWN courtyard well or fountain, and the yizhan post-yard, on this page's reading, did the same - "no well nearby" is a reason to sink one, never to carry 300-600 gallons across a city block. Every yard's troughs stand within 40 ft of a well. The trough cluster draws on open ground (GM 2026-07-23, same day): the cluster is set just far enough from the wellhead to clear the well-house roof in whichever direction it lies, and is kept off every building, road, field, water course, the wall and every other wellhead roof. Departures: trough width is floored at about 6 ft at city grain, the least a mark must measure to be seen at all (a true trough, perhaps ~2.5 ft wide by this page's estimate, would not register), the same convention the wellhead marker takes, and the buffalo WALLOW (a mud pond of perhaps 15-30 ft, this page's estimate; very China-first, on this page's reading) is deliberately NOT drawn by default - it is an available flourish for a specific map where ground and water fit.

Capacity - why 2-3 troughs absorb several dozen oxen, and why MORE troughs is the wrong lever (GM asked, 2026-07-23: "there might be several dozen oxen passing through at a time... 2 troughs doesn't feel like enough"). It is enough, and adding troughs - the naive fix - does nothing. Edge capacity: each drawn trough is ~14 ft at ~2 ft of edge per drinking head, both sides of a freestanding trough usable - so one trough serves ~14 head at once (call it 7 where the wellhead crowds one side); the caravan ground's 3 troughs are ~20-40 simultaneous stations, a plain yard's 2 are ~14-28. Turnover: cattle drink ~1-4 gal/min, so a working ox's 5-8 gal session is over in two to seven minutes - even read one-sided, the caravan cluster waters ~20 head every ~6 minutes, and several dozen oxen clear in two or three relays (~20-30 min). That fits how a halt worked: the animals were unhitched to rest - most for an hour or so, several hours in hot weather - so watering was a leisurely relay chore for a couple of teamsters, not a race - the London figure (one trough, 1,800 horses/day) is the throughput-by-turnover anchor. The binding constraint is the WELL, not the trough edge: a hand bucket-draw sustains maybe 5-10 gal/min (a GUESS - no source read gives a rate), so a train's 300-600 gallons is half an hour to 2 hours of steady drawing NO MATTER how many troughs stand there. Extra troughs would not water anyone faster; they would just hold more standing water. What the troughs actually do is BUFFER - filled between arrivals so the first relay drinks instantly while the drawing continues - which is exactly why a caravanserai watered from one courtyard well or fountain (that this was INSTEAD of multiplying basins is this page's reading), and why we expect a coaching-inn yard to show a SINGLE trough (our 2-3 is already the generous end).

The rule the map follows: the watering point is a cluster of 2 troughs for a plain stables yard and 3 for a caravan-scale ground, about 14 ft each, standing beside the nearest well at bucket-pour distance and within 40 ft of it; a yard with no well in reach, or with every flank of the near wellhead blocked, DIGS ITS OWN courtyard well and clusters the troughs beside that, which is what a caravanserai did (and, on this page's reading, a post-yard). The trough glyph's width is floored at about 6 ft at city grain, the least a mark needs to measure to be seen at all - a map drawing convention, since a true trough, perhaps 2.5 ft wide by this page's estimate, would not register. The buffalo wallow - a mud pond of perhaps 15 to 30 ft (this page's estimate), and, on this page's reading, very much the China-first answer - is deliberately not drawn by default; it is a flourish for a map whose ground and water fit it.
