How does a stable yard water its animals - troughs beside a well, filled by relay

Sources: kotobank-umabune (the horse trough as a fodder trough, from about 934), qimin-yaoshu-yangma (troughs in the yard, watering at set times), basuiso-jawiki (the first watering trough in Japan, 1906), equine-nutrition-enwiki, water-buffalo-enwiki, caravanserai-enwiki, mdfcta-enwiki, unl-beefwatch-cattle-water (how fast cattle drink), blm-oregon-trail-wagons (how long an ox team rested at a halt)

What a stable yard is on the ground - beaten earth, hitching rails, no fence, and where its muck heaps stand - is Stable yards - beaten earth, hitching rails, and watering by relay. This question is how the yard waters its animals: how much they drink, where the troughs stand, and why two or three are enough.

Is the trough a premodern form? In part. A trough in a stable yard is: the Japanese word for a horse trough is found in a dictionary of about 934, and means the container for a horse's feed, a fodder tub; the sixth-century Qimin Yaoshu gives a stable of many stallions a separate yard with many troughs, and, to harden a horse ridden on journeys, sets its trough out on ground away from the stable, not under the shed even in snow - an exception, which implies that an ordinary trough stood under a shed. So is watering by ration at set times: the same manual waters a horse three times a day, a little in the morning and its fill in the evening. What is NOT premodern is the free-standing watering trough, drinkable from both sides (this page's reading of that form, not a quoted description): the only ones found are London's Victorian granite street troughs, and the first in Japan was one that London's trough association gave to Tokyo, set up in 1906. No premodern Japanese or Chinese watering trough was found, nor any trough standing beside a well, and the drinking figures below are modern measurements with no premodern figure found beside them. The decision: the maps do not draw the two-sided London trough; a yard's trough is the plain trough of the premodern stable yard, used from one side; that it held water and stood by the well is a guess, and that it stands in the open rather than under a shed is a guess too, since the manual's ordinary trough seems to have stood under one (the placement by the well is the GM's ruling below). At map scale the glyph looks the same either way; the difference is the trough the map claims to show, and the capacity below, which counts one side only.

What the research found: a modern 450 kg horse drinks 10-12 gal/day and up to four times that under hard work (a post road's smaller horses less - this page's reasoning), a working ox about the same (this page's estimate), and a water buffalo in a hot climate wants a WALLOW (or a river, or splashing water), not just a trough - so a 20-40 head wagon-train arriving thirsty needs ~300-600 gallons (our arithmetic), drunk in one or two big sessions (this page's reading). On this page's reading, historical practice watered BY RELAY at a fixed point: Silk Road caravanserais watered whole trains from a courtyard well or fountain; an inn yard drew by bucket from a well into troughs (a GUESS: no trough beside a well was found) (the bucket and the wells, not channels, being the in-town norm - this page's reading); London's Victorian granite street troughs, the modern model set aside above, watered as many as 1,800 horses a day at one trough; and the trough dimensions these maps draw (~14 ft long, ~2 ft of edge per drinking head) are our own, a guess. On this page's reading, animals are LED to the water in relays - you water at the well, not at the hitching rail. The decision: the map draws a WATERING POINT - 2 troughs for a plain stables yard, 3 for a caravan-scale ground, ~14 ft long each - clustered BESIDE the nearest well: offset from the wellhead by only its visual radius + half a trough (~bucket-pour distance, GM 2026-07-23 - "otherwise you'd have to carry the water a long way"), on the yard side, even when the well stands at or just past the yard rim. A yard with NO reachable well - or every flank of the near wellhead blocked - DIGS ITS OWN courtyard well and clusters the troughs beside that (GM 2026-07-23). The historical form supports the dig: the Silk Road caravanserai watered whole trains from its OWN courtyard well or fountain, and the yizhan post-yard, on this page's reading, did the same - "no well nearby" is a reason to sink one, never to carry 300-600 gallons across a city block. Every yard's troughs stand within 40 ft of a well. The trough cluster draws on open ground (GM 2026-07-23, same day): the cluster is set just far enough from the wellhead to clear the well-house roof in whichever direction it lies, and is kept off every building, road, field, water course, the wall and every other wellhead roof. Departures: trough width is floored at about 6 ft at city grain, the least a mark must measure to be seen at all (a true trough, perhaps ~2.5 ft wide by this page's estimate, would not register), the same convention the wellhead marker takes, and the buffalo WALLOW (a mud pond of perhaps 15-30 ft, this page's estimate; very China-first, on this page's reading) is deliberately NOT drawn by default - it is an available flourish for a specific map where ground and water fit.

Capacity - why 2-3 troughs absorb several dozen oxen, and why MORE troughs is the wrong lever (GM asked, 2026-07-23: "there might be several dozen oxen passing through at a time... 2 troughs doesn't feel like enough"). It is enough, and adding troughs - the naive fix - does nothing. Edge capacity: each drawn trough is ~14 ft at ~2 ft of edge per drinking head, used from one side - so one trough serves ~7 head at once; the caravan ground's 3 troughs are ~20 simultaneous stations, a plain yard's 2 are ~14. Turnover: modern feedlot cattle drink ~1-4 gal/min, so a working ox's 5-8 gal session is over in two to seven minutes (a modern rate, taken as the animal's own) - the caravan cluster waters ~20 head every ~6 minutes, and several dozen oxen clear in two or three relays (~20-30 min). That fits how a halt worked: the animals were unhitched to rest - most for an hour or so, several hours in hot weather - so watering was a leisurely relay chore for a couple of teamsters, not a race. The binding constraint is the WELL, not the trough edge: a hand bucket-draw sustains maybe 5-10 gal/min (a GUESS - no source read gives a rate), so a train's 300-600 gallons is half an hour to 2 hours of steady drawing NO MATTER how many troughs stand there. Extra troughs would not water anyone faster; they would just hold more standing water. What the troughs actually do is BUFFER - filled between arrivals so the first relay drinks instantly while the drawing continues - which is exactly why a caravanserai watered from one courtyard well or fountain (that this was INSTEAD of multiplying basins is this page's reading), and why we expect a coaching-inn yard to show a SINGLE trough (our 2-3 is already the generous end).

The rule the map follows: the watering point is a cluster of 2 troughs for a plain stables yard and 3 for a caravan-scale ground, each the plain one-sided trough of a premodern stable yard (never the two-sided street trough of Victorian London), about 14 ft long by a guess, standing beside the nearest well at bucket-pour distance and within 40 ft of it; a yard with no well in reach, or with every flank of the near wellhead blocked, DIGS ITS OWN courtyard well and clusters the troughs beside that, which is what a caravanserai did (and, on this page's reading, a post-yard). The trough glyph's width is floored at about 6 ft at city grain, the least a mark needs to measure to be seen at all - a map drawing convention, since a true trough, perhaps 2.5 ft wide by this page's estimate, would not register. The buffalo wallow - a mud pond of perhaps 15 to 30 ft (this page's estimate), and, on this page's reading, very much the China-first answer - is deliberately not drawn by default; it is a flourish for a map whose ground and water fit it.
