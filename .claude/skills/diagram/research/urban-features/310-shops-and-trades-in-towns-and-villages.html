Shops and trades in towns and villages

In the towns of Edo-period Japan a shop was not a building of its own. The town house, the machiya, was a dwelling with a shop attached, the shop and the family's rooms under one roof, and an ordinary one ran some 18 to 30 ft along the street and about 30 ft deep. Trade was not only for towns: in the villages, households that farmed also kept a shop or worked a craft between their farm work - about a quarter of them across one district surveyed in 1829, and four in ten in its largest village.

Most trades - the cooper, the tofu maker, the noodle shop, the apothecary - worked and sold in that front room. A few needed more ground, and each has a section of its own: the sake brewer's vat hall, the dyer's rinsing and drying, the timber dealer's (zaimokuya) yard, the oil press, the pawnshop's (shichiya) storehouses, the bathhouse's furnace and fuel and the smith's forge.

  • An Edo street lot was 20 ken deep, and the shop at its front about 5 ken.
    In Edo the lots on the street were a standard 20 ken (about 120 ft) deep, and the shop building at the front, the front shop, was held to about 5 ken (about 30 ft) deep, with back tenements behind it: on one side where the shop was about 3 ken (18 ft) wide, on both sides where it was about 5 ken (30 ft).
  • A commoner's lot in a castle town was narrow at the front and deep behind.
    Each household had less ground than a samurai's, and the houses stood without a gap along the highway, two stories high, narrow and long: the "eel's bed".
  • Kyoto sorted its town households by frontage into three classes.
    Large houses of 10 ken (60 ft) and more, ordinary houses of 4 or 5 ken (24 to 30 ft), and small houses of 1.5 to 2 ken (9 to 12 ft), or rented ones.
  • How big was an ordinary town shop?
    About 18 to 30 ft along the street and about 30 ft deep - some 540 to 900 sq ft on the ground, the family's rooms included. The range joins the Edo front shop's frontage and depth with Kyoto's ordinary houses. No page we read measures the shop-houses of a country town.
  • Many town houses were far smaller.
    The ordinary Kyoto house at the end of the Muromachi period was about 2 ken by 2 ken (about 12 by 12 ft), and most town houses in Edo early in the Edo period were smaller again, about 1.5 ken wide by 2 ken deep (9 by 12 ft). How many of a town's shops were this small, no page we read says.

How many households kept a trade can be counted in one district, the villages of what is now Yashio city, whose side trades a survey listed in 1829.

  • Across the district's nineteen villages, about a quarter of the households lived partly by a trade or a craft.
    The villages held some 5,672 inhabitants in 1,034 households, and about 250 of those households traded or worked a craft in the intervals of farming. Between them they kept 45 taverns, 18 pawnshops, 13 hairdressers and one bathhouse, at Hachijo.
  • How far did the share vary from village to village?
    From 41 percent at Yanaginomiya down to 4 percent, a single household, at Kamibaba.
  • At Hachijo, the district's largest village, four households in ten traded.
    Hachijo was a key point of traffic. The survey counted 856 inhabitants in 166 households there, and 66 of the households traded or worked a craft between their farm work. They kept six taverns, three hairdressers, one bathhouse and three pawnshops.
  • A list of 1836 shows Hachijo with a sake brewer, an oil presser, two dyers and a stonemason beside its small shops.
    A list of 1836 names a sake brewer, an oil presser who also took pledges, a bathhouse, two dyers, a stonemason, and a run of small shops selling sake, vinegar and soy sauce with sandals, paper, tofu and sundries beside them.
  • In the Yashio villages, pawnbroking was often combined with another trade.
    The district's local history says pawnshops were often combined with another trade, and Hachijo's list of 1836 has an oil presser who also took pledges.
  • In the Yashio villages, the sake shop was a general store too.
    The district's local history says the sake shops also dealt in food and sundries.
  • The Yashio villages kept their own carpenters, roofers, smiths and coopers, among other craftsmen.
    They were paper makers, cotton beaters, carpenters (boat carpenters too), roofers, tile makers, smiths, coopers and woodcutters.
  • Some villages kept no craftsmen at all.
    In the Kyoho era, early in the eighteenth century, many shogunal villages of the Uma district of Iyo recorded none, and where they had any, both the numbers and the trades were few.
  • A place registered as a village could keep a whole row of trades.
    Kawanoe's register of 1701 counts 11 dyers, 15 sake shops, 8 carpenters, 6 smiths, 4 coopers, 3 sawyers and 1 umbrella maker - quite unlike the district's shogunal villages in the prefectural history's table, as that history notes.
  • Brewing sake to sell is licensed in this setting.
    Every brewery above the household level holds an annual license.
  • No page we read says whether a village's brewer was also its headman.
    In this setting the county town counts "the sake brewer" among its notables, but none of the village records we read ties a brewer to the headman.
  • How much of a small place's trade did its farming households carry?
    The setting's tables give a town 120 merchants in 24 households, but the Yashio survey counts its trading households apart from those that lived by farming alone, and at Hachijo they were 66 of 166. A place of this size carried a good deal more commerce than its merchant households alone.
  • How many trading households would a county seat keep?
    On the order of 50 to 90, for a county seat of about 1,200 inhabitants. Edo's country towns - post towns, ports, temple towns and other places that stood in law in the countryside - commonly had 200 to 1,000 households and 1,000 to 6,000 inhabitants, so Hachijo sits just below that range and the county seat a little above its small end. Hachijo had one trading household to every 13 inhabitants, the nineteen villages one to every 23, and at those rates a place of 1,200 holds about 50 to 90. The range is the Yashio rates carried to a county seat, not a count of one.
  • No page we read counts the shops of a city.
    No trade census of a city was found, so Hachijo's one trading household to every 13 inhabitants is the densest figure the record has.

Which trades were worked at home and which went out to the job was a line Edo drew itself, with a name for each side. The setting's own list of castes names many of these trades: coopers, weavers and every other worker in fiber, noodle makers, tofu makers, and the brewers of sake and soy sauce.

  • Edo's craftsmen were sorted into those who went out to work and those who worked at home.
    Carpenters and plasterers went out to work; a cabinetmaker worked in a workshop in his own home.
  • The cooper made and sold his tubs and barrels at home.
    From the early modern period there were coopers who worked at home, making and selling in one place. Because hoops broke often, some went out to mend them, and some walked the round of the towns and villages.
  • The tatami maker worked mostly at home, and sometimes on the job.
    So a Japanese dictionary entry on the trade says.
  • The tofu maker made and sold tofu in the shop, and peddled it too.
    Selling from a shop began in the seventeenth century, and by the eighteenth there were tofu makers in the villages around the towns as well. Tofu was sold over the counter and carried round in tubs slung from a shoulder pole.
  • A noodle shop made its noodles and served or sold them in the one place.
    So a Japanese dictionary defines it.
  • An apothecary compounded and sold its medicines in its house.
    It compounded crude drugs and dealt in Chinese-style medicine.
  • The carpenter worked on the building he was putting up.
    He was one of the craftsmen who went out to work, so his own house, in this project's reading, needed no yard. How a town's timber reached him is at Does a town keep a timber yard, and does it need a river?
  • Where did a rice dealer polish his rice?
    In his shop, in this project's reading, a GUESS. A rice dealer polished brown rice and retailed it, and the tool, in this project's reading, was the treadle pestle, a pestle on a see-saw worked by the foot over a mortar fixed in the ground; no page we read sets the pestle in the shop or the grain in a storehouse behind it. Where a town's rice was hulled is at Rice hulling and polishing (tsukigomeya)
  • Did any of these trades need a yard, a kiln or water of its own?
    Not on any page we read: in the entries above, the work and the sale share the keeper's house, or the work is done away from it. No page we read gives a workshop's size.

Of the trades that needed more ground, a few findings hold across them: where the timber merchant, the kiln and the tanner stood, and how Hangzhou's bathhouse keepers were organized; each trade's own section, linked above, has the rest.

  • Edo gathered its timber merchants into one quarter after a great fire.
    The spread of the great fire of 1641 was blamed on the timber stacked high by merchants scattered through the city, and they were gathered together on Eitaijima. After a further great fire in 1657, the district of Kiba was laid out in 1703 on the waterways opened in its wake, some 90,000 tsubo (about 73 acres) of it.
  • Bathhouse keepers in Song-dynasty Hangzhou had a guild of their own.
    In the Song capital, Hangzhou, those who kept bathhouses formed a guild, the "fragrant water" guild.
  • Kilns stood at the edge of a town or in villages nearby.
    Why they stood out - fire, or smoke - no page we read says; it is this project's reading, a GUESS. The kilns themselves are at Kiln works - a kiln is not a tile kiln, and the potters live at it.
  • Tanning went to the riverside, with the outcast quarter.
    The burakumin lived in particular places, often on riverbanks, which the tanning needed, and mostly on ground unsuited to farming, at the edge of the village or beside a river. The tanning yard is at Tanning yards - hides come from fallen draft stock, and water is the gate.

In Japan's castle towns, the households of one trade were set together by order, in wards named for the trade.

  • Merchants and artisans were moved into the castle towns by force, divided by trade.
    The commoners' ground lay outside the samurai quarters, and it was filled by moving in nearby villages and by moving merchants and artisans in by force, divided by occupation. The ward names that survive - Gofukumachi (drapers), Aburayamachi (oil sellers), Daikumachi (carpenters), Kajimachi (smiths), Konyamachi (dyers) - are these wards.
  • Provincial castle towns kept trade wards too, not only the great cities.
    A Kajimachi was often the ward of a castle town where, under that settlement by trade, many smiths lived, and the name survives in Hirosaki, Hachinohe, Kuwana and Nabari among many others. The dyers' ward, and what a capital makes of it, is at Which capital trades change their form rather than their count, and where do the official kilns go?
  • Coopers and drug sellers kept streets of their own as well.
    In the castle towns the coopers lived together in cooper streets, and in Edo the drug sellers stood eave to eave in one street, Nihonbashi Honcho.
  • No page we read counts the trade wards of one castle town.
    The pages name five trades' wards and the coopers' streets, and list the towns that keep a smiths' ward, but none says how many such wards a single town held.
  • No page we read tells of trade wards in towns smaller than a castle town.
    The pages on castle towns speak of settlement by trade there, and none carries it to a smaller town.

China grouped its trades in its own way. Hangzhou, the capital of the Song dynasty, set them by row and by market.

  • Song-dynasty Hangzhou's trades stood in named rows and markets.
    Many were named for where they stood: the fish row north of the city, the crab row east of it, the cloth row at Henghetou; the medicine market at Tan bridge, the flower market in Guan lane, the rice market north of the city.
  • Along Hangzhou's main street, every house traded.
    From the Hening gate down to Guan bridge there was not one house that did not buy and sell, so the city had both named trade rows and a main street of mixed trades.
  • No page we read describes the trade streets of a Chinese provincial city.
    The pages we read place the trade rows of the Song capital, and of no prefectural city.
