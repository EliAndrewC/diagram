How big is a notice board, and what does it look like from above?

Sources: mlit-tokaido-kosatsuba (the measured boards of the Tokaido post towns), mlit-tokaido-qa-kosatsuba (the stone base, the fence and the roof), kotobank-kosatsu (the enclosures of a board's site), shiojiri-iwadare-kosatsuba (a village board on a stone base under a roof)

Much bigger than a sign on a post. The notices themselves were wooden boards about a foot high, and they hung on a frame raised well above the heads of the passers-by. A survey of the Tokaido post towns measured the frames, and at Kanagawa-juku and Totsuka-juku they ran about 10 to 11 ft high, 16 to 18 ft along the road and 5 to 6 ft deep. The reconstructed board at Kanagawa shows the whole form: a stone base, a fence round it, the boards in two tiers inside, and a roof over them against wind and rain. The site itself was guarded: it was closed in with a palisade of crossed stakes, or with a stone wall or a bank of turf.

A village's board could be built the same way. The surviving board of Iwadare, a village now in Shiojiri, Nagano, stands on a stone base, large, with sound timbers and a sound roof. But no village board was measured on any page read, so whether a village's frame was as long as a post town's is not known, and nothing read says which of the enclosures was the usual one.

This is the town's board, the one every passer-by was meant to read. A board the magistrate's office kept at its own gate, apart from the town's, is a different question, and a guess: "Did the magistrate post notices at the office's own gate, or on the town's notice board?" (on the buildings page)

What it means for the map. From above, a notice board is a roofed rectangle about 16 by 6 ft, its long side turned to the way, on a stone footing a little larger than the roof, with a fence close round it. That is larger than the board face the map sites today (The notice board (kosatsuba) - siting is a traffic decision), which is shorter than every frame measured. At the town grain the true size can be drawn; at city grain the oversized marker is a drawing convention, as it is now.

The rule the map follows: a notice board is drawn as a roofed frame 16 ft long and 6 ft deep , its long side parallel to the way it faces, on a stone footing about 2 ft wider all round, with a fence line at the footing's edge. The size is that of the boards measured at Kanagawa and Totsuka; drawing a village's or a hamlet's board at the same size is a guess, since none was measured.
