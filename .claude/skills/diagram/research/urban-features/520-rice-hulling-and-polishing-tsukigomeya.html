Rice hulling and polishing (tsukigomeya)

Rice passed through two steps between the paddy and the pot, done in different places. Hulling, stripping the husk to leave brown rice, was the farm's work, and the rice left the village already hulled. Polishing brown rice white was a trade of its own, the rice polisher's (tsukigomeya); in the city of Edo in 1744, midway through the Edo period, there were more than two thousand of them. Rice was polished by pounding it with pestles, worked by foot or by a waterwheel.

  • Rice left the village already hulled.
    In the Edo period, before 1868, both the rice farmers paid as tax and the rice they shipped for sale were threshed and hulled brown rice.
  • Delivering the tax rice was the village's charge.
    Rice left a village as tax, and the village packed and sent it itself: it answered for the tax until it reached the lord's storehouse. In Dewa province the tax rice, packed in bales, was put in a storehouse near the village and carried to the port of Sakata the next spring.
  • The broken grains of hulling stayed on the farm.
    The clay hulling mill broke many grains, and because broken rice could not be shipped, the farm household ground it to flour and ate it itself. The Japanese Wikipedia article on hulling mills says this of the clay mill's era, which lasted until modern hullers spread from about 1935.

Polishing rice for the city was the work of specialists.

  • In the early modern period rice polishers made polishing their trade.
    Those who took it up as a specialty were called tsukigomeya, and they were the last link in the rice trade.
  • Was a town's rice dealer its polisher?
    Most likely, in this project's reading. The polishers were the last link in the rice trade, and rice reached a town already hulled, so the rice dealer of Shops and trades in towns and villages would have been a polisher, not a huller.
  • Edo in 1744 had some 2,100 rice polishers working about 5,500 mortars.
    They were organized in 18 guilds.
  • From 1744 Edo forbade the villages to polish rice for sale in the city.
    A Japanese encyclopedia's entry on rice polishers records the ban.
  • Were the polishers' mortars worked by foot?
    Most likely, in this project's reading, though the Japanese encyclopedia's entry on rice polishers, which counts their mortars, does not say what kind they were. The treadle pestle, a pestle on a see-saw worked by the foot, came into use in the Edo period, after a hand-turned clay mill was brought from Qing China in the Genroku era.
  • Waterwheels also drove pestles to polish rice.
    The woodblock-print artist Hokusai drew one on the Onden river in Edo in the 1830s, where there were said to be several.
  • No page we read says how often a town had a waterwheel mill as well as its polishers' shops.
    The encyclopedia entries on rice polishers and on hulling, and the Japanese Wikipedia articles on rice polishing and on hulling mills, give no count of mills against shops for a town of any size.
  • No page we read gives a waterwheel mill's size or how far from the houses it stood.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on rice polishing names the waterwheels on the Onden river but describes no mill building.
