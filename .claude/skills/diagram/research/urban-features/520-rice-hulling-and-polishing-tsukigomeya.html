Rice hulling and polishing (tsukigomeya)

Rice passed through two steps between the paddy and the pot, done in different places. Hulling, stripping the husk to leave brown rice, was the farm's work, and the rice left the village already hulled. Polishing brown rice white was a trade of its own, the rice polisher's (tsukigomeya); in Edo in 1744 there were some 2,100 of them. They pounded the rice with pestles, worked by foot or by a waterwheel.

  • Rice left the village already hulled.
    In the Edo period, before 1868, both the rice farmers paid as tax and the rice they shipped for sale were threshed and hulled brown rice.
  • Delivering the tax rice was the village's charge.
    The village answered for the tax until it reached the lord's storehouse. In Dewa province the tax rice, packed in bales, was put in a storehouse near the village and carried to the port of Sakata the next spring.
  • The broken grains of hulling stayed on the farm.
    The clay hulling mill broke many grains, and because broken rice could not be shipped, the farm household ground it to flour and ate it itself. The article says this of the clay mill's era, which lasted until modern hullers spread from about 1935.

Polishing was done away from the farm, by specialists.

  • In the early modern period rice polishers made polishing their trade.
    Those who took it up as a specialty were called tsukigomeya, and they were the last link in the rice trade. So the rice dealer of Shops and trades in towns and villages is a polisher, not a huller.
  • Edo in 1744 had some 2,100 rice polishers working about 5,500 mortars.
    They were organized in 18 guilds.
  • From 1744 Edo forbade the villages to polish rice for sale in the city.
    The same dictionary entry records the ban.
  • Were the polishers' mortars worked by foot?
    Most likely, in this project's reading, though the entry does not say which mortar they used. The treadle pestle, a pestle on a see-saw worked by the foot, came into use in the Edo period, after a hand-turned clay mill was brought from Qing China in the Genroku era.
  • Waterwheels also drove pestles to polish rice.
    Such a wheel worked pestles up and down to polish brown rice. Hokusai drew one on the Onden river in Edo in the 1830s, where there were said to be several.
