Iron refining forges (chao)

Chinese ironworks turned the cast iron of their blast furnaces into wrought iron by fining - 炒 (chao, "stir-frying"): broken cast iron was burned down in a small hearth under a forced blast and stirred until its carbon burned out. In Japan the same work was done at a refining forge, the okaji, from the seventeenth century on.

Smelting and refining could share one site or stand apart. A charcoal furnace laid a heavy load on the forest around it, and in Guangdong the furnaces stood out in the forested mountains while the iron came down by river to be refined in a town.

  • A Chinese fining hearth was a small, well-insulated hollow in the ground under a blast of air.
    Wood, charcoal and broken pieces of cast iron were charged into it and lit, and when the iron was semi-molten, with the consistency of bean-curd dregs, it was stirred about with an iron rod under the blast.
  • A seventeenth-century Chinese treatise describes a different way: iron run straight from the furnace into an open basin.
    Song Yingxing's Tiangong Kaiwu leads the molten iron into a rectangular hearth - 塘 (tang, "pool, basin") - built a few chi from the furnace outlet and a few cun lower, against a short wall. Several men stand in a row on the wall stirring with willow poles, which burn down two or three cun at each fining, while one spreads a dried, sieved powder, wuchaoni, "filthy wet loam". The authority on Chinese ironmaking who translates it thinks the loam may be something like the material of a nitre bed.
  • Could iron be fined in an open basin?
    The same authority doubts it. Song Yingxing's basin is open, unfueled and unblown, and cast iron flowing into it would set solid before much of its carbon was gone; he calls it a very different process from fining, and a very difficult one to explain.
  • How old is fining in China?
    It may go back to the Han dynasty, some two thousand years ago. The authority says the process seems to have been used that early, and sets out the evidence in another of his books. An excavated Han smelting-and-fining works at Xuxiebian in Sichuan is named on no page we read.
  • In Guangdong, the iron was smelted in the mountains and refined in a town.
    The large firms of the province ran ironworks in its forested mountains and shipped most of their iron by river to the industrial town of Foshan, near Guangzhou, where some of it was made into wrought iron and the rest went to the foundries.
  • Charcoal furnaces were kept apart from each other, for their fuel.
    Two charcoal blast furnaces would not normally be put close together, because that would double the load on the forest for little or no gain.
  • A charcoal kiln burns about four and a half parts wood to one of charcoal.
    A modern industrial kiln works on that ratio, so, in this project's reading, the kiln goes to the wood, and a furnace that burns charcoal goes to the kilns.

Japan's ironworks came to two stages as well, by another road, when the tatara furnaces of the Chugoku Mountains turned to making pig iron.

  • Japan's refining forge, the okaji, came in the seventeenth century.
    The tatara's chief product changed from a lump of iron and slag to molten pig iron (zuku), and that led to the invention of a two-stage refining process, the okaji, to make it into wrought iron. Pig-making by the tatara and refining by the okaji made up Japan's indirect way of making wrought iron.
  • The tatara's lump was crushed and sorted before it was refined.
    By the 1750s the lump of iron from the furnace, the kera, was being broken with a crushing device and sorted finely by quality and size.
  • The refining hearth burned the pig iron's carbon down under the bellows.
    The pig iron was heated again to a semi-molten state, and its carbon burned off in the air the bellows sent in. Part of the iron was finished by heating and forging at the smithy into wrought-iron bars, called 包丁鉄 (hochotetsu, "kitchen-knife iron") for their shape.
  • How much of Japan's iron came from the Chugoku Mountains?
    Most of the iron for its guns, at least: the iron the gunsmiths of Kunitomo and other gun-making towns used, from the Sengoku through the Edo period, came almost all from the Chugoku Mountains. No page we read gives the region's share of all Japan's iron.
