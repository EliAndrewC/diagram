Did premodern East Asia hold its timber with a floating log boom?

Sources: tsunaba-nikkoku and nishikori-tsunaba (the catch rope), guazhi-jianggui (a Qing rule for mooring timber rafts), shiratori-chobokujo-jawiki (Nagoya's timber pond), nippon-fukagawa-kiba (the ponds of Edo's lumber lots), aba-jawiki (the floating boom in modern Japan)

No - a rope across the river, rafts moored along a bank, and ponds. Three forms are on record before modern times. The catch rope: a tsunaba was a river harbor on the middle course of a timber river, where a strong holding rope was stretched across the water to stop the floated timber, and a pass of 1422 already sends a hundred rafts "from Kawakami to the tsunaba", Kawakami being, in the dictionary's reading, the barrier at Nishikori. Rafts moored along a bank: at Guazhi on the Qingshui river, in China, a rule carved on a boulder on the bank in 1797 kept the water above a boundary marker for the upland sellers to moor their timber, and forbade the downstream buyers to moor their rafts there. Ponds off the river: Nagoya's timber store at Shiratori began, tradition says, with a great pond dug about 1610; the Kiso rafts were brought out to sea and up the Horikawa canal to it, and it was enlarged from 1651 until it covered 23,900 tsubo by the end of the Edo period. On the 1858 map of Edo each lumber dealer's lot at Kiba has a large pond at its center as the timber store, dry ground only round its edge, and the pond joined to the canals.

The floating log fence is modern. The one Japanese account found of a boom of floats strung on a rope, run from the far bank at a sharp angle to draw the logs to the near bank, rests on modern forestry histories, takes the former imperial Kiso forest as its example with steel cables in its rope, and dates the form no earlier than the river floating of timber that lasted into the early 1950s. The pens, piers and chained booms of the log boom are American works of the 19th century. No earlier East Asian boom was found. The GM ruled on 2026-09-28 that the maps draw nothing attested only in modern times, so the maps do not draw a floating log fence around a pen.

The rule the map follows: a timber city's working stock is drawn in the forms attested before modern times: raft-mats moored close along a slack bank off the lumber yard they feed, taking about a third of the river's width and never more than two fifths, or a pond dug off the river and joined to it by a canal. No floating fence and no pile clusters are drawn round it.
