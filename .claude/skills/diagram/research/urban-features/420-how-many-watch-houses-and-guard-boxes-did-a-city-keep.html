How many watch houses and guard boxes did a city keep?

Sources: jishinban-jawiki (Edo's watch houses, one to a ward), tsujiban-kotobank (the samurai quarter's guard boxes, how many, how big and how manned), muramatsu-tsujiban (a guard box's plan, and one domain's)

A town's gates and watch are the question before. A city's ward gates are the government page's, the guard at its city gates is the defenses page's, and the Song capital's patrol posts, one about every three hundred paces, are the city fabric's Chinese question.

In the townsmen's wards, one watch house to a ward. Edo's ward watch houses were set up ward by ward in the Kyoho era, two or three small wards sharing one, and in 1850 there were reported to be 994 of them.

In the samurai quarter, a guard box at the crossings. The samurai quarter kept its own boxes, the tsujiban, set up mainly in castle towns and in Kyoto as well as Edo; in Edo the samurai houses' boxes numbered 936 in the Genbun era (1736-1741), and a box was normally cut into a mansion's wall, 2 ken (about 12 ft) wide by 9 shaku (about 9 ft) deep, with four or five men on watch day and night. One domain's historian gives the usual plan as 2 ken by 9 shaku, six mats, with a lean-to 2 ken by 1 ken beside it, about ten mats in all and tile-roofed, other domains' boxes coming to about 5 tsubo.

A provincial city. Nothing read counts a provincial castle town's watch houses or guard boxes; Aizu-Wakamatsu's are described but not counted.

What it means for the map. A city draws one watch house to each commoner ward, or one to two or three small wards - Edo's rule, which a provincial city takes over at its own number of wards - sized and set as the question before finds a ward watch house was: nominally about 9 by 12 ft, often larger, against the gate at the end of the ward's main street. Its samurai ward draws guard boxes at its crossings, about 12 by 9 ft, set into a compound's wall; how many to a ward is not in what was read, so one to each crossing of the ward's streets is a guess.
