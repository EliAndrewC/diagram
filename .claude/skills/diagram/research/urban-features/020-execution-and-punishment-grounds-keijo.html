Execution and punishment grounds (keijo)

Edo, the shogun's capital, had two great execution grounds, Kozukappara to the north and Suzugamori to the south, each on the highway at one of the city's entrances. Anyone going in or out of Edo had to pass beside one, and the shogunate meant the sight to forestall crime. They were small: Suzugamori was a strip about 74 m (~243 ft) by 16 m (~53 ft), in use for 220 years. In China the capital's execution ground under the Qing dynasty was a vegetable market at a crossroads of Beijing.

The lesser punishments were shown inside the town. In China an offender could be made to wear the cangue for months, sealed by the magistrate, as a public humiliation, while beating with the cane or the stick was a punishment of the code, applied by the magistrate's own runners.

The execution ground stood outside the town.

  • Edo's two great execution grounds stood on the highways at the city's entrances.
    Kozukappara stood on the Nikko road at Edo's northern entrance, Suzugamori on the Tokaido at its southern one. The pages read call the pair Edo's "two great execution grounds".
  • The grounds were placed to be seen.
    A popular-history site says that anyone entering or leaving Edo had no choice but to pass beside an execution ground, and that the shogunate exposed so cruel a sight to forestall crime. No page read gives death pollution as the reason for the siting.
  • Kozukappara stood a few miles out along its highway.
    It was placed in the town just south of the post town of Senju, and Senju lay 2 ri from Nihonbashi in Edo.
  • A village's own authority ran to banishment.
    A villager who broke the village's own code could be banished from it.
  • Qing Beijing executed in a market.
    Caishikou, also known as the Vegetable Market Execution Ground, stood at the crossroads of Xuanwumen Outer Street and Luomashi Street.
  • A Chinese death sentence was reviewed each autumn at court.
    Under the Qing, the autumn assize decided each year whether death sentences were to be carried out. The Board of Punishments, the Court of Judicial Review and the Censorate heard the cases together, and the procedure applied to the prisoners held in the provinces awaiting execution.
  • Where was a confirmed death sentence carried out?
    At the county seat where the crime was done, in this project's reading, since a public death deterred where it was seen. It is a GUESS: the page on the autumn assize gives its outcomes and nothing on where a confirmed sentence was carried out.
  • Edo's main jail held the accused and the condemned.
    The jail at Tenmacho confined those awaiting judgment and held the convicted until their sentence was carried out, and it carried out beheadings, flogging and tattooing itself. Imprisonment was imposed as a sentence only exceptionally, so the jail was nearer to a modern detention house than to a prison.   • The executioners were outcastes, whose quarters often stood at the village's edge.
    The outcaste eta did lower-official work such as the executioner's and the arresting officer's, and their quarters were often in ground unfit for farming, at the edge of the village or beside the river. In this setting, every execution but a samurai's is done by the burakumin. Where their quarters stood is at Burakumin quarters and caste zoning.
  • The executed were not buried with the town's dead.
    At Kozukappara the bodies were left exposed, with only earth thrown over them, and in 1667 the abbot of the Ekoin temple founded a hall on adjoining ground to bury the dead and pray for them. The townspeople's own dead lay in temple graveyards, which many castle towns gathered at the town's outer edge as a line of defense.
  • A boundary stone marked where the settlement ended.
    The dosojin is a deity enshrined, mostly as a stone or a stone image, at the boundary of a settlement, between the village and the world outside, and at crossroads. Its other name, sae no kami, means "the blocking deity", and it is understood to keep intruders from outside.
  • Suzugamori measured about 74 m (~243 ft) by 16 m (~53 ft).
    A survey of 1695 gives its frontage as 40 ken and its depth as 9 ken, about a third of an acre. It served Edo from 1651 to 1871.
  • How often did a county execute?
    Perhaps once in five to ten years. This is a GUESS: at one to three executions a year for every 100,000 inhabitants, a county of 7,000 to 8,000 would reach the death penalty that seldom, a province about once a year, and a domain capital a handful of times; sweeps against bandits would add batches. No page we read gives a yearly count of executions. The count said of Suzugamori, 100,000 to 200,000 put to death in its 220 years, would be far higher for a city of Edo's size, but the page gives it only as hearsay.

The punishment ground stood inside the town, where people passed.

  • The cangue was a public punishment.
    It was used for public humiliation and corporal punishment in East Asia, and could be worn for as long as several months in severe cases. One photographed cangue bears a label sealed by the magistrate of Shanghai County.
  • The beating was a punishment of the code.
    Two of the Five Punishments were beating with a light bamboo cane and beating with a large stick.
  • Where was the beating given?
    At court, before the magistrate, in this project's reading. The magistrate's runners stood on both sides of the court during a trial and applied minor punishment, but no page read says where a beating took place.
  • Was the offender's crime written on the cangue?
    No page we read says so; the photographed cangue carries the magistrate's seal.
