Communal wells and the samurai exception

Sources: kanda-josui-jawiki; the shared alley well itself is quoted at the note below, nagaya-jawiki; the households-per-well ratio was searched for and not found, and says so at the sentence that makes it; jta-aoyagi-kakunodate and semboku-bukeyashiki (the samurai house's own well)

Commoner machi shared public wells in the alleys behind the street-front, which the map draws in the block interiors, roughly 1 per ~10-20 households (this project's own reading; no source for a ratio was found) - enough that no quarter lacks water, not one on every corner. The samurai/government quarter shows NO public wells: a samurai household drew from a well inside its own fence - two of Kakunodate's surviving samurai houses show one, the Aoyagi house's roofed and standing inside its gate and the Iwahashi house's a sweep well (more in Does a samurai's house have its own well?) - though in Edo the samurai districts were served FIRST by the same municipal aqueduct, so the distinction is of priority as much as of privacy - and a communal well there would read wrong.

The well among the houses is one of two forms a shared well took; the other stood at the roadside, and how a village chooses between them is Where does a village's communal well stand - by the lane, or among the houses it serves?

The rule the map follows: a city's commoner warren is sprinkled with public wells, one within a short walk of any home: no commoner dwelling inside the walls stands more than about 870 ft from a wellhead, and no single well is the nearest for more than 26 households. Servants are interleaved with the commoners and share their wells; a transient gate market outside the wall is not housing and is not counted; the samurai and government quarter draws no public wells at all, since those households drew from private wells inside their own fenced lots. A wellhead sits in a block INTERIOR, clear of the lanes and off every building and compound, and the houses are packed around it afterward so it ends up enclosed. It is drawn as a location marker rather than at true size, and the marker scales with the map's scale: mean well diameter against the median dwelling's larger dimension lands between 0.35 and 0.85 at every tier - a well is naturally smaller than a house, but not a dot. Each well reserves only a tight courtyard, so a city's worth of wells does not visibly deflate its population; they do displace some housing, so each caste's share of the population is checked again after they go in. A city of about 600 households runs on the order of 47 wells, drawn literally rather than symbolically. The 26 is a calibration and not a finding: coverage alone can look fine on average while the busiest wells in a dense warren are swamped, and assigning each household to its nearest well over-counts where two wells stand nearly equidistant, so the ceiling sits a little above the ten to twenty households this project's own reading suggests. The reach and the size band are calibrations against the drawn cities.
