Charcoal yards: a tallied depot, a cooling ground, and a weighing floor

Sources: wagner-ming-iron, fao-charcoal-safety, tonya-enwiki, fires-in-edo-enwiki (the inline list below); economy-song-enwiki (Hartwell's 125,000 t by 1078; state charcoal supplies to iron households)

China first. Charcoal was an industrial input at state scale. Song iron-smelting households were government-regulated, with state support that explicitly included charcoal supplies, and a large late-Ming iron enterprise is recorded with more than 200 furnace tenders, 300 miners, and 200 water-carriers and charcoal producers together, moved by 200 oxen and 50 river vessels - so the fuel trade is counted in the hundreds at an enterprise of that size, though that figure is shared with the water-carriers and cannot be set against the furnace workforce as an equal. The authority reads the passage as describing a firm that operated numerous ironworks scattered over a large area, rather than the single works his own source calls it. Northern Song output reached roughly 125,000 tons of iron a year by 1078, about six times the ~800 CE level, and it ran on charcoal until it had deforested large parts of north China, after which coal largely displaced charcoal by the end of the 11th century. The decision this drove: a charcoal store is a supervised, tallied commodity depot, not a shop's back room - which is exactly the relationship the county magistracy's own plan already draws ("charcoal and bar iron, sealed here, never owned").

Japan corroborating. The ton'ya / toiya was the wholesaler-warehouseman of the Edo economy, and fire-resistant stores were built with thick plastered earthen walls precisely because urban timber burned. Edo's serious fires cluster in the dry, windy months from January into April, March worst of all - the charcoal-brazier season.

Why the yard has an open cooling apron. Freshly produced charcoal absorbs oxygen from the air fast enough to heat itself to ignition; tightly packed masses of fines are the worst case, and high-volatile charcoal is the most prone to self-ignition (the temperature at which it does so is on no page read). The documented handling rule is to stand freshly discharged charcoal in the open, separate from previously cooled and conditioned stock, for at least 24 hours; charcoal exposed to air for eight days is no longer considered hazardous. Water-spraying does not reliably prevent it. So the yard draws an open cooling apron set apart from the covered sheds, not merely sheds plus a weighing floor. That is the difference between a generic warehouse glyph and a charcoal yard.

The finding that justified a feature that would otherwise look like decoration. The charcoal hyō / tawara had no standard weight in the traditional Japanese system, unlike rice (a kan is 3.75 kg, but the charcoal bale itself was indeterminate). A commodity with no standard bale cannot be traded by count - it must be weighed at the point of sale. That is the weighing floor, and it is independently why the magistracy's hold on the trade is documentary rather than proprietary.

What determines it in reality: the commodity's physics and its measure - dryness (value), self-heating (separation and a cooling ground), and the absent standard bale (a weighing floor and a sealed tally). Not town size, and not wealth.

The 30 ft fire gap, derived. Reasoned against this project's existing separations rather than invented: ~6 ft for an attended forge against hay, 120 ft for putrefaction carried on air. The charcoal hazard is an unattended ignition in a large fuel mass, so it belongs between them, and 30 ft is about one flame-height clear of a fully-involved 10-12 ft stack - this page's own rule of thumb for radiant ignition of adjacent timber (the published safety-zone rule, for people rather than timber, is four flame heights). Rejected: reusing the 120 ft nuisance figure, which defends against smell over distance and would push the yard off the cart route it exists to serve.
