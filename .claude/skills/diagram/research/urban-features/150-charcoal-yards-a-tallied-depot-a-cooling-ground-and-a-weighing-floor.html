Charcoal yards: a tallied depot, a cooling ground, and a weighing floor

Sources: wagner-ming-iron, fao-charcoal-safety (today's handling guidance), jifpro-seitan (where charcoal is cooled), mokutan-jawiki (the closed kiln's date), to-unit-jawiki, tonya-enwiki, fires-in-edo-enwiki (the inline list below); economy-song-enwiki (Hartwell's 125,000 t by 1078; state charcoal supplies to iron households); tawara-unit-jawiki (the bale's want of a standard size, and charcoal packed by grade)

China first. Charcoal was an industrial input at state scale. Song iron-smelting households (at Hancheng, in the case recorded) were government-regulated, and 200 of the 700 had state support that explicitly included charcoal supplies, and a large late-Ming iron enterprise is recorded with more than 200 furnace tenders, 300 miners, and 200 water-carriers and charcoal producers together, moved by 200 oxen and 50 river vessels - so the fuel trade is counted in the hundreds at an enterprise of that size, though that figure is shared with the water-carriers and cannot be set against the furnace workforce as an equal. The authority reads the passage as describing a firm that operated numerous ironworks scattered over a large area, rather than the single works his own source calls it. Northern Song output reached roughly 125,000 tons of iron a year by 1078 on Robert Hartwell's estimate (which Donald Wagner questions), per capita output having risen about sixfold since 806, and it ran on charcoal until it had deforested large parts of north China, after which coke from bituminous coal was found to replace charcoal by the end of the 11th century. The decision this drove: a charcoal store is a supervised, tallied commodity depot, not a shop's back room - which is exactly the relationship the county magistracy's own plan already draws ("charcoal and bar iron, sealed here, never owned").

Japan corroborating. The ton'ya / toiya was the wholesaler-warehouseman of the Edo economy, and fire-resistant stores were built with thick plastered earthen walls precisely because urban timber burned. Edo's serious fires cluster in the dry, windy months from January into April, March worst of all - the charcoal-brazier season.

Where the charcoal cooled: at the kiln, not the yard. Freshly produced charcoal absorbs oxygen from the air fast enough to heat itself to ignition; tightly packed masses of fines, and charcoal high in volatiles, catch more readily than lump charcoal. Today's handling guidance is to stand freshly discharged charcoal in the open, separate from previously cooled and conditioned stock, for at least 24 hours. That rule is modern, and no period source puts a cooling ground at a charcoal depot. The older record puts the cooling at the kiln. Black charcoal is put out inside the kiln by closing it until no air enters, and white charcoal is drawn out red-hot and smothered beside the kiln under a damp powder of ash and river sand; black charcoal made by putting the fire out in the kiln, and taking the charcoal out once the kiln has cooled, came in from the late Muromachi into the Edo period. So the charcoal reached a town already cooled (and, in this page's guess, already baled at the kiln), and the maps do not draw a cooling apron at a charcoal yard: the yard is covered sheds and a weighing floor.

The finding that justified a feature that would otherwise look like decoration. The hyō / tawara, the straw bale, had no standard size in the traditional Japanese system - not even for rice, whose bale held anything from 2 to 5 to (a to being a tenth of a koku) by time and place - and charcoal was packed at a weight that depended on its grade, 4 kan for the best and 8 or 10 for the lower grades in one charcoal district. (How big a bale is: "How big is a bale of rice or charcoal?") In this page's reading, a commodity with no standard bale cannot be traded by count - it must be weighed at the point of sale. That is the weighing floor, and it is independently why the magistracy's hold on the trade is documentary rather than proprietary.

What determines it in reality: the commodity's physics and its measure - dryness (value), self-heating (cooled at the kiln before it was baled), and the absent standard bale (a weighing floor and a sealed tally). Not town size, and not wealth.
