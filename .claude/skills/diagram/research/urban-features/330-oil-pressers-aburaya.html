Oil pressers (aburaya)

Before electricity, a household's light came from lamp oil, and lamp oil was precious. The oil presser made it from rapeseed or sesame: the seed was roasted in a shallow pan, milled by hand, by an ox or by a water wheel, and squeezed in a press cut from a hollowed log. A large village kept one presser, who might keep another trade as well.

  • Villages where rapeseed grew kept oil shops.
    In Ishikawa county, whose soil and drainage suited rapeseed, Nonoichi village had households that kept oil shops in the Edo period, for lamp oil was precious before electricity.
  • A large village near Edo kept one oil presser, who also took pledges.
    Hachijo, the largest of the villages that now make up Yashio city and a crossroads of 856 inhabitants, had one household in 1836 entered for oil pressing and pawnbroking.
  • No page we read counts the oil pressers of a city.
    Nor does one give a press building's size or say where in a town it stood.

The Chinese technical encyclopedia Tiangong kaiwu describes the presser's tools.

  • The press was a hollowed log, a large one an arm-span round.
    Camphor was the best wood for it.
  • Where large trees were scarce, four logs were bound into one press.
    North of the Yangzi, where trees an arm-span round were few, four logs were joined and bound with iron hoops.
  • The press's slot was some 3 to 4 chi long.
    It was about 3 to 4 cun wide.
  • The seed was roasted first, in a flat pan only 6 cun deep.
    The encyclopedia gives the pan for roasting both sesame and rapeseed.
  • The plainest mill was a trough sunk in the ground, worked by two men.
    An iron wheel on a wooden pole ran in the trough, and two men facing each other lifted and pushed it.
  • A presser with capital built a stone mill turned by an ox.
    This edge-runner mill let one ox do the work of ten men.
  • In Japan the seed was also ground by a water wheel.
    A caption quoting the oil-making manual of 1836, the Seiyuroku, says rapeseed was ground by a water wheel and the powder pressed.
  • Where did each mill stand?
    The water-wheel mill needs a stream, and the ox mill a circular track for its stone. That the hand mill stood indoors, and that the ox had a stall beside its track, is a GUESS: the Tiangong kaiwu says neither.
