Which trades outgrow the shop glyph at all, and the rule every provincial city's works follow - the farrier's among them - is How our maps draw shops, and the trades that outgrow the shop glyph. This question is the farrier alone: why a Rokugani town shoes horses, what that puts on the map, and how often.

  • FARRIERY - why this setting has farriers where Edo Japan did not (GM 2026-07-25). Edo Japan all but never shod its horses: it used woven straw horse-sandals (umagutsu, also uma-waraji), and metal shoeing became systematic only with Meiji. Japan knew the iron shoe - the shogun Yoshimune imported Western horses through the Dutch trading house in 1729 and worked to bring in Western shoeing - and hardly used it before the end of the Edo period. China did use it, long before modern times: iron shoes became common there under the Yuan dynasty, with rattan and leather shoes before, and in Qing Beijing shoeing was a trade of its own, done by men who made the shoes and nailed them on (its shops date from the mid-Qing, as the gazetteer quoted below says). So the farrier is not a modern form read back into the setting; what the setting departs from is Japan. Straw is right for Japan and wrong for this Rokugan, for two reasons the GM's own notes already carried. (1) Iron. Japan's constraint was material, not ignorance - iron sand (satetsu) smelted with charcoal is a low-yield, fuel-hungry process, so iron went to blades, tools and daily wares long before hooves. Rokugan is continental, with real ore bodies and whole iron districts already in the setting's canon (Tatarano, "bellows plain"; Kuroiwa; Ubame's charcoal-sited furnaces; the Reiji hill works; the iron mines of the Crab Clan, the clan that holds the Kaiu Wall against the Shadowlands). Ore is abundant and iron is a normal industrial good, while good STEEL stays scarce and expensive - which buys horseshoes, plain low-carbon iron, without deflating a good blade. (2) Institutional mileage, more than paving. Hard surfaces likely wear hoof down faster (a guess, not searched for this page), but the sharper driver is annual miles per horse: the setting's own budget notes give ~5,000 waystations on ~50,000 miles of Imperial road at ~10-mile spacing - about 3x the station count of the Tang yi network (1,639 stations; the page's thirty-li spacing is given for the Han, so the spacing was likely much the same) - plus 500-1,000 mounted couriers and mounted anti-banditry patrols. A courier horse working a fixed stage all year is on the road every day, and straw sandals are cheap consumables that wore out fast. Straw scales for a farmer walking an ox to the next village; it does not scale for institutionalized relay. Canon already assumed this: the setting's own budget notes staff busy trunk-route waystations with "a smith for shoeing horses and weapon maintenance," and the setting's canon introduces Moto Khuyag as a farrier who offers to reshoe the player characters' horses - a Rokugani-born one, who learned the trade at Kyuden Shinjo. The Chinese shoe is the setting's precedent, not its reason: it came late, from the Yuan on, and a strap-on shoe was older still in Europe, where the Roman Empire's northwestern provinces knew the hipposandal. One argument was considered and deliberately NOT relied on: "the Mongol Yam proves it" (steppe horses on grass were, we believe, mostly ridden UNSHOD, which would make it a counterexample - a guess, not searched for this page). The ruling stands on iron price and relay mileage.
  • What it changes on the map, and what it does not. Traditionally a farrier worked from a forge with a yard and a smith from a small shop, and the big "farrier-forge-with-stable-yard" compound does not earn its footprint at city scale (our drafting judgment). So an ordinary town smith (kaji-ya) who also shoes still draws as a generic shop rowhouse - shoeing changes his REPERTOIRE, not his premises - and no new establishment joins the shop rows. What earns real ground is only the place horses CONCENTRATE: the gate caravan yard and the Imperial-road relay town, where the map draws a dedicated shed and apron. Qing Beijing's shoeing shops stood the same way, mostly beside the horse inns and the horse doctors, most with a frame before the door to tie the mules and horses to. Two rules fall out of the physical facts. It is not attached to the stables: an open forge against a hay-and-timber stall range is a fire the yard does not survive, so real yards kept the smithy across the ground (anchored on the buildings research's ~6-8 ft fire gap between wooden service buildings; the measure runs from the whole footprint, shed plus apron, which is deliberately conservative since the shed sits at the apron's far end). And the apron carries an ox-shoeing frame: Rokugan's draft animal is mostly the ox, and oxen were shod in China too - a traveler up the Yangtze in 1861 found horses and cattle shod with iron shoes and nails in the north. A cloven hoof takes two plates per foot (a guess from the hoof's form; the page cited below shows ox shoes only in a picture caption), and in the West an ox was shod slung in a frame, a village fixture from Spain to England - about 7 x 4 ft (our dimension), drawn true-size like everything else. The Chinese frame read is plainer, a post frame to tie the animal to, and no page read shows an East Asian frame that slung an ox before modern times, so the sling is the Western form here. Sizes are the historical anchors, not enlarged to read better on the map: an 18-20 ft village smithy for the shed, and an apron a horse's length (~8 ft) plus room to lead one in and turn it. Straw survives everywhere iron is not worth it - peasant pack horses, oxen on soft paddy tracks, poor and mountain provinces - because it is free, not because iron is unknown; that is setting texture rather than a drawn feature, and it lives in the setting's own budget notes.
  • Frequency by scale, and the reasoning behind it. Rokugan's horse population is LOW relative to Europe - peasants work oxen, and horses are a samurai and Imperial animal - so farriery is not a mass trade and the count must not scale with population the way the bathhouse does. Provincial city: exactly one, at a gate caravan yard (not one per gate). Town: only on an Imperial road, which is why the rule keys on the Imperial road rather than on town scale alone - at ~10-mile waystation spacing plenty of towns simply are not on the network, and that non-uniformity is the point. Village and hamlet: never; a village smith, where a village keeps one, trims hooves and tacks on straw from his own small workshop, which is a question of its own, and builds no shoeing shed. A county magistracy keeps only 2-4 horses per the buildings research, so it keeps no permanent forge either - the smith walks over from town.
