Why does this setting shoe its horses where Edo Japan did not, and what does a farrier draw on the map?

Sources: teitetsu-jawiki, tatara-enwiki, tatara-seitetsu-jawiki, yizhan-zhwiki, hoofcare-japanese-hoof-sandals, horseshoe-enwiki, hipposandal-enwiki, farrier-enwiki, blacksmith-enwiki, cattle-crush-enwiki

Which trades outgrow the shop glyph at all, and the rule every provincial city's works follow - the farrier's among them - is Trade works - which trades outgrow the shop glyph, and why. This question is the farrier alone: why a Rokugani town shoes horses, what that puts on the map, and how often.

  • FARRIERY - why this setting has farriers where Edo Japan did not (GM 2026-07-25). Edo Japan did not shoe horses: it used woven straw horse-sandals (umagutsu / umazouri), and metal shoeing became systematic only with Meiji. That is right about Japan and wrong about this Rokugan, for two reasons the GM's own notes already carried. (1) Iron. Japan's constraint was material, not ignorance - iron sand (satetsu) smelted with charcoal is a low-yield, fuel-hungry process, so iron went to blades, tools and daily wares long before hooves. Rokugan is continental, with real ore bodies and whole iron districts already in the setting's canon (Tatarano, "bellows plain"; Kuroiwa; Ubame's charcoal-sited furnaces; the Reiji hill works; Crab iron for the Wall). Ore is abundant and iron is a normal industrial good, while good STEEL stays scarce and expensive - which buys horseshoes, plain low-carbon iron, without deflating a good blade. (2) Institutional mileage, more than paving. Hard surfaces do abrade hoof, but the sharper driver is annual miles per horse: the setting's own budget notes give ~5,000 waystations on ~50,000 miles of Imperial road at ~10-mile spacing - about 3x the station count of the Tang yi network (1,639 stations; the page's thirty-li spacing is given for the Han, so the spacing was likely much the same) - plus 500-1,000 mounted couriers and mounted anti-banditry patrols. A courier horse working a fixed stage all year is on the road every day, and straw sandals are cheap consumables that wore out fast. Straw scales for a farmer walking an ox to the next village; it does not scale for institutionalized relay. Canon already assumed this: the setting's own budget notes staff busy trunk-route waystations with "a smith for shoeing horses and weapon maintenance," and the setting's canon introduces Moto Khuyag as a farrier who offers to reshoe the PCs' horses - a Rokugani-born one, who learned the trade at Kyuden Shinjo. Two arguments were considered and deliberately NOT relied on, because they do not survive checking: "later Chinese dynasties nailed iron shoes" (Chinese hoof practice ran mostly to trimming and strap-on or woven boots; iron shoes became common in China only from the Yuan, with rattan and leather before that, and Rome's roads knew the strap-on hipposandal), and "the Mongol Yam proves it" (steppe horses on grass were famously ridden UNSHOD, which is a counterexample). The conclusion stands on iron price and relay mileage alone.
  • What it changes on the map, and what it does not. Even in Europe a farrier was a one-forge smithy with a bit of yard, and the big "farrier-forge-with-stable-yard" compound is a coaching-inn image that does not earn its footprint at city scale. So an ordinary town smith (kaji-ya) who also shoes still draws as a generic shop rowhouse - shoeing changes his REPERTOIRE, not his premises - and no new establishment joins the shop rows. What earns real ground is only the place horses CONCENTRATE: the gate caravan yard and the Imperial-road relay town, where the map draws a dedicated shed and apron. Two rules fall out of the physical facts. It is not attached to the stables: an open forge against a hay-and-timber stall range is a fire the yard does not survive, so real yards kept the smithy across the ground (anchored on the buildings research's ~6-8 ft wooden-service fire gap; the measure runs from the whole footprint, shed plus apron, which is deliberately conservative since the shed sits at the apron's far end). And the apron carries an ox-shoeing frame: Rokugan's draft animal is mostly the ox, a cloven hoof takes two plates per foot (this page's reading of the form), and an ox is shod slung in a timber frame - a Western village fixture, from Spain to England, transplanted here where the record's own hoof practice was straw - about 7 x 4 ft (our dimension), drawn true-size like everything else. Sizes are the historical anchors with no legibility inflation: an 18-20 ft village smithy for the shed, and an apron a horse's length (~8 ft) plus room to lead one in and turn it. Straw survives everywhere iron is not worth it - peasant pack horses, oxen on soft paddy tracks, poor and mountain provinces - because it is free, not because iron is unknown; that is setting texture rather than a drawn feature, and it lives in the setting's own budget notes.
  • Frequency by scale, and the reasoning behind it. Rokugan's horse population is LOW relative to Europe - peasants work oxen, and horses are a samurai and Imperial animal - so farriery is not a mass trade and the count must not scale with population the way the bathhouse does. Provincial city: exactly one, at a gate caravan yard (not one per gate). Town: only on an Imperial road, which is why the rule keys on the Imperial road rather than on town scale alone - at ~10-mile waystation spacing plenty of towns simply are not on the network, and that non-uniformity is the point. Village and hamlet: never; a village smith, where a village keeps one, trims hooves and tacks on straw from his own small workshop, which is a question of its own, and builds no shoeing shed. A county magistracy keeps only 2-4 horses per the buildings research, so it keeps no permanent forge either - the smith walks over from town.
