Charcoal yards and charcoal stores

A charcoal yard was a town's stock of fuel charcoal, brought in from the kilns in straw bales, kept dry and sold on. In China charcoal was an industrial fuel moved at state scale: the Song state supplied it to iron-smelting households, and north China's ironworks burned so much of it that they deforested large parts of the north. In Edo-period Japan it was the stock of a ton'ya, the wholesaler who was also a warehouse-keeper.

Charcoal is a fire risk: fresh charcoal can heat itself until it catches, and in a town of timber houses heated by charcoal, Edo's great fires came in the dry, windy months of winter and early spring. Before modern times it was cooled at the kiln, not at the depot. Its straw bale had no standard size, and the scale for heavy everyday goods, in China and in Japan, was the steelyard.

  • The Song state supplied charcoal to iron-smelting households.
    The magistrate Bao Qingtian (999-1062) wrote of 700 iron-smelting households at Hancheng, on the Yellow River, overseen by government regulators; 200 of them had the most state support, such as charcoal supplies and skilled craftsmen.
  • How much iron did Song China's charcoal smelt?
    About 125,000 metric tons (~138,000 US tons) a year by 1078, on the historian Robert Hartwell's estimate, per capita output having risen about sixfold since 806; the historian Donald Wagner questions how Hartwell reached the figure.
  • Charcoal smelting deforested north China until coke replaced it.
    The waterwheel-driven bellows of the smelters burned so much charcoal that large parts of north China were deforested, and by the end of the 11th century coke from bituminous coal was found to replace it.
  • How many workers did the charcoal trade take at a large ironworks?
    It cannot be told apart. A late-Ming account of Guangdong gives an ironworks more than 200 furnace tenders, 300 miners, and 200 water-carriers and charcoal producers together, moved by 200 oxen and 50 river vessels, so the fuel workers are counted with the water-carriers. The historian who reports the passage reads it as a large firm running numerous ironworks over a wide area, not the single works its source calls it.
  • China's traditional market scale was the steelyard.
    The steelyard (gancheng), also called the hanging scale, was used above all in the markets. It is a wooden or bamboo beam with a large hook at one end and a weight hung at the other: the load hangs on the hook, and the weight is slid along the beam until the two balance.
  • Chinese steelyards were built for heavy loads.
    A wooden steelyard more than 200 years old, collected from Shaoxing, is 2.2 m (~7 ft) long and weighs up to 175 kg (~386 lb) against a stone weight.

In Japan the same fuel was a wholesaler's stock in towns built of timber, and what the record shows is how it was cooled, packed, weighed and kept from fire.

  • Japan's wholesalers, the ton'ya, were also its warehouse managers.
    The ton'ya were trade brokers - wholesalers, warehouse managers and shipment managers - and came to serve a crucial role in the economy of the Edo period (1603-1867).
  • Edo's great fires came in the dry months of winter and early spring.
    Counted by month, March saw the most great fires, then February, April and January, together 70% of them, in dry weather under the northwest and north winds, among wooden townhouses heated by charcoal-burning fireplaces.
  • Fresh charcoal can set itself alight.
    Freshly made charcoal absorbs oxygen from the air fast enough to heat a stockpile until it burns, and tightly packed fines, and charcoal high in volatiles, catch more readily than lump charcoal.
  • Today's handling guidance cools fresh charcoal in the open for a day.
    It is to stand freshly discharged charcoal in the open, apart from charcoal already cooled and conditioned, for at least 24 hours. The rule is modern: no period source we read puts a cooling ground at a charcoal depot.
  • Before modern times, charcoal was cooled at the kiln.
    Black charcoal is put out inside the kiln by closing its mouth and smoke outlet until no air enters; white charcoal is drawn out red-hot and smothered beside the kiln under a damp powder of ash and river sand. Black charcoal made by putting the fire out in the kiln, and taking the charcoal out once the kiln had cooled, came in from the late Muromachi into the Edo period, though the naming of white and black charcoal as two kinds is said to be modern. The white-charcoal method is said to have taken its settled form in the Heian period.
  • Was charcoal baled at the kiln?
    Probably, a GUESS: cooled at the kiln, it would be packed there for the journey, but no page we read says where or when in the Edo period charcoal was baled.
  • A straw bale had no standard size.
    Not even for rice: from the Sengoku to the Edo period a bale of rice held anything from 2 to 5 to by time and place (a to being a tenth of a koku) - 3 to 5 sho by the shogunate's measure, 5 to in Kaga domain - and the bale itself came in many sizes.   • Charcoal bales were packed to a weight set by the charcoal's grade.
    In the Iburi district of Hokkaido the best charcoal was packed at 4 kan (about 15 kg, ~33 lb) to the bale, the next grade at 10 kan (37.5 kg, ~83 lb) and a lower grade at 8 kan (30 kg, ~66 lb), rolled in straw matting. The article gives no date for it.
  • Was charcoal weighed when it was sold?
    In this project's reading, yes: a stock packed to no standard bale cannot be traded by counting its bales, so it must be weighed at the point of sale. No page we read says a charcoal dealer weighed the bales at sale.
  • One account has the steelyard weighing everyday goods, and the two-pan balance valuables.
    An Osaka prefectural history of measurement says the balance was used for valuables and gems, and the steelyard for food and daily goods, naming no country or period, and that the steelyard was, by its structure, easy to cheat with. Its account of the steelyard's invention in the ancient world says the balance is precise, but needs many weights, which make it heavy and awkward to carry.
  • Japan used steelyards from the Edo period, some for loads of 16 kan.
    Steelyards were in use from the Edo period to the mid-20th century, in sizes that included one for 5 kan and one for 16 kan - four of the best Iburi charcoal bales.
  • The platform scale that weighed rice bales came only after 1868, in the Meiji period.
    It was in use from the Meiji period to the mid-20th century.
  • After its great fire of 1641, Edo gathered its timber dealers in one place.
    After the great Edo fire of 1641, in the Kan'ei era, whose spread was blamed on the high stacks of timber dealers scattered through the town, the dealers were gathered onto Eitaijima.
  • Was a fire gap kept round a charcoal store?
    No premodern figure for one was found; the only rule found on a stock of fuel is Edo's removal of its timber dealers.
  • The plastered storehouse was built to keep its goods from fire.
    Storehouses (kura) were named for what they held - rice, unhulled rice, miso, gunpowder, money, sutras - and were for protecting it against fire, damp and theft. Their walls were often 300 mm (~12 in) thick or more, and their outer doors might be faced with earth and plaster; one kind of door, the Osaka-do, was a sliding door shut in a fire. Most were tile-roofed, and their thick walls made them ideal for storing commodities, household belongings, tools and valuables.
  • Edo spread the plastered construction by order after the Kyoho era (1716-36).
    The plastered storehouse is one of Japanese architecture's fire-resistant constructions. In Edo the same construction, used for townhouses, was at first restrained as a luxury, then spread by order for fire prevention after the Kyoho era.
  • Was charcoal kept in a plastered storehouse?
    No page we read says so, or gives the spacing kept between a storehouse and its neighbors. That it was is a GUESS resting on the storehouse's recorded purpose: it kept gunpowder behind its wall.
