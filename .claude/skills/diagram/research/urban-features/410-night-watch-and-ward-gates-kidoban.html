Night watch and ward gates (kidoban)

In the Edo period a Japanese town shut its streets at night. A ward gate, the kido, stood across the street at each end of a ward, and from ten at night to six in the morning it was closed, the only way through a small side door under its keeper's eye. The keeper, the kidoban, lived in a hut beside the gate about 6 by 9 ft, and at the ward's other end stood its watch house, the jishinban.

The watchmen were hired, and outside Edo many of them were hinin, of the lowest status. Edo alone kept nearly a thousand watch houses in 1850, while a village might hire a single watchman of its own.

  • Many castle towns shut ward gates across their streets at night.
    Edo and many other castle towns set up ward gates, which were to be shut at night, each with two keepers, mostly old men who lived in the watch hut. In 1658 Edo told any ward without a gate to put up a temporary one.
  • A large town set a gate to every block, a middle or small town one to several blocks.
    So the same encyclopedia entry says, of the ward gates in general.
  • An Edo ward gate was a pair of doors between posts about 12 ft apart.
    The posts stood about 2 ken apart, with a fence or a board wall running from each post to the edge of the road, and the keeper's hut beside it.
  • The keeper passed travelers on and walked the night round against fire.
    With the gates at both ends of the ward shut, he sent anyone who passed on to the next ward with a signal of wooden clappers, and made the night rounds against fire.
  • Kyoto's wards had set gates at both ends since the Muromachi period.
    From the Muromachi period Kyoto's wards set gates, called kuginuki, facing the crossings at both ends of the ward. In the Edo period a board-floored hut about one tatami in size stood beside each, kept by two watchmen at night and one by day, whom the ward hired from lower-status people.
  • A post town had a gate at each end, in most cases shut at night.
    The gates stood near the post town's mitsuke, the ground between them being the post town itself, and sometimes a lamp was kept burning all night beside them.
  • A post town's mitsuke was a pair of earthen banks at its entrance.
    An earthen bank was built on either side of the entrance, with a fence along its top.

Two small buildings stood at a ward's gates: the keeper's hut and the ward's watch house.

  • The gate keeper's hut was fixed at about 6 by 9 ft.
    It was set at 6 by 9 shaku, with eaves 1 jo (about 10 ft) high.
  • How big was a ward's watch house?
    Nominally 9 shaku by 2 ken, about 9 by 12 ft, but in practice some 2 to 3 ken, about 12 to 18 ft. The source does not say which side the 2 to 3 ken measures.
  • The keeper's hut and the watch house stood at the two ends of a ward's main street.
    Both were built against the gates at the two ends of each ward's main street, the gate keeper at one and the watch at the other, and the watch house usually stood at the south corner of a crossing on the main east-west street.
  • In Osaka the watch house was part of a row of townhouses.
    It was not a building of its own, as in Edo, but was built in line with the ordinary townhouses.

The men who kept the watch were hired by the town or village they served.

  • Towns and villages hired watchmen, the banta, many of them hinin.
    The banta kept the night watch and watched for fire and over the sluice gates. In Edo he was a commoner, but elsewhere many were hinin and were called ban-hinin.
  • In some villages the watchman also guarded the hills, fields and sluices.
    He ran errands, kept the night watch and controlled vagrants as well.
  • In some regions the watchman also dug graves and dealt with the dead.
    Many there were of hinin status, and they dug graves, cremated and dealt with those who died on the road.
  • Later in the period, villages invited outcastes in to be their guards.
    In Nagano, the Matsumoto, Matsushiro and Ueda domains moved outcastes into their castle towns early in the period, and in the middle and later periods a considerable number were invited by villages, mainly as village guards.
  • A provincial castle town kept its own guard boxes and night rounds.
    In Aizu-Wakamatsu the town kept guard boxes, the tsujiban, about two men to each. Twice a night, at midnight and at two in the morning, they went round their group of blocks calling "take care", asked any wanderer where they lived and passed them on to the next box. A group of several blocks paid for the men, the lamp oil and the hut.

A city kept its watch in the same two forms, a watch house in the townsmen's wards and a guard box in the samurai quarter, at a far larger count.   • Edo kept one watch house to a ward, 994 of them in the shogunate's last years.
    They were set up ward by ward in the Kyoho era (1716-1736), two or three small wards sharing one, and in 1850 there were reported to be 994.
  • The samurai quarter kept its own guard boxes, the tsujiban.
    They were set up mainly in castle towns, and in Kyoto as well as Edo. In Edo the samurai houses' boxes numbered 936 in the Genbun era (1736-1741).
  • An Edo guard box was about 12 by 9 ft, cut into a mansion's wall.
    It was normally 2 ken wide by 9 shaku deep, about 3.6 m (~12 ft) by 2.7 m (~9 ft), with four or five men on watch day and night.
  • How big was a guard box with its lean-to?
    One domain's historian gives the usual plan as 2 ken by 9 shaku, six mats, with a lean-to 2 ken by 1 ken beside it, about ten mats in all and tile-roofed. Other domains' boxes came to about 5 tsubo (~178 sq ft), against the Muramatsu domain's 13.5 (~480 sq ft) - the author's reading of a survey map of 1883-84.
  • How many watch houses and guard boxes did a provincial city keep?
    No page we read counts them. Aizu-Wakamatsu's guard boxes are described, but not counted.

A village kept its watch more simply, with one hired man.

  • A village's watchman sat in a watch hut and was paid an allowance.
    He normally sat in a watchman's hut, and a watchman's allowance was paid.
  • Where did a village's watch hut stand?
    The one placement found is from a work of 1721: in the villages, under the name of the advance hawk watch, a watch hut was built at the village's entrance. That was a watch named for the hawks, not the ordinary village watchman, so it does not show that every village watchman's hut stood at the entrance.
  • How many villages kept a watchman, and how big was his hut?
    No page we read says.
