Does a village keep a fire bell, a fire ladder or a fire pond?

Sources: hansho-kotobank (the alarm bell, the board before it, the ladder before the tower), hinomi-yagura-kotobank (Edo's towers and watch-house ladders), kotobank-hinoban (who fought fire in a village), bousaihaku-hinomi (when the standing towers were built)

The bell came with the fire brigades. The hansho, the small bronze alarm bell, was struck for fire, disaster or thieves. Before it, a thick wooden board, the bangi, was struck, and it also called people to meetings; the bell spread in the Edo period as fire brigades were organized. It was hung first on a ladder and later on a built tower, and many had a hut beside them for the pump. In Edo in the Kyoho era one fire-watch tower stood to about every ten wards, and a ward without one set a fire ladder on top of its watch house. That is the town's apparatus; the town question gives it to the walled seat.

The village. In the villages the fire was fought, until modern times, by the young men's association and the five-household groups. Most of the fire-watch towers standing today are iron ones built between 1945 and 1974. No page read puts a bell on a ladder, a watch hut or a fire pond in an Edo-period village. A village with a temple has the temple's bell tower (see what stands in its precinct); that it was rung for fire is a guess.

What it means for the map. A village draws no fire ladder, fire tower or fire pond: the bell on a ladder belongs to the town with a fire brigade, and the towers standing in the countryside now are modern. A village's firefighting is its young men's association and its five-household groups, which is nothing to draw. The pond is a guess either way, and this record leaves it undrawn rather than invent it.

The rule the map follows: no village or hamlet draws a fire-watch tower, a fire ladder or a fire-water pond; the fire bell and its ladder or tower are a town's (a finding for the town, a guess for the village, where nothing was found either way).
