The notice board (kosatsuba) - siting is a TRAFFIC decision

Sources: ogose-kosatsuba, kosatsu-jawiki, adachi-kosatsu (the inline links below), kosatsu-enwiki, shoya-jawiki

Every Edo town AND village kept the official edict board1 - standing shogunal/domain law,2 porter and packhorse rate tables,3 currency regulations,102 ban edicts - and its siting was a TRAFFIC decision, not an administrative one: busy street corners, checkpoints, bridgeheads, the gate or center of the settlement,4 in a prominent position along the main way.103 Edo's Nihonbashi board, the first the page names among the city's six great boards, stood at the bridgehead,5 post towns' at a key point of the station (Ogose's stood at its southern entrance) - not at the government office.104

The ofuregaki circulars reached the population through exactly this board (the most important of them were posted on it),105 posted where the settlement's one lane carries everyone - by the headman's frontage or the lane junction/entrance,6 since the headman (or, in this page's reading, a hamlet's senior farmer answering to the village headman) received, copied, and relayed the circulars and was REQUIRED to be functionally literate for the job.106 One reader per settlement makes the board work - notices were also read aloud by officials107 - so peasant literacy is no objection even at a 50-inhabitant hamlet; the board is also the state's standing presence in a place that may see a magistrate's deputy twice a year.203

  • The bakufu set kosatsuba at points of heavy passage: barriers and ports, the foot of large bridges, and the entrances and centers of towns and villages;75 in farming villages at the village center or entrance (the shrine precinct and the assembly place are this page's ranking of the likely spots);108 also at bridgeheads and before the gate of the village officials' houses (Fuchu kosatsuba, Tokyo Jinjacho; Ogose town cultural property H26-04; 高札 - Wikipedia (ja); Adachi museum, 高札). Every site the pages read attest is ON the way - a verge, a gate front, a bridge foot - never a plot of open ground beside it. Decision: the board stands 6 ft from tread edge to board edge at the hamlet and village tiers; the 60 ft band stays only as the fallback when no roadside seat fits, and for towns and cities. The 60 ft figure is not a finding.

Every board is sited by the map's own probe, never hand-picked (GM 2026-07-27: "fix both of them"). The reason is that a hand-picked seat loses the traffic the board exists for: on the two town maps the probe finds seats standing on 19 and 29 structures within 250 ft, against 3 and 10 for the seats a hand would choose.

The caption is part of the seat, or the board walks out of town (GM 2026-07-27, from "the notice board in Ubame does not look well-placed; I'd expect it to be in a higher-traffic area"). The drawn marker is small and fits almost anywhere; its caption does not, and the busiest frontage is exactly where there is least room for one. A siter that hunts for ground large enough to hold both therefore walks away from the traffic by construction, and a seat across the bridge from the town, 120 ft past the last shop, scores 8 structures within 250 ft against 23 on the high street. The stretch of road beyond the last house is still a road. So the probe scores the caption instead of hunting for ground: a seat whose caption fits outranks any seat whose caption does not, and among those the busiest wins; a board with nowhere to put a caption is still placed. Whether a feature stands on the traffic is a judgment call: there is no mathematical rule for "lots of foot traffic".

The board is the last thing placed, and it clears no ground at all (GM 2026-08-29). It is placed after every other feature on the map, the crop and the title included, and the reasoning is about the settlement rather than about the drawing: "where you put the notice board on the map does depend on what other features already exist ... the real humans that live in the society that decide where the notice board will go will look around at the things which already exist and then decide where to put the notice board. They may even decide to move a notice board which has already been placed." Every other feature either reserves ground or grows into it; the board does neither, so it is the one thing that should see the whole map before it chooses. Two consequences. The board clears no ground at all; the GM's ruling: "it would be very easy to put the notice board at the edge of the forest. We could even display it as being underneath the canopy because I think in many cases it would be ... humans would not need to clear any amount of space in order to put up a notice board at the side of a path."

A board under a canopy is fine, and its label is why (GM 2026-08-29): "As long as there is a label attached to it and the label is visible - which it should be, because labels are always on the topmost layer of the way the image is rendered - then someone will always be able to see it, even on the PNG version of a map. The fact that the label is present tells someone looking at the map where the notice board is." A crown cannot paint over a label: on all five scripted hamlets the caption is drawn above every tree crown. So "is the board findable under a tree" is settled - the label carries the read, the same convention that lets an oversized location marker stand for a board whose true frame (about 12 ft, in this page's reading) is too small to draw at city grain.195

The board stands on a main way (GM 2026-08-02, a probe hunting the busiest clear verge can land on a side lane one block south of the high street, 200 ft off the road the whole town walks: "The notice board should be along a main street/road on every map, never a side street... in order to be more noticed."). Every road is a main way by construction and so is a street the map declares as its gate-to-yamen avenue; where a map declares either, the probe samples only those verges, so a board on a side lane cannot arise. Two scope edges are deliberate. A map with no declared hierarchy - a lane web, a town whose streets are all unflagged - is exempt, because there is nothing "main" to stand on and the busiest-node scoring stands in for the judgment. And the punishment ground is not covered on purpose (GM 2026-08-02: "other map features like punishment grounds don't always need to be along a main road, but a notice board must be"): the ground is a display for locals who already know where justice is done, while the board must ambush the eye of everyone passing through.

The rule the map follows: every settlement carries a board, and it stands on the busiest way its own map has, squared to that way so the 7 by 3 ft face meets the traffic broadside rather than edge-on - within 30 degrees of some route segment in the siting band, which still presents about seven eighths of the face and clears the worst legitimate case in the pool, a board taking a road bend's bearing about 18 degrees off its nearest segment.194 At the hamlet and village tiers the board stands 6 ft from the tread edge; the 60 ft band is the fallback when no roadside seat fits, and the figure towns and cities still use. A CITY draws the SET and labels ONE: the principal board at the central market node plus a board on every main gate's approach, each within about 800 ft of its gate, the label going to whichever board has room for it, since an unlabeled board fits a tight gate verge that a labeled one cannot. The 30 degree tolerance and the 800 ft corridor are calibrations against the drawn maps, not figures from a record; the 60 ft band is not a finding either.
