The notice board (kosatsuba) - siting is a TRAFFIC decision

Sources: ogose-kosatsuba, kosatsu-jawiki, adachi-kosatsu (the inline links below), kosatsu-enwiki

Every Edo town AND village kept the official edict board - standing shogunal/domain law, packhorse rate tables, currency regulations, ban edicts - and its siting was a TRAFFIC decision: busy street corners, checkpoints, bridgeheads, the gate or center of the settlement, in a prominent position along the main way. Edo's Nihonbashi board, the first the page names among the city's six great boards, stood at the bridgehead, post towns' in the station itself (Ogose's stood at its southern entrance), and no page read puts a post town's board at its transport office.

How the board's notices reached a population that mostly could not read - the headman who copied and relayed them, the reading aloud, and why that lets even a 50-inhabitant hamlet carry a board - is Who read the notice board to a village that could not read, and why does even a hamlet carry one?

  • The bakufu set kosatsuba at points of heavy passage: barriers and ports, the foot of large bridges, and the entrances and centers of towns and villages; in farming villages at the village center or entrance (the shrine precinct and the assembly place are this page's ranking of the likely spots); also at bridgeheads and before the gate of the village officials' houses ( Ogose town cultural property H26-04; 高札 (kosatsu) - Wikipedia (ja); Adachi museum, 高札 (kosatsu)). Every site the pages read attest is ON the way - a verge, a gate front, a bridge foot - never a plot of open ground beside it. Decision: the board stands 6 ft from tread edge to board edge at the hamlet and village tiers; the 60 ft band is only the fallback when no roadside seat fits, and for towns and cities. The 60 ft figure is not a finding.

How the map finds that seat on the ground it has drawn - a probe rather than a hand, the caption scored as part of the seat, the board placed last and clearing nothing, a canopy over it, a main way under it - is How does the map choose the notice board's seat, and why may it stand under a tree?

The rule the map follows: every settlement carries a board, and it stands on the busiest way its own map has, squared to that way so the 7 by 3 ft face meets the traffic broadside rather than edge-on - within 30 degrees of some stretch of that way inside the siting band - the 6 ft or 60 ft distance from the way given below - which still presents about seven eighths of the face and clears the worst legitimate case on the drawn maps, a board taking a road bend's bearing about 18 degrees off its nearest segment. At the hamlet and village tiers the board stands 6 ft from the tread edge; the 60 ft band is the fallback when no roadside seat fits, and the figure towns and cities use. A CITY draws the SET and labels ONE: the principal board at the central market node plus a board on every main gate's approach, each within about 800 ft of its gate, the label going to whichever board has room for it, since an unlabeled board fits a tight gate verge that a labeled one cannot. The 30 degree tolerance and the 800 ft corridor are calibrations against the drawn maps, not figures from a record; the 60 ft band is not a finding either.
