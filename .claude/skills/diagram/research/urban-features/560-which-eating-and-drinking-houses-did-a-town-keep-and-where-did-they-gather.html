Which eating and drinking houses did a town keep, and where did they gather?

Sources: kotobank-chaya (the teahouse at the temple gate and on the road), kotobank-mizujaya (the tea stall and where it stood), kotobank-niuriya (the cooked-food shop), taishu-shokudo-jawiki (the eating house in town and on the highway), kotobank-izakaya (the drinking house, and the Chinese wine shop), izakaya-jawiki (the city's drinking houses against the village's), kotobank-ryorijaya (restaurants at a shrine gate and on a canal), dongjing-menghualu-juan2 and dongjing-menghualu-wikisource (the Song capital's teahouses and wine shops)

The kinds. The teahouse began at a temple gate: in the fifteenth century tea sellers gathered before the gates for travelers on the highways and worshippers, the first record of them being the tea sellers at Toji's south gate in 1403. In the Edo period the tea stall served tea and rest to passers-by at the roadside and in temple and shrine precincts; most such stalls were kept by people who lived elsewhere, and some opened only in blossom season. The cooked-food shop sold simple meals with tea or sake, and the simple eating houses of the Edo period stood in the cities and along the highways. The drinking house probably branched off from these by the middle of the Edo period, and took its name from sake drunk at a sake shop's storefront; Edo is said to have had more than 1,800 of them around the 1820s, while the villages did not get them until the later eighteenth century. China had the same run of houses: the wine shop spread with the towns and the market towns of the Song dynasty, and stood in every part of the country under the Yuan, Ming and Qing, marked by a blue flag.

Where they gathered. The same few places recur. The temple gate and precinct: the tea sellers at Toji's gate above; in Edo from the 1750s tea stalls sprang up at the gates and in the precincts of temples and shrines as well as in the busy quarters; Kyoto's restaurants included the two teahouses before the Gion shrine's gate. The road: the travelers' teahouses first opened in the hills between post towns and then moved to the post town's ends. The busy quarter: the travelers' kind of tea stall moved into the gate towns and the busy quarters of the cities, and cooked-food shops beyond counting crowded Edo's Ryogoku quarter alone. The bridge and the water: Osaka's drinking houses set up even on the bridges, and Kyoto's fish restaurants stood along the Takase canal. A great Chinese city was thick with them: in the Song capital, in the lanes and courtyards off Maxing Street, crowded door to door, each had its teahouses and wine shops, and trading households bought their meals from the market shops rather than cooking at home; just past the Zhou bridge, among the houses on the imperial avenue, stood the Zhang family's wine shop.

The glyph. A Song wine shop dressed its door with a decorated gateway of colored silk, and an Edo tea stall was often a reed-screened stall rather than a house; at this scale neither difference is drawn. Every eating or drinking house is the generic shop, a map drawing convention, and it is told by where it stands. How many a county town kept no page read says.

The rule the map follows: a town's teahouses and eating houses are shops in the generic rows, set where the crowd passes: beside a temple's gate (as What kind of shops stand at a temple gate? already places them), at the ends of the road through the town and along it (What lines the Imperial road where it runs through the city?), and at the foot of a bridge. Drinking houses reached the villages only late, so a village map draws none unless a road through the village carries travelers - this project's decision.
