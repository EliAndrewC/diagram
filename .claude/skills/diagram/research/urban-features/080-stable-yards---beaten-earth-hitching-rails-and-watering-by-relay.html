Stable yards - beaten earth, hitching rails, and watering by relay

Sources: qingming-shanghe-tu, caravanserai-enwiki, equine-nutrition-enwiki, water-buffalo-enwiki, mdfcta-enwiki

  • What the research found (China-first, Song/Ming-Qing; Edo Japan secondary; sources incl. the Qingming Shanghe Tu 清明上河図 gate scene, the caravanserai court, the Chinese hitching post 拴马桩, the yizhan 驿站 / shukuba post-station). A city-gate cart/animal yard was beaten tamped earth - NO grass (trampled hard, and the animals were hay-fed at mangers, not grazed there) - and, crucially, NOT a fenced paddock (the least authentic option; a rail fence belongs to an inn's private back-yard or a walled caravanserai). Its edges are the wall + flanking buildings; its "in active use" signal is carts, animals tethered at hitching rails / picket lines, and littered ground (the Qingming convention: clustered carts and pack animals; the tethering, the absent fence and the absent lawn are our reading of the picture, not the cited page's words). The hitching post as a Chinese fixture, the Qingming gate scene and the caravanserai court are attested; the beaten-earth surface and the open, unfenced edge are reasoned from them - no source read describes an ordinary gate cart-yard's ground.

What the research found: a modern 450 kg horse drinks 10-12 gal/day and up to four times that under hard work (a post road's smaller horses less), a working ox about the same (this page's estimate), and a water buffalo wants a WALLOW, not just a trough - so a 20-40 head wagon-train arriving thirsty needs ~300-600 gallons (our arithmetic), drunk in one or two big sessions. Historical practice waters BY RELAY at a fixed point: Silk Road caravanserais watered whole trains from a courtyard well/cistern; an inn yard drew by bucket from a well into troughs (wells, not channels, being the in-town norm - this page's reading); London's high-traffic granite street troughs show the throughput, one busy trough serving 1,800 horses in a day by turnover (fed from the mains, which a bucket-filled yard basin is not), and the trough dimensions these maps draw (~8 x 2 ft, ~2 ft of edge per drinking head, both sides usable) are our own. Animals are LED to the water in relays - you water at the well, not at the hitching rail. The decision: the map draws a WATERING POINT - 2 troughs for a plain stables yard, 3 for a caravan-scale ground, ~14 ft long each - clustered BESIDE the nearest well: offset from the wellhead by only its visual radius + half a trough (~bucket-pour distance, GM 2026-07-23 - "otherwise you'd have to carry the water a long way"), on the yard side, even when the well stands at or just past the yard rim. A yard with NO reachable well - or every flank of the near wellhead blocked - DIGS ITS OWN courtyard well and clusters the troughs beside that (GM 2026-07-23). The historical form supports the dig: the Silk Road caravanserai and the yizhan post-yard watered whole trains from their OWN courtyard well/cistern - "no well nearby" is a reason to sink one, never to carry 300-600 gallons across a city block. Every yard's troughs stand within 40 real ft of a well. The trough cluster draws on open ground (GM 2026-07-23, same day): the offset from the wellhead depends on which way the cluster lies from it, because the 2-3-trough stack is TALLER than it is wide, and a fixed offset that clears the well-house roof sideways clips it when the cluster lies above or below the well (as on Tango's caravan ground); the cluster is also kept off every building, road, field, water course, the wall and every other wellhead roof. Departures: trough width is floored at about 6 ft at city grain, the least a mark must measure to be seen at all (a true ~2.5 ft trough would not register), the same convention the wellhead marker takes, and the buffalo WALLOW (a 15-30 ft mud pond, very China-first) is deliberately NOT drawn by default - it is an available flourish for a specific map where ground and water fit.

Capacity - why 2-3 troughs absorb several dozen oxen, and why MORE troughs is the wrong lever (GM asked, 2026-07-23: "there might be several dozen oxen passing through at a time... 2 troughs doesn't feel like enough"). It is enough, and the reason is worth keeping because the naive fix (add troughs) does nothing. Edge capacity: each drawn trough is ~14 ft at ~2 ft of edge per drinking head, both sides of a freestanding trough usable - so one trough serves ~14 head at once (call it 7 where the wellhead crowds one side); the caravan ground's 3 troughs are ~20-40 simultaneous stations, a plain yard's 2 are ~14-28. Turnover: cattle drink ~1-2 gal/min, so a working ox's 5-8 gal session is over in ~5 minutes - even read one-sided, the caravan cluster waters ~20 head every ~6 minutes, and several dozen oxen clear in two or three relays (~20-30 min). That fits how a stage stop is read to have worked: animals unyoked and rested for hours, so watering was a leisurely relay chore for a couple of teamsters, not a race - the London figure (one 9.5 ft trough, 1,800 horses/day) is the throughput-by-turnover anchor. The binding constraint is the WELL, not the trough edge: a hand bucket-draw sustains maybe 5-10 gal/min, so a train's 300-600 gallons is 1-2 hours of steady drawing NO MATTER how many troughs stand there. Extra troughs would not water anyone faster; they would just hold more standing water. What the troughs actually do is BUFFER - filled between arrivals so the first relay drinks instantly while the drawing continues - which is exactly why a caravanserai watered from one courtyard well or fountain (that this was INSTEAD of multiplying basins is this page's reading), and why we expect a coaching-inn yard to show a SINGLE trough (our 2-3 is already the generous end).

These maps draw no animals, because they draw no humans (GM 2026-07-25). The rails, the litter and the furniture carry the "in active use" signal, exactly as the empty streets already imply the crowds.

A muck heap stands 72 ft clear of every rail on the map (GM 2026-07-25). Both flanks of a hitching rail are working tie-up space, so a heap dumped against one sits where the animals stand and blocks a side. A heap 42 ft out has its edge about 8 ft off the animals' rumps, which IS the working row, and it still reads as a heap against the posts; and one map carried a heap about 68 ft from a NEIGHBORING yard's rail. So the floor is 72 ft against every rail the map draws and not merely the yard's own, which leaves the heap's edge about 38 ft past the animal row - out of the tie-up space, still reading as that yard's muck pile.

A well, a trough cluster and a hitching rail may stand side by side, and may not stand on top of one another (GM 2026-07-25). A rail across a wellhead with the trough cluster stacked on both is three glyphs on one spot, which a reader cannot resolve into anything and which implies a layout nobody would build - you do not draw water through a rail, and no yard ties its stock over its own draw-point. The rule is deliberately GLYPH-level: it asks only that the drawn extents not intersect and attaches no clearance in feet, because these three are SUPPOSED to stand close - the troughs hug their well for the bucket pour and the animals stand between rail and trough. Near is right; on top of is not. Two wellheads sunk on one spot count too, being one unreadable blob. Placement keeps about 6 ft of margin over that floor everywhere except against the one well a cluster deliberately hugs, whose bucket-pour gap must not be widened.

The rule the map follows: a gate caravan yard is drawn as ground in use, not as blank parchment: a feathered scatter of beaten earth and straw litter over the open pocket, two or three hitching rails, a stone trough or two, and a dung heap or two. The first rail runs parallel to the nearest way and is set back from it, its animals facing into the yard with their rumps to the roadbed, which is what keeps tethered stock off the through-road; the rest stand at clear interior spots. No rail tip and no heap edge may lie on any tread - road, ring road, street or alley - or on the rampart's clearance strip, and the whole yard is drawn only when the map is complete, because a yard drawn early cannot see a street laid later. A heap keeps 72 ft from every rail on the map. The watering point is a cluster of 2 troughs for a plain stables yard and 3 for a caravan-scale ground, about 14 ft each, standing beside the nearest well at bucket-pour distance and within 40 ft of it; a yard with no well in reach, or with every flank of the near wellhead blocked, DIGS ITS OWN courtyard well and clusters the troughs beside that, which is what a caravanserai or a post-yard did. The yard itself sits a little inside the wall behind the wall-base setback, near the gate and fronting the road where carts pull in - never flush against the masonry and never on the rampart - with the inn and stables inside the gate for secure overnight holding and the cheap flophouse outside it in the gate suburb. The open berth kept clear around a gate stables is about 255 ft across. The trough glyph's width is floored at about 6 ft at city grain, the least a mark needs to measure to be seen at all - a map drawing convention, since a true 2.5 ft trough would not register. The buffalo wallow - a 15 to 30 ft mud pond, and very much the China-first answer - is deliberately not drawn by default; it is a flourish for a map whose ground and water fit it.
