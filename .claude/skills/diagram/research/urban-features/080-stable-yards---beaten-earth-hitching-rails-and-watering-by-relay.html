Stable yards - beaten earth, hitching rails, and watering by relay

Sources: qingming-shanghe-tu, shuanmazhuang-zhwiki (the hitching post at a gate)

  • What the research found (China-first, Song dynasty / Ming-Qing; Edo Japan secondary; sources incl. the Qingming Shanghe Tu 清明上河図 gate scene, the caravanserai court, the Chinese hitching post 拴马桩, the yizhan 驿站 / shukuba post-station). A city-gate cart/animal yard was beaten tamped earth - NO grass (trampled hard, and the animals were hay-fed at mangers, not grazed there - this page's reading) - and, crucially, NOT a fenced paddock (the least authentic option; a rail fence belongs to an inn's private back-yard or a walled caravanserai - this page's reading). Its edges are the wall + flanking buildings; its "in active use" signal is carts, animals tethered at hitching rails / picket lines, and littered ground (the Qingming convention: clustered carts and pack animals; the tethering, the absent fence and the absent lawn are our reading of the picture, not the cited page's words). The hitching post as a Chinese fixture, the Qingming gate scene and the caravanserai court are attested; the beaten-earth surface and the open, unfenced edge are reasoned from them - no source read describes an ordinary gate cart-yard's ground.

How the yard waters its stock - how much a train drinks, the troughs beside the well, the courtyard well dug where none is near, and why two or three troughs are enough - is How does a stable yard water its animals - troughs beside a well, filled by relay.

These maps draw no animals, because they draw no humans (GM 2026-07-25). The rails, the litter and the furniture carry the "in active use" signal, exactly as the empty streets already imply the crowds.

A muck heap stands 72 ft clear of every rail on the map (GM 2026-07-25). Both flanks of a hitching rail are working tie-up space, so a heap dumped against one sits where the animals stand and blocks a side. A heap 42 ft out has its edge about 8 ft off the animals' rumps, which IS the working row, and it still reads as a heap against the posts; and a heap can land as close to a NEIGHBORING yard's rail as to its own. So the floor is 72 ft against every rail the map draws and not merely the yard's own, which leaves the heap's edge about 38 ft past the animal row - out of the tie-up space, still reading as that yard's muck pile.

A well, a trough cluster and a hitching rail may stand side by side, and may not stand on top of one another (GM 2026-07-25). A rail across a wellhead with the trough cluster stacked on both is three glyphs on one spot, which a reader cannot resolve into anything and which implies a layout nobody would build - you do not draw water through a rail, and no yard ties its stock over its own draw-point. The rule is deliberately GLYPH-level: it asks only that the drawn extents not intersect and attaches no clearance in feet, because these three are SUPPOSED to stand close - the troughs hug their well for the bucket pour and the animals stand between rail and trough. Near is right; on top of is not. Two wellheads sunk on one spot count too, being one unreadable blob. Placement keeps about 6 ft of margin over that floor everywhere except against the one well a cluster deliberately hugs, whose bucket-pour gap must not be widened.

The rule the map follows: a gate caravan yard is drawn as ground in use, not as blank parchment: a feathered scatter of beaten earth and straw litter over the open pocket, two or three hitching rails, a stone trough or two, and a dung heap or two. The first rail runs parallel to the nearest way and is set back from it, its animals facing into the yard with their rumps to the roadbed, which is what keeps tethered stock off the through-road; the rest stand at clear interior spots. No rail tip and no heap edge may lie on any tread - road, ring road, street or alley - or on the rampart's clearance strip, and the whole yard is drawn only when the map is complete, because a yard drawn early cannot see a street laid later. A heap keeps 72 ft from every rail on the map. The yard itself sits a little inside the wall behind the wall-base setback, near the gate and fronting the road where carts pull in - never flush against the masonry and never on the rampart - with the inn and stables inside the gate for secure overnight holding and the cheap flophouse outside it in the gate suburb (this page's reading). The open ground kept clear around a gate stables is about 255 ft across.
