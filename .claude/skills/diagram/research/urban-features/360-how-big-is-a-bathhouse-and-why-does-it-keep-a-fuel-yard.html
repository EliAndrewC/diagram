How big is a bathhouse, and why does it keep a fuel yard?

Sources: joetsu-furoya-pdf (what a bathhouse consumed, and the fire rule)

Its costs were firewood and water. Bathhouses stayed mixed from their beginnings into the middle of the Meiji era, because separate tubs for men and women would have needed more space and more firewood and water, and the business would not have paid. Its fire was a danger to the town: for fire prevention, the bathhouses were forbidden to operate in strong wind. So a bathhouse is a furnace with a stock of firewood beside it, and the stock is what shows on the ground.

Its size was not found. No public page read gives the floor size of an Edo-period bathhouse, of its furnace room or of its woodpile.

What it means for the map: a bathhouse is drawn, as a guess, as one street-front building with a furnace at its back and a fenced fuel yard behind that, the fuel yard its visible extra. The building of about 40 by 30 ft, and the fuel yard of about 1,000 to 2,000 sq ft, are a guess, sized to hold a single mixed bath with its changing floor and a season's stacked firewood.
