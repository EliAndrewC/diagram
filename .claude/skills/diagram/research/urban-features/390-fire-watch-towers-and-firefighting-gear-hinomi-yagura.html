Fire watch towers and firefighting gear (hinomi yagura)

Wooden cities burned. Edo was called the City of Fires, and its Meireki fire of 1657, the worst in its history, killed up to 107,000. Against fire a town kept a fire-watch tower, the hinomi-yagura: a lookout for spotting a fire early and calling out the fire brigade, with an alarm bell, the hansho, at its top, whose strokes told the neighborhood how near the fire was. The first towers, built for the shogunate's own fire brigade from 1658, stood some 30 to 50 ft high, and a town's towers were kept lower.

In Edo one tower stood to about every ten wards, and a ward without one set a ladder on the roof of its watch house, where it also kept its fire gear.

  • How tall were the first fire-watch towers?
    The published accounts give two heights. The first towers were those of the shogunate's standing fire brigade, the jobikeshi, at its fire-brigade mansions from 1658. One encyclopedia gives 3 jo, about 9 m (~30 ft), and the Tokyo Fire Department's history gives the same, though it dates the brigade to 1650; the Fire and Disaster Management Museum gives about 5 jo, about 15 m (~49 ft).
  • Towers at daimyo mansions and ward gates had to be lower than the shogunate's brigade towers and lacquered black, and a town's had open legs.
    So says the Fire and Disaster Management Museum: a townsmen's tower was not allowed to have its legs boarded in.
  • Only a daimyo of 80,000 koku or more might build a tower at their mansion.
    So says the Tokyo Fire Department's history of the tower.
  • Only the shogunate's brigade towers kept a watchman on them.
    The brigade's tower had a great drum and a bell and two watchmen always on it; the daimyo's and the townsmen's towers kept no watchman, only a wooden clapper-board or a bell hung on them.
  • The simple form was a fire-watch ladder.
    An encyclopedia calls the simple kind of fire-watch tower a fire-watch ladder, the hi-no-mi-bashigo.
  • The alarm bell spread in the Edo period, as fire brigades were organized.
    The hansho, a small bronze bell, was struck for fire, natural disaster or thieves.
  • Before the bell, a thick wooden board, the bangi, was struck.
    It also called people to meetings.
  • The bell hung first on a ladder and later on a built tower, many with a hut for a fire pump beside it.
    So says the same encyclopedia entry on the bell.
  • The bell's strokes told how near the fire was.
    A near fire was rung as a continuous peal, and the farther off the fire, the fewer the strokes. The way a bell was rung was settled town by town.
  • No page we read gives the size of a tower's base.
    The accounts give a height or a rule, and none the width of a tower's base.

In a town the fire watch belonged to the ward, and each ward paid for its own watch house.

  • In Edo one fire-watch tower stood to about every ten wards.
    In the Kyoho era one tower was provided to every ten wards, and a ward without one set a fire-watch ladder on top of its watch house; a second encyclopedia agrees.
  • Did a town of only a few wards keep a tower at all?
    By Edo's ratio, a town of a few wards falls below one tower, and would have kept the ladder on its watch house instead - this project's reading.
  • Many a ward's watch house carried a ladder or a lookout, and a bell, on its roof.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on the watch house calls the ladder a small-scale fire-watch tower. The article on the tower says a frame was raised on the watch house so that its watchman, on duty day and night, could look over the whole town, and calls that lookout a hinomi-yagura too.
  • A town's fire-watch tower stood beside the ward gate.
    The townsmen's tower, a ladder tower, stood beside the ward gate, the kido, which is why the gate's keeper also struck its bell when there was a fire. What the gate and its keeper's hut were is at Night watch and ward gates (kido).
  • Was the night round, calling "mind your locks, mind your fire", walked in the Edo period?
    A night round is recorded that was walked with that call, the watch striking wooden clappers as it went. The account names only modern bodies for it, a wartime civil-defense guard and the volunteer fire brigade, and does not date it.

The tower only raised the alarm. The fire was fought with gear kept in the watch house and water kept in tubs.

  • A ward kept its fire gear inside its watch house.
    The company standard, fire hooks, the hand pump, carrying buckets, ladders and well buckets were kept ready there, and when the bell was struck the ward's officers and firemen ran to the watch house, took the gear out, assembled and went to the fire together.
  • Edo fought fire by pulling down the houses downwind of it.
    Edo's firefighting was demolition, to stop the fire spreading, and the hand pump mostly wet the men on the roofs against the sparks.
  • Rain water was kept against fire in tubs on the roofs, at the eaves and at the street corners.
    The tubs, tensuioke, were fed from the gutters. They became general, it is said, after the Kansei era (1789-1801), and at a street corner stood a large tub with hand buckets stacked on top of it, ready for use. In the Edo period they served mainly as firefighting water in the towns.
  • How much did a corner tub hold?
    About 6 koku, the encyclopedia says, but its own conversion, about 108 liters (~29 gal), disagrees by a factor of ten. A koku is about 180 liters (~48 gal), so 6 koku is about 1,080 liters (~285 gal); the page's figure is exactly 6 to, a to being a tenth of a koku. No page we read says which figure is the slip.
  • How wide was a corner tub?
    No page we read gives its width or depth. At the larger figure, and taking it as a GUESS to be round and as deep as it was wide, it was about 3 1/2 ft across.
  • No page we read describes a fire pond in a town street.
    The pages name tubs, buckets and firebreaks.

Edo's townsmen fought fire in companies of their own, raised by the town magistrate.

  • Edo's townsman fire companies were organized in 1718, more than a century into the Edo period.
    The South Town Magistrate Ooka Tadasuke issued the ordinance that organized them, the machi-bikeshi.
  • By 1720 Edo had sixty-three townsman fire companies, one to about twenty blocks.
    There were forty-seven iroha companies west of the Sumida River and sixteen more for Honjo and Fukagawa east of it.
  • The companies were manned by townsmen, paid for by their blocks and commanded by the town magistrate.
    Their men were at first ordinary townsmen: land tenants, house tenants and servants.

In our setting the town's fire watch is kept by the magistrate's own people. This is a deliberate deviation, and narrower than it sounds, since in Edo too the fire companies answered to the town magistrate; what changes is who climbs the tower. In a setting pitched earlier and more martial than Edo, the watch is kept by the magistrate's yoriki, doshin and ashigaru rather than by townsmen enrolled block by block.

A fire grew great where the roofs touched. Which towns kept a tower follows from that, in this project's reading.

  • Edo's fires grew great where buildings that burned easily stood packed together.
    The townsmen's dwellings came to stand densely packed in a narrow area, and the close packing of buildings of materials that burn easily was a large part of why fires there became great fires.
  • This project guesses that a town's wall kept a fire in, as it kept raiders out.
    So a watch over the rooftops is wanted where there is a wall. No page we read connects a wall to fire.
  • Did the open ground of an unwalled town work as a firebreak?
    This project reads it so, as a GUESS: a road town strung along a highway, or an unwalled country town whose houses stand apart like a village's, has the road, the gaps between holdings and the fields all round to break a fire. The deliberately cleared firebreak is well attested in the shogunal capital, but no page we read says a country town's field gaps did the same work.

An unwalled seat of government, like the town round an intendant's office, had its own stores to guard from fire.

  • An intendant's office held its own rice stores.
    The office of a shogunal district intendant, the daikansho, was also called a daikan-jin'ya. Its grounds held the intendant's residence, the office for public business, the houses of the subordinate staff, the rice store and the levy store, and a judgment yard and jail.
  • A town could grow round an intendant's office.
    Such a town was called a jin'ya-machi, and often flourished as the central town of its territory.
  • A plastered storehouse was built to keep its contents from fire.
    Storehouses were named for what they held, a rice store among them, and were built to protect it from fire, damp and theft. Their walls were often 300 mm (~12 in) thick or more and their outer doors sometimes faced in earth and plaster, and in the great fires of the Edo period the fire often never reached the inside. The storehouses themselves are at Fire, and the fireproof storehouses (dozō).
  • The tax rice had a store of its own, kept under strict guard.
    The gogura stood one to a village or to several, a holding place for the tax rice and other produce on its way out.
  • One intendant's office survives, and no fire ever took it.
    Takayama Jin'ya, in Hida, is the only one of more than fifty still standing, and it suffered no fire. Its ground keeps the trace of one water-supply pond, and a grain storehouse framed in hinoki in the Keicho era (1596-1615), one of the foremost in the country for age and size. The city's page describes no water kept against fire.
  • The tower began in Edo and had reached almost every region of the country by the early Showa era.
    Towers were built up together with the firefighting system, beginning with Edo in the Edo period.
  • Did an unwalled seat fireproof its storehouse rather than watch the horizon?
    This project reads it so, as a GUESS: losing the tax rice was the disaster an intendant feared, so the store was fireproofed, while the town kept its bell, ladder and gear at its own watch house. Every passage found for them attaches them to the town, not to the intendant's office.
  • Did the tower reach a country seat only after the Edo period?
    This project reads it so, as a GUESS: the tower began with Edo and had reached almost every region by the early Showa era, but the page dates only that reach, not the tower's arrival in any one place, so it does not say when a country seat first had one.
  • This project guesses that an unwalled seat's temple bell may have served as its fire alarm.
    No page we read names a temple bell among the alarms: the fire-tower and watch-house pages name only the hansho and the watch hut.

A village fought fire with the groups it already had, and kept no fire apparatus that any page we read describes.

  • Villages fought fire with their young men's association and their five-household groups.
    So they did until modern times.
  • No page we read puts a fire bell, a fire ladder, a watch hut or a fire pond in an Edo-period village.
    The encyclopedia entries and the fire museum's essay put no fixed fire apparatus in a village before modern times.
  • Most of the fire-watch towers standing today are iron ones built after the Second World War, between 1945 and 1974.
    So says the Fire and Disaster Management Museum's essay on fire towers.
  • This project guesses that a village temple's bell was rung for fire.
    A village with a temple has the temple's bell tower (the village temple). No page we read puts a fire bell in a village, and no search was aimed at the temple bell itself.

Chinese cities watched for fire too. At the Northern Song capital the watch was a garrison.

  • Kaifeng, the Northern Song capital, kept a brick fire-watch tower with over a hundred soldiers below it.
    The tower, the wanghuolou, stood on high ground with men keeping lookout on top. Below it official rooms quartered more than a hundred soldiers with buckets, swabs, axes and saws, ladders, fire-forks, ropes and grapnels.
  • Kaifeng's wards kept a patrol post of five soldiers about every three hundred paces.
    So says the same memoir of the city.
  • A Ming county town kept an organized night watch against fire.
    A headman was in charge of fire and theft. The county town of Wanping in Beijing was organized into wards and posts, each post with a head and three to five firemen, who kept watch by night in turn, striking clappers and ringing bells to warn the inhabitants. Some places also set up a patrol post where the watch sheltered and kept weapons, fire-hooks and buckets.
  • In 1743, under the Qing, the governor of Hunan ordered every department and county to raise fire companies.
    They were registered on rolls, reported to the authorities and paid a fixed sum of silver each year.
  • No page we read shows a brick lookout tower in a Chinese city other than Kaifeng.
    The account of the Ming watch and the Hunan companies names patrol posts and gear, and no standing lookout tower.
  • Most Chinese county seats of the later dynasties were walled.
    The Song discouraged walling a seat away from the borders, but by the late Ming more than two-thirds of the country's county seats had walls, it is estimated, and by the end of the Qing the great majority of prefectural and county seats did.
