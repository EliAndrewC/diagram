Kiln works - a kiln is not a tile kiln, and the potters live at it

Sources: kawara-jawiki (commoners board-roofed, tile general only at the end of Edo - tile demand confined to the few), jianghu-zhwiki, jingdezhen-porcelain-enwiki, imado-yaki-jawiki, anagama-kiln-enwiki, stoneware-enwiki, earthenware-enwiki, six-ancient-kilns-enwiki, kawarabuki-jawiki, tokoname-jawiki, edo-no-kaji-jawiki, hinin-jawiki, sueki-jawiki, pottery-enwiki, alfred-weber-enwiki

What the works looks like on the map - the chambered climbing kiln, its 60 ft fire gap and its sizes in feet - is What does a kiln works look like on the map.

  • Two GM questions: "would the tile kiln have housing close to it, i.e. would whoever works the kiln also live next to it?" and "why is it specifically a 'tile kiln' and not just a kiln? Are there potters in the city with their own non-tile kilns? Or are we not accounting for them as well?"
  • THE VOLUME REASONING RUNS THE WRONG WAY - it is a kiln, not a tile kiln. The GM's instinct ("I wouldn't have expected that to be such a huge difference as to warrant one getting a map glyph and the other not") is right, and if anything understates it. Tile demand is lumpy and project-driven. A fired clay roof outlasts the timber under it (this page's reading; no lifespan figure was read) - Japanese kawara roofs are repaired in patches, not replaced - and in a county seat tile reaches only the temple halls, the gates, the governor's compound and a handful of rich merchants; the rest of the town is thatch and shingle. So the tile kiln has a huge year when a temple hall is reroofed and repair work otherwise. Pottery demand is continuous. Every household breaks and replaces bowls, pots and jars on a steady cycle, and fired clay does not rot, which is why ceramics are the most reliable thing in an archaeological layer: the stuff never stops being consumed. The map's own features make it worse - the brewery needs fermentation vessels and big storage jars (kame), and a large storage jar is heavy and cheap per pound, so it is made near where it is used rather than freighted in (location theory's material-index rule, applied by this page to jars). Per YEAR, a seat of this size burns more clay into vessels than into roof tiles.
  • So why one glyph rather than two. Because at county-seat scale it is genuinely one works. Coarse tile and coarse domestic ware were fired at the same works - tile kilns and ware kilns built side by side from the Nara period on - so there is no reason to draw the same establishment twice. What does NOT belong here is fine stoneware, which needs its own hotter firing - that is a province's specialty (the Seto/Bizen/Jingdezhen pattern, a whole district known for one ware), not something every county seat keeps. A map that knows what its particular works fires says so in its caption; the default caption is just "kiln".
  • THE WORKERS LIVE AT THE KILN. Three independent reasons, any one of which would be enough. (1) A firing runs for days. An anagama or noborigama is stoked round the clock until the ware is done, then left to cool for as long again - nobody walks back into town at dusk in the middle of one. (2) The kiln stands at the CLAY, not at the customer. Siting follows the clay, a slope to build the chambers into and (this page's addition) the fuel supply, and those constraints pull digging, weathering, throwing, drying and firing out to one spot together. (3) It is how the trade was organized. CHINA FIRST: Ming kiln districts were worked under the registered artisan-household system, with Jingdezhen an entire city grown around its kilns - the imperial kilns at its center and the others four kilometers out. Japan corroborates rather than leads - Seto, Tokoname, Bizen, Imado on Edo's fringe, Awataguchi at Kyoto's edge are kiln VILLAGES and kiln quarters, not workshops somebody commutes to.
  • Banishment is about the CITY, not the operator. Fire law is a real constraint - Edo's ordinances set a bathhouse's hours and banned fireworks manufacture in town - and the firing does not happen inside the dense blocks (that kilns in particular were pushed out by law is this page's reading). But that rule is about keeping an uncontrollable fire out of a packed timber town, and it says nothing whatever against the households whose trade it is. They stand off the kiln by the same fire gap anybody else would and no further. Getting this backwards - "banished" read as "banished including its people" - is the tannery's caste logic borrowed by a trade that does not carry it. Potters are not hinin. The tanning yard's siting rules turn on the burakumin quarter; the kiln's turn on clay, fuel and fire, and the two must not be conflated.
  • The null result, which is what answers the GM's second question directly. The town's potters are NOT missing from the map. Only the FIRING is banished: a crockery dealer or a potter's shopfront inside the walls is an ordinary shophouse and stays in the generic shop rows with the tofu maker and the cooper, exactly as the null-results list above has it. The relationship worth remembering is that the ware is MADE at the works outside and SOLD by a dealer inside, which is the same shape as the charcoal district's "burned where the wood grows, tallied where it is sold".
  • Deliberate departures from literal reality. (a) One works per seat. A real kiln district could be several kilns and dozens of households (Imado ran to about fifty; medieval Tokoname's kiln sites number in the thousands); we draw one works with two or three cottages, because the map's job is to say "this is where the seat's pots and tiles come from" and a second identical works teaches a reader nothing. (b) The cottages count as the works' own, not as the settlement's housing stock. This is a bookkeeping decision, not a claim about the setting: every dwelling rule - public-well reach, ward classification, the burakumin standoff - is written about the settlement's own houses, and a satellite works' two cottages would be judged by rules that were never about them. The one place they must count as dwellings is the works' own well.
