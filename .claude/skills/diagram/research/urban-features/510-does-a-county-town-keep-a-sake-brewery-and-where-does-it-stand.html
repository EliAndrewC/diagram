Does a county town keep a sake brewery, and where does it stand?

Sources: l7r-budgets (the setting's county sake brewer), kotobank-sakaya (where Edo law let brewers work, and their sideline in lending), tsukurizakaya-jawiki (brewers along lonely roads), bunka-yachiya-sakagura (a castle-town brewery's store)

Whether. In the setting a county has its sake brewer: an agrarian county's specialized trades are named as the local clothier and the sake brewer, and the county magistrate's gifts come from wealthy artisans and tradesmen such as the sake brewer, the master smith and the dye-house owner. That matches the law the setting's towns are drawn from. The shogunate first confined brewing to the towns - castle towns, post towns, ports and the other country towns - and forbade it to farmers, and it licensed every brewer, so no one could open a brewery at will. Only after rice prices fell in the early eighteenth century did it drop the ban and encourage brewing in the villages. Brewers stood even along fairly lonely roads, as old travel diaries suggest, though the page does not date them. So a county town keeps a brewer, and a village keeps one only where the map says it has grown into something more.

How many. No page read counts the brewers of a town of a given size. The setting's county has "the" sake brewer, one house; the one or two per seat of about 3,000 that Trade works expects for a city stays this record's estimate.

Where, and how big. A brewer kept a solid shop at the center of the town (Trade works). He was also a lender: brewers commonly lent money at interest, and in the provinces not a few also kept a pawnshop. One measured Edo-period example stands in a castle town: the sake store of an old brewery north of Kanazawa castle, built between 1751 and 1829, a long, narrow two-story earthen storehouse about 19 m (62 ft) long with a building area of 132 square meters, about 1,400 sq ft, standing beside the tool store in the middle of the brewer's long plot and thought to be of about the same age as his house. That is one storehouse of a compound that also held the house and the tool store, at the small end of the halls Trade works sizes.

The rule the map follows: a county town keeps one brewery - a shopfront at the center with its stores behind it on a deep plot. The Kanazawa store gives the size of one of them, about 62 by 23 ft; larger halls are in Trade works. A village keeps none: a deliberate departure, disclosed - villages were allowed to brew after the early eighteenth century, but the setting names the brewer as the county's, so the map keeps it in the county town.
