Wells - the research, and the deliberate liberty

Sources: sphere-2004-water; the size for the reader: saijo-mizu-rekishikan, kotobank-idoyakata, idobata-kaigi-jawiki, nagaya-jawiki, ido-jawiki, jawiki-yosuiro

What the research found: a pre-modern south-China rice village of ~70 households ran 1-3 communal drinking wells total, 2 typical (this page's estimate) - drinking water came mostly from SURFACE water (canal/pond/stream, stored in vats, settled, boiled), which is why schistosomiasis was endemic in the Yangtze rice belt for thousands of years and why >70% of rural China still drank untreated surface water in the early 1980s; wells were expensive durable capital financed by per-family subscription and dug only as surface quality forced; the classical jingtian "8 families per well" is a Warring-States ideal nobody practiced.150 Japan corroborates from the other side: valley villages sited on springs/streams needed few or no communal wells, the famous dense pattern (the idobata gossip circle at a shared draw-point - roughly ten to twenty households to one, in this page's estimate) is URBAN nagaya tenement density,98 and in Edo the tenement 'well' was often an aqueduct intake rather than a shaft; per-household wells appear only on shallow-water-table plains (this page's reading). Capacity is never the constraint (one open well serves ~400 inhabitants by Sphere/UNICEF anchors,39 and a nucleated village is ~250m across so carrying distance never binds) - digging cost and morning queueing are.176

The size: the true well against the head the map draws. The wellhead is a map drawing convention (GM ruling 2026-07-21: a location marker, not a to-scale footprint): at one foot per pixel the curb is drawn at a radius of 9.36 ft - about 19 ft across - under a well-house roof, so the well is visible at map scale and keeps about half a dwelling's width at every grain. What the record gives for the real thing: a hand-dug well's SHAFT is40 「A vertical shaft about 1 m in diameter, large enough for a person to get into, is dug, and the digging is carried on by human labor until the hole reaches the vein of groundwater (the aquifer).」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「人が入れる直径1ｍほどの縦穴を掘り、人力で地下水の水脈（帯水層）に到達するまで穴を掘りつづけていきます。」) - a vertical hole about 1 m across, big enough for a person to work in (Saijo City water-history museum); the roof over it is a 井戸屋形 (idoyakata, the well house),41 「A simple structure consisting of nothing more than posts set up beside the well with a roof provided on top of them.」 (Digital Daijisen) (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「井戸のそばに柱を立てて、その上に屋根を 設けただけの簡単な建物」) - posts beside the well with a roof on them and nothing more (Digital Daijisen via kotobank). Not found: a measured width for the curb frame (井桁 / 井戸枠, igeta / idowaku) itself - three pages define it and none sizes it42 - so the map's "~3-4 ft" curb stays an unsourced estimate. Nor was the PREVALENCE of the well house read: the dictionaries define it, they do not say how common it was.

The rule the map follows: every rural settlement keeps communal wells - a town about one per 20 to 25 households, a village one per 8 to 26, a hamlet as richly as one per 2 to 20, which is the liberty above and not a finding; so a hamlet draws one or two, a village of 60 households six to nine, a town about sixteen. Ablution wells beside a shrine are not counted among them. A well stands AMONG the houses it serves and never out in the fields: at the hamlet and town grain of one foot per pixel a well must have a dwelling within about 95 ft, and the placement is seeded a little tighter than that. A household counts as watered by a well OR by the irrigation network it sits beside - a channel, pond, stream or moat within about 380 ft at that same grain - because a dispersed farm household drew from the water it already had;174 the count makes wells present, the reach makes sure no household is left dry. A shrine or temple set apart from the houses, its nearest dwelling more than about 150 ft off, keeps its OWN ablution well beside the hall, and specifically a well rather than a ditch or a pond, which are not ablution sources; the distance is measured to the hall's edge, so a large hall pushes its well outward, and a hilltop shrine is exempt because its water comes from a spring or a cistern rather than a village well (a well CAN be dug on high ground where the water table allows, as on the Kanto uplands, so this is a drawing rule rather than a law).175 The per-tier bands are the disclosed liberty; the reaches are calibrations against the drawn maps rather than figures from a record.
