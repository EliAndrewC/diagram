Refining forges: smelting goes to the fuel, refining comes to the town

Sources: wagner-ming-iron, wagner-fining-puddling, tetsu-to-hagane-91 (the inline list below), fao-industrial-charcoal, tatara-seitetsu-jawiki, tamahagane-jawiki

China first. Ming ironworks converted blast-furnace pig iron to wrought iron by fining, Chinese chao 炒, literally60 "stir-frying": a fire under a forced blast, in a small well-insulated hollow in the ground, into which wood, charcoal and broken cast iron were charged together and then stirred with an iron rod once semi-molten. Song Yingxing describes a rectangular hearth (tang 塘, "pool, basin") set a few chi from the blast-furnace outlet and a few cun lower, up against a short wall, with the men standing in a row on that wall, stirring with willow poles (which burned down 2-3 cun per cycle) while one of them spreads a sieved powder, wuchaoni - "filthy wet loam", probably nitre-bed material rather than a mineral additive.61 Those are two different things, and the authority on Chinese ferrous metallurgy says so: his chao hearth is an insulated pit under a blast, while Song Yingxing's basin is open, unfueled and unblown, which is why he calls it a very different process and a very difficult one to explain technically.62 The practice may run back to the Han, and the authority hedges it in his own words - the process seems also to have been used as early as the Han period, with the evidence for it deferred to another of his books rather than set out where he says so.88 The one thing that cannot be supported is the site: the excavated smelting-and-fining works at Xuxiebian in Sichuan is named in neither of the two works of his that were read in full - one of them searched character by character in its Chinese translation - and neither names any excavated Sichuan ironworks at all.89

Japan corroborating. The tatara's chief product was pig iron (zuku), and the 17th century answered it with a two-stage refining process, the ōkaji 大鍛冶.63 Kera went first to the crushing floor to be broken and sorted; the pig-iron fraction then went to the ōkajiba, a refining hearth that burned its carbon down and turned out wrought-iron bars called hōchō-tetsu 包丁鉄.171 At their 18th-19th century peak the ironworks of the Chugoku Mountains produced 80% of Japan's iron this way.116 Two operations, two sites.

WHY THE TWO SITES ARE NOT A DEPARTURE. A Chinese single-site arrangement is attested - the seventeenth-century technical treatise describes a fining basin a few feet from the blast-furnace outlet, so the iron runs in still molten. But it is not the only Chinese arrangement. The large-scale sector of the Guangdong trade is the other: ironworks out in the forested mountains, the pig iron shipped downriver to an industrial town near the provincial capital, and the conversion to wrought iron and the casting done there.100 That is the same two-site arrangement these maps draw, in China, and for the same reason. Rokugan's charcoal counties cannot do that: a kiln burns four or five parts wood to one of charcoal by weight, so the kiln goes to the wood and the furnace follows the fuel into the hills, miles from the seat.148 So the design follows the Japanese two-site pattern - cold pig comes down and is re-melted at the town forge.

The reason is economic, and it is the fuel. A single site is viable where ore, fuel and labor can all be concentrated; two charcoal blast furnaces are not normally put close together at all, because that doubles the load on the forest for little or no gain.101 A county-scale trade strung along dispersed oak stands cannot concentrate the one input that matters most. Dispersed fuel forces two sites. Per the China-first rule, GM setting canon overrides the historical default where they differ - and here the canon and the Japanese practice agree.

What determines it in reality: economy and topography. Whether smelting and refining share a site is decided by whether fuel, ore and labor can be concentrated - one site where they can, two where the fuel is dispersed through hill forest, with the smelter at the fuel and the refinery in the valley settlement where the carts, the market and the tally are.

Rejected: drawing kilns or a tatara furnace on a town map at all. Canon and arithmetic both forbid it, and the Guangdong account puts the smelting out in the forested mountains rather than in the town that finishes and sells the iron.
