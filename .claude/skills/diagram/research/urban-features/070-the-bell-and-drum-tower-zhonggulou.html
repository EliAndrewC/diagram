The bell-and-drum tower (zhonggulou)

A walled Chinese seat of government marked the hours of the night from a bell-and-drum tower. The night was divided into five watches.

Pingyao's tower, in a county seat, is under 40 ft square and about 60 ft tall. Edo had time bells instead, most of them hanging at temples.

  • Did every walled seat of government have a bell-and-drum tower?
    The Ming state did lay down one of a seat's buildings: in 1369, early in the Ming dynasty, the founding emperor graded the City Gods of the empire in four ranks - capital, prefecture, department and county - and City God temples went up everywhere. That the drum-and-bell set stood with the magistrate's office (yamen), the Confucian temple and the City God temple as the standard fittings of a walled northern seat is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • What did the tower signal besides the night watches?
    The five watches are recorded. That the tower also signaled the opening of the gates at dawn and their closing at dusk, which began the street curfew, and sounded alarms and ceremonies, is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • Beijing's drum tower and bell tower struck 108 strokes each evening.
    The drum tower struck its drum eighteen times, then the bell tower its bell eighteen times, three rounds of each.
  • How large a city could one pair of towers serve?
    Beijing's inner city, served by that one pair, had walls 24 km (~78,740 ft) around. Its sides, about 6.5 km (~21,325 ft) by 5.3 km (~17,388 ft), are this project's reading of a map, agreeing with that perimeter - a GUESS.
  • In Chinese towns, night watchmen also struck the watches, walking the streets.
    A watchman on his rounds struck a bamboo clapper, a gong or a wooden block through the streets and lanes to announce the time.
  • A drum tower and a bell tower commonly stood as a pair, facing each other.
    The bell tower usually stood on the east and the drum tower on the west; at Beijing the pair stand north and south, the bell tower to the north.
  • A single building could hold both, as at Shanhaiguan and Tianjin.
    The Chinese Wikipedia article on bell-and-drum towers names both as single-building towers.
  • What form did a county seat's tower take?
    A drum tower, a combined bell-and-drum tower, or a market tower straddling the main street - this project's reading, a GUESS, from the forms recorded. Pingyao, a county seat, has one: its Market Tower, in the middle of South Main Street at the center of the town, the only multi-storied pavilion in the old city.
  • Pingyao's Market Tower covers 133.4 square meters (~1,436 sq ft), three bays square.
    That is about 38 ft on a side.
  • Pingyao's Market Tower is 18.5 m (~61 ft) tall.
    The Chinese Wikipedia article on the tower gives that height, and so does the Baidu encyclopedia.
  • Xingcheng, a Ming garrison city, set its tower on a brick platform 21 m (~69 ft) square at the exact center of the town.
    The platform, built in 1454, is pierced by a cross-shaped vaulted passage 4.85 m (~16 ft) wide and 3.3 m (~11 ft) high, so the town's two main streets presumably run through it - this project's reading, a GUESS. Xingcheng is the best-preserved Ming garrison city. That a tower raised on an arched platform over the crossing was a garrison city's form, larger than a county seat's, is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • An official compound kept a drum tower of its own, the qiaolou.
    The qiaolou held drums and horns to strike the hours and to receive guests, and Yuanzhou's formed part of its prefectural office compound. The name was used more widely: the Bianjing Tower of Dai county, also called a qiaolou, stands at the heart of the town's crossroads, not in a compound.

Edo, Japan's largest city, rang the hours from many bells instead of one tower.

  • Most of Edo's time bells hung at temples.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on the Kokucho time bell lists Edo's others, at Ueno Kan'ei-ji, Ichigaya Hachiman, Senso-ji, Shiba Zojo-ji and five more places; that most of them are temples is this project's reading of the list.
  • In the Japanese town of Kawagoe, the time bell hangs in a three-story tower 16 m (~52 ft) tall.
    No page we read gives the size of its base.
  • How far could Edo's Kokucho time bell be heard?
    It collected a fee from the roughly four hundred blocks - about three hundred at first - within which it could be heard: 4 mon for each ken of a townsman's street frontage. On the block count that is a radius of about 1.2-1.4 km (~3,937-4,593 ft), this project's arithmetic, a GUESS, and a low one, since only the blocks that paid were counted.

In Song China, fire watch was a different office from timekeeping; in Japan the record is thinner.

  • Kaifeng, the Northern Song capital, kept fire watch in towers of its own, each manned by soldiers.
    Each ward had a patrol post every three hundred paces, and a fire-watch tower (wanghuolou) stood on high ground with a hundred soldiers quartered below it. The towers and their gear are at Fire watch towers and firefighting gear (hinomi yagura).
  • Were Edo's time bells and its fire towers separate institutions?
    Edo's Kokucho time bell was run at public expense, unlike the bells of temples and shrines. The page says nothing of fire towers, so that the time bell stood apart from the fire towers (hinomi yagura) is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • In some Japanese towns, the fire tower's alarm bell also rang the time.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on fire watch towers says some towns used the alarm bell (hansho) for the time signal and for news, each town fixing its own pattern of strokes.
