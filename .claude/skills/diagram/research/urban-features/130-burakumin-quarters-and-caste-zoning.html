Burakumin quarters and caste zoning

Before 1868, in the Edo period, Japan's castes lived apart. The burakumin, the lowest of them, were registered separately and lived in segregated villages or in their own parts of towns. Besides leatherwork, they worked as gardeners and well-diggers, carried out punishments and handled the dead. The lords who took the leather workers under their control housed them, as far as the record can tell, at the edge of the castle town, at a chokepoint on the roads, or on a riverbank, and the ground they were given was often poor.

The quarters ranged from a few households to the great enclosure in Edo where the hereditary head of the region's outcastes lived, a couple of hundred houses crowded together behind temples and walls. The other castes were zoned too: the commoners' blocks lay apart from the samurai quarter and shut their gates at night, and the poor of a city lodged in a quarter of their own.

  • A seventeenth-century painting shows a buraku outside the walls of Kyoto.
    The English Wikipedia article on the burakumin carries it, beside its account of their separate registration and segregated villages.
  • Where did the lords of the warring-states era house their leather workers?
    At the edge of the castle town, at a chokepoint on the roads, or on a riverbank, so far as a Japanese dictionary of history can tell; it hedges the siting with "it seems". It infers this from the cases of the Imagawa of Suruga, the Later Hojo of Izu and the Takeda of Kai, whose leather workers were in every case called kawata.
  • Matsumoto gathered its kawata at the entrances of its castle town.
    In 1588 the kawata, who had worked leather in the countryside, were gathered there and set to cleaning, to the police's menial work and to the executioner's work at the execution ground. Ueda did the same in 1649, and the other castle towns of Shinano placed theirs likewise.
  • In Shinano, and in Tsuruoka and Sakata in the north, the quarter was called machi-hanare, "apart from town".
    In the castle town of Tsuruoka the quarter's inhabitants kept the jail, escorted criminals and cleared up after executions; in the port of Sakata they handled criminals and funeral goods. In Shinano the name is given beside the Matsumoto case.
  • Takada set its quarter at the town's northwest edge, along the Kaga road.
    When the castle town was laid out around 1614, those who bore its guard duties and its cleaning were moved there from Naoetsu.
  • Others were settled at post towns and road checkpoints, or invited into villages as guards.
    In Shinano some were moved to the key points of traffic, the post towns and checkpoints, some moved when an intendant's office was set up, and in the middle and later Edo period a considerable number were invited into villages, mainly as village guards.
  • Kyoto's oldest quarter, Tenbu, was a village of riverbank outcastes.
    A historian of Kyoto's quarters calls it the oldest village of the kawaramono, the outcastes named for the riverbank.
  • Edo's outcaste head was moved outward by stages.
    His dwelling was pushed from north of Nihonbashi to beside the Torigoe shrine, and then to Shinmachi, beyond the Sanya canal.
  • Was a quarter's ground worse than its neighbors'?
    Often, it seems. Many quarters today sit on sites badly placed for flood, sun or water, a state of things the dictionary says is even said to owe much to the forced removals of the Edo period. In the province of Kawachi, the land the kawata of Oji village first held was of poor quality, reflecting their status as recent arrivals.
  • How far from its village did an outcaste hamlet stand?
    At no fixed distance that we found. At Saraike, in Kawachi, the kawata lived within the village's own territory, their house plots clearly separate from the peasants', yet the peasants lived nearer to two neighboring villages than to them. The dissertation that reports it gives no distance in any unit, and though it records the older name of the status, kawaramono, which it glosses by the riverbank, it draws no siting from the name.
  • Did a town's quarter ever stand inside a town wall?
    None that we found. Most Japanese castle towns had no circuit wall to be inside (Tanning yards), and every siting found is at the edge, at an entrance, on a riverbank or beyond the city; nothing ties a quarter's place to a siege.

Most of what is recorded about a quarter's size and buildings is about Edo's great enclosure at Asakusa, the seat of a regional head rather than a town's quarter.

  • The Asakusa enclosure held 232 houses.
    The head of the outcastes of the Kanto, the eastern region around Edo, made Asakusa his base, and 232 houses were crowded together there, 15 of them monkey-trainers'.
  • The head's whole jurisdiction counted some 7,700 households.
    It took in the eight Kanto provinces, Izu and parts of three more, and in a return of 1800 counted 5,664 eta households, 1,995 hinin households and 61 monkey-trainers' households.
  • How many households did a provincial town's quarter hold?
    No page we read counts them; every count found is of the Edo enclosure or of a whole region.
  • The Asakusa enclosure was screened from view, and held the head's residences, storehouses and a shrine.
    It was ringed by temples, shrines and walls so that no one could see in, and held housing for three to four hundred members of his officials' families. The head's own lot was 740 tsubo (~26,000 sq ft), with a pond in it, and the eta bathed at a bathhouse he ran.
  • Many quarters kept the god of Mount Haku as their tutelary god.
    Not a few quarters, above all in eastern Japan, worshiped the Hakusan god as their ujigami, and the Asakusa enclosure had its Hakusan shrine.
  • Jodo Shinshu was the commonest sect among the quarters, and their temples go back before the Edo period.
    Shinshu was the most common sect of the quarters. In Yamashiro, Tanba and Harima, around Kyoto, their temples were founded 39 between 1501 and 1572, 57 between 1573 and 1614 and 57 more between 1615 and 1670, and a temple, as the historian who reports the count puts it, presupposes a community settled enough to keep it.
  • No page we read says whether a quarter had a well of its own, or what its houses were like.
    The only buildings described are those of the Asakusa enclosure.

The commoners and the samurai were zoned apart as well, though by a different means: a gate and a watchman rather than open ground.   • A castle town's commoner quarter lay outside the samurai quarter, and each block shut its gates at night.
    The townsmen's quarter, of merchants and artisans, was laid out outside the samurai quarter, and each town block had its palings and kido gates, shut at night with a gatekeeper posted. After the night hour of yotsu, anyone with business was inspected by the watchman and passed through a wicket door beside the gate.
  • China's walled wards did not outlast the Song and Yuan dynasties.
    The Chinese Wikipedia article on the ward system says it ceased to exist after them.
  • Osaka's Nagamachi, where the highway south left the city, was lined with inns and flophouses.
    Nagamachi was the built-up street at the start of the Kishu road, which ran south through Sakai, and many inns and kichin-yado lined it. Migrant laborers and the destitute gathered in its back streets, living in tenements rented by the day, or unregistered. Its first five blocks were renamed in 1792, it is said, to shed its image as a poor quarter.
  • Edo's first flophouse opened in a block where street mendicants were later recorded living.
    By the account of the researcher Kusama Yasoo, it opened in the Meiwa era (1764-1772) in Shitaya Yamazaki-cho, one of the blocks where, by a report to the town magistrate's office in 1842, the gannin, mendicants who begged in the streets, lived.
  • Was a city's flophouse in its poor quarter before 1868?
    Yes, in this project's reading. After the lodging rules of 1887 the flophouses spread from the highways into the cities' poor quarters, but Nagamachi and Yamazaki-cho show the form already in the Edo period. In Nagamachi the poor quarter and the road in were one place. A town's flophouse is at The market-day flophouse - who actually stays over.
  • The great houses kept garden estates in the outskirts, and their working residence nearest the castle.
    In Edo a domain's lower residence was chiefly a secondary residence for gardens and the like, mostly in the outskirts away from the castle, and a refuge for the daimyo when great fires struck the center; the residence nearest the castle was normally the upper one.
