Night watch and ward gates (kido)

In the Edo period many Japanese castle towns and post towns shut their streets at night: a post town's gates were closed against crime, and a gate's keeper walked the night round against fire. A ward gate, the kido, stood across the street at each end of a ward, and from ten at night to six in the morning it was closed, the only way through a small side door under its keeper's eye. The keeper, the kidoban, lived in a hut beside the gate about 6 by 9 ft, and at the ward's other end stood its watch house, the jishinban.

The watchmen were hired, and outside Edo many of them were hinin, of outcaste status. Edo alone kept nearly a thousand watch houses in 1850, while a village might hire a watchman of its own.

  • Many castle towns shut ward gates across their streets at night.
    Edo and many other castle towns set up ward gates, which were to be shut at night. In 1658 Edo told any ward without a gate to put up a temporary one.
  • Each castle-town gate had two keepers, mostly old men who lived in its hut.
    They were called bantaro or banta, and for the most part were old men living in the watch hut.
  • A large town set a gate to every ward, a middle or small town one to several wards.
    So Heibonsha's encyclopedia entry on the gate keeper says, of the ward gates in general.
  • An Edo ward gate was a pair of doors between posts about 12 ft apart.
    The posts stood about 2 ken apart, with a fence or a board wall running from each post to the edge of the road, and the keeper's hut beside it.
  • The keeper passed travelers on and walked the night round against fire.
    With the gates at both ends of the ward shut, he sent anyone who passed on to the next ward with a signal of wooden clappers, and made the night rounds against fire.
  • Kyoto's wards had set gates at both ends since the Muromachi period.
    From the Muromachi period Kyoto's wards set gates, called kuginuki, facing the crossings at both ends of the ward.
  • In Kyoto two watchmen kept each gate at night, and one by day.
    In the Edo period a board-floored hut about one tatami in size stood beside each gate, kept by watchmen whom the ward hired from lower-status people.
  • A post town had a gate at each end, in most cases shut at night.
    The gates stood near the post town's mitsuke, a pair of earthen banks with a fence along their tops, one on either side of the entrance; the ground between the gates was the post town itself, and sometimes a lamp was kept burning all night beside them.

Two small buildings stood at a ward's gates: the keeper's hut and the ward's watch house.

  • The gate keeper's hut was fixed at about 6 by 9 ft.
    It was set at 6 by 9 shaku, with eaves 1 jo (about 10 ft) high.
  • How big was a ward's watch house?
    Nominally 9 shaku by 2 ken, about 9 by 12 ft, but in practice some 2 to 3 ken, about 12 to 18 ft. The source does not say which side the 2 to 3 ken measures.
  • The keeper's hut and the watch house stood at the two ends of a ward's main street.
    Both were built against the gates at the two ends of each ward's main street, the gate keeper at one and the watch at the other.
  • A ward's watch house usually stood on the south corner of a crossing.
    The crossing was on the main street running east and west.
  • In Osaka the watch house was part of a row of townhouses.
    It was not a building of its own, as in Edo, but was built in line with the ordinary townhouses.

The men who kept the watch were hired by the town or village they served.

  • Towns and villages hired watchmen, the banta, many of them hinin.
    The banta kept the night watch and watched for fire and over the sluice gates. In Edo he was a commoner, but elsewhere many were hinin and were called ban-hinin.
  • In some regions the watchman also dug graves and dealt with the dead.
    Many there were of hinin status, and they dug graves, cremated and dealt with those who died on the road.

A city kept its watch at a far larger count, in two forms: a watch house in the townsmen's wards and a guard box, the tsujiban, in the samurai quarter.

  • Edo kept one watch house to a ward, 994 of them in the shogunate's last years.
    They were set up ward by ward in the Kyoho era (1716-1736), two or three small wards sharing one, and in 1850 there were reported to be 994.
  • The samurai quarter kept its own guard boxes, the tsujiban.
    Guard boxes stood mainly in castle towns, and in Kyoto as well as Edo. In Edo the samurai houses kept their own, 936 of them in the Genbun era (1736-1741).
  • An Edo guard box was about 12 by 9 ft, cut into a mansion's wall, with four or five men on watch.
    It was normally 2 ken wide by 9 shaku deep (about 12 by 9 ft), with four or five men on watch day and night.
  • How big was a guard box with its lean-to?
    A local historian writing on the Muramatsu domain's guard box in Edo gives the usual plan as 2 ken by 9 shaku, a 6-mat room, with a lean-to 2 ken by 1 ken beside it, the whole a 10-mat building with a tiled roof.
  • Were some domains' guard boxes much larger?
    Reading an army survey map of 1883-84, made in the Meiji era, the same historian puts the Muramatsu domain's box at about 13.5 tsubo (~480 sq ft), against about 5 tsubo (~178 sq ft) for other domains'.
  • Aizu-Wakamatsu, a provincial castle town, kept its own guard boxes and night rounds.
    There the town kept its guard boxes, about two men to each. Twice a night, at midnight and at two in the morning, they went round their group of wards calling "take care", asked any wanderer where they lived and passed them on to the next box. A group of several wards paid for the men, the lamp oil and the hut.
  • No page we read counts a provincial city's watch houses or guard boxes.
    Aizu-Wakamatsu's guard boxes are described, but not counted.

A village that kept a watch kept it more simply, with a hired man.

  • A village's watchman sat in a watch hut and was paid an allowance.
    He normally sat in a watchman's hut, and a watchman's allowance was paid.
  • In some villages the watchman also guarded the hills, fields and sluices.
    He ran errands, kept the night watch and controlled vagrants as well.
  • In Nagano, many villages later in the Edo period invited outcastes in as their guards.
    In the middle and later periods a considerable number were invited by villages and moved there, mainly as village guards. Early in the period the Matsumoto, Matsushiro and Ueda domains had moved them into their castle towns.
  • Where did a village's watch hut stand?
    The one placement found is from a work of 1721: in the villages, under the name of the advance hawk watch, a watch hut was built at the village's entrance. That was a watch named for the hawks, not the ordinary village watchman, so it does not show that every village watchman's hut stood at the entrance.
  • No page we read says how many villages kept a watchman, or how big his hut was.
    The dictionary entries give his duties, his status and one hut at a village's entrance, but neither figure.
