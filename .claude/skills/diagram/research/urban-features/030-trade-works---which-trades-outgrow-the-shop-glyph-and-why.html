Trade works - which trades outgrow the shop glyph, and why

Sources: the pawnshop and kiln rest on their own entries (tontine-monastery-lending; "Kiln works") fukagawa-kiba-summary , burakumin-enwiki, eta-jawiki, tsukurizakaya-jawiki, kotobank-tsukigomeya, karausu-jawiki

  • The GM's question, during the provincial-city audit: "are there trades whose real premises, with their attached works, yards, and outbuildings, are materially bigger than a shophouse, i.e. significant space our maps are not representing?"
  • The frame. Song/Ming China first, Edo Japan second. City maps already draw commerce as generic ~48x32 ft shop rowhouses plus merchant kura and dedicated inn/stables/flophouse glyphs, so the only question is which trades BREAK that glyph.
  • The shortlist - premises visibly bigger than a rowhouse at this scale. Sake/miso/soy brewery (sakagura; Chinese zaofang) is the big one: even a small county-seat brewer needed a vat hall of 5-6 ft fermentation tanks, a koji room, a rice-steaming area with masonry kamado and chimney, its own well, and a rice kura - a minimal hall runs 60-120 ft x 30-40 ft BEHIND a normal shopfront, 3-8x the shophouse, and surviving small-town compounds reach whole-block scale (one Nagano brewer at ~1,500 tsubo / ~53,000 sq ft). It was very often the largest commercial building in a small town; expect 1-2 per seat of ~3,000 (this page's estimate), sited in town, with a solid shop at the center, because brewers were town elite. Dyer (kon-ya): modest building, large GROUND signature - the workshop's sunken indigo vats and boiling hearth fit a deep shophouse, but the DRYING does not (poles, racks, and rooftop platforms dominate the block in Hiroshige's Kanda Konya-cho print) and rinsing happened in open water (nunosarashi), so a town dyer needs a ~2,000-5,000 sq ft drying yard (bolts run 35-40 ft) or riverbank frontage. Lumber dealer (zaimokuya): stacked timber and floated logs need a ~3,000-8,000 sq ft open yard with a river landing, at the edge of town near the downstream gate or wharf. Oil presser (aburaya / youfang): the wedge-and-beam press is a massive timber machine (beam 15-30 ft) plus a seed-roasting stove and an ox-driven edge-runner mill on a ~20-25 ft circular track - a fire-conscious press barn ~40-60 x 25-30 ft toward the edge. Pawnshop (shichiya): an ordinary shop whose tell is STORAGE - pledges are bulky, so a prosperous broker kept 2-3 fireproof kura in a walled rear court, i.e. the existing kura glyph multiplied rather than a new building type. Bathhouse (sento): an ordinary streetfront building with a rear furnace and chimney, whose visible extra is a ~1,000-2,000 sq ft firewood stack yard; commercial baths are China-first correct, attested from the Song, so a city may keep 1-2.
  • The null results, which are equally load-bearing. The long tail genuinely fits the shop glyph - tofu maker, noodle and confection shops, apothecary, teahouse, cooper, tatami maker, carpenter's shop (his big work happens on site), rice dealer (treadle-pestle hulling in-shop, grain in a rear kura). And some trades belong OUTSIDE town entirely: tile, pottery, charcoal, and lime kilns stand at the periphery or in nearby villages (fire law and smoke being this page's reading of why), and tanneries and slaughter go riverside with the outcast quarter, which stood at the edge, beside the water the tanning needed.
  • What a city draws (GM 2026-07-24, "I'd like all of them"): brewery compound (vat hall + shopfront + rice kura + its own PRIVATE well; both cities keep one, and Tango's stands on the pond); dyer's drying yard (must sit ON water - both cities, since dyeing needs rinsing water rather than bulk transport, so a landlocked city keeps its dyers; Tango's rinses to the DRAINAGE side, never the field-supply channels); riverside lumber yard (a river port only - Nagahara, since timber is the one trade that genuinely needs water transport at scale, and the yard abuts the bank but never overlaps the water); oil-press barn; pawnshop; bathhouse (count rolled from population); kiln (outside the walls); farrier - why this setting shoes horses at all where Edo Japan did not, and what a farrier's shed, apron and ox frame draw and how often, is Why does this setting shoe its horses where Edo Japan did not, and what does a farrier draw on the map?. The tanning yard is drawn too and has its own entry below.

The rule the map follows: every provincial city keeps a brewery (the largest commercial building in the place - a vat hall, a shopfront, a rice storehouse and its own private well, sited on good water and excluded from every public-well count), a dye works whose rinsing and drying yard stands on water within about 120 ft of a stream, channel, canal, pond or moat and rinses to the drainage side rather than into a field-supply channel, an oil press toward the edge, a pawnshop whose tell is its walled two-storehouse pledge court, a bathhouse with a fenced fuel yard behind it, a kiln works strictly outside the walls, and a farrier standing on a gate caravan yard within 250 ft of a stables and at least 6 ft clear of every other footprint. Three works are declared rather than universal: a RIVER-PORT city also keeps a riverside lumber yard within about 180 ft of the bank, abutting the water but never overlapping it, since stacked timber stands on dry ground and a landlocked city has none; a town keeps a farrier only if it stands on an Imperial road, a relay town working courier and caravan stock hard enough to keep a forge where an ordinary market town does not; and a charcoal district or an iron district is drawn only where the map declares one. The tanning yard is the one work of this kind that is not city-only. The bathhouse COUNT rolls from population: one per full 2,000 inhabitants, plus a chance of one more equal to the remainder, floored at one - so a seat of 2,500 keeps one and rolls a quarter chance of a second, and a seat of 4,000 keeps exactly two. The 2,000 is Edo's own peak ratio, about 523 bathhouses for some 1.1 million residents in 1808 (this rests on general reading; no source is cited). Everything else a seat trades in - tofu, tea, medicine, cooperage, carpentry, rice dealing, and the ordinary smith, whose hoof work changes his repertoire rather than his footprint - stays inside the generic shop rows, and that null result is load-bearing: a works earns a glyph of its own only when its premises outgrow a rowhouse.

The rule the map follows: nuisance separations run on four rungs, and each rung is a different hazard rather than a different degree of the same one. About 6 ft between an attended open forge and anything that burns, which is the farrier's shed against hay and timber. 30 ft around an unattended charcoal stack, about one flame-height clear of a fully involved stack. 60 ft around a kiln body and around a refining forge, the attended-fire rung: a very large fire, but one being stoked and watched. And 120 ft between a nuisance carried on the air - the crematory, the tanning yard - and any dwelling not of the caste that works it. The rungs are reasoned against one another rather than measured from a record: only the 30 ft carries a derivation of its own, and reusing the 120 ft figure for charcoal was considered and rejected, because it defends against smell over distance and would push a charcoal yard off the cart route it exists to serve.
