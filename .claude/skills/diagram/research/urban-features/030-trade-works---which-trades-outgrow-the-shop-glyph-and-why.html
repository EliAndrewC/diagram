Trade works - which trades outgrow the shop glyph, and why

Sources: the pawnshop and kiln rest on their own entries (tontine-monastery-lending; "Kiln works") fukagawa-kiba-summary , cattle-crush-enwiki, teitetsu-jawiki, tatara-enwiki, tatara-seitetsu-jawiki, hoofcare-japanese-hoof-sandals, burakumin-enwiki, eta-jawiki, tsukurizakaya-jawiki, farrier-enwiki, blacksmith-enwiki, yizhan-zhwiki, horseshoe-enwiki, hipposandal-enwiki, kotobank-tsukigomeya, karausu-jawiki

  • The GM's question, during the provincial-city audit: "are there trades whose real premises, with their attached works, yards, and outbuildings, are materially bigger than a shophouse, i.e. significant space our maps are not representing?"
  • The frame. Song/Ming China first, Edo Japan second. City maps already draw commerce as generic ~48x32 ft shop rowhouses plus merchant kura and dedicated inn/stables/flophouse glyphs, so the only question is which trades BREAK that glyph.
  • The shortlist - premises visibly bigger than a rowhouse at this scale. Sake/miso/soy brewery (sakagura; Chinese zaofang) is the big one: even a small county-seat brewer needed a vat hall of 5-6 ft fermentation tanks, a koji room, a rice-steaming area with masonry kamado and chimney, its own well, and a rice kura - a minimal hall runs 60-120 ft x 30-40 ft BEHIND a normal shopfront, 3-8x the shophouse, and surviving small-town compounds reach whole-block scale (one Nagano brewer at ~1,500 tsubo / ~53,000 sq ft).138 It was very often the largest commercial building in a small town; expect 1-2 per seat of ~3,000 (this page's estimate), sited in town, with a solid shop at the center, because brewers were town elite.139 Dyer (kon-ya): modest building, large GROUND signature - the workshop's sunken indigo vats and boiling hearth fit a deep shophouse, but the DRYING does not (poles, racks, and rooftop platforms dominate the block in Hiroshige's Kanda Konya-cho print) and rinsing happened in open water (nunosarashi), so a town dyer needs a ~2,000-5,000 sq ft drying yard (bolts run 35-40 ft) or riverbank frontage.140 Lumber dealer (zaimokuya): stacked timber and floated logs need a ~3,000-8,000 sq ft open yard with a river landing, at the edge of town near the downstream gate or wharf.16 Oil presser (aburaya / youfang): the wedge-and-beam press is a massive timber machine (beam 15-30 ft) plus a seed-roasting stove and an ox-driven edge-runner mill on a ~20-25 ft circular track - a fire-conscious press barn ~40-60 x 25-30 ft toward the edge.141 Pawnshop (shichiya): an ordinary shop whose tell is STORAGE - pledges are bulky, so a prosperous broker kept 2-3 fireproof kura in a walled rear court,15 i.e. the existing kura glyph multiplied rather than a new building type. Bathhouse (sento): an ordinary streetfront building with a rear furnace and chimney, whose visible extra is a ~1,000-2,000 sq ft firewood stack yard; commercial baths are China-first correct, attested from the Song, so a city may keep 1-2.142
  • The null results, which are equally load-bearing. The long tail genuinely fits the shop glyph - tofu maker, noodle and confection shops, apothecary, teahouse, cooper, tatami maker, carpenter's shop (his big work happens on site), rice dealer (treadle-pestle hulling in-shop, grain in a rear kura).190 And some trades belong OUTSIDE town entirely: tile, pottery, charcoal, and lime kilns stand at the periphery or in nearby villages (fire law and smoke being this page's reading of why), and tanneries and slaughter go riverside with the outcast quarter, which stood at the edge, beside the water the tanning needed.137
  • FARRIERY - why this setting has farriers where Edo Japan did not (GM 2026-07-25). Edo Japan did not shoe horses: it used woven straw horse-sandals (umagutsu / umazouri), and metal shoeing became systematic only with Meiji.134 That is right about Japan and wrong about this Rokugan, for two reasons the GM's own notes already carried. (1) Iron. Japan's constraint was material, not ignorance - iron sand (satetsu) smelted with charcoal is a low-yield, fuel-hungry process, so iron went to blades, tools and daily wares long before hooves.135 Rokugan is continental, with real ore bodies and whole iron districts already in the setting's canon (Tatarano, "bellows plain"; Kuroiwa; Ubame's charcoal-sited furnaces; the Reiji hill works; Crab iron for the Wall). Ore is abundant and iron is a normal industrial good, while good STEEL stays scarce and expensive - which buys horseshoes, plain low-carbon iron, without deflating a good blade. (2) Institutional mileage, more than paving. Hard surfaces do abrade hoof, but the sharper driver is annual miles per horse: the setting's own budget notes give ~5,000 waystations on ~50,000 miles of Imperial road at ~10-mile spacing - about 3x the station count of the Tang yi network (1,639 stations; the page's thirty-li spacing is given for the Han, so the spacing was likely much the same) - plus 500-1,000 mounted couriers and mounted anti-banditry patrols.188 A courier horse working a fixed stage all year is on the road every day, and straw sandals are cheap consumables that wore out fast.136 Straw scales for a farmer walking an ox to the next village; it does not scale for institutionalized relay. Canon already assumed this: the setting's own budget notes staff busy trunk-route waystations with "a smith for shoeing horses and weapon maintenance," and the setting's canon introduces Moto Khuyag as a farrier who offers to reshoe the PCs' horses - a Rokugani-born one, who learned the trade at Kyuden Shinjo. Two arguments were considered and deliberately NOT relied on, because they do not survive checking: "later Chinese dynasties nailed iron shoes" (Chinese hoof practice ran mostly to trimming and strap-on or woven boots; iron shoes became common in China only from the Yuan, with rattan and leather before that, and Rome's roads knew the strap-on hipposandal),189 and "the Mongol Yam proves it" (steppe horses on grass were famously ridden UNSHOD, which is a counterexample). The conclusion stands on iron price and relay mileage alone.
  • What it changes on the map, and what it does not. Even in Europe a farrier was a one-forge smithy with a bit of yard, and the big "farrier-forge-with-stable-yard" compound is a coaching-inn image that does not earn its footprint at city scale.186 So an ordinary town smith (kaji-ya) who also shoes still draws as a generic shop rowhouse - shoeing changes his REPERTOIRE, not his premises - and no new establishment joins the shop rows. What earns real ground is only the place horses CONCENTRATE: the gate caravan yard and the Imperial-road relay town, where the map draws a dedicated shed and apron. Two rules fall out of the physical facts. It is not attached to the stables: an open forge against a hay-and-timber stall range is a fire the yard does not survive, so real yards kept the smithy across the ground (anchored on the buildings research's ~6-8 ft wooden-service fire gap; the measure runs from the whole footprint, shed plus apron, which is deliberately conservative since the shed sits at the apron's far end).187 And the apron carries an ox-shoeing frame: Rokugan's draft animal is mostly the ox, a cloven hoof takes two plates per foot (this page's reading of the form), and an ox is shod slung in a timber frame - a Western village fixture, from Spain to England, transplanted here where the record's own hoof practice was straw - about 7 x 4 ft (our dimension), drawn true-size like everything else.132 Sizes are the historical anchors with no legibility inflation: an 18-20 ft village smithy for the shed, and an apron a horse's length (~8 ft) plus room to lead one in and turn it.133 Straw survives everywhere iron is not worth it - peasant pack horses, oxen on soft paddy tracks, poor and mountain provinces - because it is free, not because iron is unknown; that is setting texture rather than a drawn feature, and it lives in the setting's own budget notes.
  • Frequency by scale, and the reasoning behind it. Rokugan's horse population is LOW relative to Europe - peasants work oxen, and horses are a samurai and Imperial animal - so farriery is not a mass trade and the count must not scale with population the way the bathhouse does. Provincial city: exactly one, at a gate caravan yard (not one per gate). Town: only on an Imperial road, which is why the rule keys on the Imperial road rather than on town scale alone - at ~10-mile waystation spacing plenty of towns simply are not on the network, and that non-uniformity is the point. Village and hamlet: never; the village smith trims hooves and tacks on straw with no dedicated premises. A county magistracy keeps only 2-4 horses per the buildings research, so it keeps no permanent forge either - the smith walks over from town.
  • What a city draws (GM 2026-07-24, "I'd like all of them"): brewery compound (vat hall + shopfront + rice kura + its own PRIVATE well; both cities keep one, and Tango's stands on the pond); dyer's drying yard (must sit ON water - both cities, since dyeing needs rinsing water rather than bulk transport, so a landlocked city keeps its dyers; Tango's rinses to the DRAINAGE side, never the field-supply channels); riverside lumber yard (a river port only - Nagahara, since timber is the one trade that genuinely needs water transport at scale, and the yard abuts the bank but never overlaps the water); oil-press barn; pawnshop; bathhouse (count rolled from population); kiln (outside the walls); farrier. The tanning yard is drawn too and has its own entry below.

The rule the map follows: every provincial city keeps a brewery (the largest commercial building in the place - a vat hall, a shopfront, a rice storehouse and its own private well, sited on good water and excluded from every public-well count), a dye works whose rinsing and drying yard stands on water within about 120 ft of a stream, channel, canal, pond or moat and rinses to the drainage side rather than into a field-supply channel, an oil press toward the edge, a pawnshop whose tell is its walled two-storehouse pledge court, a bathhouse with a fenced fuel yard behind it, a kiln works strictly outside the walls, and a farrier standing on a gate caravan yard within 250 ft of a stables and at least 6 ft clear of every other footprint. Three works are declared rather than universal: a RIVER-PORT city also keeps a riverside lumber yard within about 180 ft of the bank, abutting the water but never overlapping it, since stacked timber stands on dry ground and a landlocked city has none; a town keeps a farrier only if it stands on an Imperial road, a relay town working courier and caravan stock hard enough to keep a forge where an ordinary market town does not; and a charcoal district or an iron district is drawn only where the map declares one. The tanning yard is the one work of this kind that is not city-only. The bathhouse COUNT rolls from population: one per full 2,000 inhabitants, plus a chance of one more equal to the remainder, floored at one - so a seat of 2,500 keeps one and rolls a quarter chance of a second, and a seat of 4,000 keeps exactly two. The 2,000 is Edo's own peak ratio, about 523 bathhouses for some 1.1 million residents in 1808 (this rests on general reading; no source is cited).83 Everything else a seat trades in - tofu, tea, medicine, cooperage, carpentry, rice dealing, and the ordinary smith, whose hoof work changes his repertoire rather than his footprint - stays inside the generic shop rows, and that null result is load-bearing: a works earns a glyph of its own only when its premises outgrow a rowhouse.

The rule the map follows: nuisance separations run on four rungs, and each rung is a different hazard rather than a different degree of the same one. About 6 ft between an attended open forge and anything that burns, which is the farrier's shed against hay and timber. 30 ft around an unattended charcoal stack, about one flame-height clear of a fully involved stack. 60 ft around a kiln body and around a refining forge, the attended-fire rung: a very large fire, but one being stoked and watched. And 120 ft between a nuisance carried on the air - the crematory, the tanning yard - and any dwelling not of the caste that works it. The rungs are reasoned against one another rather than measured from a record: only the 30 ft carries a derivation of its own, and reusing the 120 ft figure for charcoal was considered and rejected, because it defends against smell over distance and would push a charcoal yard off the cart route it exists to serve.
