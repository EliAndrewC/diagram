The log boom - a shore-fast holding pen, not sticks in the stream

Sources: nishikori-tsunaba,kiso-unzaihou, susquehanna-boom, stcroix-boom-nps, hudson-big-boom, kiba-koto, shangxinhe-gazetteer, timber-rafting-wiki (cited inline below)

  • The GM's question, looking at a drawn boom: "it just looks like a bunch of logs in the middle of the river. How did log booms really work? Did they block the whole river? I assume they were at least partially against the shore."
  • Two systems moved timber by water, and the full-river boom belongs to only one of them. Loose-log driving (Japanese kanagashi, one log at a time) ran the upper reaches, too swift for rafts; rafting ran the lower river. The one place a boom legitimately spans the whole water is the catch point where driving hands off to rafting: on the Kiso this was the tsunaba ("rope place") at Nishikori, sited exactly where the river leaves its gorge - a great rope stretched bank to bank, in use from the 1340s, catching ~300,000 logs a year in a season running autumn equinox to spring equinox, administered by a timber magistracy of 138 permanent officials. Rafts were assembled there (three-man crews) and floated on to the storage yard at Nagoya and to Kuwana on Ise Bay. A full-span catch boom is legitimate ONLY where the river is not a navigation route - upstream, at the gorge mouth, never at a port.
  • Where the river carries boats, the boom must leave them a channel - and the law said so. The great American boom works are the same furniture at the largest scale this page found. The Susquehanna Boom at Williamsport ran seven miles, its crib line running diagonally across the river from the south bank near Duboistown to the north bank near Linden, strung from 352 man-made islands (cribs of stone and timber, 22 ft high), enclosing some 450 acres of river as its holding pen; it held up to 300 million board feet afloat at once - on the order of a million logs - and corralled ~40 million over its working life. That crib line crossed the river, and how boats got past it is not in the source, so it shows the scale of a boom, not how one shared a navigated river. The St. Croix boom works filled miles of river with piers, pilings and floating log fences into log channels and holding pens, a navigation channel maintained alongside and a hinged "sheer boom" swinging open and closed between islands as the volume of logs rose and fell. On the St. Croix an act made the entire boom works illegal unless the Corps approved them, logs spilled from the logging channel into the navigation right-of-way, and by 1888 Wisconsin had ruled the Boom Company negligent for log jams backed up at its sorting gap. Each of these booms was anchored to fixed ground: bank abutments, stone-filled cribs, driven piles - the Hudson's Big Boom rode on hewn timbers bolted four-together raftwise, chained end to end over friction rollers to abutments on the banks. A boom is a floating fence; a fence anchored to nothing holds nothing.
  • At the destination city, timber sits in slack water, in bulk. Edo kept its stock at Kiba - a dedicated lumber district of canals and storage ponds where logs floated in lumber ponds until sawn and sold (water storage keeps timber sound and safe from fire, in this page's reading; the source gives fire risk as the reason the trade moved across the river). Nanjing kept its stock at Shangxinhe, a ~9 km side channel off the Yangtze where timber rafts mostly moored along the banks at the Jiajiang mouth and the channel's banks grew into a timber market that became Shangxinhe town, said at its height to be "always sufficient in all four seasons". Nagoya's rafts came to rest at the Shiratori timber-storage yard. In none of these three cities is the working stock a scatter of loose logs in mid-channel: it is an enclosed pen against the bank, moored raft-mats lining a slack side-water, or off-river ponds.
  • The decisions it drove. The floating log fence around a pen, with its pile clusters, is not drawn: in East Asia it is known only from modern records, and a timber city's stock is drawn instead as raft-mats moored along a slack bank or a pond off the river ("Did premodern East Asia hold its timber with a floating log boom?"). The moored stock never spans a navigated channel: it takes about a third of the river's width and never more than two fifths, its bank edge riding ON the shore line, off the lumber yard it feeds. For Minami specifically: the Hayakawa is 120 ft wide and navigated (wharf, dock basin, cargo canal), so the city gets rafts moored along its bank; the full-span tsunaba rope is off-map lore - the Fox catch-works at the gorge mouth upstream, where the loose drive becomes the rafts that arrive at the city.
