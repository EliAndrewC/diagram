What does a communal well look like from above - curb, sweep or pulley, roof?

Sources: kotobank-tsurube-ido (the old wooden curbs and the two lifting gears), ndl-crd-tsurube-ido (a measured wellhead), bunka-tokugawaen-ido (a stone curb with a roof and a pulley), hiratsuka-tsurube (the sweep on a shallow well), yokkaichi-hanetsurube (the pulley as the ordinary gear), seiyo-karihama-ido (the paved wellside), whb-gujing (the Chinese town well's curb and platform)

The curb. A square or round frame round the shaft's mouth - in older wells a bent-wood hoop or a frame of squared timbers, in finer ones cut stone laid in the crossed-timber pattern of the igeta. One bucket well photographed and measured for a book of old implements is 118 cm by 118 cm, about 4 ft square. Round a communal well the ground was often paved with stones.

The lifting gear: two forms. The bucket was raised either by a balance-beam lever - a sweep - or on a rope over a pulley. A museum guide calls the rope and pulley the ordinary gear, and the sweep a pole worked like a seesaw with a weight at the far end. Which one a well had followed its depth: on the shallow wells of the Hiratsuka plain the sweep was the rule, a pole crossed over a post with a bag of stones at its butt, and the pulley well was less common because the wells were shallow. From above the two read differently: a sweep is a long pole lying across the well from a post beside it; a pulley hangs from a beam on two posts, or from a roof.

The roof. A well house was nothing more than posts beside the well carrying a roof (Wells - the research, and the deliberate liberty). A registered well of about 1900 on the Owari Tokugawa estate in Nagoya shows the finished form: a cut-granite curb, dressed posts beside it, a gabled roof, and an iron pulley hung from it, the whole on about 1.6 square meters. How many village or town wells had a roof, and how long a sweep's pole was, no page read says.

The Chinese town well. The curb of an old Chinese well might have two, three or four openings on one shaft so the buckets would not knock together, and the platform round it usually carried separate ditches for clean and for waste water.

What it means for the map. The wellhead stays a location marker drawn larger than life, a drawing convention; the real head is a curb about 4 ft square on a paved apron. The lifting gear is a knob, rolled per settlement from the map's seed and weighted by the water: a settlement on shallow water (a valley floor, a river plain) mostly draws sweeps, one on deeper water (an upland, a terrace) mostly draws pulley frames. A roof over the well is a guess in its frequency, and a map drawing a roof on every well draws the finished form, not the common one.

The rule the map follows: a wellhead is a square curb on a paved apron, drawn at the marker size the grain requires; each settlement rolls its gear from the seed - on shallow water about three wells in four take a SWEEP (a pole about as long as the marker is wide, crossing the curb from a post outside it), on deeper water about three in four take a PULLEY FRAME (two posts and a beam across the curb) - and a roof is drawn over about one well in three. The three-in-four split and the one-in-three roof are guesses; the two gears and the depth that chooses between them are attested.
