What does a communal well look like from above - curb, sweep or pulley, roof?

Sources: yamaguchi-ouchi-ido (a Muromachi shaft), fukuoka-museum-ido (why no curb survives), kotobank-tsurube-ido (the old wooden curbs and the two lifting gears), ndl-crd-tsurube-ido (a measured wellhead), bunka-tokugawaen-ido (a stone curb with a roof and a pulley), hiratsuka-tsurube (the gear chosen by the well's depth), yokkaichi-hanetsurube (the pulley as the ordinary gear), seiyo-karihama-ido (the paved wellside), whb-gujing (the Chinese town well's curb and platform)

The curb. A square or round frame round the shaft's mouth - in older wells a bent-wood hoop or a square frame of timbers, in finer ones cut stone laid in the crossed-timber pattern of the igeta. One bucket well photographed and measured for a modern book of old implements is 118 cm by 118 cm, about 4 ft square; the book does not date it. No page read here measures a premodern curb, and an excavated well never keeps the curb that stood above ground, but the shaft it stood round has: a stone-lined well of the Muromachi period in Yamaguchi is about 1 m across inside. A curb of about 4 ft round a 1 m shaft leaves a frame of about 4 in (0.11 m) a side, so the 4 ft is calibrated to the premodern shaft, and its exact figure is a guess. Round the communal wells of Karihama, a village on the Ehime coast, the ground was often paved with stones.

The lifting gear: two forms. The bucket was raised either by a balance-beam lever - a sweep - or on a rope over a pulley. A museum guide calls the rope and pulley the ordinary gear, and the sweep a pole worked like a seesaw with a weight at the far end. Which one a well had followed its depth: in the Hiratsuka city area the shallowest wells were worked with a bare bamboo pole, a somewhat deeper one took a sweep - a pole crossed over a post with a bag of stones at its butt - and the pulley well was less common because the wells were shallow. From above the two read differently: a sweep is a long pole lying across the well from a post beside it; a pulley hangs from a roof, or from a frame set over the well.

The roof. A well house was nothing more than posts beside the well carrying a roof (Wells - the research, and the deliberate liberty). A registered well of about 1900, in the Meiji period, at Tokugawa-en in Nagoya shows the finished form: a cut-granite curb, dressed posts beside it, a gabled roof, and an iron pulley hung from it, the whole on about 1.6 square meters. How many village or town wells had a roof, and how long a sweep's pole was, no page read says.

The Chinese town well. The curb of an old Chinese well might have two, three or four openings on one shaft so the buckets would not knock together, and the platform round it usually carried separate ditches for clean and for waste water.

What it means for the map. The wellhead stays a location marker drawn larger than life, a drawing convention; the real head is a curb about 4 ft square on a paved apron. The lifting gear is a knob, rolled per settlement from the map's seed and weighted by the water: a settlement on shallow water mostly draws poles and sweeps, one on deeper water mostly draws pulleys. Which ground held shallow water and which deep is a guess - a valley floor or river plain taken as shallow, an upland or terrace as deep. A roof over the well is a guess in its frequency (how many village or town wells had one is not known), and a map drawing a roof on every well draws the finished form, not the common one. The 4 ft curb is calibrated to a premodern shaft; the gears and the roof are premodern forms (the question linked above); the one-in-three roof is a guess.

The rule the map follows: a wellhead is a square curb on a paved apron, drawn at the marker size the map's scale requires; each settlement rolls its gear from the seed - on shallow water about three wells in four take a SWEEP (a pole about as long as the marker is wide, crossing the curb from a post outside it; the bare pole of the shallowest wells, which had no post, is drawn the same way, a convention), on deeper water about three in four take a PULLEY FRAME (two posts and a beam across the curb: a premodern frame, its drawn proportions a guess) - and a roof is drawn over about one well in three. The three-in-four split and the one-in-three roof are guesses; the two gears and the depth that chooses between them are attested.
