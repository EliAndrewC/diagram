Public bathhouses (sento)

A public bathhouse served townspeople who had no bath of their own: in the castle towns ordinary townspeople went to one, while country households each had a bath at home. Its costs were firewood and water, and its fire was a danger to the town. Edo kept about one for every two thousand inhabitants, and a district of villages near Edo a single one among nineteen villages.

  • Ordinary townspeople had no bath and went to the bathhouse.
    So it was in the Edo period in the castle town of Takada and in Naoetsu-imamachi.
  • Country households each had a bath, and heated them in turn.
    In the farming districts every house had a bath, but to save firewood and labor the households of a settlement took turns heating one and bathed at one another's.
  • The commercial bathhouse is old in China too.
    In Hangzhou, the capital of the Southern Song, its keepers had a guild of their own, the "fragrant water guild".

How many bathhouses a place kept rose with its size.

  • How many did a great city keep?
    About one for every 1,700 to 2,000 inhabitants. Edo in the Bunka era (1804-1818) is said to have had more than 600 bathhouses for some 1.0 to 1.2 million inhabitants.
  • A provincial castle town's bathhouses formed a guild and held at one number for a century.
    Takada's formed a guild of 19 in 1769 and stayed at 19 until the end of the shogunate; Naoetsu-imamachi's kept to 8 to the same end.
  • How many inhabitants did each of Takada's bathhouses serve?
    Roughly 1,300. Takada counted 24,571 inhabitants in the national ranking of 1886, which against the 19 is one bathhouse per 1,300 or so. The figure is rough: that census came nearly twenty years after the count it is set against, and neither source gives the castle town's population in the Edo period itself.
  • A district of nineteen villages kept one bathhouse, in its largest village.
    In 1829 the nineteen villages of what is now Yashio city, 5,672 inhabitants, had a single bathhouse, in Hachijo, a village of 856, the largest of them and a hub of traffic.

What a bathhouse burned and what it risked shaped its premises.

  • Bathing stayed mixed because separate tubs cost space, firewood and water.
    The bathhouses were mixed from their beginnings into about the middle of the Meiji era: separate tubs for men and women would have needed more space and more firewood and water, and the business would not have paid.
  • Bathhouses were forbidden to open in strong wind, for fear of fire.
    The rule was one of fire prevention.
  • How big was a bathhouse?
    No page we read gives the floor size of a bathhouse of the Edo period, of its furnace room or of its woodpile.
