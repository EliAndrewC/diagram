Pottery kilns (noborigama)

A noborigama, a climbing kiln, is a long tunnel of joined firing chambers built up a slope: a firebox at the bottom, the chambers climbing above it, and a flue and chimney at the top. The early kilns we found measured ran some 55 to 80 ft up their hills and about 7 ft wide, and the porcelain villages later built shared kilns over 300 ft long. A firing was stoked round the clock for days, and the kiln stood where its clay was.

The same works fired roof tiles and everyday pots, and the households that worked it lived at it. A kiln district was a village of kiln houses, not a workshop its potters walked out to from town.

  • A climbing kiln is a row of chambers up a slope, not a mound.
    Its lowest chamber is the firebox, the kiln mouth, and several firing chambers follow one another up the slope above it; the chambers grow larger toward the top. A charcoal kiln, by contrast, is a low heap of logs built in a hemisphere or cone around a central chimney.
  • How long was one works' kiln?
    About 55 to 80 ft, in the two early kilns we found measured. The smallest, thought to have been the hobby kiln of Hosokawa Tadaoki, lord of Kokura at the start of the Edo period, has a stoke mouth and four chambers and is 16.6 m (~54 ft) long. The oldest climbing kiln in Gifu, at Motoyashiki in Mino, is 24 m (~79 ft) long and on average 2.2 m (~7 ft) wide, with fourteen chambers above its firebox.
  • The porcelain villages built kilns of 100 m (~328 ft) and more, shared among several kiln houses.
    The newer kilns of Arita were built larger, some 100 m (~328 ft) or more, and used jointly by several kiln houses. Mikawachi fired an east kiln 120 m (~394 ft) long from the end of the 1600s to 1937, and a west kiln of 100 m (~328 ft) from the early 1700s to 1942. A kiln of that length served several kiln houses at once, a different thing from one works with its own kiln.
  • The works' own workshop stood right beside its kiln.
    Around the Motoyashiki kilns their workshops and waste heaps have been found, and an older kiln there became the workshop of the No. 3 kiln once that was built.
  • How far did a kiln stand from the houses round it?
    No page we read says.
  • A firing ran for days, stoked round the clock, and took as long again to cool.
    An anagama's firing takes anywhere from 48 hours to twelve days or more, depending on the kiln's volume, with stoking round the clock, and the kiln generally takes the same time to cool. That a climbing kiln fired the same way is this project's reading.
  • Why did a kiln stand at its clay?
    Early kilns were sometimes dug as tunnels into banks of clay, and Jingdezhen, a remote town in hilly country and an unpromising place for potteries, lies close to the best deposits of petuntse, the stone its porcelain was made from. That the clay, a slope for the chambers and the fuel drew digging, throwing, drying and firing to one spot is this project's conclusion.

What the kilns fired was mostly the town's everyday ware; roof tile was a smaller trade.

  • Tile kilns and pottery kilns were built side by side from the Nara period on.
    From the eighth century, kilns for Sue ware were built in each region alongside the kilns that fired roof tiles for the provincial temples.
  • Few houses had tile roofs before the end of the Edo period.
    Even in the Edo period the houses of ordinary commoners were generally roofed with boards, and tile roofing became widespread only at its end. A tile roof was mended a tile at a time: a broken tile can be taken out and replaced. No page we read gives how long a tile roof lasts.
  • Did a town use more pots than tiles?
    In this project's reading, yes. Pottery lasts, and survives in the ground as shards long after other things have decayed, but bowls, pots and jars break and are replaced on a steady cycle, which is our premise, not the page's. A heavy, cheap storage jar, a kame, would be made near where it was used, by Alfred Weber's location theory, which puts a trade near its raw material when its goods are costly to carry; that a jar falls on that side is this project's application.
  • Fine stoneware needs a hotter firing than coarse ware.
    Stoneware is fired at about 1,100 to 1,300 degrees C, and earthenware can be fired as low as 600 degrees; Seto ware is made in Seto. That stoneware was a whole district's specialty rather than every town's is this project's reading.

The people who fired the kilns lived among them, in China and in Japan.

  • Ming China's artisans were registered as households of their own.
    The artisan households formed a class of their own in the registers, were bound to serve in the government's craft bureaus and could not change their registration at will. At Jingdezhen the imperial kilns stood in the center of the city, with many other kilns four kilometers (~2.5 mi) away at Hutian.
  • Did the potters live at the kilns?
    In this project's reading, yes: a kiln district was a village of kiln houses. At Imado, on the edge of Edo, about fifty households made Imado ware at the end of the Edo period; medieval Tokoname's kiln sites are said to number over a thousand, and perhaps several thousand. No page we read says outright where the kiln workers slept.
  • Edo's fire law kept open fires out of the town.
    In 1653 the city ordered, for fire prevention, that bathhouse fires be kept burning only until about six in the evening, and in 1648 it banned the setting off of fireworks anywhere but the river mouth, and their making in town. That kilns in particular were pushed out by law is this project's reading.
  • Potters were not hinin.
    The hinin of the Edo period were at root beggars, who also served in cleaning and as executioners; no craft trade is among their offices. The rules that set a tanning yard apart turn on the outcast quarter; a kiln's turn on clay, fuel and fire.
