Tanning yards

In Edo-period Japan, before 1868, the hides a tanner worked did not come from a butcher. When an ox or a horse died, it went, without payment, to the outcast kawata community that held the right to fallen animals over that district, and that community held the trade in leather, which armies could not do without. So a tanning yard worked the dead draft stock of a whole district, and its work was small and seasonal.

Tanning was a water trade. The Japanese method steeped salted hides in a river for several days, until the action of microorganisms loosened the hair, scraped the hair off with a blade, and finished the hide with salt and rapeseed oil in the sun. The tanners lived at the edge of the castle town, at a chokepoint on the roads or on the riverbank, and the trade was ranked noxious and kept to the outskirts.

  • Hides came from fallen draft stock, not from butchery.
    In the Edo period the right to take the carcasses of cattle and horses that had died was held, as a monopoly, by the outcast community, and the district in which a holder collected them was called the kusaba or danna-ba. An owner of whatever status, under whatever lord, had to hand a dead ox or horse over to the right-holder without payment.
  • The outcast community held a monopoly on leather, which armies needed.
    It worked the dead cattle and horses for its income and held the leather production that arms and armor depended on. The warlords of the Sengoku period, building their castle towns, placed the leather producers under special protection and control to keep a steady supply of the leather their armies needed.
  • In this setting, a domain's surplus horses go to its farms.
    Daimyo with more horses than they need give them to village headmen to share out among the farms as draft animals, and such horses are often slaughtered and eaten when the need for meat outweighs the farmers' means to feed them. The farms' draft stock is where a tanner's hides come from.
  • How many carcasses did a district send its tanners in a year?
    No page we read gives a count for any district.
  • The largest medieval tannery excavated at Northampton, England, had 36 or 37 pits.
    The excavated tanneries there were of round and rectangular pits, and all were small; the largest, the Western Tannery, had 36 to 37.

The method set where a tannery could stand: by running water, and at the edge of town.

  • The Japanese method soaked the hides in a river for several days.
    The white-tanned leather of Himeji, shironameshi, is made by steeping salted hides in the river for several days while microorganisms loosen the hair roots, scraping the hair off with a blade, then adding salt and rapeseed oil, beating the hide dry and kneading it soft in the sun. The hide is salted before the soak as well as after it.
  • Other tanning traditions used urine, lime and dung.
    Tanners elsewhere took the hair off by soaking the skin in urine, painting it with lime or letting it rot, and softened it by pounding in dung, commonly a dog's or a pigeon's. The steps the Himeji account lists use none of these.
  • Tanning was ranked a noxious trade and kept to the outskirts of town.
    It was counted an "odoriferous trade" and pushed to the edge of town among the poor, and the first step was to soak the skins in water.
  • The medieval leather workers were named for the riverbed.
    They slaughtered animals and worked hides, and were called kawaramono, "those of the riverbed", because they lived on the riverbeds and around them. The article on them gives two theories of why: that the riverbed was untaxed, and that leather work needs a great deal of water. In the medieval centuries the riverbed was their workplace, for executions as well as for slaughter and leather.
  • Hides were salted so that they would keep until they could be worked.
    Curing a hide with salt stops it rotting in the time between taking it and working it; wet-salted hides are pressed into packs for about 30 days.

What a tannery let into its water mattered to the rice fields below it.

  • What in a tannery's waste water would harm a rice field?
    In this project's reading, the salt. The Himeji method's waste is the soak's rot, salt and an oil film. Rice is moderately sensitive to salt, and it answers to the water of the flooded field it stands in, so brine coming down an irrigation ditch is the wrong thing to send it. The rot is the weaker objection: farmers spread night soil on their fields on purpose.
  • Rice is moderately sensitive to salt.
    The FAO's table of crop salt tolerance ranks paddy rice moderately sensitive, with full yield up to 3.0 dS/m in the soil and 2.0 in the irrigation water.
  • Irrigation water was held by custom and shared out by the villages.
    Over the Edo period the management of water rights passed from the lord's judgment to self-government among villages, through irrigation associations upheld by customary practice, and disputes that in the medieval period ended in violence went to the courts. Rights to farm water drawn before the River Act of 1896 are recognized under modern law.
  • Could an outcast quarter hold a share of the water upstream?
    In this project's reading, no: a community of the lowest caste would not have been granted an intake at the head of a system its villages fought over in the courts, and the water it could hold is the water no one upstream wanted, the tail below every intake. The untaxed-riverbed theory of the kawaramono reads the same way.
  • Did lower fields take up the drainage of the fields above?
    Commonly, in this project's reading, in Japan and China alike - a drain at one level is a supply at the next. It is a GUESS: no page we read says so.
  • When did a field drain run?
    Through the irrigation season, with its heaviest flow when the fields are drained before harvest, and near-dry from harvest to transplanting. It is a GUESS: no page we read describes a drain's flow across the year.
  • When did draft animals die most?
    In late winter and early spring, when the fodder was gone and the animals were worn from the autumn hauling. It is a GUESS: no page we read gives the season.
  • Some tanneries soaked their hides in standing vats instead of a river.
    At the Chouara tannery of Fez, in Morocco, hides are soaked in a series of liquids of urine, pigeon droppings, quicklime, salt and water, held in numerous stone vats.

Where the tanners lived was a matter of their caste as much as of their trade. For where a town's outcast quarter stood and how large it was, see Caste geography and status zoning.

  • Most castle towns had no wall around the town at first.
    In the first castle towns only the lord's castle had a moat and wall, and the town had none; enclosing a whole town in a moat and earthworks, the sogamae, came later.
  • Where in a town did the tanners live?
    At its edge. The outcast community often lived on ground unsuited to farming, at the edge of the village or beside a river; the leather workers the Sengoku warlords took under their control were housed, it seems, at the edge of the castle town, at a chokepoint on the roads, or on the riverbank. That a castle town's quarter stood at the town's edge as a village's did is this project's reading.
  • Takada set its outcast quarter at the town's northwest edge, on the Kaga road.
    When the castle town was laid out around 1614, those who bore its guard and cleaning duties were moved from Naoetsu to a block along the Kaga road at the town's northwest edge.
  • Edo's outcast head lived at the city's northern edge.
    Danzaemon, the hereditary head of the outcasts, lived near the path and canal that ran from the Sumida riverbank along the city's northern edge to the Yoshiwara pleasure quarter. The north was where the Kozukappara execution ground lay and where the pleasure quarter was moved, behind Senso-ji, though Edo had a second great execution ground to the south.
  • Danzaemon's compound was an enclosed precinct within Edo.
    The Asakusa Shinmachi block was screened by temples, shrines and fences so that no one could see in. It held his official and private residences, storehouses, a shrine, a court and a jail, and housing for three or four hundred family members of his officials. That it stood inside the city is this project's reading of its Asakusa address.
  • Which edge of town did the outcast quarter take?
    It differed from town to town: no page we read ties it to the unlucky northeast or to any other bearing. Within one town, though, the quarter, its works and its execution ground went out the same way, toward the edge where the road leaves and the river runs - this project's reading of Edo and Takada, which no page states as a rule.
  • Did towns route the carcass haul around their streets?
    No page we read mentions a designated carcass route; one says only that the handling of fallen cattle and horses was strictly controlled.
  • Where did Chinese towns put their tanners?
    In this project's reading, China had no hereditary outcast caste tied to leather, though it had debased statuses of its own, and its tanners and butchers were ordinary craftsmen, with the noxious water trades in the suburbs outside the walls, the guanxiang, below the water gate. It is a GUESS: no page we read places them.

Only one part of the tanners' work needed the river.

  • The caste's other work was done in town.
    It served as executioners, constables, watchmen and wardens of the hills and the water, made and sold straw and leather-soled sandals, and made and sold lamp wicks. That it worked drum heads and armor lacing too, and that this work was done at home or in town, is this project's reading.
  • Each outcast quarter was a community under its own headman.
    Each settlement had a head who guarded it and was paid by its residents, and in the Kanto the shogunate ruled the outcast and hinin statuses through Danzaemon. That no lone outcast house stood away from its quarter is this project's reading of that corporate form.
  • Did the tanners sleep at the works?
    No page we read says they did. The hides lay several days in the river, where no watchman could guard them anyway. In this project's reading a stolen hide was worth little: whoever took one had handled death-pollution, kegare, and could not sell it, since the outcast community held the right to every carcass in its district and the trade in leather.
