Polder siting - full enclosure, fluctuating water, and where the village sits

Sources: fei-xiaotong, polder-enwiki, dongting-lake-enwiki

(1) Does the dike really surround the paddies completely? Yes - full enclosure IS the defining feature, not a drawing convenience. A weitian is wetland "enclosed with dykes to be hydrologically separated from the surrounding fluctuating water and then drained"44 (the character 圩 means the enclosing embankment); Shen Kuo (Northern Song, Illustrated Records of Wanchun Polder) is credited with describing "dikes and dams... built along the rivers to enclose the farmland, which is called a polder" (unsourced - the zh.wikipedia 万春圩 page carries no such quotation and no sentence about enclosure; it records only the polder's history - built by the Qin family in the late Tang, rebuilt by 14,000 laborers in 90 days, with Shen Kuo possibly taking part and writing the 万春圩图说). The enclosed floor sits at or below the surrounding FLOOD stage, so on a flat lake plain there is no safe side to leave open - any gap re-floods the whole block at the next high water, which is why water crosses only at the two gated sluices.123 The scale is attested too: fragmented small polders were each "cultivated by a group of 10-20 households" behind dikes ~1.5 m high114 - Enokida's 16 households are exactly this unit. (2) Should there be marsh/wetland on all sides then? The outside is "fluctuating water," but that means neither open lake on every side nor water year-round. Attested surroundings: the lake/creek network the polder was dug from; reed marsh and mudflat where reclamation had not yet reached; NEIGHBOR polders across a shared creek (the mature Taihu landscape was "islet-like" fish-scale polders packed into the water net);45 and - at the district margin - the natural shore, because reclamation advanced FROM the shore ("mudflats along rivers and lakes were transformed"115). The water also FLUCTUATES seasonally: at low stage much of the outside is exposed mudflat and reed, at flood it is sheet water - the dike is built against the flood stage, not the average day.124 Both pool polders are drawn as the landward-margin case: wild header pond NW (the source), reed marsh at the low south outfall side, village on naturally dry ground east. GM ruling (2026-07-24): both polders carry a reed fringe along the west flank, so the map shows that the west dike holds water back. (3) Should the farmhouses be INSIDE the dike? Both siting patterns are attested, split by what dry ground exists. Where the polder abuts the natural shore, the village sits on the landward dry ground at the dike's shoulder - the way both maps are drawn, and the natural one for a margin polder (nobody lives on a flood-fighting earthwork when real dry ground is a few steps east). In the DEEP-water landscape with no natural dry ground, settlement used the polder's own raised earth instead: linear villages ON the dikes (canal-dike settlement "taking advantage of the elevated typology" is attested from the 8th century on116) or houses inside the polder along its interior streams (Fei Xiaotong's Kaixiangong / the Xichang polder layout).46 So Enokida and Kuwabata are correct AS margin polders - but an "islet" polder map, water-ringed on all sides, would REQUIRE dike-top or interior housing. GM ruling (2026-07-24): dike-top housing is attested and is available as a variation the GM has not yet taken up; the GM chose NOT to convert any existing settlement. One more confirmation from the same research: the polder community IS its dike-maintenance community ("sustaining a polder necessitated a close-knit community," often one extended family, which named its polder)117 - so a polder village always sits hard against its own block.
