Polder siting - full enclosure, fluctuating water, and where the village sits

Sources: fei-xiaotong, miragenews-polders, polder-enwiki, dongting-lake-enwiki

(1) Does the dike really surround the paddies completely? Yes - full enclosure IS the defining feature, not a drawing convenience. A weitian is wetland "enclosed with dykes to be hydrologically separated from the surrounding fluctuating water and then drained". The oldest account of a polder's dike with figures, Shen Kuo's record of Wanchun Polder (1061), is read below. The enclosed floor sits at or below the surrounding FLOOD stage, so on a flat lake plain there is no safe side to leave open - any gap re-floods the whole block at the next high water, which is why water crosses only at gated openings, the sluices opened when the water outside is low. The pool polders draw two; a larger polder had as many as its fields needed, drains outnumbering intakes (below). The scale is not attested: that fragmented small polders were each cultivated by a group of 10-20 households, behind dikes about 1.5 m high, is on no page read - Enokida's 16 households fall inside that unverified range, and the dike heights that were read are below. (2) Should there be marsh/wetland on all sides then? The outside is "fluctuating water," but that means neither open lake on every side nor water year-round. What lay outside, in this project's reading: the lake and creek network the polder was dug from; reed marsh and mudflat where reclamation had not yet reached; NEIGHBOR polders across a shared creek (by the early 20th century the old polders had been cut into even smaller "islet-like" fish-scale polders); and - at the district margin - the natural shore, because reclamation advanced FROM the shore, a claim no page read carries. The water also FLUCTUATES seasonally: a great lake shrinks from thousands of square kilometers in the flood season to a few hundred in the dry season, so at low stage much of the outside lies bare; that it lies as mudflat and reed, and that the dike is built against the flood stage and not the average day, is this project's reading. Both pool polders are drawn as the landward-margin case: wild header pond NW (the source), reed marsh at the low south outfall side, village on naturally dry ground east. GM ruling (2026-07-24): both polders carry a reed fringe along the west flank, so the map shows that the west dike holds water back. (3) Should the farmhouses be INSIDE the dike? Houses on the dike margin are attested (Fei, below); houses on the landward dry ground are how both maps are drawn, and splitting the two by what dry ground exists is this project's reading, a guess. Where the polder abuts the natural shore, the village sits on the landward dry ground at the dike's shoulder - the way both maps are drawn, and in this project's reading the natural one for a margin polder, where real dry ground is a few steps east (a guess: Fei's houses, below, stood on a margin that was also the dike). Where there was no natural dry ground, settlement used the polder's own raised earth instead: linear villages ON the dikes (that canal-dike settlement dates from the 8th century is on no page read) or houses along the village's streams and on the raised margin of each polder, which is also its dike (Fei Xiaotong's Kaixiangong / the Xichang polder layout). So Enokida and Kuwabata are correct AS margin polders - but an "islet" polder map, water-ringed on all sides, would REQUIRE dike-top or interior housing. GM ruling (2026-07-24): dike-top housing is attested and is available as a variation the GM has not yet taken up; the GM chose NOT to convert any existing settlement. One more point, which no page read carries: the polder community was its own dike-maintenance community, often one extended family, which named its polder - so, in this project's reading, a polder village sits hard against its own block.
