Polder dikes: what they were made of, how big, and what grew on them

A polder's dike was a bank of earth heaped round fields reclaimed from a lake, a river or a marsh, to hold the water out. In the dike-pond country of the Pearl River delta it was made of the mud dug out of the ponds, and the one polder dike whose size survives from before modern times, a great one of the Song dynasty, was about five times as broad at its foot as it was high. A village's own dike stood far lower than that.

Dikes were planted along their length: willows by the water, whose roots bind a bank against the waves, and, where silk was made, mulberry along the crest. Those trees were cut low for their shoots and leaves and stood bare in winter, so in this project's reading they did not do the work of the tall groves that sheltered farmhouses from the wind.

  • The one premodern polder dike with recorded figures is a great one: the Wanchun polder's.
    Shen Kuo's record of the Wanchun polder, whose restoration was taken up in 1061, gives its dike as six zhang broad and one zhang two chi high, and 84 li long, with a road inside it 22 li long, wide enough for two carts and lined with willows, and five water gates. So the dike was five times as broad as it was high.
  • The Wanchun dike was broad at its foot and narrow at its crown.
    To the fear that wind and water would wear the dike away, its rebuilders answered that its foot would be several zhang broad and its crown, the flat top, only a few chi, with a hundred rows of willows set along it and reeds planted below them, so that the water would bite a hundred paces out and never meet the dike.
  • Polders ran from a few tens of mu to over a hundred thousand.
    Their shapes were square, round, oblong, many-sided or irregular, as the ground gave. The greatest were described in the Song dynasty as several tens of li across, "like a great city", with channels inside and gates outside.
  • How high and how broad was a village polder's dike?
    No page we read gives a small polder's own dike. The figures that were read bound it from above: in this project's reading a village's own ring stood far below a great official polder's dike, and below a domain's river dike, which in Echizen was about 3.6 m (~12 ft) high on a base of about 5.5 m (~18 ft) (the next bullet).

Japan's records give the river dikes, and the ring dikes of the waju, the diked communities of the Nobi plain.

  • Early Edo river dikes in Echizen were about 3.6 m (~12 ft) high on a base of about 5.5 m (~18 ft).
    The dikes built on the Kuzuryu river in the early Edo period were, with differences from place to place, about 3 ken across the base, 1 ken across the crown and 2 ken high: 5.45 m (~18 ft), 1.82 m (~6 ft) and 3.64 m (~12 ft). They ran for kilometers along the river, and up to the Edo period the usual forms there were open-ended dikes and ring dikes.
  • A ring dike's height was not its villagers' alone to choose.
    On the Nobi plain it was settled that the dikes on the Mino side should stand 3 shaku, about 1 m (~3 ft), lower than the great Oakoi dike, so the Mino side had to defend itself locally and built waju.
  • Inside a waju, the banks raised against the fields' own standing water were smaller than the ring dike.
    They were built as flood defense required, and went by many local names.
  • How thick was a great waju's ring dike at its foot?
    Perhaps of the order of 40 m (~135 ft). The shared drain of eighteen villages in one waju, on the Ogure river, was seven culverts, each 41.1 m (~135 ft) long, passing through the dike. That the dike there was about that thick at its foot is this project's reading: a culvert may run out beyond the dike's toes, the feet of its two slopes, on either side.

What a dike was made of is recorded best in the Pearl River delta, where the fields were dug into fishponds and the spoil heaped into the dikes between them.

  • A dike-pond's dike was the mud dug out of its pond.
    A Shunde account has the mud taken and spread round the sides to make the dike, the hollow sunk in the middle being the pond.
  • A pond's dike was raised and repaired with pond mud two or three times a year.
    Each time a pond was drained, the mud at its bottom was dredged up onto the dikes, and an aged pond's dike near the pond gate tended to collapse in heavy rain.
  • Did a polder's outer dike follow the water's edge?
    This project's GUESS is that it did, in gentle curves and bends, while the ditches and paddies inside ran straighter; no page we read describes a polder's outline. The polders of the Tai Lake basin were not one fixed shape: by the early twentieth century the small polders were divided into still smaller "fish-scale polders", named for their shapes on aerial photographs.
  • Rectangular polders are a modern form.
    After China's economic reform of 1978, the organic forms of the Tai Lake polders were replaced by standardized rectangles for industrial farming.

Every dike that was read was planted, and the planting held the bank together.

  • Willow roots protect a bank against the water.
    Willows are often planted along streams so that their interlacing roots protect the bank. That a bare bank of heaped mud gullies in the rain, is gnawed at its foot by the waves and slumps into a breach is this project's reasoning.
  • On the Wanchun polder, rows of mulberry flanked the dike's ridge and rows of willows ran along it, with reeds below.
    The record counts some tens of thousands of mulberry trees on the dike, and has the willows and reeds keep the water a hundred paces off.
  • In the Pearl River delta, the pond dikes were planted with mulberry, fruit trees and sugarcane.
    Mulberry was planted on the dike and fish stocked in the pond, and the dug earth was built into dikes with fruit trees and cane on top. Mulberry leaves fed the silkworms and the silkworms' droppings fed the fish.
  • Which trees stood on which side of the dike?
    Willow by the water and mulberry on the crest, in this project's reading of the Wanchun record, whose willows had reeds below them and whose mulberry flanked the ridge. Willow grows on moist ground, and its rod-like shoots, often grown on pollards, were woven into baskets, fish traps and fences. A Ming river manual has the long willows along a dike yield big branches every year for fascines.
  • By Lake Tai, a polder's raised margin was both its dike and its mulberry ground.
    In Fei Xiaotong's village in the 1930s, ten to thirty meters (~33-98 ft) of land along the margin of each polder was left for mulberry, with a wider space for houses, and this higher margin served as the farm's dike. Dredged pond mud raised the dikes and fertilized their soil.
  • Today's pond dikes are narrower and barer than they were.
    In one modern study, dikes that used to be 20 m (~66 ft) wide have been eroded to less than 4 m (~13 ft) as the ponds were enlarged, and the cane and mulberry that once stood on them have declined.
  • Dike trees were planted in rows along the bank.
    The Wanchun dike's mulberry and willows stood in rows, and so did the willows on the levees of the Willow Palisade, a border earthwork of the Qing dynasty in the northeast.
  • How close together were the trees in a row?
    The nearest figure read is not from a polder: the Willow Palisade's statute set three willow stakes every 5 chi, about 1.67 m (~5.5 ft), along the embankment, each four cun, about 13 cm (~5 in), thick and six chi, about 2 m (~7 ft), long. It is an analog, from a border embankment, not a figure for a polder's dike.
  • How far apart were the rows?
    About 3 chi, roughly 1 m (~3 ft), on the great river dikes of the north. Pan Jixun's Ming river manual plants lying willow densely about 2-3 chi from the dike's foot and long willow about 5-6 chi from the dike, both on the ground off the dike's foot rather than on its faces. The distance between the rows is this project's arithmetic from those two figures, and the manual is for the Yellow River, an analog for a polder.

Farmhouses were sheltered from the wind by groves of their own (Groves of trees around farmhouses (yashikirin)), and the trees on a dike did not do that work.

  • Could the dike's trees shelter a village from the wind?
    Very little, in this project's reading. A windbreak slows the wind for up to 30 times its height downwind, and prevents wind erosion for 10 to 20 times its height, so its shelter grows with its height. Willows cut back for their shoots stand low, and a single row of them is thin. That dike willows were cut so, and what follows for shelter, is this project's reasoning.
  • Willow and mulberry stand bare in winter, the season of Japan's northwest wind.
    Willows are typically deciduous, and so is the mulberry, while in Japan the northwest wind prevails in winter. That winter is the season when shelter matters most is this project's reading.
  • On the Sendai plain the homestead groves stand on the north and west, of cedar, pine, hinoki and zelkova.
    Many igune stand on the north and west sides of the homestead as windbreak and snowbreak groves, and their trees are sugi, pine, hinoki and keyaki.
  • Does a sheet of ponds slow the wind less than land?
    In this project's reading, yes. Over open water the wind slows near the surface far less than over land: by 20 to 30 percent, against 40 to 50 over rough ground. That a sheet of ponds does the same on a smaller scale, so a village on the downwind dike gets the wind little slowed, is this project's reading.
  • Dike trees and homestead groves are recorded in different landscapes.
    The Pearl River delta planted mulberry on its pond dikes, and inside a Japanese waju a homestead had a windbreak grove to its north and a flood-protection grove to its south. No page we read shows both in one landscape: they are different plantings for different jobs.
