What does rice land look like where there is no valley floor to spread across?

Sources: tanada-jawiki (the terrace, when and where it was built, and its small cells), suido-ishizue-tanada (terraces opened by hand, their paddies small and irregular), titian-zhwiki (where China's terraces lie), suido-ishizue-yachida (the valley paddy, everywhere and old), yachida-jawiki (the valley paddy is long and narrow), kotobank-yatoda (its regional names, and that it was the typical medieval farm landscape)

Wet rice needs a level basin that holds water, and the valley-bottom fan most of these maps draw is simply the cheapest way to get one. Where the ground will not give it, two other forms do.

On a hillside, the terrace. The basin is cut INTO the slope: a tanada is a paddy made on sloping ground. Read that way, as this page reads it, the terrace is the answer to ground without a floor, not a rarity. In the Edo period, once the plains had little room left for new fields, paddies were built up steep mountain slopes in western Japan, while much of the east never cut terraces that steep. It was small work, not the planned development of lords or great merchants, done with little more than a hoe, and each paddy it made was small and irregular in shape. In China the terraces lie mainly in the southern mountains, most of them in Guangxi and Yunnan; the terraces of the Ailao mountains in Yunnan and the Longji terraces of Longsheng are the better known.

In a narrow stream valley, the valley paddy. The basin runs WITH the valley instead. Paddies cut into small valleys are found all over Japan; one view holds that in the middle ages they were the most stable, standard farmland of all. The yachida is a long, narrow paddy lying along a valley's wet floor. It went by many local names - yatoda and yachida in the east, tanada and yamada around the old capital, sakoda further west - and such valley fields made the typical farm landscape of the middle ages. That the brook threads down the middle of the chain is this project's reading of the form; no page read says where the stream ran.

In both, the cell stays small. A terraced paddy covers only a small area apiece. That the cap is set by flooding a basin to an even depth by hand is this page's reasoning, so a terrace step and a valley floor alike read as a ROW or a CHAIN of small paddies rather than as one long sheet.

The rule the map follows: on hill ground, rice is drawn as stacked contour terrace bands stepping down the slope from the catchment above, a supply channel running down one flank and cascading from step to step into a drain at the foot. A map that draws terraces carries at least eight retaining banks across the slope, and every step is divided along the contour into cells of the leveled-basin size the record gives, so a step reads as a row of small paddies rather than one continuous band. Hill ground is what unlocks the form; a map with no hill ground is drawn as the ordinary valley fan.

The rule the map follows: in a confined valley, rice is drawn as one long narrow field meandering down the valley floor with the brook down its center, stepped down the fall by cross banks and split across its width into cells of the same leveled-basin size, so it reads as a chain of small paddies rather than a single sheet; the field's outline stays markedly longer than it is wide. Narrow-valley ground is what unlocks the form.
