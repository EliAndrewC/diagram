What does rice land look like where there is no valley floor to spread across?

Sources: tanada-jawiki; this project's general reading of the terraced rice landscapes of south China and mainland Southeast Asia (Yuanyang 元陽, Longsheng 龍勝); no page is cited for it

Wet rice needs a level basin that holds water, and the valley-bottom fan most of these maps draw is simply the cheapest way to get one. Where the ground will not give it, two other forms do. On a hillside the basin is cut INTO the slope and held by a retaining bank, and the stepped hill terrace is the standard answer wherever valley-bottom paddy is impossible - it is the defining alternative to the valley fan, not a rarity (this rests on general reading; no source is cited). Where a stream valley is too narrow to spread across, the basin runs WITH the valley instead: a chain of small fields following the floor down, the brook threading between them (this too rests on general reading; no source is cited). In both, the leveled cell stays the size it is everywhere else - a basin has to be flooded to an even depth by hand, which is what caps it - so a terrace step and a valley floor alike read as a ROW or a CHAIN of small paddies rather than as one long sheet.

The rule the map follows: on hill ground, rice is drawn as stacked contour terrace bands stepping down the slope from the catchment above, a supply channel running down one flank and cascading from step to step into a drain at the foot. A map that draws terraces carries at least eight retaining banks across the slope, and every step is divided along the contour into cells of the leveled-basin size the record gives, so a step reads as a row of small paddies rather than one continuous band. Hill ground is what unlocks the form; a map that declares no ground of its own is drawn as the ordinary valley fan.

The rule the map follows: in a confined valley, rice is drawn as one long narrow field meandering down the valley floor with the brook down its center, stepped down the fall by cross banks and split across its width into cells of the same leveled-basin size, so it reads as a chain of small paddies rather than a single sheet; the field's outline stays markedly longer than it is wide. Narrow-valley ground is what unlocks the form.
