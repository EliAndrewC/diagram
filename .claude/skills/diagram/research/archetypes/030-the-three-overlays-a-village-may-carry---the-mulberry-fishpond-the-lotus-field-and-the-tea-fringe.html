The three overlays a village may carry - the mulberry fishpond, the lotus field and the tea fringe

Sources: fortune-1843, nelumbo-nucifera-enwiki

  • The mulberry fishpond (桑基魚塘, sangji yutang) - dug where 「the low-lying ground was dug deep to make fishponds」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「将地势低洼处深挖为鱼塘」), the low flood-prone hollows, as a flood adaptation that turned the hollow into a pond and heaped its mud into a dike. A scatter of dike-ponds among rice was the system's NORMAL state. Shunde county was ~4.6% dike-pond in 1581 (our arithmetic - the pond figure is on a readable page, the county's cultivated total that makes the denominator is not; Longshan, one of its two earliest pond districts, 18% of its hill, dry land and ponds - 8,124 of 44,947 mu, a total that by this page's reading leaves out its paddy, so not a share like the county's); a 1980s survey of the heartland found rice at 25% against the fishponds' own 35%; and at Lake Tai, in Fei's 1930s village, mulberry sat on the raised margin of each polder (yu), which served as its dike, with rice remaining the main crop. Where the change went furthest it was singled out: at Longjiang and Longshan, 「within the district there are mulberry ponds and no fish ponds」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「境内有桑塘无鱼塘」). The wall-to-wall landscape is the rare end state of a plot-by-plot process that arose in the early Ming and peaked in the late Qing (dig one low plot into a pond, pile the spoil into a dike; by this page's guess a one-household, one-dry-season job), which is what the whole-landscape mulberry-dike-fishpond ARCHETYPE is for. At that scale the ponds really had engulfed the ordinary ground too, so the whole-landscape form is not held to the low-ground rule. Negative result: the outer-delta 沙田 shatian were equally low and equally wet and stayed in rice - low ground is necessary but not sufficient; the dike-pond zone also had smallholder tenure inside an established polder and water access to Canton.
  • The lotus field (藕田, outian) - models deep-water lotus (深水藕, shenshui ou, the deep cultivars grown in more than 40 cm of water, the species ranging to 2.5 m) against the shallow water a paddy holds, so it physically cannot sit on high ground. Shallow-water lotus (浅水藕, qianshui ou, 10-20 cm) in ordinary paddy is equally real but is deliberately NOT modeled: it is an economic choice rotating with rice (稻藕轮作, dao ou lunzuo, the rice-lotus rotation), driven by a choice these maps do not model, and drawing it would be visually indistinguishable from uniform random scatter. The lotus field is a premodern form in both China and Japan (below). How much of a village it took is above and below.
  • The tea fringe (茶, cha) - tea took the lower, FERTILE hillside, never the low lands (Robert Fortune, eyewitness, 1843: "always situated on the lower and most fertile sides of the hills, and never on the low lands"; the upper slopes were forest, sweet potato and peanut). The boundary rule states itself - the line is the highest irrigation ditch. Form is smallholder: recognizable rows in acre-scale patches, one small garden to a cottager, so a hillside carries many small gardens, not one contiguous block (this page's reading of that passage). Two anachronisms to avoid: neatly contour-terraced tea is post-1949, a state program; and bund-margin tea (畦畔茶, keihan-cha) is a Japanese practice with no Chinese equivalent.
