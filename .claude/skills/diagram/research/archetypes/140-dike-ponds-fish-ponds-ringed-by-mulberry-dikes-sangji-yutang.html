Dike-ponds: fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes (sangji yutang)

A dike-pond was a fish pond dug out of low ground, with its mud heaped round it into a dike planted with mulberry. The mulberry leaves fed silkworms, the silkworms' droppings fed the fish, and the mud dredged from the pond was banked back up around the mulberry. Most ponds in the Pearl River delta were rectangles of an acre or so and a few feet deep, inside dikes some 20 to 30 ft wide.

Water and dike were kept in proportion, so that the dike's mulberry could feed the fish in the water beside it. Modern writers put the split at about six parts water to four of dike, though some give it the other way round; the oldest figures found, from the seventeenth century, are one township's shares of its land. A pond was not sealed off: it lay among the creeks and canals of the polder around it, which was watered and drained through sluice gates in the polder's own dike.

  • A dike-pond's water and dike were kept in proportion, so that the dike could feed the fish.
    A modern working paper on integrated fish farming says the mulberry and the fish ponds must be in harmonious proportion to supply the fish with enough feed and fertilizer. From the experience of the Pearl River delta's farmers, it says, one mu of mulberry yields the worm waste, pupae and waste water to feed one mu of pond, so where the mulberry alone feeds the fish, dike and water are equal, one to one.
  • How much of a dike-pond was water?
    About six parts in ten, in the modern figures. The same paper's worked example, a pond of the Dongshan commune near Suzhou, by Lake Tai, has 3.6 mu of water to 2.4 mu of dike, water to dike six to four. Where the fish had feed or fertilizer from beyond the dike, it says, the split could be six to four or seven to three, though in practice it was more complicated and was generally governed by tradition.
  • Accounts differ on which way round the split goes.
    An essay on the dike-pond says the old pattern of "six dike, four pond", six parts dike to four of pond, can no longer be found in the Pearl River delta, while the working paper has water to dike at six to four. The Guangdong gazetteer office names its split water-to-dike and gives it as three-seven to four-six. Read in the order it names, that is three or four parts water to seven or six of dike, the dike-heavy order again, though a split written this way does not by itself fix which side comes first. The sources carry both orders and a seven-to-three variant, so the difference may be regional rather than an error.
  • Every page read that writes the split as a number is modern.
    The six-to-four split is a worked example in the modern working paper, and the gazetteer office's present-day article gives its three-to-seven and four-to-six as the split in general, dated to no period. A modern essay calls "four water, six dike" a scene of former days that survives only in documents, but it names no document and gives no date. No text older than the twentieth century that gives the split as a ratio was found.

The oldest figures found are a seventeenth-century writer's, for one township of the Pearl River delta.

  • Late in the seventeenth century, Qu Dajun gave the fish half of Jiujiang's land, and its fish ponds eight tenths.
    Qu Dajun's account of Guangdong was completed in 1678, early in the Qing dynasty. He says of the township of Jiujiang that its land was cramped and the fish took up half of it, the ponds raising fish and the dikes planted with mulberry. In another passage of the same chapter he puts the township's fish ponds at eight tenths of its land and its fields at two.
  • What split of water and dike do Qu Dajun's two figures give?
    About five parts water to three of dike, if they are read together, and that reading is this project's GUESS. He never sets the two figures side by side, and neither says whether the ponds' eight tenths counts their dikes. Taking the fish's half as the water, and the ponds' eight tenths as water and dikes both, leaves the dikes about three tenths of the land: water to dike about five to three, the water-heavy order and close to six to four. No premodern text read states it.
  • How large was a pond?
    Most modern ponds are 0.4 to 0.6 ha (~1-1.5 acres) and 2 to 3 m (~7-10 ft) deep. Before modern times only the largest are measured: Qu Dajun says that in the villages of Guangzhou's great counties the larger ponds below the planted dikes reached several tens of mu, and ponds built out into the sea were reckoned by the hundred mu. At the working paper's reckoning of six mu to 0.4 ha (~1 acre), the modern pond is six to nine mu, by this project's arithmetic, well below his larger ponds; he gives no ordinary size.
  • How wide was a pond's dike?
    Six to 10 m (~20-33 ft) in modern practice, standing 0.5 to 1 m (~2-3 ft) above the water. Dikes once ran wider: in one Pearl-delta village, Ruxi, a 2024 study reports dikes that used to be 20 m (~66 ft) wide worn to under 4 m (~13 ft) as the ponds grew. No page we read gives a dike's width before modern times.

The dike's mulberry was a crop in its own right, grown for its leaf.

  • Mulberry grown for silkworms was mostly kept as low bushes, picked for leaf many times a year.
    Cultivated mulberry was for the most part trained as low bushes, in one plantation practice pruned once a year to 1.5 to 1.8 m (~5-6 ft). In the Pearl River delta, a modern Chinese agricultural encyclopedia says, the leaves were picked to rear seven or eight crops of silkworms a year.
  • How thickly did mulberry stand on a pond dike?
    In the Pearl River delta's silk district, the same encyclopedia says, the dikes were planted much as any mulberry field on flat land, 8,000 to 10,000 bushes to the mu, cut back to the root. In Jiangsu and Zhejiang the mulberry stood in rows along the pond bank, mostly trained to a low or medium trunk. It gives the delta's figure as current practice, with no date. A mu is about 7,200 sq ft, so that is about one bush to the square foot, and by this project's arithmetic the crowns did not stand apart at all. That figure is one end of a range; the older and sparser plantings are at How thickly was dike mulberry planted, and how wide did a bush grow?
  • No page we read gives how wide a mulberry bush's crown grew.
    The pages that give the pruned height and the spacing are silent on the crown.

A pond's water came and went through the polder it stood in.

  • A dike-pond was not a sealed basin: the polder it stood in was watered and drained through sluices in its dike.
    The villagers of the Sangyuan polder, in the Pearl River delta, watered and drained their land through its dikes, creeks and sluices, reworking ponds to raise fish and planting mulberry beside them to raise silkworms. Around Lake Tai a polder had gates through its dike, opened in drought to draw the river's water in and shut in flood to keep it out. How a polder as a whole was drained is at Polders: fields diked against the fluctuating water (weitian, waju).
  • In the Pearl River delta such a sluice is a dou, and one old dou still bears its carved name.
    The Minle dou, in Xiqiao town, is an old sluice gate, one of the many of the Sangyuan polder, its name carved above its opening in the fourth year of the Guangxu era (1878). A local saying, "Minle has a dou, and every household the sound of spinning and weaving", is remembered as witness to the silk weaving of the district in those days.
  • The ponds were drained two or three times a year, and their mud dredged onto the dikes.
    A modern account of the delta's dike-pond system says so.
  • Did each pond have a sluice through its own dike?
    Only a modern fish-farming training manual describes one: a protected opening in the pond dike, closed with wooden boards to set the water level and screened against losing fish, its inside width best kept to 0.80 m (~31 in) at most. The manual describes no place and no period, and no source from before 1912 was found for a sluice in each pond's dike.
  • No page we read says a pond took its water in at its high side and let it out at its low side.
    The search for a pond fed high and drained low, before 1912, found nothing.
