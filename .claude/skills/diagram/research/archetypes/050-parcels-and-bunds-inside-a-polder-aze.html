Parcels and bunds inside a polder (aze)

A polder's straight lines were its canals. On the lower Yangtze the crossing canals were laid out on a planned lattice, but the land between them was cut into many small parcels: one register of 1897 counts 3,700 parcels in nineteen polders, about three mu (~half an acre) apiece.

In Japan a household's land lay in small parcels scattered among its neighbors', and nothing we read marks on the ground whose a plot was or who rented it. Between the paddies ran narrow ridges of earth, the bunds, about two feet wide or less.

  • The surveyed grid of a lower Yangtze polder was its canals.
    From the tenth century to the thirteenth, the plain around Lake Tai was laid out in tangpu polders, with a cross-canal every five or seven li and a long channel every seven or ten li. The passage spaces the canals; it says nothing of the fields between them.
  • How large was a parcel in a Chinese polder?
    About three mu (~half an acre), on one rough count. The fish-scale register of 1897 for the polders of Taipingqiao, outside Suzhou, counts 3,700 parcels on 11,000 mu over nineteen polders. The average is this project's arithmetic: the count is rough, it takes in about 3,000 mu of marsh and waste as well as rice land, and it is good only to its order of magnitude. No page we read gives a mean parcel size before 1912.
  • No page we read says how many parcels made up a lower Yangtze household's farm.
    Nor how large the farm was.
  • In the Pearl River delta, fish ponds were dug in the low ground inside a dike, and their earth piled into the pond dikes.
    The Shunde account says that pits were later dug in the low-lying places inside the dike to raise fish, the dug earth built into pond dikes, and fruit trees and sugarcane planted on top. How the ponds lay together is at Polders: fields diked against the fluctuating water (weitian, waju).

Japanese village registers show paddy plots of the same small order, and the record shows how a household's plots lay among its neighbors'.

  • How large were the paddy plots in a Japanese village register from before modern times?
    From about half a mu to nearly three. The survey register of Iwamuro village, in Omi province, made in 1602, lists paddy plots from 3 se 22 bu in cultivation up to 1 tan 8 se 1 bu, beside one of 2 se marked permanently waste. At about an are to the se, that runs from about 370 to 1,800 square meters (~4,000-19,400 sq ft); the conversion is this project's arithmetic, and the page gives no average.
  • A Japanese household's land lay in small parcels scattered among other households'.
    An encyclopedia names this "scattered and intermixed holding" - each farmer's holding made of tiny pieces, scattered in various places and intermixed with other land - as the old framework that the land reform after the Second World War could not break.
  • Terraces that look like one landscape are intricately intermixed in their ownership.
    The ownership sorted out when the plains' fields were enlarged was never sorted out in the terraces, so their holdings are intermixed, with little land to a household.

In some villages the holdings did not even stay put.

  • Under warichi, a village re-divided its fields among its landholders by lot.
    A custom of the Edo period, it evened out the tax each household bore against the quality of its ground, and each cultivator's land moved at every redistribution. It was commonest on unstable land: the new fields of the low wet ground along great rivers, and landslide country.
  • A warichi share was made to scatter on purpose.
    The village's land was measured and graded into groups by paddy or dry field, soil, lie of the land and how often it flooded. Each group was divided into as many parts as there were lots, and one part from every group made up one lot.
  • How often were the fields re-divided?
    Every five, ten or twenty years by rule, though in practice the term tended to slip.
  • No page we read says what a warichi share looked like on the ground.
    Whether its parts were cut as equal strips within each group, or marked in any way, is not described.

Who held a plot is one question; who worked it is another.

  • In the setting, most farmers are tenants.
    And most landlords are merchants.
  • No page we read says a rented plot looked any different from a held one.
    A tenant worked plots that were already there, and the pages read speak of holdings and their scatter, none of any sign on the ground that a plot was rented.

The parcels were divided by bunds, and in recent times many were made over into larger, regular plots.

  • The bund dividing two paddies was a narrow ridge of earth.
    In Japan's earliest paddies, of the Yayoi and Kofun periods, the small bunds were 50 cm (~20 in) wide or less and are thought to have been rebuilt every season, inside larger bunds of a meter (~3 ft) or more. A paddy replanned in Gifu in 1869 had bunds two shaku (~2 ft) wide. What else the record found of bunds - how high they stood, how wide one built to be walked on was, why a hand-piled bund's line wandered - is at Bunds between the paddies (aze).
  • Plots of one large, regular rectangle came with machine working, in recent years.
    Japan's consolidation of fields (hojo seibi) has, with mechanized working, made plots of about 30 ares (~0.7 acre), 24 m (~79 ft) by 125 m (~410 ft), up to about a hectare (~2.5 acres), 80 m (~262 ft) by 125 m (~410 ft), common.
