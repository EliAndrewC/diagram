What a polder's dike was made of, and whether it followed the water's edge

Sources: sdlib-shunde-jitang (the dike as pond spoil, planted), miragenews-polders (fish-scale polders named for their aerial outlines; organic forms replaced by standardized rectangles after the 1978 reforms), wuhurec-wanchun (a Song polder dike planted with mulberry and willow, reeds at its foot)

What the research found: a wei-tian 圩田 / dike-pond dike was dredged pond-mud heaped and packed (the 挖塘培基 dig-and-pile cycle that also formed the ponds), PLANTED with mulberry, fruit and cane, crops whose roots bind the soil; on a great polder of the Song dynasty, rows of mulberry flanked its crest and willows ran along its length, with reeds at its foot to hold the water off, and a pond dike was constantly breached and repaired with pond mud - so it read as a mottled, vegetated green-brown band of VARYING width, not a uniform stroke. The polders of the Tai Lake basin were not one fixed shape: the small lake polders were divided again into still smaller "fish-scale polders" (鱼鳞圩, yulin wei), named for their shapes as seen from the air. That the OUTER perimeter dike followed the natural water edge (lake / creek / marsh) in gentle curves and non-square bends, while the ditches and paddies inside it ran straighter, is this project's GUESS. What was read is that the organic forms gave way to standardized rectangles only after the 1978 reforms ("the organic forms of polders were replaced by standardised rectangular patterns"), so a dead-straight rectangular polder would be the one genuinely anachronistic thing on the map.
