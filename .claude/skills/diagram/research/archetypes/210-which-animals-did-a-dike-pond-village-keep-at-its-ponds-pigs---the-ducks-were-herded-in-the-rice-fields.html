Which animals did a dike-pond village keep at its ponds? Pigs - the ducks were herded in the rice fields

Sources: pwsannong-zhusanjiao-nongyeshi (pigs as the dike-pond district's stock and ducks as the sand fields', and the Qing loop that fed the pigs from the ponds), nongzheng-quanshu-41 (a late-Ming instruction to pen livestock on a fish-pond bank), guangdong-xinyu-20 (the delta's ducks herded in the coastal rice fields), zhwiki-nongzheng-quanshu (the compendium's date of publication), zhwiki-xu-guangqi (its compiler's home in Shanghai), zhwiki-guangdong-xinyu (when Qu Dajun's account was completed)

This question is about which animals belonged to the premodern dike-pond village, and how many households kept them. What the modern manuals say about a pig shed or a duck run on a pond dike, and how wide such a dike is, is Does a dike-pond hamlet keep pigs and ducks on its pond dikes?; where a sty stands against the sluice is Does a pig sty have to stand back from the water, or from the pond's sluice?

Pigs were the dike-pond district's animal; ducks were the sand fields'. A modern history of farming in the Pearl River delta, describing how trade reshaped it from the mid-Ming on, names the two side by side: duck-raising in the sand-field (沙田, shatian) district and pig-raising in the dike-pond district. The same history puts the pig inside the Qing loop: feed raised on the pond surface went to the pigs, and pig dung and pond mud went to manure the mulberry. In that telling the dung goes to the dike, where the modern manuals flush it into the water; either way the pig is fed from the pond and feeds the loop back.

A pen on the pond bank is an old instruction. Xu Guangqi's agricultural compendium, printed in 1639, advises in its compiler's own words that a flock of sheep may be penned on the bank of a fish pond, the grass and dung swept into the pond each morning to feed the grass carp, the sheep dung feeding silver carp as well - 「one act, three gains」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「一舉三得」). The book tells it a second time in its fish section, where it is the grass carp's dung that feeds the silver carp. Xu wrote as a man of Shanghai, far from the Pearl River delta, and of sheep, but the passage shows the form - a livestock pen standing on a fish-pond bank so that its waste feeds the fish.

The delta's ducks were field ducks. Qu Dajun, in his account of Guangdong, completed in 1678, says the coastal fields of Guangzhou bred small crabs that ate the rice sprouts and that only ducks could eat them, so the villages kept many ducks, herded in the fields - crabs in spring and summer, gleaned rice in autumn. Duck-keeping ran through landings (埠, the stations through which a district's duck-keeping was organized), each with a holder chosen from those with settled property, answerable when the ducks ate someone's rice. Nothing read places a premodern duck pen at a fish pond; the fenced dry run on a pond dike is a modern manual's form.

How many households kept them is not recorded. No page read gives the share of households in a dike-pond village that kept a pig, or ducks; Qu Dajun's "many" is the nearest. The map's bands - a sty for a quarter to half of the households, a duck pen for a tenth to three tenths - stay guesses.

What this means for the map. A pig sty on a dike-pond hamlet is the village's own animal, attested for the Qing, and stays. A duck pen at a pond is, by the record, a modern form on a premodern map: the delta's ducks of the period were herded in the rice and sand fields, a stock of a paddy village rather than a pond village. The duck pen was drawn because the GM chose it; whether it stays, moves to the paddy hamlets' fields, or is labeled as a later form is the GM's call.
