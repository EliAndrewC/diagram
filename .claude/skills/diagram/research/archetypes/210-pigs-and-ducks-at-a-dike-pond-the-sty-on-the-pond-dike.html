Pigs and ducks at a dike-pond: the sty on the pond dike

In the dike-pond district of the Pearl River delta, the farm animal fed from the ponds was the pig. A modern history of the delta's farming pairs pig-raising with the dike-pond district, as it pairs duck-raising with the sand fields, and puts the pig inside the Qing dike-pond loop: feed raised on the pond surface went to the pigs, and their dung with the pond mud went to manure the mulberry. The delta's ducks were herded in the rice fields, where they ate the small crabs that ate the rice sprouts.

A pen standing on a fish-pond bank, its dung swept into the water each morning to feed the fish, is in a farming compendium printed in 1639. Modern manuals of integrated fish farming go further, building the sty on the dike so that its waste flushes straight into the pond, and no page we read from before 1912, when the Qing dynasty ended, does that.   • A modern history pairs pig-raising with the dike-pond district, and duck-raising with the sand fields.
    A modern history of farming in the delta, describing how trade reshaped it from the mid-Ming on, names the two side by side: duck-raising in the district of 沙田 (shatian, "sand fields") and pig-raising in the dike-pond district.
  • In the Qing, the pig was part of the dike-pond loop.
    The same history has feed raised on the pond surface go to the pigs, and pig dung and pond mud go to manure the mulberry. In that telling the dung goes to the dike, where the modern manuals flush it into the water; either way the pig is fed from the pond and feeds the loop back.
  • A late-Ming farming compendium pens sheep on the bank of a fish pond.
    Xu Guangqi's Nongzheng quanshu, printed in 1639, advises in its compiler's own words that a flock of sheep may be penned on the bank of a fish pond, the grass and dung swept into the pond each morning to feed the grass carp, the sheep dung feeding silver carp as well: "one act, three gains".
  • Xu Guangqi's compendium describes the pond-bank pen a second time, in its section on fish.
    There a sheep pen is built on the pond bank, its dung swept in to feed the grass carp, and the grass carp's dung feeds the silver carp. It saves cutting grass, though the fish grow a little more slowly.
  • The 1639 compendium's pond-bank pen held sheep, and its compiler was from Shanghai, far from the delta.
    Xu was a man of Shanghai, and his pen holds sheep, but the passage shows the form: a livestock pen standing on a fish-pond bank so that its waste feeds the fish.
  • The delta's ducks were herded in the rice fields.
    Qu Dajun, in his account of Guangdong completed in 1678, says the coastal fields of Guangzhou bred small crabs that ate the rice sprouts and that only ducks could eat them, so many in the villages kept ducks, herded in the fields: on the crabs in spring and summer, on the gleaned rice in autumn.
  • Duck-keeping in the delta ran through duck landings, each with a holder answerable for the rice the ducks ate.
    Qu Dajun says each duck landing, a 埠 (bu, "landing"), had a holder chosen from those with settled property; when ducks ate someone's rice the holder answered for it, and passed the claim to the duck-keeper, who paid by name.
  • How many households in a dike-pond village kept a pig, or ducks?
    No page we read gives the share of households in a dike-pond village that kept either. Qu Dajun's "many" is the nearest.

Modern accounts of the delta, and the modern manuals of integrated fish farming, put the animals on the dike itself and their waste straight into the water.

  • An article that cites Ruddle and Zhong's modern study of the delta has pigs, chickens and ducks reared on the dikes.
    Their 1988 monograph could not be read; an article that lists it among its references says the animals are reared on the dikes to provide manure for the fish ponds. That article does not attach this sentence to them, so nothing here is read from their work directly.
  • In a summary of the delta's dike-ponds, sugarcane leaves and refinery waste fed fish and pigs.
    Where cane was planted, its young leaves were fed to fish and pigs, and the refinery's waste went back to the dike-pond as feed.
  • A modern article has farmers raising pigs on frames built over the ponds.
    It calls the dike-pond for that reason three-dimensional farming.
  • A modern manual builds the pig shed on the dike, or over the water, so its waste runs into the pond.
    The manual of integrated fish farming in China describes the simple pig shed on the pond dike or over the water, suited to a household for its low cost, its excreta flowing or flushed into the pond; pigsties are usually built on the dike so the excrement can be flushed straight in.
  • A modern manual allows a sty draining straight in on a pond under 8 mu (~1.3 ac).
    On a pond that size, it says, a pigsty can be set up on the dike and its wastes flow directly into the pond.
  • The modern fish-cum-duck pond fences a dry run on the dike and a wet run in the water.
    The same manual calls it the commonest way of farming fish with ducks: part of the dike fenced as a dry run, a corner of the pond as a wet run.
  • Was there a duck pen at a fish pond before modern times?
    No page we read places one; the fenced dry run on a pond dike is a modern manual's form.
  • Was a sty built to drain into its pond before modern times?
    No page we read from before 1912 builds a shed so that its waste flows into the pond, or over the water. What the older record holds is the pig as the dike-pond district's stock and the 1639 pen with its dung swept in by hand.

The modern manuals treat the manure as feed for the fish, and the faults they warn of are of quantity and of place within the pond, not of a sty standing at the water; and a sixth-century farm manual asks for no clean place for a pen either.

  • The modern manuals count the manure as feed, not fouling.
    Animal manure fertilizes the water, producing plankton the fish eat, by three routes at once: it stimulates the algae, it feeds bacteria, and it feeds the tiny animals of the water.
  • A modern manual gives labor and the animals' comfort as the reasons for a pen's place.
    A pen on the dike, close to the pond, saves the labor of loading waste; a pen over the pond is cooler and more humid.
  • Too much manure tells on the fish.
    The fish come to the surface, short of oxygen, when pig manure sinks to the pond bottom or too much flows in.
  • A modern manual limits a sty's manure by the number of pigs and the season, not by distance.
    More than thirty pigs in one spot is too much manure for the shed that drains straight in, and usually no manure is applied after mid-October.
  • A second modern manual leaves the sty on the dike and spreads its manure about the pond instead.
    It builds pig sties on the dikes and spreads the manure so the water beside the livestock does not go foul, a failure it had watched: fish lost their appetite at the manuring places, presumably for lack of oxygen.
  • A sixth-century Chinese farm manual says a pig pen need not be clean.
    The Qimin Yaoshu, a farming treatise completed about 544, says a pen is not too small, since a small pen fattens the pigs fast, and its place not too filthy, since mud and muck let them escape the heat. Its pig chapter says nothing of fish ponds, so it does not settle how a pig and a pond stood together.
  • Did a sty have to stand back from its pond's sluice?
    A pond's sluice is "a protected opening in the pond dike", closed with wooden boards to set the water level. No page we read relates a shed's or a pen's place on the dike to the pond's inlet, outlet or sluice, for it or against it. The sources place the sheds only in relation to the pond itself.

Only modern sources give a width for a dike-pond's dikes: a manual's figure for a dike carrying a pigsty, and one village's dikes before commercial fish farming wore them away.

  • A modern manual makes a dike carrying a pigsty 5 to 10 m (~16-33 ft) wide.
    Dikes between ponds are kept within 5 m (~16 ft); those for pigsties, cow-shed piping or traffic run 5 to 10 m (~16-33 ft).
  • In one delta village the dikes ran 20 m (~66 ft) wide before commercial fish farming wore them away.
    In Ruxi, a village of the delta's collectively managed dike-ponds, the arable dikes that had been 20 m (~66 ft) wide were eroded to under 4 m (~13 ft) as the ponds grew after the reform and opening-up.
  • In Ruxi, ground transport replaced boats as fish farming turned commercial.
    The growing trade in fish abandoned water transport for transport over land.
  • How wide was a dike-pond's dike before modern times?
    No page we read gives a width for a dike carrying a shed, or for any dike-pond dike, before 1912.

Japan kept pigs too, but nothing we read puts one at a pond.

  • The usual account of Japan's pigs is an assumption, a recent survey says.
    That pigs, brought in early, never took hold, and that religious and other proscriptions limited meat until the nineteenth century, is named by a 2023 scholarly survey as an assumption that still shapes the writing, not a finding.
  • How were Japan's pigs kept, outside Okinawa?
    The 2023 survey sets China, where pigs were raised on household waste in the villages, against the European way of letting them loose to feed on nuts, and says Japan, Okinawa excepted, seems to have followed the European way.
  • From the early modern period, Japan's towns ate pork more widely.
    Pig bones turn up in numbers at the Satsuma domain's residence in Edo, at Osaka castle, at Hakata and at Dejima in Nagasaki.
  • Was a Japanese pig ever kept at a pond?
    No page we read links a Japanese pig to a pond at all, so the pen on a fish-pond dike is, so far as we read, a Chinese form.
