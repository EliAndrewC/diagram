How tall is a terrace wall, and how big is a paddy on a slope?

Sources: kotobank-tanada (the narrow strips and the high walls between them; four paddies to a tan), tanada-jawiki (what counts as a terrace; stone walls in the west, earth banks in the east; one terrace's paddy count), sakaori-ishizumi (steeper ground, higher walls and smaller paddies), chikuma-obasute (a slope and its paddy count), bunka-shiroyone (the smallest paddies, and earth bank faces)

A terraced paddy is a paddy on a slope of one in twenty or steeper. Where rice is carried up steep mountain ground, the paddies become narrow strips, with a fairly high vertical wall between each tier and the next, kept up with stone. What the map draws is therefore a stack of long narrow benches, each held up by its wall, rather than the broad fan of the valley floor (the form itself is above).

The wall: higher on steeper ground

No page read gives a Japanese terrace wall's height in meters; the one figure in the record, a riser of 0.8 to 1.5 m for a traditional bench terrace, is on the fields page, from a page about terraces in general. What the pages do give is the direction. In one valley at Sakaori, the steep side carries high walls of small stones with the bund on top, and the gentle side lower walls of big stones - and there, where the slope is gentler, a paddy is larger. The two are tied by geometry: a level bench cut into an even slope is as deep, down the slope, as its wall is high times the slope's run for each unit of rise. On a slope of one in seven a wall of 1 m leaves a bench about 7 m deep; on the one-in-twenty slope at the edge of what counts as a terrace, about 20 m.

The paddy: from a few tens of square meters to a few hundred

A terrace paddy is small everywhere, and smaller the steeper the ground:

  • At Shiroyone, on a slope that falls about 50 m to the sea, about eight hundred paddies average 18 to 20 m2 each - the extreme of smallness.
  • A notation of a terrace's extent put four paddies to one tan - about a quarter of a tan each (our arithmetic).
  • Obasute lies on a slope of one in seven, about 1,800 paddies over 75 ha, and about 1,500 of them in 40 ha of it - an average of about 270 to 420 m2 a paddy (our arithmetic).
  • The Terasaka terraces hold about 250 paddies in about 4 ha - about 160 m2 each (our arithmetic).

The wall: a stone face or an earth bank

Two forms of wall are attested, and they are distinct forms rather than degrees of one. In western Japan, where terraces were cut into steep mountainsides, the banks were built as stone walls able to withstand the steep slope; in eastern Japan, where terraces were fewer, the banks were gentle earth mounds, a terrace landscape the page sets in contrast to the west's. Shiroyone's bank faces, too, are earth.

What this means for the map

A terrace paddy's size is a degree along a continuum set by the slope, so it is a calibrated liberty tied to the ground: on a steep slope the benches are narrow and the paddies small, down to a few tens of square meters; on a gentle one the benches are deep and a paddy reaches a few hundred. The wall's height moves the other way, higher where the ground is steeper. The wall's FORM is a choice between two attested forms, so it is a knob rolled per settlement: a near-vertical stone face, or a sloping earth bank that takes up more of the slope.
