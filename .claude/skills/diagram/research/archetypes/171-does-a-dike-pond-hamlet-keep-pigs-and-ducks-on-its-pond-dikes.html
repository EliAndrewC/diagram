Does a dike-pond hamlet keep pigs and ducks on its pond dikes?

Sources: isis-dykepond (carrying Ruddle & Zhong's Pearl-delta figures), fao-ac264e, cssn-sangji-yutang, fao-ac264e-ch10, chi-2024-dike-pond-commons, hudson-munoz-2023

What the research found. One of the things a silk-and-fish hamlet has that a rice hamlet does not (the rest are in What stands on a dike-pond hamlet that a paddy hamlet lacks?) is pigs and ducks ON the pond dikes - the Pearl-delta loop. Ruddle & Zhong (1988) as carried by the i-sis summary: "Pigs, chickens and ducks are reared on the dykes, to provide manure to fertilise the fishponds"; the modern FAO/NACA manual: "the simple pig shed constructed on the pond dyke or over the water surface" lets the excreta flow to the pond; fish-cum-duck ponds fence a dry run on the dike and a wet run in a corner of the water. The premodern prevalence is not in anything read; a saying that the cane pressings left from sugar-making were fed to pigs (蔗榨糖后的蔗渣喂猪, zhe zha tang hou de zhezha wei zhu) was looked for and not found on any page fetched, but isis-dykepond records sugar-cane leaves fed to fish and pigs and refinery waste returned to the dyke pond as feed, and cssn-sangji-yutang records farmers building frames over the pond to raise pigs, which puts pigs in the traditional loop. A pig sty on a pond dike and a duck pen at a pond corner are both attested forms, then, though how common they were before the modern period is unquantified. Both are drawn. Which of them belonged to the premodern village - the pig, by the record, while the delta's ducks of the period were herded in the rice fields - is Which animals did a dike-pond village keep at its ponds? (Whether a sty must stand back from the water or the sluice: Does a pig sty have to stand back from the water, or from the pond's sluice?)

A dike that carries a shed is a WIDER dike, which is the one constructional number the record gives: "The width of the dikes between fish ponds and inflow and outflow canals should be kept within 5 m; the width of the dikes for pigsties, cow sheds piping, or traffic should range from 5 to 10 m". Only the floor of that range bears on a map like this one: the page itself sets the ceiling by pigsties, piping and traffic together, and the traffic at least, if it ran on the dikes as this page infers, is later than this map - ground transport came with commercialization, when water transport was abandoned for it - and in Shunde the dikes ran 20 m wide until commercial fish farming eroded them, later than this map and wider than the manual's ceiling either way.

And measured against the drawing, the map does NOT meet it. The ground between one pond's water and the next runs about 13 m, but that is not one bank - a canal runs down the middle of it, and the planted collar a sty actually stands on is 2 to 5 m wide. So the strip under the shed is narrower than the 5 m a shed-carrying dike is told to have. The mismatch is recorded rather than drawn away: that figure is a modern manual's design requirement, not a measurement of any old dike, and nothing read gives the width of a premodern one. A reader should know the drawn collar is snug by a modern standard and that no older standard was found to judge it by.

And the pig on the dike is a CHINESE form, which the map should say rather than leave implied. The Japanese record does not carry it: the standard account - that pigs were introduced early, never took hold, and that religious proscription limited meat until the nineteenth century - is named by a 2023 scholarly survey as an assumption that still shapes the literature rather than a finding, and where mainland Japanese pigs WERE kept the reading is pannage, loose in forest, with Okinawa the stated exception: "With the exception of Okinawa, Japan seems to have followed the pannage system". Japanese pigs WERE kept, and the same survey records them in the early modern towns - "Sites with significant numbers of Sus bones (both wild and domesticated) include the Satsuma domain residence in Edo, Osaka castle, Hakata, and Dejima harbour in Nagasaki". What has no Japanese counterpart is the FORM - a pen standing on a fish-pond dike. (No source: nothing in the material read - that survey, the integrated-farming reports, and Japanese carp-culture pages searched 2026-09-12 - links a Japanese pig to a pond at all.) A dike-pond hamlet is a Chinese-derived form throughout, so its pond stock is Chinese too, and a hamlet of any other archetype has no business with either.

Departures and honesty. The Pearl-delta figures come from a summary of Ruddle & Zhong's monograph, which itself could not be read, and a modern manual - every Ruddle & Zhong number here rests on the summary, not the monograph.
