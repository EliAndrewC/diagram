How thickly was dike mulberry planted, and how wide did a bush grow?

Sources: pwsannong-gudai-zaisang (how spacing varied in old plantations, and a late-Qing figure of about 300 mid-trunk trees a mu), pwsannong-sangji-yutang (8,000-10,000 root-cut bushes a mu in the Pearl River delta), kotobank-souen (Japanese planting numbers for root-cut and mid-cut fields), kotobank-negari (root-cut bushes kept small, about a thousand to ten ares)

This question gathers what the record holds on how close a dike's mulberry stood. How the map draws it, and the gap between the drawn rows and the planting, is The 6:4 water-to-dike ratio, and coppiced mulberry.

The lower the bush, the closer it stood. Old mulberry plantations were spaced very differently by climate, lie of the land and the form the tree was trained to; in Jiangsu and Zhejiang the planting was fairly sparse before the mid-Qing and grew denser in the late Qing, when a new silk district south of the Qiantang River planted mid-trunk mulberry at about 300 a mu - one tree to about 24 sq ft. From the Ming mid-trunk mulberry gradually spread in the Taihu district, and later low-trunk mulberry appeared. At the other end, the Pearl River delta's silk district cut its mulberry back to the root and planted 8,000 to 10,000 a mu, the dikes planted in much the same way as a flat field - about one bush to a square foot, a figure the page gives as current practice with no date. A modern Japanese encyclopedia, which gives no date for its figures, sits between them: a root-cut mulberry field carried 800 to 1,000 bushes to ten ares and a mid-cut field 400 to 600 - one bush to 11-13 sq ft root-cut, to 18-27 sq ft mid-cut - the root-cut bushes being kept small.

So the figures lie along one continuum set by how low the bush was cut, from about a square foot a bush in the Pearl delta to about 24 sq ft in the late-Qing Yangtze delta. Only the last is dated before the modern period, and none of them was measured on a pond dike; the Pearl-delta page says the dikes were planted much as a field was.

How wide a single bush grew is on no page read. The crown width the map draws stays a guess.

What this means for the map. A density along that continuum is calibrated liberty, not a choice between forms: the map may stand anywhere on it and say where. The drawn rows, at about one bush per 23 sq ft, stand at the late-Qing Yangtze-delta figure and inside Japan's mid-cut range; they are some 25 to 32 times sparser than the Pearl delta's ground mulberry. Where the dike-pond hamlet should stand on the continuum is the GM's open decision; the record gives it the dated point and the two ends.
