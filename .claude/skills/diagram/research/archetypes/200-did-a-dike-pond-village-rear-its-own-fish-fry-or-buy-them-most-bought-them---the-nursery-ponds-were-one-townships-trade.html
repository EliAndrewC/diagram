Did a dike-pond village rear its own fish fry, or buy them? Most bought them - the nursery ponds were one township's trade

Sources: guangdong-xinyu-22 (Qu Dajun's account of the fry households of Jiujiang, the landings granted to them, their fry ponds and the share of their water those ponds took), pwsannong-zhusanjiao-nongyeshi (Jiujiang's hold on the West River fry catch and its supply of the other pond districts), fao-ac264e-ch10 (the nursery share a modern manual sets for a farm that stocks itself)

This question is about where a dike-pond village's young fish came from. Whether fry were a trade at all, and how big a fry pond is, is Were fish fry a trade, and which ponds were the nursery ponds?

The fry were caught wild, by one township. In the Pearl River delta the young carp that stocked the ponds were not bred there: they were netted in the West River and sold. Qu Dajun, writing of Guangdong in the late seventeenth century, says most of the people of Jiujiang village in Nanhai lived by netting fry and were called fry households, and that they held the fry-catching landings on the river and paid a levy on each. The landings had been granted to them: in the Hongzhi reign (1488-1505) a governor-general memorialized that the landings on both banks of the West River be given to the people of Jiujiang. A modern history of the delta's farming says the same from the other end - from the mid-Ming on Jiujiang held the right to catch fry on the West River and supplied fry to the pond districts.

Only that township kept nursery ponds. Qu Dajun is plain about it: fry ponds were found only in Jiujiang; elsewhere the ponds mostly raised grown fish, and where they tried to raise fry the fry did not thrive. In Jiujiang itself it was the other way round. He describes its land as a chessboard - the black lines the dikes, the squares the ponds - and says that of its pond water seven parts in ten raised fry and three raised grown fish; the fry water was turbid and the grown-fish water clear, so that the color of a pond told what it held.

A modern farm does it differently. A modern manual of Chinese integrated fish farming gives nursery ponds a quarter to three tenths of the pond area on a farm whose main stock is grass-eating or grain-fed fish. That is a farm that stocks itself, a design the manual prescribes; it is not what Qu Dajun saw, where the fry came in by trade and a village's ponds were either nearly all nursery or none.

How many nursery ponds any village other than Jiujiang kept is on no page read.

What this means for the map. The record now supports two distinct forms, not one share: an ordinary dike-pond hamlet that raises grown fish and buys its fry, with no nursery ponds at all, and a fry village of the Jiujiang kind, where most of the pond water is nursery water. By the rule for distinct forms that is a choice each hamlet rolls, not a share it draws. The one small parcel in ten the map now draws as a fry pond matches neither form and stays a guess until the GM rules. The turbid fry water against the clear grown-fish water is a difference a map could show in the pond's color.
