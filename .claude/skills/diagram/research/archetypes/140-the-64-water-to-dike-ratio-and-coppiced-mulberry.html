The 6:4 water-to-dike ratio, and coppiced mulberry

Sources: gd-gazetteer-sangji (the water-to-dike split 水基比, shui ji bi, 「a split of three to seven up to four to six」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「三七至四六开」)), fao-ac241e (6:4; 1:1 where the worms alone feed the fish), isis-dykepond (the 0.4-0.6 ha ponds and 6-10 m dikes), chi-2024-dike-pond-commons (the dikes eroded from 20 m to under 4 m as the ponds grew), cssn-sangji-yutang (「six parts dike, four parts pond」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「基六塘四」)), kuwa-jawiki (mulberry kept as a low shrub), pwsannong-sangji-yutang (8,000-10,000 root-cut bushes a mu on the Pearl River delta's dikes). The drawn density (one bush per 10-20 sq ft) is this page's guess, sparser than the one figure read, and the crown width (4-6 ft) has no readable source, sdlib-shunde-jitang, morus-enwiki

The dike-pond ratio was written as an explicit area ratio - 6 parts water to 4 parts dike in a worked example, and 6:4 or 7:3 wherever the fish had feed from beyond the dike, with actual practice set by local tradition; measured on the Kuwabata map, water is 80% of the parcels and the planted banks 20%, falling to 50% of the whole block once the canal corridors between them count - so the drawing falls either side of the prescribed six parts water: water-heavy inside a parcel, under it across the block. The band is not marginal ground: each pond's ~5,600 sq ft (~0.13 ac) bank was the SILK side of the loop (leaf -> silkworm -> frass -> fish -> dredged pond mud -> dike fertility), and the ratio existed because the dike's mulberry had to yield enough feed and fertilizer for the fish in the water beside it.

Silkworm mulberry was not grown as trees: it was COPPICED into low bushes - pruned, in one plantation practice, once a year to five or six feet - (crowns ~4-6 ft) so the leaf could be stripped several times a year, planted in dense rows on the dike at roughly one bush per 10-20 sq ft - i.e. 300-600 bushes per pond, tens of thousands across the map. That spacing is this page's guess, and a sparse one: a modern Chinese agricultural encyclopedia, saying the dikes are planted the same way as any flat mulberry field, gives the Pearl River delta's silk district 8,000 to 10,000 bushes a mu, cut back to the root as ground mulberry - about one bush to every square foot - so real crowns do not stand apart at all. How wide a single crown grew is on no page read; this page takes four to six feet, at which adjacent crowns touch even at its own sparser spacing, so drawing the actual bushes at honest density IS a packed band of dots; individually distinguishable tree glyphs would mean three to five times the true crown size, which a to-scale map does not do. No berries either: whether leaf-stripped coppice fruits is on no page read, and a mulberry fruit is sub-pixel at this scale - crown color carries the read.

What the drawn rows fall short of, and by how much. At the guessed density above - one bush per 10-20 sq ft, crowns four to six feet, three to five feet apart in the row - real adjacent crowns TOUCH, and a row drawn at that density honestly is a fused continuous hedge line, which on the map reads as a solid green band rather than as bushes. The GM asked for the touching-crown read without the fusion: bushes close enough to run together in stretches, with bank earth showing through the notches, rather than a chain of separate dots or one unbroken stripe. Spacing the bushes four and a half feet apart in the row gets that read, at about one bush per 23 sq ft (10,613 crowns over some 243,000 sq ft of bank on the dike-pond map), with the crowns themselves honest at four and a half to seven feet against the four to six of the guess. It is still short of the low end of that guessed band, and the reason is that only two rows fit the width of a bank legibly: the remaining gap is the price of the crowns reading as separate plants at all, and it is disclosed here rather than closed.

A bush stands on the bank, so its trunk never stands in the canal. The planting is coppiced BUSHES on the dike, not canopy trees arching over water, so a crown whose CENTER fell over open water would be a bush growing in a dug channel - impossible. A crown's edge may overhang, though, and the channels are drawn beneath the canopies, so an overhanging leaf edge covers part of a channel while a channel never slices across a crown: the canal runs between the bushes at ground level, which is what it does on the ground. A rule that kept the whole crown circle clear of the water would pull the canopies artificially back from the bank edge; it is the crown's CENTER that must stand on the bank, not the whole circle. Grazing the edge is deliberate (2026-07-24): on this map the canal is drawn along the toe of the dike, where earth meets water, and a bent ditch grazes a parcel edge by a foot and a half or so; that is the toe line clipping the rim of the bank, not a canal cut through the dike, and a rule that fired on it would be wrong about the place. Widening each bank by five feet to cover the drawn parcel line put banks over the wavering ditches in 72 places, up to six and a half feet deep, so the bank stays on the true parcel line and that line is covered another way.

The ORDER of the ratio is contested: the one page read writes the classic prescription as 「six parts dike, four parts pond」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「基六塘四」) - six parts DIKE to four parts POND, while the FAO's worked example has water to dike at six to four - six water to four dike, the reading Kuwabata is drawn to at 80% water across the parcels and a 78% median parcel. The literature carries both orders and a 7:3 variant, so this may be a regional split rather than an error.

GM ruling (2026-08-28) - a DEVIATION, a disclosed regional reading: Kuwabata keeps its 6 water : 4 dike parcels. The record carries the prescription in BOTH orders - 「six parts dike, four parts pond」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「基六塘四」) on the page read (cssn-sangji-yutang), six parts water to four parts dike in the FAO's worked example - and a 7:3 variant; the map follows the water-heavy reading, and says so on every pond and bank of Kuwabata: "a regional reading; the classic prescription is also recorded as six parts dike to four parts pond".

The bank is a ring, not a disk under the pond. A crop dike is drawn as the BAND between its outer edge and the water's own outline, with the pond showing through the middle - the same shape the perimeter dike has, and for the same reason. This is a map drawing convention, ruled by the GM on 2026-09-12: hovering a dike on the interactive page lights the bank alone, since lighting "the fish ponds Inside each Mulberry dike" with it is "confusing" - "basically the same behavior that we give to the perimeter dyke". Priced and declined: a stylesheet fix, which cannot cut a hole in a filled shape; drawing the pond above the lit feature, at a measured cost of a fifth of a second on every hover; and a second outline element for the outer edge, which doubles a dike's ink for a fringe nobody sees.
