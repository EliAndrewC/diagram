Wet-rice hydrology has no crossings to draw

Sources: fusekoshi-jawiki (the inverted siphon under a watercourse, with historical examples), suirokyo-jawiki (the aqueduct bridge)

Why any crossing is wrong here: wet-rice hydrology has no crossings to draw - a ditch either feeds another or is fed by it, and where two courses genuinely had to pass at different levels the builders put in27 an aqueduct28 or an inverted siphon, a distinct structure these maps do not draw.

How a junction is told from a crossing - and why it is measured ACROSS the other channel, never along the ditch (2026-07-24). The GM, looking at the rice polder: "many of the irrigated channels intersect rather than joining". Two things had to be settled. The first is where a ditch's tip is put. The field's lattice is bent, so a tip solved on the straight line between two points of the trunk lands a few feet off the curve the trunk is actually drawn along - two to five feet, which is precisely the range in which a near-perpendicular ditch pokes a visible tail out the far side and the pair reads as a four-way crossroads instead of a T. Tips are therefore projected onto the trunk's own drawn line, which is exact whatever the bending does, and the residual is a fraction of an inch. The second is how the result is judged, and that is the part worth keeping. At every meeting of two drawn channels, take the nearer end of each and ask whether it sits inside the OTHER channel's drawn width: if a tip is buried, the meeting is a junction and the overrun hides under the ink; if neither is, the stub shows. The measure runs across the other channel and never along the ditch's own line, and that is the whole trick - a delivery ditch taking off at a shallow angle overruns the meeting by four to eight feet along its own line and still never leaves the trunk's width, which is why a shallow offtake reads as a clean Y on Honda, Ikegami, Nagahara and Hoshizora alike, while the same overrun on a right-angled T pokes straight out.
