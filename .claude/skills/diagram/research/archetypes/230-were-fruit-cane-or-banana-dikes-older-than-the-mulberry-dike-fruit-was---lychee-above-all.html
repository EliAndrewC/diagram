Sources: guangdong-xinyu-22 (Qu Dajun on the fruit trees, tea and mulberry of the villages' pond dikes), guangdong-xinyu-25 (lychee and longan on Guangzhou's embankments, and how thickly they stood in a field), pwsannong-zhusanjiao-nongyeshi (fruit dikes first, in the mid-Ming, then mulberry; the cane, rice and fan-palm dikes), guangdong-xinyu-27 (banana and cane as field crops, rotated on the same ground)

This question is about the age of each dike crop. What the modern surveys say of the delta's cane, banana and fruit dikes, and how the map rolls a hamlet's crop, is What else was planted on a pond dike besides mulberry?

The fruit dike came first. Qu Dajun, writing in the late seventeenth century, says that in the villages of Guangzhou's large counties people often gave up good fields to make dikes and planted them with fruit trees - lychee most, tea and mulberry next, then mandarin and orange; longan stood mostly beside the houses but on the dikes as well. Below the dike was a pond for fish, drained at the year's end and sown with rice seedlings in spring. In his chapter on trees he adds that the embankments of Guangzhou - most plausibly those of its diked polders and rivers, though he does not define his term - were all planted with lychee and longan, and that some gave up rice fields to plant them. A modern history of the delta's farming dates the order: fruit-dike fishponds arose first, in Nanhai and Shunde in the mid-Ming, and mulberry-dike fishponds replaced them in the late Ming and early Qing and became the dominant type in the Qing. So the fruit dike is not a late form: it is the oldest dike-pond planting read, and in Qu Dajun's day it still stood beside the mulberry.

How thickly the fruit trees stood is known only for a field. Converted to orchard, a mu of field took twenty-odd lychee trees or twice as many longan, each on a mound of silt about two chi high against the floods. That is one lychee to about 300 sq ft of field; it is a field figure, and nothing read gives the spacing along a dike.

The cane dike is named, not dated. The same modern history lists rice-dike, cane-dike and fan-palm-dike fishponds among the delta's dike-pond types, in its account of the Ming and Qing, with no date for any of them. Qu Dajun knows cane and banana well, but as field crops: in Zengcheng many grew banana for three or four years, then cut it all down to plant white cane, and after two years of cane planted banana again, and he says the cane fields nearly equaled the rice fields. Neither is placed on a pond dike on any page read from before the modern period, nor is a vegetable dike.

What this means for the map. The fruit dike is the best-attested premodern alternative to the mulberry dike, and its fruit can be named: lychee above all, with longan, mandarin and orange. A tea dike is attested beside it and is not drawn. The cane dike rests on one undated modern listing, and the banana dike on nothing earlier than the modern surveys. Which of these a premodern dike-pond hamlet may roll, and whether the banana dike is labeled a later form, is the GM's open decision.
