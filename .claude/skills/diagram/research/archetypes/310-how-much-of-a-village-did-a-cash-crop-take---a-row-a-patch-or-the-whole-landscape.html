How much of a village did a cash crop take - a row, a patch, or the whole landscape?

Sources: chaen-jawiki (tea planted as one row along the bunds), wuhurec-wanchun (mulberry planted along a polder's dike), tokyo-ja-kasai-renkon (a lotus district grown only a little before the Meiji market), katsushika-renkon (lotus arriving late in the villages east of Tokyo)

How far a cash crop spreads depends first on its FORM, because the three the maps carry did not all take plots. Some took whole plots; some took only the edges of plots; and the plot-taking form grew from a scatter to a whole landscape over centuries. The share the maps convert, and the liberty taken with it, is above; this question is what the history says each crop covered.

Tea: a hillside garden, or a row along the bunds

The hillside tea garden - small cottagers' plots on the lower, fertile slopes - is the Chinese form above. Japan had a second form, and it took no plot at all: bund tea, tea planted as a single row along the banks of fields and dry fields, which marked the boundary with the neighbor, broke the wind and held the soil, and gave the household its own tea with any surplus sold to a merchant. It was commonly seen. Bund tea's extent is a line, not an area - one row on the edges the fields already have.

Mulberry: along the polder dike

The dike-pond, where mulberry took a converted plot's own banks, is the plot-taking form, and its spread from a scatter in 1581 to whole townships by the eighteenth century is above. On a rice polder mulberry had a place that took no paddy: the crest of the dike itself. The Song record of the Wanchun polder has rows of mulberry planted along the ridge of its dike, some tens of thousands of trees. There, as with bund tea, the crop's extent is the length of the dike.

Lotus: a little, before the market came

Lotus took whole plots, and the deepest, wettest ones. What was read puts the whole-landscape lotus field late and near a great city. At Kasai, east of Edo, lotus seems to have been grown only a little from the mid-Edo period; it spread fast from the mid-Meiji, once an improved strain made it pay far better than rice, on ground with rich soil, many deep paddies and the city's night soil at hand - until the district was covered in lotus leaves as far as the eye could see. In the Katsushika villages next door it came later still: the survey of 1872 lists no lotus in the villages later known for it, and in 1917 four settlements together grew 7 cho 6 tan. No page read gives lotus as a share of a village's paddy, before or after.

What this means for the map

Tea is a knob between two attested forms: the hillside garden, which takes plots on the lower slope, and bund tea, which takes none and draws a single row of bushes along field banks. Mulberry on a polder that is not a dike-pond village belongs on the dike crest rather than in the paddies. Lotus, before the modern market, belongs at the low end of its range: a few deep plots, not a lotus district; the calibrated liberty that draws it thicker, for legibility, now sits further from what was read than when it was taken, and the GM may wish to revisit it.
