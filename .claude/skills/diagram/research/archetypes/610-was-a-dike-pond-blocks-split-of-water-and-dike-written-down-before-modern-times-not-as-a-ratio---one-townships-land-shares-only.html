Was a dike-pond block's split of water and dike written down before modern times? Not as a ratio - one township's land shares only

Sources: guangdong-xinyu-22 (fish took half of Jiujiang's land, its fish ponds eight tenths of the township and its fields two, and the larger ponds of the Guangzhou villages reached tens of mu), zhwiki-guangdong-xinyu (when Qu Dajun's account was completed), fao-ac241e (the six-to-four worked example, and six mu to the 0.4 ha), gd-gazetteer-sangji (the three-to-seven and four-to-six splits), cssn-sangji-yutang (the undated 「four water, six dike」 (translated; original: 「四水六基」) of former days), isis-dykepond (the modern pond size and dike width)

The split of a dike-pond block between water and dike is on the map as a share: Kuwabata's parcels are drawn water-heavy, six parts water to four of dike or wetter, as The 6:4 water-to-dike ratio, and coppiced mulberry records. This question is whether anyone wrote such a split down before modern times.

Every page read that writes the split as a number is modern. The six-to-four split comes from a worked example in a modern working paper on integrated fish farming, and the three-to-seven and four-to-six splits from a present-day article of the Guangdong gazetteer office, which gives them as the split in general and dates them to no period. A modern essay on the dike-pond calls 「four water, six dike」 (translated; original: 「四水六基」) a scene of former days that survives only in documents, but it names no document and gives no date. No text older than the twentieth century that gives the split as a ratio was found.

The oldest figures read are one township's. Qu Dajun, writing of Guangdong in 1678, says of Jiujiang that its land was cramped and the fish took half of it, the ponds raising fish and the dikes planted with mulberry. In another passage of the same chapter he puts the township's fish ponds at eight tenths of its land and its fields at two. He never sets the two figures side by side, and neither says whether the ponds' eight tenths counts their dikes. Read together, with the fish's half as the water and the ponds' eight tenths as water and dikes both, they leave the dikes about three tenths of the land: water to dike about five parts to three, the water-heavy order and close to six to four. That reading is this page's GUESS, from two shares one observer gave for one township; no premodern text read states it.

The ponds' size and the dikes' width. Qu Dajun also says that in the villages of Guangzhou's great counties the larger ponds below the planted dikes reached several tens of mu, and ponds built out into the sea were reckoned by the hundred mu. The modern pond of 0.4 to 0.6 ha is six to nine mu at the reckoning of six mu to 0.4 ha, so it sits below his larger ponds, but he gives no ordinary size. How wide a dike was before modern times is on no page read: the six to ten meters the map's banks rest on is modern practice.

What this means for the map. No premodern text writes the split as six to four in either order, so the ratio as a number is modern. The premodern figure nearest a split is Qu Dajun's: fish water about half of the land. Kuwabata's whole block, water about half once the canal corridors count, matches it. Its parcels, 80% water, are wetter than any figure read, premodern or modern; if the premodern reading above holds, a parcel's water would be about six parts in ten. The pond size and the bank width rest on modern pages alone.
