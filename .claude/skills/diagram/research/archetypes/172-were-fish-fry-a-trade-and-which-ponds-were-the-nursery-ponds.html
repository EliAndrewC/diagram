What the research found. One of the things a silk-and-fish hamlet has that a rice hamlet does not (the rest are in The dike-pond hamlet: what stands there that a rice hamlet lacks) is fish fry (鱼花, yuhua) as a trade, and nursery ponds. Jiujiang in Nanhai traded fry: Qu Dajun's seventeenth-century observation of Jiujiang village in Nanhai, in his Guangdong xinyu, 「the men trade fish fry, the women feed the silkworms」 (translated; original: 「男贩鱼花，妇女喂爱蚕」) - men trade fry, women feed the worms. How the township came to hold the trade, and why most villages kept no nursery ponds at all, is Did a dike-pond village rear its own fish fry, or buy them?

The nursery pond was the smallest pond, and a very small one. The late-Ming farming compendium, the Nongzheng quanshu (printed 1639), gives the sequence twice. An older record it quotes has the young fish taken from the river in mid-spring and kept in a small pond, moved on to a second pond as they grew, and moved at about a foot long to a wide pond where they were fed grass. The compiler's own method from Jiangxi grades the ponds by size: a small pond one zhang square and eight chi deep, stocked with fry about an inch long, six hundred silver carp and two hundred grass carp to the pond; a middle pond two or three zhang square, holding seven or eight hundred; and a big pond sized to the number of fish, one twenty or thirty paces square or more being big enough for fish of three or four jin. The small pond was always set beside the big one, so that in winter the fish could be lodged in it; in winter a pond was drained and its fish lodged in another pond. At the Ming foot of about 30 to 32 cm, that fry pond was about ten feet square - a pit beside a pond, not a parcel of its own - the middle pond some twenty to thirty feet, and the big pond some 30 to 57 meters a side, depending on whether a pace was five feet or six. Earlier still, a thirteenth-century note by Zhou Mi has the fry of Jiangzhou bought by dealers, carried home to Jianchang, Fujian, Qu and Wu, and let go into a large cloth pen hung from bamboo in open water, reared there a month or half a month, and sold. These are other provinces' ways; how big Jiujiang's own fry ponds were is on no page read.

The size the map uses is modern. A modern manual keeps separate ponds for fry and for fingerlings, sizing a fry pond at one to three mu and a fingerling pond at two to five. A fry pond of that size, a whole parcel of the block, is the modern form. Before modern times the fry pond was the smallest of a graded set, which is where the map puts it, but it was a pit about ten feet square beside a larger pond, or, in the fry township, most of the pond water.
