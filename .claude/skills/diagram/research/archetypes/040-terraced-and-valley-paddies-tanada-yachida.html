Terraced and valley paddies (tanada, yachida)

Wet rice needs a level basin that holds water, and the broad valley floor, this project reasons, is the cheapest place to make one. Where the ground gives no such floor, two other forms of rice land do the work. On a hillside the basin is cut into the slope as a terrace, a tanada: a paddy made on sloping ground, stacked in narrow strips, each held up by a wall between it and the next. In a narrow stream valley the basin runs with the valley instead, as the yachida, a long, narrow paddy along the valley's wet floor.

Both forms keep their paddies small. A terrace paddy runs from about 20 square meters (~215 sq ft) to a few hundred (a few thousand square feet), smaller the steeper the ground, and its wall stands higher the steeper the ground, built of stone in western Japan and of earth in the east.

  • Japan's Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries counts a paddy on a slope of one in twenty or steeper as a terrace.
    That is a slope that rises 1 m (~3 ft) over 20 m (~66 ft), a modern definition.
  • In the Edo period, terraces were carried up steep mountain slopes in western Japan once the plains were full.
    When the plains had little room left for new fields, paddies were made on steep slopes to raise a domain's yield; in much of the east, even in the Edo period, terraces were never cut that steep.
  • Terraces were small work, opened by hand.
    They were not the planned development of lords or great merchants but small pieces of land opened with little more than a hoe.
  • Each terrace paddy was narrow and irregular in shape.
    A history of Japan's water works describes each as not only narrow but irregular, watered largely by the rain, on a mountainside reached by steep paths.

Terraces were not Japan's alone: China cut them too.

  • In China the terraces lie mainly in the mountains south of the Yangtze, most of them in Guangxi and Yunnan.
    The terraces of the Ailao mountains in Yunnan and of Longji, in Longsheng in Guangxi, are the better known.

The valley paddy is the other form, made where a stream has cut a narrow valley among hills.

  • Valley paddies are found all over Japan.
    Paddies made by opening small valleys among the hills are seen everywhere in Japan.
  • The valley paddy went by many local names.
    It was yatoda and yachida in the Kanto, in the east; tanada and yamada in the Kinki, around the old capital; and sakoda in the Chugoku, further west. So in the Kinki tanada, elsewhere the terrace's name, meant a valley paddy. The yachida is also called yatsuda.
  • Were valley paddies the standard farmland of Japan's middle ages, before the Edo period?
    One view holds that they were the most stable, standard farmland of that age. A Japanese encyclopedia calls them the typical farm landscape of the middle ages, because a valley's spring water could irrigate them and they could be opened in small pieces.
  • No page we read says where the stream ran through a valley paddy.
    Each places the paddy on the valley floor. That the brook threads down the middle of the chain of paddies is this project's reading of the form, a GUESS.

On a slope, the size of a paddy and the height of its wall are tied together.

  • For a wall of a given height, a level bench cut into a slope is deeper the gentler the slope.
    A bench runs back until the slope has risen by its wall's height, so its depth is the wall's height times the slope's run for each unit of rise. On a slope of one in seven a wall of 1 m (~3 ft) leaves a bench about 7 m (~23 ft) deep; on a slope of one in twenty, about 20 m (~66 ft).
  • How big was a terrace paddy?
    Small: the area of each terraced paddy is small, from about 20 square meters (~215 sq ft) to a few hundred square meters (a few thousand square feet), larger where the slope is gentler. At Sakaori, on the gentler side of one valley, a paddy is relatively larger.
  • Did flooding by hand keep a paddy small, in a valley as on a slope?
    This project reasons that it did: a basin flooded by hand to an even depth can only be so large, so a valley paddy, like a terrace, was a chain of small paddies rather than one long sheet.
  • At Shiroyone, about eight hundred paddies average 18 to 20 square meters (~194-215 sq ft) each.
    They spread over ground that falls about 50 m (~164 ft) to the sea, and are marked out by how extremely small they are.
  • How large is a paddy in the Terasaka terraces?
    About 160 square meters (~1,722 sq ft) on average, by our arithmetic: the terraces, in Saitama, hold about 250 paddies in about 4 ha (~10 acres).
  • How large is a paddy at Obasute, on a slope of one in seven?
    About 270 to 420 square meters (~2,906-4,521 sq ft) on average, by our arithmetic: the terraces hold about 1,800 paddies over 75 ha (~185 acres), and about 1,500 of them in 40 ha (~99 acres) of it.
  • One way of writing a terrace's extent put four paddies to a tan.
    With a tan of about 990 square meters (~10,656 sq ft), that is about 250 square meters (~2,691 sq ft) a paddy, by our arithmetic.

The wall between one bench and the next is what holds a terrace up. The pages describe it by its direction and its form rather than its height, and they give two forms, a stone face and an earth bank, which this project reads as distinct forms rather than degrees of one.

  • How tall was a terrace wall?
    No Japanese page we read gives a height in meters. The one figure in the record, a riser of 0.8 to 1.5 m (~3-5 ft) for a traditional bench terrace, is from a page about terraces in general, at Rice paddies and their plots (suiden).
  • The steeper the ground, the higher the wall.
    Where rice is carried up steep mountain ground, a fairly high vertical wall stands between each bench and the next, kept up with stone. In one valley at Sakaori, the steep side carries high walls of small stones with the bund on top, and the gentle side lower walls of big stones.
  • Western Japan built its terrace walls of stone.
    Where terraces were cut into steep mountainsides, the banks were built as stone walls able to withstand the slope.
  • Eastern Japan, which built fewer terraces, built them with earth banks.
    There the banks were gentle earth mounds, a terrace landscape the page sets in contrast to the west's. Shiroyone's bank faces, too, are earth.
