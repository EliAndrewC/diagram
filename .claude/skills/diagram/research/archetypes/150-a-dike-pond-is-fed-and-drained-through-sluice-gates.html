A dike-pond is fed and drained through sluice gates

Sources: minle-dou-people (the Minle dou, an old sluice of the Sangyuan polder carved with its name in the Guangxu era), cssn-sangyuanwei (a proverb naming Minle's 窦, dou), cssn-jiangnan-weitian (gates through the dike, opened in drought and shut in flood - the polder case), fao-x6708e (a modern training manual's sluice gate through a pond's dike). The inlet-high / outlet-low plumbing comes from Ruddle & Zhong (Cambridge 1988), which has no publicly readable copy.

What the research found: a mulberry-dike fishpond (桑基魚塘) is NOT a sealed basin, and the polder it stands in was watered and drained through sluices in its own dike. In the Pearl delta such a sluice is a dou: the Minle dou of the Sangyuan polder is an old sluice gate, its name carved above its opening in the fourth year of the Guangxu era (1878), and the villagers watered and drained their low ground through the polder's dikes, creeks and dou, reworking ponds for fish and planting mulberry beside them. The dike ponds were drained two or three times a year. A sluice through EACH POND's dike - "a protected opening in the pond dike that can be easily closed with wooden boards to regulate water level" - is defined only by a modern training manual, and that a pond on a slope is plumbed INLET-HIGH, OUTLET-LOW so water flows downhill through it (the whole dike-pond net running in series from a high intake to a low outfall) comes only from Ruddle & Zhong's study of the delta's dike ponds, published in 1988, which has no publicly readable copy; no premodern source was found for either. So the maps do not draw a sluice through every pond's dike: the ponds lie in the creek and canal network, and the sluices drawn are the polder's own, through its outer dike. The channels do not "irrigate" the ponds paddy-style; they are the conveyance-and-drainage network the ponds exchange water with. How a polder as a whole got rid of its water - through gated culverts in its dike opened when the water outside fell, by chain pump, or by raising its rice above the water - is below.
