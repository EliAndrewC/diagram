Mulberry and other crops on pond dikes (sangji, guoji)

A pond dike in the Pearl River delta was a field in its own right, a bank of ground with a fish pond below it, planted with a crop. From the Qing the crop was mostly mulberry, 桑基 (sangji, "mulberry dike"), kept as low bushes and stripped of its leaves to feed silkworms. Fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes are found in Jiangsu and Zhejiang, on the Yangtze delta, too. How thickly the mulberry stood depended on how low it was cut: from about one bush to the square foot in the Pearl River delta's root-cut planting today to about one to 24 sq ft in a late-Qing field of mid-trunk trees in Zhejiang.

Mulberry was not the first crop on the dikes. The fruit dike, 果基 (guoji, "fruit dike"), came first, in the middle of the Ming dynasty, and the mulberry dike took its place in the late Ming and early Qing. In 1678 the fruit trees, lychee above all, still stood on the dikes with tea and mulberry beside them. Sugarcane, banana and vegetable dikes are named only by modern pages, and no page we read from before modern times puts them on a dike.

  • Mulberry grown for silkworms was mostly kept as low bushes.
    Cultivated mulberry was for the most part trained as low bushes, in one plantation practice pruned once a year to 1.5 to 1.8 m (~5-6 ft).
  • In modern practice in the Pearl River delta, the leaves are picked for seven or eight broods of silkworms a year.
    A modern Chinese agricultural encyclopedia says so of the delta's dike mulberry, as current practice, with no date.
  • The lower a mulberry was cut, the closer it was planted.
    Old plantations were spaced very differently by climate, the lie of the land and the form the tree was trained to. A modern Japanese encyclopedia, which gives no date for its figures, puts 800 to 1,000 bushes on ten ares (~0.25 ac) of root-cut field and 400 to 600 on mid-cut: one bush to 11-13 sq ft root-cut, to 18-27 sq ft mid-cut. The root-cut bushes were kept small.
  • How thickly did mulberry stand on a Pearl River delta dike?
    About one bush to the square foot, though the figure was not measured on a dike. A modern Chinese agricultural encyclopedia says the dikes were planted much as any mulberry field on flat land, and gives the delta's silk district as a whole 8,000 to 10,000 bushes to the mu, cut back to the root. It gives the figure as current practice, with no date. A mu is about 7,200 sq ft, and at that spacing, by this project's arithmetic, the crowns did not stand apart at all.
  • In Jiangsu and Zhejiang, dike mulberry stands in rows along the pond bank, on a low or medium trunk.
    A modern Chinese agricultural encyclopedia says the mulberry on the fishpond dikes there is planted mainly in rows along the embankment and mostly trained to a low or medium trunk, and describes this as current practice, with no date.
  • How thickly did mulberry stand before modern times?
    The one figure dated before modern times is from the late Qing, and not from a pond dike. In Jiangsu and Zhejiang mulberry was planted fairly sparsely before the middle of the Qing and more densely in the late Qing. South of the Qiantang River, a silk district newly opened in the late Qing, mid-trunk mulberry stood about 300 to the mu, one tree to about 24 sq ft by this project's arithmetic.
  • Around Lake Tai, mid-trunk mulberry spread from the Ming, and low-trunk mulberry came later.
    A modern history of mulberry planting gives that order.
  • No page we read gives how wide a mulberry bush's crown grew.
    The pages that give the pruned height and the spacing are silent on the crown.
  • No page we read says whether mulberry stripped for leaf bore fruit.
    Neither the Japanese nor the English Wikipedia article on mulberry says anything linking the leaf harvest to fruiting.

Before the mulberry the dikes carried fruit trees, and beside it fruit trees and tea.

  • The fruit dike came first, in Nanhai and Shunde in the middle of the Ming dynasty.
    A modern history of the delta's farming says fruit-dike fishponds arose first there, and that mulberry-dike fishponds replaced them in the late Ming and early Qing and became the dominant type in the Qing.
  • Late in the seventeenth century, lychee was the commonest tree on the pond dikes of Guangzhou's villages, with tea and mulberry next.
    Qu Dajun's account of Guangdong was completed in 1678. He says that in the villages of Guangzhou's large counties people often gave up good fields to make dikes and planted them with fruit trees: lychee most, tea and mulberry next, then mandarin and orange. Longan stood mostly beside the houses, but on the dikes as well. So in his day the fruit dike still stood beside the mulberry.
  • Tea grew on the pond dikes too, but Qu Dajun only names it.
    Tea is second in his 1678 list of the dikes' trees, beside mulberry, and he says nothing of how it stood.
  • Lychee and longan were planted on Guangzhou's embankments as well.
    In his chapter on trees Qu Dajun adds that the embankments of Guangzhou were all planted with lychee and longan, and that some people gave up rice fields to plant them. He does not define the embankments; this project reads them as most plausibly those of the diked polders and rivers.
  • How thickly did the fruit trees stand?
    Qu Dajun gives it only for a field. Turned to orchard, a mu of field took twenty-odd lychee trees or twice as many longan, each on a mound of silt about two chi (~2 ft) high against the floods. That is one lychee to about 300 sq ft of field, by this project's arithmetic; he gives no spacing along a dike.
  • In Japan, tea was often grown in a single row along the banks of fields.
    For comparison, the Japanese Wikipedia article on tea gardens describes this bund tea: a single row on the banks of paddies and dry fields, which also marked the boundary with the neighbor, broke the wind and held the soil. More on it is at Cash crops on rice land: dike-ponds, lotus fields and tea rows.

Cane, banana and vegetables reached the dikes later, as far as the record shows.

  • A modern history names a cane dike among the delta's dike-pond types of the Ming and Qing, without a date.
    It lists rice-dike, cane-dike and fan-palm-dike fishponds beside the mulberry and fruit dikes, in its account of the Ming and Qing, and dates none of them.
  • Late in the seventeenth century, in Zengcheng, cane and banana were field crops, grown in turn on the same ground.
    Qu Dajun says that in Zengcheng many grew banana for three or four years, then cut it all down to plant white cane, and after two years of cane planted banana again, and that the cane fields nearly equaled the rice fields.
  • No page we read from before modern times puts cane, banana or vegetables on a pond dike.
    Qu Dajun knows cane and banana well, but places neither on a dike.
  • In the late 1980s, cane dikes covered more of the delta's dike-pond land than mulberry did.
    A survey by the Guangzhou Institute, as a modern website summarizes it, found sugar cane on 18% of the main dike-pond area and mulberry on 12%.
  • A 2005 magazine remembers the delta of twenty years before as banana groves and seas of cane.
    The magazine Chinese National Geography recalls a flat country of dark-green banana groves, seas of sugarcane and fishponds, with mulberry and fruit trees planted on the dikes.
  • The Guangdong gazetteer office describes the fruit, cane and vegetable dikes as a modern succession to the mulberry dike.
    As the delta urbanized and industrialized, it says, the mulberry on the pond dikes gave way to fruit and vegetables, chiefly banana, sugarcane and citrus, and the mulberry-dike fishpond slowly became the fruit-dike, cane-dike and vegetable-dike fishpond. The fruit dike it names is not where the fruit dike began: that was in the middle of the Ming dynasty.
