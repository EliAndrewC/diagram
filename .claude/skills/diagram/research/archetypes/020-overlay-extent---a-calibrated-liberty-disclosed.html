Overlay extent - a calibrated liberty, disclosed

Sources: alic-iwakuni-renkon (the one lotus area share read, a modern one); no readable page gives the lotus area share of a village before the modern market (a few percent to 10-15%); the interpolation is this page's and is labeled so, and the liberty is the GM's

A CALIBRATED LIBERTY is recorded here (GM 2026-07-19): the wet low band an overlay may cover is taken as the bottom two levels of the valley floor rather than only the single hem along the drain. How wide a valley bottom's wet backswamp ran is unrecorded, and the researched extent for a lotus-growing village, a few percent to about 10-15% of field area, is itself interpolated from a national average that includes regions growing none. The single-hem reading put lotus at ~2% - inside the range, but so sparse that it stopped making villages look different from one another, which is the whole reason the overlay exists. We chose the upper part of a plausible range for a stated non-historical reason (legibility) and disclosed it.

What the history says of the band's two ends. The fishpond's share is premodern: Shunde's dike-ponds in 1581 are the county reading below. Lotus has no premodern share on any page read. Premodern lotus districts did exist - Kadoma and Iwakuni in the Edo period, Hunan's paddies turned to lotus in the Qing, all below - but none of those pages gives an area, and the one share read is modern: in the old Iwakuni city (the area before the municipal merger) in 2015, the lotus planted area was about six times the rice. So the upper part of the band fits a village in a lotus district, where the history has rice given up for lotus on low or reclaimed ground, and not an ordinary village, whose lotus before the modern market was a few deep plots (below). The figure itself stays the GM's calibrated liberty; which villages it applies to is raised for the GM.

Two terms decide how far an overlay spreads, and they are independent: the GROUND decides which plots are eligible, the ECONOMY decides how many of the eligible plots convert. A pond, a lotus bed and a hill tea garden each need their own ground (below), so the plots an overlay may take are the lowest, wettest ground of the field - or, for tea, the fertile lower hillside - and a map with none of that ground draws no overlay at all rather than scattering one across ordinary rice. The second term is then a share of that ELIGIBLE ground, never of the whole field: as a share of the whole field it would be inert, because the eligible ground is always the smaller of the two, so every settlement would convert all of its low ground and the economic term would decide nothing. What supports a share well short of everything is the county reading below - Shunde about 4.6% dike-pond in 1581, its earliest pond district at 18% - which is a district deep in ponds inside a county still mostly in rice.

What could not be checked: conversion spread plot by plot from one household to its neighbors, so the converted plots should sit in patches rather than evenly scattered, and the maps place them that way. The patchiness cannot be demonstrated on the map itself: the eligible ground is always a thin continuous strip along the wet bottom, and every subset of a strip counts as clustered by any measure that compares the gaps between neighboring plots with the length of the strip - an even random scatter of the same number scores exactly as well. So the patchwork is asserted from the history rather than shown by the drawing.
