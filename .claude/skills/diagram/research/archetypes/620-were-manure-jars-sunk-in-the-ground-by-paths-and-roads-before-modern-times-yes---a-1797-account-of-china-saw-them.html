Were manure jars sunk in the ground by paths and roads before modern times? Yes - a 1797 account of China saw them

Sources: staunton-1797-embassy (earthen vessels buried to the rim on farms and near paths and roads in China), pwsannong-gudai-feiliao (Wang Zhen's brick pits at the head of a southern field, the Shen manual's deep manure pits, and night soil sealed in buried jars), wang-zhen-enwiki (the date of Wang Zhen's farming book), shimogoe-jawiki (the Hyakusho Denki's storage jar for night soil)

The silk-and-fish hamlet draws its manure store as an earthenware jar half buried in the ground, behind the house, at the edge of a field or beside a road. That form came into the record from one village studied in 1936 (What stands on a dike-pond hamlet that a paddy hamlet lacks?), and this question asks whether it is older than that.

It is: a British visitor saw the roadside jar in the eighteenth century. The account of Britain's embassy to the Qing court, printed in 1797, says of Chinese farmers' care for manure: "In various parts of a farm, and near paths and roads large earthen vessels are buried to the edge in the ground for the accommodation of the labourer or passenger who may have occasion to use them." So the jar, the ground it is sunk in, and its place by a path or a road are all older than modern times. The passage stands in the chapter on the embassy's overland journey toward Canton, but it is written of Chinese practice in general and names no province, so it does not tie the form to the Lake Tai country or to the Pearl River delta in particular. It has the jars buried to the rim where the 1936 village's were half buried; seen from above, as the map sees them, the two are the same mouth in the ground.

A pit at the field's edge is older still. A modern Chinese encyclopedia of farming history quotes Wang Zhen's farming book of 1313: 「Southern households that farm fields commonly set a brick enclosure at the head of the field and use [the manure] after it has ripened in the pit; their fields are very fine」 (translated; original: 「南方治田之家，常于田头置砖槛，窖熟而用之，其田甚美」), and says the Ming-dynasty Shen family farm manual wants its manure pits 「deep and large」 (translated; original: 「潭要深大」). That is a manure store out at the field rather than by the house, as the map rolls for some households - though lined with brick, not made of earthenware. The same encyclopedia has jars in the ground of another kind: its "golden juice" is got by 「sealing human excrement and urine in jars, pots and other vessels and burying them in the ground for a long time」 (translated; original: 「将人粪尿放在缸罐等容器内密封后长期埋于地下」). It gives that practice no date, and a sealed jar left to ripen is not the open jar a passer-by uses, so it bears on the buried jar only weakly.

In Japan the storage jar is on record by 1681. The Hyakusho Denki, as the Japanese Wikipedia quotes it, warns against fresh night soil on the fields and says it should be "rotted well inside the storage jar (tametsubo)". The passage does not say whether the jar was buried, or where it stood.

What this means for the map. The half-buried manure jar is not a modern-only form: an account printed in 1797 puts earthen jars sunk in the ground on farms and by paths and roads, and Wang Zhen's farming book puts manure pits at the head of the field. The maps keep drawing it, behind the house, at a field edge or beside a road. The jar's size stays a guess: none of these passages measures one.
