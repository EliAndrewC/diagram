Does a pig sty have to stand back from the water, or from the pond's sluice?

Sources: fao-ac264e-ch9, fao-ac264e, fao-y1187e, qimin-yaoshu-yangzhu, fao-ac257e, fao-x6708e, isis-dykepond

No, and standing at the water is the whole point of the fixture. A pig shed on a fish-pond dike is not a shed that happens to be near water - it is put there so the waste goes in: "Pigsties are usually built on the pond dike of an integrated fish farm so that the pig excrement can be directly flushed into the pond", and a pond the size of the ones drawn here is exactly the case where that is done directly rather than through a pit: "If the area of a fish pond is less than 8 mu, a pigsty can be set up on the pond dyke and pig wastes will flow directly into the pond". A dike-pond hamlet's ponds are about 4 mu of water each as drawn, inside that band - though they are small ponds even so, below the 0.4 to 0.6 hectares reported for the delta at second hand, because a hamlet's ponds are small, not because of anything in this question.

The manure is feed, not fouling. It fertilizes the water rather than poisoning it: "this technique of feeding animal manures to fish fertilizes the water, producing plankton which is a good natural fish food", by three routes at once - "stimulating phytoplankton production; and acting as substrate for bacterial production (detritus) and as feed for zooplankton". So the fish the household cultivates are, in part, eating what the pigs produce. Where the reasons for a pen's position are given at all, they are the labor of carrying waste and the comfort of the animals - "On the pond dike: Pens close to the pond to reduce labour cost of loading waste", "Over the pond: Pens are cooler and more humid" - and no page read gives contamination as a reason.

The oldest instruction runs the other way. The Qimin Yaoshu of about 540 AD gives exactly one rule for where a pig pen goes, and it is the opposite of a cleanliness rule: 「The pen is not disliked for being small - a small pen makes them fatten fast. The place is not disliked for being filthy - mud and muck let them escape the heat.」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「圈不厭小。〈圈小則肥疾。〉處不厭穢。〈泥污得避暑。〉」) It is northern China a thousand years before these settlements and it says nothing of fish ponds, so it does not settle how a pig and a pond stood together - but it does show that the instinct to hold a pen back from filth and water is not one the record supplies.

What CAN go wrong is a rate, and a place within the pond. Too much manure tells on the fish, and the symptom is visible from the bank: "Fish surfacing increases (dissolved oxygen content decreases) when pig manure sinks to the bottom of the pond or when too much manure flows into the pond". Every handle the record gives for that is a quantity or a date rather than a distance - more than thirty pigs is too many for the direct-flow shed, and none applied after mid-October. The one source that speaks to WHERE the waste enters keeps the shed where it is and moves the manure instead: "manure distribution techniques generally need to be employed to avoid the development of anaerobic conditions in the pond adjacent to the livestock quarters", the failure it is curing having been watched - "It was observed that the fish lost their apetite, presumably due to lack of dissolved oxygen because of anaerobic conditions at the manuring - feeding sites". So there is a dead patch in the water under the shed, and it is managed with buckets and channels rather than by moving the shed. Nothing of that is drawn: a bucket round is not a map feature, and a tinted patch would assert a condition no source ties to a premodern pond.

On the sluice itself, the record is silent. (No source: searched 2026-09-12 across the integrated-farming reports of the United Nations' Food and Agriculture Organization - chapters 7 to 10 of Integrated Fish Farming in China, the training manual, the agriculture-aquaculture primer, the consultancy report and the training series - the Institute of Science in Society's dike-pond summary, and Chinese and Japanese material including the Qimin Yaoshu and the Nogyo Zensho, the 1697 Japanese farming encyclopedia. Nothing relates a shed's or a pen's position on the dike to the pond's inlet, outlet, sluice or drainage gate, for it or against it. The sources place the sheds only in relation to the pond itself, and describe the canals with no building on them at all.) So the map claims nothing about it, and the reason a sty is not drawn standing on its pond's sluice is not a sanitary one.

What the map does, and why. A sty or a pen is held about 6 ft clear of the short culvert that feeds or drains its pond. That is a CONSTRUCTIONAL clearance, not a sanitary one: nobody builds a shed over the opening they have to reach in order to work it, a sluice being "a protected opening in the pond dike that can be easily closed with wooden boards to regulate water level", and 6 ft is about the room one person needs to stand there and draw a board. The 6 ft itself is a GUESS - the record gives dike WIDTHS and no spacing along a dike at all - and it is small enough to cost the household nothing: the sty simply stands a few paces further round the same bank.
