How did a polder get rid of its water, and what shows of it?

Sources: waju-jawiki (the downstream dike that made draining hard), wajyu-nogyo (drain culverts opened when the river fell, how many one waju had, and the horita), ishizue-waju (the intake at the high end and the outlet at the low), wuhurec-wanchun (a Song-dynasty polder's five water gates), people-longgu-shuiche (the chain pump lifting water out in flood), ishizue-ariake (a sea polder's outlet lost to the growing tidal flat), njg-horita (the horita's dug channels used by field boat)

A polder's floor lies at or below the water outside it (above), so the rain that falls inside and the water that seeps in have nowhere to run. Closing a ring on its downstream side as well made its foul water hard to drain, and from then on the waju suffered from its own standing water as well as the river's. Three answers are attested.

By gravity, through a gated culvert, when the water outside fell

The first answer was to wait. With no power to pump, a waju could not drain whenever it liked, because the river outside often stood higher than its fields; so it drained through a hakehi, a culvert through the ring dike that was shut while the river was high and opened when it fell. Water came in at the top of a waju and left at the bottom: the intake was set at its upstream head and the outlet at its downstream tail. There could be many. The villages of the Fukutsuka waju had, between 1789 and 1872, thirty-four hakehi and one water gate for drainage against four intake culverts; intakes were fewer because a gate strong enough to let river water in safely was very hard to build through the dike, and after one blew out in the flood of 1722 the Fukutsuka waju set no other for a long time, so that surprisingly many waju paddies waited on the rain. A Chinese polder worked its gates the same way. The Wanchun polder of 1061 had five water gates; they were opened in drought to let the river in and shut against it in flood, and when the polder was waterlogged and the river outside fell below the gate, it could be opened to let the water out.

A polder on the sea drained the same way against the tide. On the Ariake Sea, where the tide rises and falls about 5 m and the flats run out 5 to 7 km at low water, a reclaimed field's outlet opened onto the flat; as the flat grew, the older reclaimed land behind lost its outlet and could no longer drain - one reason each generation reclaimed another band beyond it. That such an outlet was opened on the ebb and shut on the flood is the same rule as the river culvert, applied to the tide; no page read says it in so many words.

By lifting: the chain pump

The second answer was to lift the water over the dike. The chain pump, a trough through which a loop of paddles drags water uphill, worked where the water had to rise only a little, and both ways: besides raising water into the fields in drought, it could throw water out in flood, which helped the Jiangnan polders spread. Where on a polder the pumps stood, and how many, no page read says.

By raising the rice: the horita

The third answer, where the water would not go, was to lift the rice above it. In a waju that stayed waterlogged, part of each paddy was dug out and the mud piled on the rest to raise the planting surface - the horita. Chronic poor drainage gave the waju this look, and the dug strips between became channels traveled by field boat. From above it looked like a field of long ridges drowned so that only the ridges showed, spread across the whole view; the form spread across the waju from about 1753 and was gone by 1975.

What this means for the map

What shows of a polder's drainage is its openings: culverts and gates through the dike, drains at the low end and intakes at the high, and on a large polder many of them, drains outnumbering intakes. Gravity drainage through gated openings is every polder's, and the number of openings is a degree set by the polder's size; the maps draw two sluices for a village polder, which a small polder may well have had. The horita is a distinct field form, attested for Japanese polders that stayed waterlogged from the middle of the eighteenth century, so it is a knob such a polder may roll in place of ordinary paddy: long raised strips of rice with water channels between. The chain pump leaves no fixed mark on the plan that anything read describes.
