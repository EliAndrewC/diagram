What stands on a dike-pond hamlet that a paddy hamlet lacks?

Sources: fei-1939, cssn-sangyuanwei, gd-gazetteer-sangji

What the question was. The GM (2026-08-27), looking at the rice hamlet's outhouses and the rest: "this is a different type of map ... I could imagine there being map features which would exist in Kuwabata but not in Inashiro." The question was what PHYSICAL features stood on a silk-and-fish hamlet's ground - farmsteads, dikes, water's edge - that a rice hamlet's did not, and which rice features it should lack.

What the research found. The one primary ethnography of a silk village that could be read in full is Fei Hsiao-tung's Peasant Life in China (1939), on Kaihsienkung south of Lake Tai - a rice-AND-silk village on a stream net, not a converted dike-pond block, so it answers the SILK half of the loop directly and the FISH half only by the Pearl-delta secondary literature.

  • Silkworms were reared INSIDE the dwelling, not in a separate building. "Silkworms are raised in rectangular and shallow containers about [1.5] by [1] metre in size. They are put on the shelves of a stand. Each stand has eight containers. Each house has enough room for five stands." And during the two-week peak "all the rooms except the kitchen may be used for sheltering silkworms. All the members of the household will crowd in one bed-room." The front room "is used for working, such as raising silkworms, manufacturing silk, threshing rice"; the old reeling machine ("a furnace to boil water, a wheel ... a rotating axle connected to a plate for treading") stood in the house too. So a sericulture hamlet has no silkworm house and no reeling shed: the record puts both indoors. (Communal rearing halls (蚕房, canfang) are, in this page's reading, a 1950s collective form - the one dating found, 1958 for Shunde examples, is in a search summary rather than on any page read - and are not premodern.)
  • Boats, and a village laid out along its water. "Nearly every household possesses one or more boats except those who are not engaged in agricultural or fishing work." "The importance of the boat in communication means that the houses must be near the water and consequently determines the plan of the village. Villages grow up along the streams." "Every village has its own agent boat which serves as the buying and selling agent of the villagers in the town market." The Pearl-delta proverb of the Sangyuanwei polder says the same of silk: 「a boat of silk goes out, a boat of silver comes back」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「一船蚕丝出，一船白银归」) - a boat of silk out, a boat of silver back. A cash-crop hamlet that sells silk and fish, in a district that 「abandoned rice to plant mulberry」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「废稻树桑」), and so buys its grain in (this page's inference from the abandoned rice), is a hamlet that LIVES by water transport. Kuwabata as drawn has a header reservoir and a ring canal and no navigable water at all. It was put to the GM as a candidate (a creek along the landward flank with boats and a landing) and DECLINED (2026-08-28): "would it necessarily be the case that any settlement of this sort would have one. I wouldn't think so" - Kaihsienkung's water is that village's geography, not the archetype's; the hamlet's connector lane leads to the market, or to whatever water carries its goods there, and the map presumes rather than draws it.
  • Manure PITS, not heaps. "Human manure, being the most important fertilizer in the farm, is preserved in the pits made of earthenware, half buried in the ground at the back of the building. Along the southern bank of the Stream A, the public road is lined up with these manure pits." Two attested forms for the same fixture: the rice hamlet (Inashiro) draws a heap, and a roadside pit would be a distinct thing to draw.
  • Mulberry on the raised margin, houses beside it, vegetables beneath it. "Along the margin of each yu, ten to thirty metres of land is left for plantation of mulberry trees and a wider space for house building. This land on the margin is higher. It also serves as a dyke for the farm." - the yu being, in Fei's words, 「the unit of land surrounded by water」. "Limited space under the mulberry trees for growing vegetables." Kuwabata draws exactly this: the planted perimeter dike, the village on the dry flank.
  • Sheep huts (Fei: "little huts for the sheep and sometimes also for storage", "the sheep hut has become a common appendage of a house") are a 1920s-30s Lake Tai innovation ("in the past ten years") - so none stands on a premodern map.
  • Pigs and ducks ON the pond dikes - the Pearl-delta loop, attested and drawn; what it rests on, the wider dike a shed needs, and why the form is Chinese rather than Japanese: Does a dike-pond hamlet keep pigs and ducks on its pond dikes?
  • Fish fry as a trade, and nursery ponds - a candidate, not drawn: Were fish fry a trade, and which ponds were the nursery ponds?
  • Other dike crops: sugar cane, fruit, banana - rolled per hamlet and drawn, Kuwabata being the mulberry case: What else was planted on a pond dike besides mulberry?
  • The dike-pond's own sluices - already drawn ('A dike-pond is fed and drained through sluice gates', above).
  • Pond-side huts (塘头屋, tangtouwu / a watchman's hut): searched twice in Chinese and English, nothing attested.
  • A silk-goddess shrine (蚕花娘娘 Canhua Niangniang, 蚕神庙 canshen miao): the Huzhou-Jiaxing festival temples are VILLAGE and town institutions (the Qingming 轧蚕花 gatherings, ya canhua, the crowded silkworm-flower fair); a hamlet has no shrine by the tier's own definition, so a shrine of this kind belongs on a village map.
  • What a no-rice hamlet should LACK. Fei's village carried its rice to the open space in front of the house, and threshing "is done in the open space or in the front room" - the yard's threshing use is a RICE use. Kuwabata draws a threshing yard on every farmstead as the rice hamlet does; with no rice within its bounds the yard is at best a work yard (cocoons are sorted and leaves handled somewhere). The straw rick is already not drawn. The in-field pocket pond, the dry hem and the comb deliveries are absent by archetype already.
  • Holdings. Not settled: nothing read gives a silk-and-fish household's ground, so the ground per household (the paddy hamlet's 1.3 ac) stays a labeled GUESS on this archetype.

Departures and honesty. Fei is Lake Tai, 1936; the Pearl-delta items here rest on a proverb and a gazetteer page, and the Pearl-delta secondary literature behind the pigs, the fry and the dike crops is weighed in those questions. No single source describes a WHOLLY converted hamlet's farmsteads; the list is assembled across the two systems and says so per item.
