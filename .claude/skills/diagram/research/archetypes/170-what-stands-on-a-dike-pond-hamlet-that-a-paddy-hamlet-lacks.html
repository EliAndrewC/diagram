What stands on a dike-pond hamlet that a paddy hamlet lacks?

Sources: fei-1939, fao-ac264e, isis-dykepond (carrying Ruddle & Zhong's Pearl-delta figures), cssn-sangyuanwei, cssn-sangji-yutang, fao-ac264e-ch10, hudson-munoz-2023, dili360-2005-sangji, fao-ac264e-ch4, chi-2024-dike-pond-commons

What the question was. The GM (2026-08-27), looking at the rice hamlet's outhouses and the rest: "this is a different type of map ... I could imagine there being map features which would exist in Kuwabata but not in Inashiro." The question was what PHYSICAL features stood on a silk-and-fish hamlet's ground - farmsteads, dikes, water's edge - that a rice hamlet's did not, and which rice features it should lack.

What the research found. The one primary ethnography of a silk village that could be read in full is Fei Hsiao-tung's Peasant Life in China (1939), on Kaihsienkung south of Lake Tai - a rice-AND-silk village on a stream net,47 not a converted dike-pond block, so it answers the SILK half of the loop directly and the FISH half only by the Pearl-delta secondary literature.

  • Silkworms were reared INSIDE the dwelling, not in a separate building. "Silkworms are raised in rectangular and shallow containers about [1.5] by [1] metre in size. They are put on the shelves of a stand. Each stand has eight containers. Each house has enough room for five stands."48 And during the two-week peak "all the rooms except the kitchen may be used for sheltering silkworms. All the members of the household will crowd in one bed-room."49 The front room "is used for working, such as raising silkworms, manufacturing silk, threshing rice"50; the old reeling machine ("a furnace to boil water, a wheel ... a rotating axle connected to a plate for treading") stood in the house too.51 So a sericulture hamlet has no silkworm house and no reeling shed: the record puts both indoors. (Communal rearing halls (蚕房, canfang) are, in this page's reading, a 1950s collective form - the Shunde examples once seen in a search summary were dated 1958 - and are not premodern.)122
  • Boats, and a village laid out along its water. "Nearly every household possesses one or more boats except those who are not engaged in agricultural or fishing work."52 "The importance of the boat in communication means that the houses must be near the water and consequently determines the plan of the village. Villages grow up along the streams."53 "Every village has its own agent boat which serves as the buying and selling agent of the villagers in the town market."54 The Pearl-delta proverb of the Sangyuanwei polder says the same of silk: 「a boat of silk goes out, a boat of silver comes back」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「一船蚕丝出，一船白银归」)55 - a boat of silk out, a boat of silver back. A cash-crop hamlet that sells silk and fish and buys grain in is a hamlet that LIVES by water transport. Kuwabata as drawn has a header reservoir and a ring canal and no navigable water at all. It was put to the GM as a candidate (a creek along the landward flank with boats and a landing) and DECLINED (2026-08-28): "would it necessarily be the case that any settlement of this sort would have one. I wouldn't think so" - Kaihsienkung's water is that village's geography, not the archetype's; the hamlet's connector lane leads to the market, or to whatever water carries its goods there, and the map presumes rather than draws it.
  • Manure PITS, not heaps. "Human manure, being the most important fertilizer in the farm, is preserved in the pits made of earthenware, half buried in the ground at the back of the building. Along the southern bank of the Stream A, the public road is lined up with these manure pits."56 Two attested forms for the same fixture: the rice hamlet (Inashiro) draws a heap, and a roadside pit would be a distinct thing to draw.
  • Mulberry on the raised margin, houses beside it, vegetables beneath it. "Along the margin of each yu, ten to thirty metres of land is left for plantation of mulberry trees and a wider space for house building. This land on the margin is higher. It also serves as a dyke for the farm."57 - the yu being the polder itself, the diked block the fields lie inside. "Limited space under the mulberry trees for growing vegetables."58 Kuwabata draws exactly this: the planted perimeter dike, the village on the dry flank.
  • Sheep huts (Fei: "little huts for the sheep and sometimes also for storage", "the sheep hut has become a common appendage of a house") are a 1920s-30s Lake Tai innovation ("in the past ten years"59) - so none stands on a premodern map.
  • Pigs and ducks ON the pond dikes - the Pearl-delta loop. Ruddle & Zhong (1988) as carried by the i-sis summary: "Pigs, chickens and ducks are reared on the dykes, to provide manure to fertilise the fishponds"60; the modern FAO/NACA manual: "The simple pig shed constructed on the pond dyke or over the water surface" lets the excreta flow to the pond;61 fish-cum-duck ponds fence a dry run on the dike and a wet run in a corner of the water.62 The premodern prevalence is not in anything read; the specific proverb about cane pressings fed to pigs (蔗榨糖后的蔗渣喂猪, zhe zha tang hou de zhezha wei zhu) was not found on any page fetched, but isis-dykepond records sugar-cane leaves fed to fish and pigs and refinery waste returned to the dyke pond as feed,85 and cssn-sangji-yutang records farmers building frames over the pond to raise pigs,86 which puts pigs in the traditional loop. A pig sty on a pond dike and a duck pen at a pond corner are both attested forms, then, though how common they were before the modern period is unquantified. Both are drawn. A dike that carries a shed is a WIDER dike, which is the one constructional number the record gives: "The width of the dikes between fish ponds and inflow and outflow canals should be kept within 5 m; the width of the dikes for pigsties, cow sheds piping, or traffic should range from 5 to 10 m".94 Only the floor of that range bears on a map like this one: the page itself sets the ceiling by pigsties, piping and traffic, uses that a hand-piled mulberry dike did not serve - dike-borne traffic came with commercialization, when water transport was abandoned for ground transport121 - and in Shunde the dikes ran 20 m wide until commercial fish farming eroded them, later than this map and wider than the manual's ceiling either way.145 And measured against the drawing, the map does NOT meet it. The ground between one pond's water and the next runs about 13 m, but that is not one bank - a canal runs down the middle of it, and the planted collar a sty actually stands on is 2 to 5 m wide. So the strip under the shed is narrower than the 5 m a shed-carrying dike is told to have. The mismatch is recorded rather than drawn away: that figure is a design requirement for farms built in the 1980s, not a measurement of any old dike, and nothing read gives the width of a premodern one. A reader should know the drawn collar is snug by a modern standard and that no older standard was found to judge it by. And the pig on the dike is a CHINESE form, which the map should say rather than leave implied. The Japanese record does not carry it: the standard account - that pigs were introduced early, never took hold, and that religious proscription limited meat until the nineteenth century - is named by its most recent scholarly treatment as an assumption under review rather than a finding,95 and where mainland Japanese pigs WERE kept the reading is pannage, loose in forest, with Okinawa the stated exception: "With the exception of Okinawa, Japan seems to have followed the pannage system".96 Japanese pigs WERE kept, and the same survey records them in the early modern towns - "Sites with significant numbers of Sus bones (both wild and domesticated) include the Satsuma domain residence in Edo, Osaka castle, Hakata, and Nagasaki harbour".109 What has no Japanese counterpart is the FORM - a pen standing on a fish-pond dike. (No source: nothing in the material read - that survey, the integrated-farming reports, and Japanese carp-culture pages searched 2026-09-12 - links a Japanese pig to a pond at all.) A dike-pond hamlet is a Chinese-derived form throughout, so its pond stock is Chinese too, and a hamlet of any other archetype has no business with either.
  • Fish fry (鱼花, yuhua) as a trade, and nursery ponds. Jiujiang township (Nanhai) was the delta's fry center from the Ming (Miles 2003, which has no publicly readable copy: the township's rise 1395-1657 on fry collected from the Xijiang);87 Qu Dajun's seventeenth-century observation of Jiujiang village in Nanhai, in his Guangdong xinyu, 「the men trade fish fry, the women feed the silkworms」 (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「男贩鱼花，妇女喂爱蚕」) - men trade fry, women feed the worms63. Fry are reared in small ponds, then fingerling ponds, before the grow-out ponds are stocked, so a block's smallest parcels would be its nursery ponds - a modern manual puts them at 25 to 30 percent of pond area.120
  • Other dike crops: sugar cane, fruit, banana (蔗基 / 果基 / 蕉基, zheji / guoji / jiaoji). The 1980s survey carried by i-sis: sugar-cane dikes 18% of the district against mulberry 12%64; 中国国家地理 2005 on the lost landscape: 蕉林蔗海 (jiaolin zhehai, banana groves and a sea of cane65); gd-gazetteer-sangji, the gazetteer office, which frames the types as a SUCCESSION - the mulberry dike giving way to fruit, cane and vegetable dikes over the region's history - rather than as three plantings side by side.83 Attested alternative FORMS of the dike planting. Each is drawn: a hamlet rolls one crop and plants every dike with it, cane, banana or fruit, and Kuwabata is the mulberry case.
  • The dike-pond's own sluices - already drawn ('A dike-pond is fed and drained through sluice gates', above).
  • Pond-side huts (塘头屋, tangtouwu / a watchman's hut): searched twice in Chinese and English, nothing attested.
  • A silk-goddess shrine (蚕花娘娘 Canhua Niangniang, 蚕神庙 canshen miao): the Huzhou-Jiaxing festival temples are VILLAGE and town institutions (the Qingming 轧蚕花 gatherings, ya canhua, the crowded silkworm-flower fair); a hamlet has no shrine by the tier's own definition, so a shrine of this kind belongs on a village map.119
  • What a no-rice hamlet should LACK. Fei's village threshed rice "in the open space in front of the house, or in the front room"66 - the yard's threshing use is a RICE use. Kuwabata draws a threshing yard on every farmstead as the rice hamlet does; with no rice within its bounds the yard is at best a work yard (cocoons are sorted and leaves handled somewhere). The straw rick is already not drawn. The in-field pocket pond, the dry hem and the comb deliveries are absent by archetype already.
  • Holdings. Not settled: nothing read gives a silk-and-fish household's ground, so the ground per household (the paddy hamlet's 1.3 ac) stays a labeled GUESS on this archetype.

Departures and honesty. The gazetteer office frames 桑、蔗、蕉 (mulberry, cane, banana) as a SUCCESSION of dike-pond types (the mulberry dike 桑基 giving way to fruit, cane and vegetable dikes, 果基、蔗基、菜基, over the region's history), not one dike carrying all three - so a hamlet would be of one dike type rather than mixing crops on a dike. Fei is Lake Tai, 1936; the Pearl-delta reads are 1980s surveys and a 2006 summary of the 1988 monograph, which itself could not be fetched (403 on every host) - every Ruddle & Zhong number here rests on the summary, not the monograph. No single source describes a WHOLLY converted hamlet's farmsteads; the list is assembled across the two systems and says so per item.
