Cash crops on rice land: dike-ponds, lotus fields and tea rows

On the rice land of China and of Japan, a village could give some of its ground to a crop it grew for sale while the rest stayed in rice. Three such crops each needed ground of their own: the dike-pond, a fish pond dug out of a low, flood-prone hollow with mulberry planted on the bank heaped from its mud; lotus, which grows in standing water about 30 cm (~12 in) to 2.5 m (~8 ft) deep; and tea, which in the north of China grew on the lower, fertile hillside and never on the low land.

Most of the time a cash crop took only part of a village. Dike-ponds arose in the early Ming and came to take three-quarters of the farmland at Longshan in the Qianlong reign; lotus districts were some places, not everywhere; and in Japan tea was also grown as a single row along the field banks, taking no plot at all.

The dike-pond itself - its water and its dike, its fish and its silkworms - is at The 6:4 water-to-dike ratio, and coppiced mulberry.

  • A dike-pond was dug in a low hollow that flooded.
    Around the ancient Lake Tai, where floods and waterlogging were frequent, farmers dug the low-lying ground deep to make fishponds and heaped the pond mud around them into dikes that both held off floods and could be planted. At Shunde, in the Pearl River delta, they were made the same way: the mud was spread round the sides as the dike, and the hollow left in the middle became the pond.
  • In the Pearl River delta, dike-ponds arose in the early Ming and reached their height at the end of the Qing.
    They arose in the early Ming dynasty, flourished in the middle of the Qing dynasty and reached their height at its end.
  • This page guesses that a pond was one household's work, and that ponds spread from neighbor to neighbor.
    That one household dug one pond in one dry season is a GUESS: the passages describe the digging but not who did it or how long it took. So is the reading that ponds spread from one household to its neighbors, so that a district's ponds lay in patches among the rice rather than evenly scattered: no page we read says how they spread.
  • How much of the land did dike-ponds take before modern times?
    Only a township's figure is on record. In the land survey of 1581, Shunde county had 40,084 mu of fishponds, and Longshan, one of its two earliest pond districts, 8,124 mu - 18% of its 44,947 mu of hill, dry land and ponds. That total leaves out Longshan's paddy, by this page's reading, so the 18% is not a share of the township's farmland, and the county's cultivated total, which would make its ponds a share, is on no page we read.
  • In the eighteenth century, dike-ponds took three-quarters of Longshan's farmland.
    In the Qianlong reign fields were abandoned to build ponds, and at Longshan (Longshanbao) "outside the dwellings all was pond": the dike-ponds reached 30,000 mu, 75% of the cultivated area. At Longjiang and Longshan, the Shunde library's history says, there were mulberry ponds and no fish ponds - every pond there was a mulberry pond.
  • As late as the 1980s, a quarter of the Pearl River delta's dike-pond heartland was still in rice.
    A survey of the main dike-pond area of the late 1980s found 35% of it fishponds and 25% mainly irrigated rice.
  • This page guesses that low ground alone did not make a dike-pond district.
    The sandy alluvial fields of the outer delta (shatian) were as low and as wet and, as this page reads it, did not go over to ponds. Its GUESS is that low ground was needed but was not enough: the dike-pond district also had small family holdings inside an established polder and water access to Canton. No page we read says what the outer-delta fields grew, or why ponds did not spread there.

Mulberry did not need a pond at all.

  • On a rice polder, mulberry grew on the dike and the paddy stayed in rice.
    In Fei Xiaotong's village by Lake Tai in the 1930s, a strip ten to thirty meters (~33-98 ft) wide along the raised margin of each polder was left for mulberry, with a wider space for houses, and this higher margin served as the farm's dike; rice remained the main crop. The record of the Wanchun polder, written in 1061, has mulberry planted in rows flanking the ridge of its dike, some tens of thousands of trees. There a cash crop's extent was the length of the dike. Why polder dikes were planted is at Why dikes were planted, and what the row spacing rests on.

Lotus needed the opposite of a dike: the deepest, wettest ground a village had.

  • Lotus grows in water about 30 cm (~12 in) to 2.5 m (~8 ft) deep.
    The sacred lotus grows in water from about 30 cm (~12 in) to 2.5 m (~8 ft) deep. No page we read gives the depth of water a paddy holds.
  • Lotus could be grown in turn with rice.
    Where its roots are dug in July, rice can be planted after them in the same field.
  • A sixth-century Chinese farm manual plants lotus in the mud of a fish pond.
    The Qimin Yaoshu, believed completed in 544, gives a method for planting lotus root: dig up its node-heads at the start of spring and plant them in the mud of a fish pond, and the same year there will be flowers.
  • Before the Song dynasty, lotus grown for its root was almost all deep-water lotus.
    A history of China's water vegetables says the lotus grown for its root before the Song dynasty was essentially all deep-water lotus, and that shallow-water lotus first appears in Song texts.
  • In the Ming and Qing, Wu county was the main lotus-root district around Suzhou.
    Of the water-vegetable districts around Suzhou's lakes, Wu county was the main lotus-root district.
  • In Qing Hunan, farmers gave up rice fields for seed lotus.
    Seed lotus, grown for its seeds rather than its root, needs rich soil, so in the Qing most counties of Hunan grew it in paddy fields, and from the Daoguang reign on, lotus seed being dear, farmers one after another gave up rice fields to plant it.

In Japan the lotus districts on record are later, from the end of the Edo period, before Japan began to modernize in 1868.

  • At Kadoma, near Osaka, lotus-root growing became active in the late Edo period.
    Kadoma's villages were rich farm villages on low wetland the Yodo River flooded, and in the late Edo period they grew rapeseed and cotton, and lotus root too. Its peak, and the fame of Kawachi lotus root, came only in the Taisho period, with strains brought in from elsewhere.
  • At Iwakuni lotus is said to have begun in 1796, late in the Edo period, and the domain ran it from 1811.
    The lord's order brought seed lotus from Bitchu, and his successors spread it on the reclaimed land they filled, ground fit for no other crop. In 1811 it began as an enterprise of the domain.
  • At Kasai, lotus was a small crop until the middle of the Meiji period.
    Lotus seems to have been grown there only a little from the middle of the Edo period. It spread fast in the middle of the Meiji period, once an improved strain made it pay far better than rice, on ground with rich river soil, many deep paddies and the city's night soil at hand, until the district was covered in lotus leaves as far as the eye could see.
  • In the Katsushika villages next to Kasai, lotus came later still.
    The Tokyo prefecture record of 1872 lists no lotus even in the villages later known for it, and in 1917 four settlements together planted 7 cho 6 tan (about 19 acres, by this page's arithmetic).
  • How much of a village did lotus take before modern times?
    No page we read gives a share, before modern times or after, for an ordinary village. The premodern lotus pages give dates, output and districts, never an area, and the nearest estimate, a few percent to about 10-15% of a lotus-growing village's field, is an interpolation from a national average that includes regions growing none. The one share read is modern: in the old city of Iwakuni in 2015, lotus was planted on about six times the area of rice. So, by this page's reading, an ordinary village grew lotus in a few deep plots, and only a lotus district gave up much of its rice.

Tea took different ground again: not the wet bottom of the valley but the slope above the rice, and in Japan the banks between the fields.

  • Robert Fortune found the tea plantations of north China on the lower, fertile hillsides, never on the low land.
    He saw them in the 1840s, and says they always stood on the lower and most fertile sides of the hills. What grew on the slope above the tea, his passage does not say.
  • The tea farms Fortune saw were smallholders' gardens, in rows about four feet apart.
    Fortune saw the bushes planted in rows about four feet apart, on farms of one to four or five acres, every cottager with a little tea garden of their own. A hillside therefore carried many small gardens rather than one block - this page's reading of that passage.
  • This page takes neat contour terraces of tea to be a modern form.
    It places them in the second half of the twentieth century - a GUESS: no page we read dates them.
  • In Japan tea was also planted as a single row along the field banks.
    Bund tea, 畦畔茶 (keihan-cha, "bund tea"), was a row of tea planted on the bunds of paddies and dry fields. It was often seen. It marked the boundary with the neighbor, broke the wind and kept the soil from washing away, and what the household did not drink was sometimes sold to a wholesaler. Its extent was a line, not an area. No page we read records bund tea in China.
