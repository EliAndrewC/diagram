How high and how broad was a polder's dike?

Sources: wuhurec-wanchun (the Wanchun polder's dike: breadth, height, crown and planting), pwsannong-zhuwei (the range of polder sizes), cssn-jiangnan-weitian (the great polders "like a city"), fukui-kuzuryu-edo-dikes (an early Edo earth dike's base, crown and height), ishizue-waju (a waju dike held lower than the great dike by agreement), waju-jawiki (the smaller inner banks), wajyu-nogyo (a drain culvert's length through a waju dike)

The one polder dike with figures that survive from before the modern era is a great one. Shen Kuo's record of the Wanchun polder, rebuilt in 1061, gives its dike as six zhang broad and one zhang two chi high, eighty-four li round, with a road inside it twenty-two li long, wide enough for two carts and lined with willows, and five water gates. So the dike was five times as broad at its foot as it was high. The same record says the foot was several zhang broad and the crown only a few chi, that a hundred rows of willows were set along it and reeds planted below it, so that the waves broke a hundred paces out and never reached the dike.

Wanchun was the top of the range. Polders ran from a few tens of mu to over a hundred thousand, their shapes square, round, oblong, many-sided or irregular as the ground gave; the greatest were described as several tens of li across, "like a great city", with channels inside and gates outside. A village polder of a few households is the other end of that range, and no page read gives its dike's height or breadth.

In Japan, the early Edo earth dikes on the Kuzuryu river in Echizen were, with differences from place to place, about 3 ken (5.45 m) across the base, 1 ken (1.82 m) across the crown and 2 ken (3.64 m) high; before them the usual forms had been open-ended dikes and ring dikes round a community. These were a domain's river dikes guarding a castle town, which puts them above a village's own ring. A ring dike's height was not the villagers' alone to choose: on the Nobi plain the Mino side's dikes were fixed, by agreement, at 3 shaku (about 1 m) lower than the great dike across the river that guarded Owari. Inside a waju, the banks raised against the fields' own standing water were smaller than the ring dike. One measure of how broad a great waju's ring could be: the main drains of the Fukutsuka waju were seven wooden culverts each 41.1 m long, passing through the dike. A culvert that long says the dike there was of that order through at its foot (our reading: a culvert may run out beyond the dike's toes on either side).

What this means for the map

A dike's height and breadth are a degree along a continuum, set by the size of the polder and the water it faces, so they are a calibrated liberty rather than a knob. What was read gives the bounds and the profile, not a village's own figure: a broad foot several times the height, narrowing to a crown a person or a cart can walk, planted along its length. A village polder's dike belongs below a domain's river dike - under about 3.6 m high on a foot of about 5.5 m - and far below a great official polder's. The figure of about 1.5 m for a small polder's dike, above, sits inside those bounds and remains unsourced.
