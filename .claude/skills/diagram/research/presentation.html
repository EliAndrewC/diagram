Presentation: the map's drawing conventions - labels, captions, framing and cropping

The findings behind the labels, captions, titles and frames on these maps, the decisions they drove, and - where no generator draws the feature yet - the rules a map follows.

Almost everything on this page is a map drawing convention: a choice about how the sheet is drawn so a human eye can read it, with no historical finding behind it. A settlement had no title lettered across its fields and no bar showing a hundred feet; those are ours, and so is every rule about where a caption may sit. Where a decision does rest on something about the world - a pond that feeds a field by gravity has to stand above the field's water entry - the entry says so. Everywhere else the honest label on the decision is "convention", and the entries use that word rather than dressing a drawing choice as a finding.

Why is there no legend, no compass and no caption on the obvious?

Sources: the GM's rulings of 2026-07-20; this project's own measurements of the shipped maps

Because a settlement map earns more by being self-evident than by being annotated. The sheet carries the place name and nothing else that explains itself: no key box pairing swatches to meanings, no descriptive line under the title telling the reader this is "an average farming village", and no caption on anything the drawing already says. A reader can count the torii, so the map never states how many there are; it can see that a building stands on a hill, so no subtitle says "atop the hill". Captions are spent on the few things the ink cannot carry - which plots lie fallow, which are tax-free, what a particular building is - and the rest of the sheet is left to be looked at.

There is no compass either. These maps are always drawn north up, so a rose would repeat a fact that never varies while adding a non-diegetic object to a sheet that otherwise shows only the place; it was removed everywhere. What the sheet does carry, since the GM's ruling of 2026-07-20 and matching the building sheets, is a scale bar drawn directly under the place name: a ruled bar with a tick at each end and one in the middle, the real distance written beneath it, and a line of fine print giving the feet in one drawn unit. The bar is a fixed length on the page rather than a fixed distance on the ground, which is what makes it cheap: the same bar reads 100 ft on a hamlet or town sheet, 200 ft on a village one and 300 ft on a provincial city's, all of them round numbers, so no tier needs the bar tuned by hand.

The name itself is dropped into blank ground. The placard - title and bar together - is seated by scanning the framed window from the top for a text box that clears every feature on the map, so the place name never lies across buildings, fields, water or a grove. The search runs over the final window, after the frame is fixed, or it would seat the name against an edge that then moves.

The rule the map follows: every settlement sheet, at every tier, carries the place name and a scale bar beneath it, and nothing else of that kind - no legend, no key, no compass, no descriptive subtitle, and no caption stating a count or a fact the drawing already shows. The bar's stated distance agrees with the scale the map declares. The placard stands clear of every drawn feature. Caption text renders above all other ink, so no part of a caption is ever covered by something drawn later.

The rule the map follows: a building's subtitle adds information or is dropped. A subtitle that restates the name is cut - a monastery named for its patron needs no "(town monastery)" under it - and so is one that describes what the drawing shows. Subtitles that say something the sheet cannot are kept: that a shrine is still tended, that a compound is a hunting lodge by a named forest. A building is never subtitled with the settlement's own tier: "county seat" names the town, not the building standing in it.

How is the map framed, and why is there so little bare ground around the edge?

Sources: the GM's rulings of 2026-07-23; this project's own measurements of the shipped maps

Because the image is cut down to what it shows. A fixed canvas leaves dead margins on a map whose settlement sits in one corner and whose paddy runs off to one side, so the frame is computed instead: take the bounding box of the hard content - the houses, the fields, the water, the groves, the pond - add a margin, and make that the picture. A hamlet sheet takes a margin of about 30 ft and a village one about 60 ft. The image size that falls out is not a round number, and that is expected.

Only what is really drawn counts toward the box. A field contributes the extent of its visible plots, never the larger envelope the placer uses to keep houses off it: that envelope can run on past the last plot with nothing in it, and framing to it puts dead ground back - on one sheet it dropped the south margin some 210 ft below anything drawn, and twice that on a village map.

Everything that is not hard content clips at the edge and never extends it. The scrub commons, the streams, the channels and the lanes are drawn right up to the frame and cut there, reading as "more wild ground, more map, this way" - which is true, and is what the reader should take from it. A bleed rule was tried and is not kept: the frame used to open far enough to preserve about two thirds of a trailing commons, and in practice that held the edge out over empty back-slope grazing - past a graveyard on one flank, some 130 ft past a lone pond on the other, more on a village sheet - which is exactly the dead space the crop exists to remove. So the commons clips like the marsh, and the frame hugs the real settlement rather than the scrub around it. If a map ever wants a wilderness feature to widen its frame, the honest change is to count that feature as hard content, not to bring a bleed back.

One frame-setting rule lives with the homestead grove rather than here: the communal windbreak's depth clips at the edge while its inner face - the canopy edge toward the houses - holds the frame open at the standard margin. The ruling, the measurement behind it and the alternatives declined are at The garden's sun, and how far the windbreak shades.

Because the frame is computed from what has been drawn, order decides what survives. Anything the picture must contain has to exist before the frame is taken: the large features, and in particular an outlying one that sits well away from the cluster - a back-slope graveyard, a shrine set apart. Only things that drop into the framed space afterward - the wells among the houses, the monk's plots - and the placard itself come later. A hard feature added after the frame is fixed is silently sliced by it, which is how one village's west-set graveyard came to be cut once the surrounding scrub stopped padding the edge.

A provincial city is framed on its wall rather than on its scattered belongings. A town map is as much about its surroundings as about the town; a city map is about the city, and the countryside around it matters little, so the window just barely encompasses the walled place - the rampart, its moat, and the satellites that are the point of being outside (the gate markets, the flophouses, the funerary grounds, the wharf stalls) - with the farmland, the outlying estates and the far rows of a gate market simply running off the edge. The GM settled the margin on 2026-07-23: "the aggressive crop [is] the default for all cities unless I state otherwise", about 105 ft of air past the outermost thing the frame is taken on. The reason the margin is stated rather than left to taste is that the first cities were framed the other way, on the principle that a city map is about the city and little else, and the result cut every irrigation fan outside the wall to a sliver about 138 ft wide. The fans had been drawn all along; they were invisible, and the ring of farmland read as bare through two features of work spent filling ground that was already full. A frame widened by hand to some 750 to 780 ft past the moat proved they were there, and the settled answer is the content crop at the aggressive margin, which shows them without paying for empty ground. The GM's rule for what clips is the same one the villages follow: "it's okay if farms get cut off... the point is to show they're there, not to show the entire field".

A caption never pins the frame (GM, 2026-07-23). Where a caption was the only thing holding a side of the picture open, the answer is to move the caption inward, among the things it names, rather than to pad the crop around it - one city's "samurai estates" caption was moved in among its manors for exactly this reason.

The rule the map follows: at hamlet and village scale the frame is the bounding box of the hard content plus a margin of about 30 ft on a hamlet sheet, 60 ft on a village one; a field contributes its drawn plots and not its keep-out envelope; the commons, the streams, the channels and the lanes clip at the edge; and every hard feature - the houses, the groves, the graveyard, the shrine, the wells, the torii - lies wholly inside the finished window, so a feature placed too late and quietly sliced is caught rather than shipped. No view edge stands more than about 56 ft of hamlet ground beyond the outermost feature the frame was taken on, which leaves the working margins of 30 to 44 ft a little slack and fails an edge held open by something that should have clipped.

The rule the map follows: a town or provincial-city map is framed on the settlement itself - for a walled city the wall, the moat and the satellites kept outside it, plus about 105 ft of margin at the city scale and room above for the placard. The outlying estates, the surrounding farmland and the far rows of a gate market run off the edge and are not drawn whole. A single side may be widened to keep a representative band of farmland on a flank that has no satellite to anchor it. Every check that asks whether something "runs off the edge" reads the cropped window, not the full drawing area, as the edge of the map.

May one outlying feature make the whole map bigger?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25; this project's census of the shipped maps

Not silently. The previous question asks whether the margin is generous; this one asks the opposite - whether a single element, standing far outside everything else, is by itself forcing a bigger image. Move that one thing inward and the whole map crops tighter, which is worth knowing before shipping a sheet fifteen per cent taller than it needs to be.

The hard part is telling a legitimate outlier from an accident, and the discriminator is the ratio, not the gap - which only measuring the pool revealed. A pond or a forest that pushes the frame out is the outlying content: it is large, and it is meant to be out there. Every legitimate case on the shipped maps has a gap roughly equal to the feature's own size - ponds at 1.03 to 1.35 times their own extent, one hamlet's forest at 1.14. Against that, a city's tanning-yard caption stood some 534 ft past everything else on a box 30 ft tall: 17.8 times its own size. So the test is that the gap must exceed both a floor of about 60 ft of hamlet or town ground and three times the feature's own extent - "it drags the frame further than its own size". A flat distance threshold, which is the obvious first guess, would have condemned every hamlet's source tameike.

The test reads the same list of contributors the frame itself was computed from, rather than a second list written to resemble it. The two crops deliberately take different sets - a city frames on its moat ring, its satellites and its captions while its paddy fans and farmhouses clip - so the one function answers for both, and a check that re-derived what the generator had already computed would drift from it feature by feature. Folding both crops onto it was proved to change nothing: all seventeen maps were re-framed and every window came back identical to the byte.

One consequence is worth knowing before chasing a footprint that is not in the set: a city's extramural trade works are framed by their caption, not by the works. The city's contributor list carries every placed caption box but not the tanning yards themselves, which is why one city's frame is set by the words "tanning yard" rather than by the yard. The kiln works left that exclusion on 2026-07-27, and both directions are worth recording: the exclusion was right while a kiln was a mound of about 28 by 18 ft whose caption was wider than it was, and wrong once the kiln became a works of 140 by 120 ft with its own housing - wider than any caption of it, and a satellite whose entire point is where it stands must not clip at the frame.

Where the outlier is legitimate, the map declares it, with the reason, rather than the rule being relaxed. One city is the worked example, and it is a genuine conflict between two rules rather than a slack placement: the tanning yard must stand below every irrigation intake, and the tap channel's corridor blocks everything above a line, so the shallowest legal seat saves about 24 ft of a sheet some 4,200 ft tall and is flagged anyway. The declaration turns silent bloat into a cost somebody chose.

The commonest outlier of all is exempt on physical grounds. A valley-head pond that feeds a field has to stand above the field's water entry, because the feed is gravity; pulling it inside a tighter frame would put it downhill of the field it waters, which is backwards. So it is not relocated - but the cheap part of the slack is still reclaimed with a nudge. A pond only has to contain its own sluice to feed the field, so the drawn water can slide a little, independently of the sluice and the field it serves, until its far edge sits flush with the dry plots instead of poking past them. One village's pond is nudged about 314 ft west, its east edge flush with the dry-field strip, for an image about nine per cent smaller; the poke that remains on its uphill side is the irreducible cost of a valley head and is simply accepted. Re-orienting the whole field to dodge the poke was declined: a mirrored layout only flips which side the pond pokes out of, and it erases the deliberate difference between two neighboring villages, which is worth more than the pixels.

The rule the map follows: no single feature holds the frame open by more than three times its own size, and by more than about 60 ft of hamlet or town ground, unless the map says why. A feature that does is moved inward, or the map records the reason it cannot be - a conflict with another rule, a gravity feed that requires the high ground - as a declaration a reader can be shown, not as a loosened threshold. A pond that sources a field is exempt by construction and is nudged rather than moved: it slides until its far edge is flush with the plots, keeping its sluice inside the water.

Where does a caption sit, and why is it never left floating in bare ground?

Sources: the GM's rulings of 2026-07, 2026-07-26 and 2026-08-27; this project's own measurements of the shipped maps

Two things are being traded against each other, and the order between them is the whole rule. First, empty ground wins: if a caption can sit somewhere that still plainly names its subject and covers nothing else, it must, and only when every candidate covers something does it take the least damaging one. The defects that forced this were both of the same kind - a ministry's name lying over samurai houses while the ground just below the office was bare, and an "Imperial Road" caption over a farmstead's trees while the road's other flank was empty.

But empty ground is plentiful, which is the trap the first version fell into (GM, 2026-07-26). Counting overlaps alone made every clear candidate tie at nothing, and the winner then fell out of the order things happened to be drawn in - so a caption could float 120 to 165 ft out in bare ground and score exactly as well as one tucked against the thing it names. One city shipped both halves of that failure: an "Imperial Road" caption some 165 ft clear of the roadway, and a "gate market" caption about 126 ft east of its own stall rows, close enough to the execution ground that it read as naming the execution ground. So nearness is the second term, and a caption that names a thing - a market row, a works, a road - is seated against that thing rather than at coordinates somebody picked.

The seat is chosen by stepping outward from the subject: about five feet of air at a hamlet's scale, in steps of about six, nine rungs deep, taking the nearest seat that covers nothing. Only when the whole ladder is blocked does it fall back to the least-covered seat. Within a rung, a tie goes to the seat least crowded by other captions - two seats equally tight against the subject are not equally good if one of them is also up against a neighbor's words, which is precisely how "gate market" came to read as a second line of "execution ground". A caption may sit anywhere along its subject, so a long subject is also tried at slides along its own length; without that freedom the ladder has one seat per flank per rung and abandons a whole flank whenever a neighbor's caption happens to hold that latitude. Two constraints are honored while the seat is being chosen rather than left for a later check: captions already placed and the placard count as obstacles, and any candidate outside the cropped window is thrown away outright, because a clipped caption is unreadable.

Captions are seated last, once the map is finished. Seating one when its subject is drawn judges it against half a map: a city places its gate markets before its execution ground exists, so the north market's caption took the flank that later filled up. The deferred captions seat first and the road's caption last - most constrained first, since a road caption can slide along a long roadway while a market row's frontage is short. A consequence, and a welcome one: a caption seated after the frame is taken cannot hold a side of the frame open, which is what the GM wanted anyway.

A road caption is seated against the roadway's cross-section and its tangent, not against the bounding box of the road segment. For one town's diagonal Imperial road that box is a square some 486 by 256 ft whose edge is about 280 ft from the road itself, so a caption seated a few feet off the box sat nowhere near the road. An anchor written into a map by hand is therefore only a hint - which flank, and where along the road - and can no longer set the distance, which is what had let a hand anchor 102 ft out survive as a 55 ft gap. And a caption lying along a road needs a stretch of roadside that is actually bare: the natural anchor is the road's midpoint, which on that town is the middle of a core lined two rows deep on both flanks, so the honest answer is to name the road where the road is clear - out past the last shopfront on the approach - rather than to widen what counts as near.

How close is close enough? A backstop catches a caption that ran the whole ladder and still landed nowhere near its subject: the finished gap is measured from the subject's own box and capped at three times the size of the lettering. On 2026-08-27 the GM asked for the opposite move as well - "move the label fifty percent of the way toward the thing that it is labeling. Let's try that and see how it looks" - so a seated caption is measured against its subject's true footprint and pulled half that gap toward it, and the pull is refused whenever the moved block would touch the subject or anything else. It is applied today to one hamlet's notice-board caption, which came in from about 11 ft to 5.5 ft, and it is provisional: a general rule for every caption seat if the GM likes it on the sheet, and until then the one worked example.

One lesson underlies all of this and is worth stating on its own: the shape that decides where a caption may sit must be the same shape that judges it afterward. While captions were probed generously - measured by an upright box drawn around a tilted block - the two disagreed invisibly, and the moment the probe became honest about a caption's real reach, a caravan-inn caption settled into the corner slack of a tilted stable and was caught as covering it. The overstatement is large for a long caption: the upright box around a 97 ft caption lying at some 27 degrees claims about 3.3 times the text's real thickness, which is enough to make the ladder reject the one seat a road caption wants - lying along the roadway, in the lane between the roadbed and the shopfront setback - and walk out to 64 ft of bare ground instead. The placement and the verdict now measure the same drawn shape.

The rule the map follows: a caption naming a particular feature is seated against that feature, at the nearest position that covers nothing else, preferring a position that is not crowded by another caption; a caption naming an area is seated among the things it names. Captions are seated after the map is complete and inside the finished frame. A caption ends up within three times its own lettering height of the thing it names, or the map is wrong.

Why does a caption tilt with the thing it names?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-08-27; this project's measurements of the three drawn cities

So that the words read as part of the thing, the way a level caption reads as part of a level building. The GM stated it as a general rule on 2026-08-27: "the notice board is at an angle, therefore, the notice board label should be at exactly the same angle". A caption takes its subject's own rotation - exactly, not approximately - normalized only so far as is needed to keep the text from reading upside down, and it is seated off the subject's rotated edge rather than off an upright box around it. A road's caption takes its bearing from the stretch of roadway it is seated against; a row of shopfronts takes the axis of the street it fronts, read off the street rather than copied by hand, because a copied number goes stale the next time the row is laid.

This ruling replaced two narrower ones, and the difference it makes is visible on the sheet. Under the first, a caption aligned with whichever of a building's two edge families lay nearer the horizontal, so a yard standing at 102 degrees was captioned at 12. Under the second, a caption naming a line went level once the line passed 45 degrees, so a notice board at 57 degrees had level words beside it. Both are gone: the caption takes the subject's angle.

One reading of the ruling is the session's own and is recorded here for the GM to overturn if it is wrong: a subject at a square rotation - a quarter, a half or three quarters of a turn - has a horizontal edge of its own, so its level caption is aligned with it and stays level. If a steep caption ever proves genuinely unreadable on a sheet, that is a question to put to the GM against this ruling, not a clamp to restore quietly.

What does not tilt is everything with no axis to follow: a caption over an upright block of packed houses ("laborers' dwellings", "samurai houses", "barns"), a caption on a point fixture such as a boundary stone, and a city district's caption. The fabric under all three drawn cities' district captions was measured and is level, so tilting them would align them with nothing on the map. Tilt follows the subject's axis; it is not decoration.

One ripple is worth knowing when a caption at the map's edge tilts: the block it occupies is taller than its level form, so a frame taken partly on captions can grow by a few feet - one city's grew about 45 ft off its tanning yard's caption, which stranded an off-map channel anchor that had been written against the older frame.

The rule the map follows: a caption naming a rotated feature is drawn at that feature's own angle and seated off its rotated edge; a caption naming a road or a row laid along one runs along that line at its bearing; a caption naming an area, an upright block or a point fixture is level. The text never reads upside down.

Why does a caption sometimes break across two lines?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-08-27

Because wrapping is sometimes the only thing that clears the ground, and the GM ruled that clearing the ground is what decides it: "if the label was split across multiple lines, and that caused it to not overlap with anything, then we should do that. If running the label on one line caused it to not overlap with anything, then we should do that. If both things cause the label to overlap with something, then we can just pick one ... this should be a rule for all labels". So a caption is drawn on one line if that clears the sheet; otherwise on the first of two, then three, lines that does; and if nothing clears, on one line again.

"Clears" is measured on the block as it will actually be drawn - its true rotated outline, turned about its own center exactly as the finished text is - against the true outlines of everything built and every caption already placed. Ground cover and land parcels are not obstacles: the scrub commons is a rectangle the size of the whole drawing, and counting it had every probe reporting blocked ground everywhere.

The cutting itself follows a few plain rules, all of them about how the words look. Words are never broken across lines. Among the ways of cutting a caption into a given number of lines, the one whose longest line is shortest wins, with ties going to the most even lengths. A line holding a single short word - "of", "the" - is refused whenever there are more words than lines, so a shrine named for a Fortune is cut as "Shrine of / Benten" or "Shrine / of Benten" and never leaves "of" stranded on a line of its own. A wrapped caption keeps the one-liner's center and its tilt, and the map records the block rather than the line, so what is checked is what is drawn. One hamlet's notice board is the worked example: "notice / board" at about 57 degrees, off the farmhouse the single line crossed.

The rule the map follows: a caption is drawn on as few lines as clear the ground beneath it, preferring one; words are never broken; a wrapped caption's lines are as even as they can be and never leave a two- or three-letter word alone on a line; and the wrapped block keeps the seat and the angle the single line would have had.

What does a town or a city map label, and what may a label cover?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-21; this project's own review of the drawn towns and cities

A town or a city carries far more captions than a hamlet does - districts, ministries, temples, markets, gates, roads - and on a dense sheet the question stops being where a caption looks best and becomes what a caption is allowed to touch. A caption lying across a building tells the reader that building is the thing named. That is exactly right when it lies on its own subject and exactly wrong one door along, which is the whole of the rule below; nothing in it is a historical finding, and none of it is drawn by a generator yet.

The judgment that keeps recurring is about identity rather than about ink. A "flophouse" caption sitting across a flophouse says something true; the same caption a few feet over, across a merchant's estate, has silently relabeled the estate. The same went for a gate guard-house caption that came to rest on a flophouse, and for a temple's name that ended up over a merchant house. Sibling buildings of one kind are the subtler case: two ministries are the same kind of thing, so a rule that only knows kinds will happily let one ministry's name sit on the office next door - which is how a Ministry of Justice caption came to stand on the Ministry of Works.

The rule the map follows (town and city): no two captions overlap, and no caption runs off the edge of the picture. Plenty of things rightly run off the edge - fields, roads, country estates, farmhouses, the countryside continuing - but a caption that spills past it is clipped and unreadable, so every caption's block sits wholly inside the framed window.

The rule the map follows (town and city): a caption may lie on the feature it names - its own building or compound, or for a district caption any building of that district - and may clip a street-fronting shop or a servant house interleaved among the households it serves, since those line every quarter and are never mistaken for the subject. It may not lie on a building of a different identity. Each captioned structure belongs to a group, and a caption's own wording says which groups it may cover: a flophouse caption the flophouses, a guard-house or inspection caption the gate furniture, a ministry's name the ministries, a temple's or shrine's name the temples, a samurai caption the samurai houses and estates, a graveyard, cremation-ground, mausoleum or ossuary caption any of the funerary structures but never a temple, a street's or road's name the frontage it runs along. A caption that names no building at all - farmland, a market, the place name itself - may clip nothing. Funerary ground is a subject in its own right, so a temple's caption may not lie on a graveyard any more than a graveyard's caption may lie on a temple; where two such subjects stand together, one caption goes above its building and the other below.

The rule the map follows (city): a named civic building's caption - a ministry by name, the governor's yamen, a temple by name - additionally may not touch any other named civic building, because the group rule alone would permit one ministry's name to sit on the next ministry. The remedy is to separate the two offices, which the rule against civic buildings abutting already wants, or to move the caption onto its own building.

The rule the map follows (city): a district caption - "samurai neighborhood", "merchant district", "burakumin neighborhood", "samurai estates" - sits with the cluster it names. It stands entirely on the same side of the wall as its subject (the laborers', outcaste, samurai, merchant and in-wall farming captions inside the rampart, the extramural estates' caption outside), among its own buildings - the nearest of them within about 390 ft for a cluster of houses, further for sparse manors, temples or fields - and never floating over a foreign field or another compound. The remedy for a misplaced district caption is always to move it onto its own cluster, never onto the district's edge over a neighbor and never out over the moat.

The rule the map follows (town and city): one caption per named district (GM, 2026-07-21). A neighborhood that already carries its name never gets a second caption for a pocket of itself - the commerce strip outside a temple's gate is part of the temple neighborhood, and naming it again is clutter. A feature inside a district that needs naming is named by its own caption on its own glyph - a particular temple, a graveyard - not by a second district-scale caption.

The rule the map follows (town and city): "quarter" is reserved for a genuine quarter of the settlement, which in practice means a city has quarters and a town does not. A small sub-area of a town is a neighborhood, an enclave or a row: an outcaste neighborhood, a samurai neighborhood.
