Why is a city ringed by farmland on every side?

Sources: the GM's rulings of 2026-08-11 and 2026-08-12; shizen-teibo-jawiki and kohai-shicchi-jawiki (which ground of a lowland is built on and which is left to paddy); this project's measurement of the three drawn provincial cities, chang-morphology-walled-capitals

A city is not a thing set down on a plain. It is the market and the garrison of the land that feeds it, and a single token field on one flank draws that relationship backwards.26 The GM ruled on 2026-08-11, after a first pass at a domain capital drew one field: "The city should be surrounded by farmland. Cities grow up around fertile land, so we should do as we do for Tango and Nagahara and Minami and keep adding rice paddies and farmhouses until there are no more places to put them. Farmland ringing a city is the DEFAULT which should always happen in the future." The three cities already drawn follow that practice - one carries eleven fields, another seven, the third six.

Farmsteads stand upslope of the drain. Within an alluvial lowland the badly drained ground is the low ground behind the slight rise a river builds along its own channel, and it is that low ground which is reclaimed as paddy;13 the settlements of such a lowland sit first of all on the rise itself, which within the wet ground around it drains well.6 The record applies that rule to a feature those accounts do not name - the strip immediately below a dug collector rather than a natural back marsh - and takes the wettest ground in the valley to be there. A farmstead's seat is measured against the drain LINE rather than the field's center, since a seat can be above the middle of a field and still below the drain where it bends. The provincial cities never needed that, because their drains march off the view; the capital, whose drain runs on the sheet, did.

The rule the map follows: a city is ringed with farmland until the ground genuinely refuses it, and the floor is at least four fields across at least three flanks. Fewer than that means the ring was not attempted, not that the ground ran out. Each field is a whole water chain rather than a colored polygon: a tap that sits ON the water and is derived from the watercourse's own line rather than picked by eye, a head race that bends rather than ruling straight, a gate at each end, a moat offtake swept downstream with the moat's current, a stated source and a stated drain, the drain carried clear off the sheet rather than stopping in mid-air, and the farmsteads that work it. A farmstead may stand hard against a strip of dry crop but never on it.

The rule the map follows: at the city tier a field's paddy strips run about 78 ft across , its cascade rows step 39 to 57 ft , its supply canals span 180 to 780 ft , its fan falls 270 to 570 ft , and the dry hem quilting the head of the fan is 140 to 265 ft deep . A field counts as large enough to be worth irrigating from the moat past about eleven and a half acres . These are calibrations against the three drawn cities, chosen so that a city-fringe field stays the size of the field it replaces, not figures from a source; the hem's depth in particular is per field, because the pooled value is deeper than some ground is.

The rule the map follows: every field outside the wall is worked by villagers, so every one of them carries farmhouses around it even where it runs off the sheet - and what is counted is the part a reader can see. Along the field's boundary that lies inside the cropped window, the map draws about seven farmhouses for every 3,000 ft of that boundary ; village fields run between four and nineteen by the same measure, averaging about twelve, so seven is a floor rather than a target. Because most of a fringe field lies off the sheet, the ring is drawn densely enough that enough houses land on the visible edge, and the ones that fall off-map are simply not drawn. None of these farmhouses counts toward the city's own population: the farmers who work a city's land live in the surrounding villages and are counted there. On a town sheet the same houses ARE part of the county's farmer cohort and do count, which is the one place the two tiers differ here.
