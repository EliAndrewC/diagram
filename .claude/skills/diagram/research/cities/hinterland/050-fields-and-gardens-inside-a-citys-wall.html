Fields and gardens inside a city's wall

The walls of important Chinese cities were often drawn round far more ground than the city had built on, to leave room for it to grow and to hold timber and farmland inside in time of war. So fields and gardens inside a wall were common, and old: rice and vegetables were grown inside Chinese cities for a long time, and fields and gardens could be seen inside almost every Chinese city until two generations ago.

The ground was worked by the city's own households, beside their other work, and some of it went to vegetables for their own tables. We think those gardens were worked in small hand-tended beds and fed with the city's night soil, though no source we read says so of the ground inside a wall. In the setting of these maps a city has no farmer households inside its walls at all: a provincial city's population counts only those who live inside its walls, and the fertile land around it is farmed by villagers, who are counted in the villages' populations (Who lives in a provincial city?). A laborer or a merchant who works a plot inside the wall is still a laborer or a merchant.

  • The walls of important Chinese cities often enclosed more ground than the city had built on.
    In 1945 a quarter of the ground inside the wall of Quanzhou, in Fujian, was still vacant, and in the Republican era the wall of Suzhou still enclosed large tracts of farmland.
  • Under the Sui and Tang, the south of Chang'an was plowed fields.
    South of the Xingshan temple, east and west to the outer wall, there were in general no houses; the few who lived there lived far apart, and the ground was plowed and planted, with field paths running between.
  • Ming Beijing held rice paddy and vegetable land inside its walls.
    Beijing's Shichahai and Jishuitan were lake and rice paddy in the Ming, turning to water covered with lotus only under the Qing. The outer wall Beijing built in the Jiajing years of the Ming took in the eastern half of the old capital of the Jin dynasty, and with it the vegetable land the Yuan had opened on that site.
  • In 1853 there was cultivated land among the streets inside Nanjing's wall.
    Two Frenchmen writing of the early Taiping found the ground inside the wall at least three times larger than Paris, and large pieces of cultivated land among its desolate streets.
  • As late as 1910, a sixth of the residents inside Jining's wall were farmers who owned land.
    Many residents inside the walls of Shandong's department and county towns did not live by trade and industry but still had land of their own, and even in Jining, a commercial city, a sixth of those inside the wall were farmers owning farmland in 1910.
  • In China, the city's own households worked the ground inside the wall.
    Chinese city residents for a long time grew rice and vegetables inside the city, and even kept pigs and chickens. A historian of the Song dynasty, quoted in the same essay, puts it most plainly: before the Northern Song, city residents mostly grew their own vegetables on the various idle plots inside the city.
  • In Japan's small castle towns, some townsmen farmed beside their trade.
    The castle towns gathered the warriors, the merchants and the artisans and separated them from the farmers, but among the townsmen of the small provincial castle towns there were those who farmed alongside their trade. Farming beside a trade did not make these townsmen farmers by status.
  • No page we read has villagers coming in through the gates to farm the ground inside a wall.
    It is what the setting implies for a city's in-wall fields, since it puts no farmer household inside a wall, and it is plausible for a field near a gate.

A Chinese farm manual of the Northern Wei says how a vegetable garden was laid out, bed by bed, and where a market garden stood against a city wall.

  • A Northern Wei farm manual sets a vegetable bed at two paces long by one wide.
    The Qimin Yaoshu, written under the Northern Wei, says mallow must be grown in beds and watered in spring, when there is much wind and drought. Beds economize the ground and give many vegetables, one bed feeding one mouth. A larger bed is hard to water evenly, and no one should have to set foot in it. The manual lays out every bed-grown crop the same way.
  • How big was a Northern Wei vegetable bed, in modern feet?
    About 4 by 8 ft or 5 by 10 ft, on our own arithmetic. A table of Chinese measures by dynasty gives a pace (bu) as five or six Chinese feet (chi), and a Chinese foot of 0.255 m (~10 in) for the Wei and Sui dynasties (220-266 and 581-618), so a pace of 1.275 m (~4 ft) or 1.53 m (~5 ft). The table has no row for the manual's own decades, and that foot may make the bed too small: on its longer Tang foot of 0.2955 m (~12 in) the same bed is about 5 by 10 ft or 6 by 12 ft.
  • The Northern Wei farm manual sets a market garden against a city wall.
    For the winter crop of mallow (kui) it takes thirty mu of good land backing onto the wall of a market town, sinks ten wells along its length, and gives each a well-sweep and a windlass. The wells are set in line, since a skew line wastes the ground: one row where the plot is long and narrow, two or three where it is square. The manual sets this garden against the wall, not inside it, and we have not found the thirty mu converted on a page we could read.

Gardens like these were fed with night soil. Most of what we read of that trade is of Japan, where it is on record from the 1500s.

  • In Japan, a city's night soil was feeding the fields near it by the 1500s, before the Edo period.
    A painting of Kyoto and its outskirts dated 1525 shows a farmer top-dressing rice with a bucket and a ladle, and pit privies inside the capital, from which the historian Watanabe argues that farming near the city built a system that used its townspeople's night soil as fertilizer. In 1585 the missionary Luis Frois wrote that where Europeans pay to have excrement carried away, the Japanese buy it with rice and money.
  • In 1789-90, 1,016 farming villages near Edo petitioned together to lower the price of collecting the city's night soil.
    In the first year of the Kansei era (1789-90), holding that the price had risen nearly three- to fourfold, they joined to petition the finance commissioner for an order lowering it.
  • In the early twentieth century, night soil went out of a Japanese city to the fields by the cartload from early morning.
    An American agronomist, in his 1911 account of what he saw in China, Korea and Japan, saw it carried from early morning, within the city and along the roads leading to the fields, on his rides from Yokohama to Tokyo, mostly on carts bearing six to ten covered wooden containers of forty to sixty pounds and more.
  • In China, liquid night soil was watered down before it went on the plants, so a garden fed with it needed water close at hand.
    The same 1911 account says the Chinese generally diluted liquid manure with water before it was "fed" to the plants, hence their need of an abundant and convenient water supply.
  • Did the gardens inside a wall get the city's night soil?
    Probably, on our own reading. The wall's enclosure of farmland and the night soil carried to the fields are both on record, but no source we read joins them on the ground inside a wall, and none of the night-soil evidence we read is of premodern China.
