Does the moat feed the fields, or do the fields drain into it?

Sources: the GM's rulings of 2026-08-11; this project's measurement of the drawn provincial cities and the capital; jin-zhongdu-water-gate-bjd (a capital's storm water let into its moat), yanagawa-jogo-mizuochi (a castle-town moat holding stored water, and canal mud spread on the fields), mizu-no-bunka-32-yanagawa (canals as a store of fresh water and of flood water), zaichi-ryoshu-jawiki (a lord's moat irrigating the fields at his gate), masuami-yosui-ueda (a channel drawn at a castle town to water the villages below)

Both, and which one it is depends on where the field sits on the ring. A moat has a direction of flow - the feeder stream enters on one side, and that heading is the way the water goes - so a field on an arc UPSTREAM of the city's own draw cannot be fed from the moat: an offtake there would have to run against the current, and a channel whose field lies upstream of its tap reads as water running out of the field into the moat, backwards. The GM ruled on 2026-08-11 that the relationship simply reverses in that case, and that the other maps already use it: such a field takes its head from high ground off the sheet and DISCHARGES into the moat below, gated at the outfall and swept with the current. Which way the water runs between a particular field and the moat is that ruling and the slope, not something a source sets.

The moat is the city's storm drain as well as its reservoir, and both halves are attested. As a drain: at the Jin capital on the site of Beijing (twelfth century), the water that gathered inside the city in the flood season ran out through a water gate into the moat south of the city. As a reservoir: the moat ringing Yanagawa's old castle town is filled from a river through a sluice and holds stored water, which is let out to the sea when the moat is drained, and the town's canals serve as a store of fresh water that also holds the rain when a high tide stops it draining. Moat water reaching the fields is attested too. The moat around a medieval resident lord's seat irrigated the paddy and dry field at its gate; at Ueda a channel drawn at the foot of the castle town watered three villages downstream; and the mud dredged from Yanagawa's canals was dried and spread on the paddies and fields. Yanagawa is a delta town laced with canals and Beijing a capital, so what they show is that a moat did both jobs, not how a provincial seat's moat was worked.

The north shelf of the capital stays unfarmed, and the reason is depth. Between the rampart and the sheet edge there is about 360 ft , and a fan cannot be carved in that at any hem, because the fall plus the head band is deeper than the shelf. It is not the current that rules that strip out - a field there could have drained into the moat like any other upstream field - it is the room.

The rule the map follows: a moated city irrigates at least three of its large fields from the moat. Each tap stands about 90 ft outside the moat's rim with a short stub bridging rim to gate, and the channel it feeds ends INSIDE the field rather than near it. Taps sit upstream of the field they serve, so the water runs downstream into it; a field lying upstream of the whole moat is fed from the source stream instead or runs off the sheet with its water implied, and the moat-fed count is made up from a downstream field. A field's collector may empty back INTO the moat, or run off the cropped view. Where a channel meets the moat its mouth is snapped onto the open water's edge so that it covers the rim rather than drawing its bed as a colored line across the water - a map drawing convention, and the same one a channel meeting a pond gets. Channels are drawn with a bend, never ruler-straight, at a hairline stroke whose width on the sheet is a drawing convention rather than a measured ditch, and the ground under each one is kept clear of farmsteads for about 100 ft either side.

The rule the map follows: a field INSIDE the walls records the moat as its sink, but its visible ditch stops short of the patrol road: the runoff drops into an implied underground stone conduit that passes beneath the ring road, the rampart and the moat. The GM's rule is flat - never draw a ditch running through the city wall; the water gate is underground.
