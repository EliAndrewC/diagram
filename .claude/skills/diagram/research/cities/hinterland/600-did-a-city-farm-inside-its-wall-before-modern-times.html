Did a city farm inside its wall before modern times?

Sources: thepaper-city-fields (farmland inside the walls of Chang'an, Beijing, Nanjing and Jining, dated from the Sui and Tang to 1910), visitbeijing-caiyuan (vegetable land enclosed by Beijing's Ming outer wall), shimogoe-jawiki (the night soil of Japanese cities bought for the fields near them, from 1525 on)

Yes, long before. The two cases Does a city farm inside its walls? opens with are twentieth-century, but the arrangement is far older. Under the Sui and Tang the south of Chang'an, east and west to the outer wall, had in general no houses, and its ground was plowed and planted with field paths running between. In the Ming, Beijing's Shichahai and Jishuitan were lake and rice paddy, turning to lotus water only under the Qing; and the outer wall Beijing built in the Jiajing years of the Ming enclosed vegetable land the Yuan had opened on the site of the old Jin capital. In 1853 two Frenchmen found large pieces of cultivated land among the desolate streets inside Nanjing's wall, and as late as 1910 a sixth of the residents inside Jining's wall, a commercial city of Shandong, were farmers who owned land.

The night soil is older too, on the Japanese record. A painting of Kyoto and its outskirts dated 1525 shows a farmer with a bucket and a ladle putting liquid manure on rice, and pit privies in the capital, from which a historian argues that farming near the city built a system that used the townspeople's night soil as fertilizer. In 1585 the missionary Luis Frois wrote that where Europeans pay to have excrement carried away, the Japanese buy it with rice and money. And in the first year of the Kansei era (1789-90) 1,016 farming villages near Edo joined to petition the finance commissioner to lower the price of collecting it. The one Chinese account of the night-soil carts read is an American agronomist's of 1909, and no earlier Chinese one was sought for this question.

So the farming quarter a city may declare inside its wall, and the vegetable tracts in it, rest on premodern practice, and the maps go on drawing them.
