Did a city's lords keep a retreat just outside town?

Sources: kotobank-shimoyashiki (the lower mansion: what it was and what it held, that castle towns other than Edo had them, and the lower-mansion street at Sumoto), edo-hantei-jawiki (lower mansions mostly in the suburbs, and mansions built on bought farmland), liuyuan-zhwiki (a Ming official's private garden outside a Suzhou gate)

Yes, in both halves of the setting's history, though the sources say what such a retreat was far better than how far out it lay. In Japan it had a name, the lower mansion: a daimyo's residence set up in the outskirts of Edo apart from the main one, mostly a secondary house with gardens, and mostly placed in the suburbs away from the castle. At Edo it held gardens for the lord's amusement and rest, and vegetable plots and bamboo groves that supplied the upper residence, and one on a shore or a riverbank served as a landing place for cargo. Some stood on farmland a daimyo bought from its private owners. The same dictionary says that in castle towns other than Edo there are cases of domain lords and senior retainers keeping one besides the castle or their main house - cases, not a count. A case on the same Kotobank page, from its gazetteer of place names, shows a second form: at Sumoto a street named for its lower mansion ran east to west through the south of the castle town's outer town, three ken wide and 237 ken long (about 18 ft by 1,400 ft ), the Inada house's lower mansion and its retainers' houses on both sides. In China the same thing appears as the private garden outside the wall: Suzhou's Lingering Garden stands outside the Changmen gate, northwest of the old city, and was begun in 1593 as the private garden of an official who had been removed from office.

How far out it lay is a guess. No source read gives a distance from a castle or a city wall to a retreat: the pages say "the outskirts" and "the suburbs", and name Edo's by district. The "within a mile or a few" the question on dispersed estates gives is this record's own.

What the map draws. Two forms are attested, so the record calls for a knob rolled per city from its seed rather than a single choice: a retreat on its own ground just outside the town, with its garden around it (the Edo lower mansion, the Suzhou garden) and, at Edo, its kitchen plots, or a street in the town's outer town lined by a lower mansion and its retainers' houses (Sumoto). The nearest estate a city map draws (the question on how many a map draws) is the first form; the second has no rule on these maps yet, so until one is written every city takes the first. That a provincial seat's samurai kept retreats like a daimyo's at Edo, scaled down, is this record's reading: the dictionary's castle-town cases include senior retainers, but it gives none of their sizes.
