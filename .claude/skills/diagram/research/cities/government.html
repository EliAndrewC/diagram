The government quarter: the research behind the ministries and martial training

The findings behind the government quarter, the samurai ward and martial training on these maps, the decisions they drove, and - where no generator draws the feature yet - the rules a map follows.

Where does a province's government stand in its city?

Sources: the GM's setting notes for the Six Ministries; subai-ancient-city-sites (the square city's great cross, and the yamen and market in the middle of it)

The six ministries are the setting's own government - Rites, Revenue, Retainers, War, Works and Justice - so a provincial seat carries one office of each, and that much rests on the GM's notes rather than on any history. Where they STAND is the researched half. A provincial governor's compound was a whole city block in its own right - main hall, residence, quarters for the retinue, a jail, a granary, gardens, dozens of buildings behind one wall - which is why it is drawn here as walls with its interior implied, and why its sheer size on the sheet is correct rather than inflated. In a PLANNED seat the bureaus did not scatter. The regular Chinese form is a square city with one gate to a side and the streets between them meeting in one great cross, with a smaller cross set in each of the four quarters it makes; and the yamen and the market were laid out in the MIDDLE of such a city, which is where those main streets meet - so plainly that when a Ming prince's city was built on the central ground, the yamen, the market and part of the dwellings had to be demolished for it, and a yamen moved elsewhere in the city needed its streets and its attached offices laid out afresh.35 That the offices LINED the avenues around the compound is a step further than any page read goes: what the passage supports is that they belonged with it and were arranged around it, not that they fronted the great streets. The one office that sits apart is Rites, among the temples it oversees; ritual buildings did take fixed positions in a city - two Shanxi Confucian temples were both built in the southeast corner - though nothing puts a Rites office there, and the setting's arrangement is its own.

The rule the map follows: the governor's compound, the six ministry offices and a samurai neighborhood all stand in the city's INTERIOR, inside the rampart and clear of the wall's stroke and its moat. The compound is the grandest thing in the city - at least three times the size of any ministry office, and at least as large as any country estate. Five ministries cluster around it, within about 1,440 ft, threading into the samurai quarter; only Rites stands apart, in the temple neighborhood, and there is exactly one of it. Every ministry FRONTS a city street, within about 255 ft of one, which in practice means laying a short government avenue - an L wrapping the compound serves well - and seating the offices along it; a street fronted by ministries counts as a built-up street, as much as one lined with houses and shops. And a government office stands in its OWN ground: ordinary city houses may touch one another, but a ministry or the governor's compound keeps about 42 ft clear of every other building and compound.

What stands between the temples in a temple neighborhood?

Sources: akiba-jinja-jawiki (how small a neighborhood shrine is, how many stand in one town block, and what they were built of)

A quarter holding several temples is not only those temples. The ground between them carries small wayside shrines, put up by whoever had reason to and tended by the neighborhood around it. Small neighborhood shrines of that kind are attested and so is their density: the shrines of the fire-quelling Akiba cult, the most widely spread of them, are often small, and several of them may stand within a single town block.36 Two things about the drawn shrine are not: that such shrines stand specifically in the ground BETWEEN the temples of a temple quarter, and the form the map gives one - a vermilion-roofed shed with its own little gate - which for the Akiba cult runs the other way, since the account notes the small shrines are often built of stone because stone does not burn. The vermilion shed is an Inari form and, on these sheets, a map drawing convention: one glyph stands for the whole class of small shrines so that a reader can tell it from a temple at a glance.38 Nobody lives in one, and none of them has an approach avenue of its own. A single temple standing among houses is not a neighborhood and gathers none of them: the city's warrior-fortune temple in the middle of the samurai quarter is a building, not a precinct.

The rule the map follows: where two or more temples cluster within about 1,200 ft, at least three small wayside shrines are dotted among them, clear of the temples, the wall and the streets, and seated BEFORE the housing packs so that the rows flow around them. Religious buildings at city scale are temples, and a city may have several. Where a city street runs straight up to a temple and ends at its front, a torii stands on that street just in front of the temple; how MANY arches the approach carries is that temple's own roll rather than however much street is left over, and the arches draw in the top layer so the street passes beneath them. A temple set back from the grid, with no street running up to it, needs none.

Where do a city's samurai live, and how many of them are drawn?

Sources: the GM's setting notes for the caste shares and the rank bands

A provincial city is about a tenth samurai - some 300 of 3,000, which is about 60 households - and the setting's rank table splits that resident cohort roughly a quarter senior to three quarters junior. Most of them live in the samurai neighborhood as individual houses; the governor's compound and the walled country estates outside the rampart hold the rest. A quarter drawn as a token cluster of a couple of dozen houses is not this cohort, and a quarter whose samurai houses are all one size is not this rank table.

The rule the map follows: the samurai neighborhood is sized to the cohort - at least about two thirds of the households the city's own declared population implies are drawn as samurai houses. They VARY by rank: a minority of large senior houses among many small junior ones, at least three of the large and a clear majority of the small, interleaved through the rows so the large ones front streets prominently. Unlike the poor, samurai LINE their streets - at least a third of the houses stand within about 270 ft of a lane, the deeper lots sitting behind them. NONE of them is walled: a walled samurai estate stands OUTSIDE the rampart, and the only walled samurai compound within the city is the governor's. The reverse holds too. No free-standing samurai house stands outside the wall, because the only extramural samurai residences are those walled estates, and an unwalled house beyond the rampart is defenseless and anchored to nothing.

Is the samurai quarter walled, or only gated?

Sources: changan-enwiki (how many walled wards a great capital had and how large they were); the ward's share of the walled area is this project's measurement of the three drawn cities

The continuous internal wall around every quarter - the Chinese ward system - was a great-capital feature and an early one. Chang'an's grid ran to more than a hundred walled wards, the smallest of about 27 hectares and the largest about 94, each ring of ward wall standing 9 to 10 ft high37 - so the largest single ward was larger than a whole provincial city at the scale these maps draw. That the system was pulled down as the cities outgrew it is the shape of what happened, and no readable page dates its end.39 A provincial seat is the wrong scale for it. What a seat of this size does instead is SEAL one quarter - the samurai and government ground - with a light earthwork and palisade whose ends abut the city wall, pierced only where a street crosses it and pierced there by a barred wooden ward gate. The point of it is that the gate cannot be walked around: the fence between the gates has to be continuous and its ends have to reach solid rampart, or the seal is decorative. Full walled wards become plausible again only at the domain-capital tier.

The rule the map follows: the samurai and government quarter is enclosed by a FENCE, not a wall, with at least two ward gates near the governor's compound and a gate at EVERY crossing - every street, road, alley, and the wall's own patrol road, which crosses the fence at both of the points where it approaches the rampart - within about 96 ft of where the way meets the fence. The quarter's access streets are run THROUGH the fence line so that each crossing is a gate rather than a gap, and commoner housing and alleys stay outside. Both fence ends come within about 135 ft of the city wall and land on SOLID rampart, never in a gate's opening, where the wall is cut and the fence would meet nothing. The rampart draws OVER the fence at each end, so the fence reads as passing under the wall rather than painting across it, and the short cap that does this FOLLOWS the rampart through any corner in its span rather than running off as a straight tangent - within about 12 ft of the wall line at every point - because a straight cap laid where a fence abuts at a CORNER juts past it and reads as two wall sections overlapping instead of one wall bending. The enclosed quarter is the SMALLER of the two regions that the fence and the wall arc cut between them: a ward is a piece carved off the city, never the larger half, and on the drawn cities it runs a fifth to a quarter of the walled area.

The rule the map follows: a commoner lane that APPROACHES the ward fence reaches it and ends AT a gate. Laborers come in to work in the samurai quarter, so the lanes they use pull right up to a gate rather than stopping a sliver short of the fence. A lane end within about 180 ft of the fence, heading at it within about 37 degrees, with nothing built in between, sits ON the fence and has a gate within about 102 ft of it. Two things are exempt: the main city wall, which a lane may legitimately stop short of, that being the city's own edge rather than a neighborhood boundary; and a lane INSIDE the ward, which is not an entry at all. The run-out past an alley's last building to reach its gate is a legitimate gate spur, and does not count against the rule that an alley must serve the buildings along it.

Which way does a ward gate face?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-26; this project's measurement of the drawn cities

A ward gate exists to SHUT A WAY - it is barred at night against traffic - so its roofed bar stands square across the ROADBED running through it, and the fence meets the gate at whatever angle the fence happens to run. Squaring the gate to its FENCE instead looks right, because the two readings agree wherever a lane crosses its fence squarely, which is most crossings. They diverge on an OBLIQUE crossing, and there the fence reading is plainly wrong: on a 44 degree jog of fence barring a patrol road that runs at 172 degrees, a gate squared to the fence stands 38 degrees off square to its own roadbed and reads as a glyph dropped on the road. A gate with NO way through it falls back to the local fence tangent, where the rule against stamped-on glyphs still holds - a fence running at 30 degrees gets a gate at 30 degrees and never a box squared to the page, exactly as a rampart tower is turned to its wall.

The rule the map follows: a ward gate's bar stands SQUARE across the way it bars, within about 7 degrees, and only a gate with no way through it squares to the fence instead. A lane running ALONGSIDE the fence, within 25 degrees of it, is not a way that gate bars and is ignored. A gate standing more than about 48 ft from every fence has nothing to align to and is left alone.

Where does the gate watch stand?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-26; this project's measurement of the drawn cities

The watch at a ward gate belongs to the WARD it seals, so the guard box hangs on the ward's own flank of the gate and turns with it. But the box is a small BUILDING - the one solid thing in the group - and a patrol road with a shack standing in its bed is not a road anyone can patrol. So the box walks outward from the end of the bar, trying the near side of the opening and then the far side at the same distance, until it clears every roadbed by about a 12 ft verge and stands off the rampart, the compound walls and any wall tower.

Where the gate cannot move and a thinly covered stretch of curtain needs its tower, the BOX is what yields: it takes the other flank of its own gateway, an equally plausible spot for a watch shack.

The rule the map follows: the guard box stands on the ward-interior flank of its gate, on the VERGE and never in a roadbed - clear of every lane by about 12 ft and clear of the rampart, the compound walls and any wall tower - and where its own flank cannot hold it, it takes the other flank of the same gateway.

Martial training is an URBAN institution

Sources: hanko-jawiki (255 schools, martial arts (武芸) and the training hall (演武場)),1 edo-three-dojos-jawiki (the three dojos' founding dates, 1773/1822/1826),2 genbukan-jawiki (the disciple count is contested, ~3,000 against 6,000+, and no readable page gives one). The "boom of 1830-1860" range is not on any page read; it is drawn from the three founding dates and late-Edo growth

What the research found. The standalone dojo with a resident sensei and enrolled students is an urban institution in both anchor cultures. In Edo Japan formal martial instruction lived in the castle town: the domain school (hanko) and its martial hall (bugeijo) were built in the domain's own territory (藩地)28 for the domain's own retainers,3 and by late Edo nearly every one of the ~260 domains had one.4 Private commercial halls - machi-dojo, run by low-income bushi and eventually by commoners teaching for a living - are a late phenomenon and a metropolitan one (unsourced): the boom runs roughly 1830-1860 and its famous examples (the Three Great Dojo of Edo;5 Chiba Shusaku's Genbukan, founded at Nihonbashi in 1822 and later moved to Kanda Otamagaike)6 sit in a city of a million (unsourced). The Chinese anchor is starker: military examinations were held at county and prefectural level and candidates prepared at schools, but there was no dense network of private martial academies at a county seat - drill happened at garrison grounds (unsourced).

What the research did NOT find. The GM asked whether "roughly one martial establishment per ~100 resident samurai" had a historical basis: "I don't know where the 1 per resident samurai number came from since I don't recall that offhand, so do you know whether there's a historical basis for that?" There is none.

The attested content is narrower and supports a different shape: one state institution per castle town (the hanko pattern, which is a program item rather than a count that scales) and a deliberately thin private tail (because the machi-dojo boom needed a metropolis). So a capital carries one private hall per ~200 samurai, and gains the domain school instead.

The arithmetic is kept only as a rough plausibility read, never as an authority: where it disagrees with the number the map actually draws, the map wins. On that read a county town comes to 0.2 and rounds to none, a provincial city to about 3, a domain capital to about 15 and the Imperial capital to about 400, of which three or four would be famous. Note that it is a ratio on the SAMURAI count and not on the population, a hall serving samurai and nobody else; the samurai share of each tier already carries the difference in density between them.

The countryside is deliberately not counted (the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25). A province holds about 550 samurai in all - roughly 300 in its city, 120 across its six county towns, 90 on country estates - but only the city cohort is large enough to support an institution: the rural samurai either ride in or make do with the magistracy's practice ground. Asking separately what the countryside supports would double-count, because a provincial city's size already scales with the countryside that feeds it.

Two departures are disclosed. The training compound is drawn as its wall plus three interior features with the rest implied, the way the governor's compound is; a real martial hall also had a changing room, an armory and a viewing platform. And state violet against private buff is a MAP DRAWING CONVENTION rather than a historical difference - nothing about a domain school's construction differed from a private hall's, and the color is carrying an ownership distinction the map could not otherwise show.

The rule the map follows: a provincial city is the first tier that trains INDOORS. It keeps exactly ONE state martial hall - a state institution is a program item, not a count that scales - and rolls its private halls at one per full 200 resident samurai plus a chance of one more for the remainder, floored at one, which puts a city of about 3,000 at one or two of them. A county town draws a practice ground and no hall at all. Both kinds stand in or against the samurai neighborhood, a hall serving samurai and nobody else, and the state hall is its own compound rather than a wing of the governor's - standing near the government it answers to, but not inside it.

Servant housing in the samurai ward - servants are drawn as WALLS, not as houses

Sources: jta-nagayamon, jta-ashigaru-kaga, fukui-bushi-jutaku, matsue-bukeyashiki, hikone-ashigaru, shibata-ashigaru-nagaya, bukeyashiki-wiki, buke-hokonin-wiki, neixiang-yamen, daozuofang, siheyuan-zhwiki

What the research found

Japan (the cultural surface, so it decides the drawn form). Domestic servants of a samurai household - genan/gejo, chugen, komono - lived INSIDE their master's walled plot,7 never in their own street-fronting house in the buke-chi8. They were live-in staff on annual contracts (the degawari changeover,9 4th day of the 3rd month (unsourced)) hired out of the merchant quarter through labor brokers, so a castle town's servant population is partly recruited from the chonin-chi10 while its servant housing sits inside samurai walls. Three plan positions, in descending order of household size:

  1. The perimeter nagaya - a long, narrow, single-story range running along the INSIDE of the street boundary, forming the plot wall itself. The Fukui archive's reading of the Suganuma (菅沼) plan31 (1,000 koku, 1839: a 28 x 32.5 ken plot, ~167 x 194 ft)11 states plainly that the street-facing buildings were the nagaya where the servants lived12.
  2. The nagayamon gate rooms - where the range is short it survives only as the gatehouse: servants' quarters and stable,13 a gatekeeper's room, a chugen room, a storeroom, sometimes a projecting guard box, with barred lookout windows onto the street (the Kanazawa page names only the servants' quarters and the stable; the gatekeeper's and chugen rooms, the storeroom, the guard box and "barred" are unsourced, a GUESS; the lookout windows themselves are attested, unbarred).29 Measured examples run ~15 ft deep by 70-80 ft long (Omura Yahei, Kishu, 12 ken x 2.5 ken = 76 x 15 ft; the Tokyo Important Cultural Property (ICP) gate 72 x 15 ft) (unsourced). It is a barracks range with a hole cut in it, not a gate with rooms attached.
  3. Rooms off the kitchen - below roughly 100-300 koku there is no room for a range at all, and the servants sleep in nando and service rooms under the main roof (unsourced). Even at the top this persists for women: Aizu's Saigo Tanomo residence sorts its 38 rooms into reception, retainers' office, family, and maids'/servants' groups, all under one roof (unsourced).

Small uniform houses in ranks DO exist in a castle town - but never here. Ashigaru, kachi and doshin got individual plots in dedicated collective blocks (kumi-yashiki) at the town's EDGE:14 Kanazawa put them on the fringe beyond the townspeople's quarter (unsourced), Hikone ringed the whole town with ~700 households as a defensive screen,15 Sendai and Hachinohe placed them at the highway ends (unsourced). Files of about ten identical plots per row,16 and the enclosure marks the rank - earthen wall for middle and up,17 board fence for lower (unsourced), hedge for the lowest,18 who were forbidden walls (unsourced). Form is a terraced range by default (Shibata's ICP ashigaru-nagaya: 8 households under one thatch roof, 143 x 24 ft (梁間, a depth of 7.3 m)32, 18 ft frontage each)19 and detached-with-garden only in rich domains (Kaga, Hikone34 - both flagged as unusual in their own documentation).20

China (checked second, and it agrees). An elite or official quarter reads as a field of walled rectangles with a single dark notch each. Household servants occupy the daozuofang - the south row whose windowless back IS the compound's street wall, its doors facing inward21 - and the rear service row (houzhaofang), which the same account puts at the boundary of the innermost court and gives to the owner's daughters40 - so the inner rows house family rather than servants. The one attested staff-housing BUILDING is the Ming clerks' lodging (lishe / gongxiefang) - a single named courtyard INSIDE the yamen wall (Pingyao's, built 1619 behind the west three chambers;22 Neixiang's west line held a guest hall, dining hall and jail (寅宾馆、膳馆、监狱) instead, not a clerks' lodging30), not a district. The group numerous enough to have formed a servant quarter, the runners (yayi), was not housed by the state at all: their banfang were improvised sheds against the inside of the yamen wall, with no fixed position and no standard size, and they went home to houses scattered in the town (unsourced).

The two traditions therefore give the same drawing rule from opposite directions, which is as strong as this kind of finding gets: service accommodation is part of the boundary or the interior of the compound it serves, and a rank of small uniform dwellings inside an elite quarter is the one thing neither tradition produces.

The decision it drove

A servant dwelling inside a samurai ward is not a freestanding house. It is service accommodation BOUND to the samurai household it serves - drawn on that household's street edge as a long, thin range (the nagayamon proportion, ~15 ft deep), or not drawn at all for the smallest households, whose servants sleep under the master's roof.

The count is NOT the problem: the setting's own budget notes give a provincial city 120 servant families, of which 72 are attached to samurai households (30 to wealthy samurai at property 2, 42 indentured to non-wealthy at property 1) against 60 samurai families - about one servant household per junior samurai household and two per senior. They are property-holding families in the GM's economic model, so they are real households that must be housed, counted, and taxed. What the GM saw on Minami (2026-08-02) - "more commoner houses in the samurai neighborhood" - was the ARRANGEMENT: a rank of detached servant houses unattached to any samurai household reads as exactly what the fence exists to exclude.

Disclosed departures

  • Our in-city samurai houses are UNWALLED (our city plans put the walled estates outside the rampart). Both traditions above put the servants inside a compound WALL that our city plans do not draw. The nagaya range is what resolves this without reversing the GM's settled doctrine: historically the range IS the wall,23 so drawing the range alone - a long thin building hard on the street line - carries the same read at 3 ft/px without introducing walled compounds into the ward.
  • Ashigaru blocks are not drawn as a distinct texture at this tier. The edge-of-town kumi-yashiki is a real and attested pattern24 and would be the correct home for any genuinely ranked small housing: ranks of uniform small dwellings belong OUTSIDE the ward on the town fringe, not inside the fence.

Samurai and commoner ground were zoned apart by law

Sources: jokamachi-jawiki (侍町 by rank around the castle, 町人地 outside it, 寺町 at the rim),25 jokamachi-zoning (chonin strips separating samurai groups)26

An Edo-period jokamachi assigned samurai ground (武家地) and townsman ground (町人地) as separate districts,2733 and a Chinese provincial seat kept commerce off the yamen quarter (unsourced); a laborer terrace between two samurai houses inside the palisade is not variety, it contradicts the fence around it.

The rule the map follows: the sealed ward holds ONLY its own residents. Samurai houses large and small, their live-in domestics, and government ground stand inside the fence; laborers, merchants, shops, inns and burakumin do not, and a housing pass that reaches across the fence seats those households elsewhere in the city instead. A monks' house is deliberately allowed, because a temple may legitimately stand inside the ward - the warrior fortune's temple beside the garrison quarter is the case - and its clergy row belongs with its temple. The ward is fixed before the commoner quarters are laid, and no barred household is placed inside it. A ward that cannot take commoner rows closes its share of the city's population with MORE SAMURAI HOUSEHOLDS instead, each with its own servant range - which is the right answer for the city's emptiest quarter and keeps the samurai cohort inside its caste band.
