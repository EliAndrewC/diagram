Gate furniture at the throat - barbican and tax-barrier practice

Sources: hakone-seki-jawiki (the menbansho and mukaibansho lists - that they stand opposite each other across the road is this page's reading), wengcheng-zhwiki (the barbican), chaoguan-zhwiki (the Ming-Qing customs barriers, which stood on the canals rather than at a county seat's gates), lijin-zhwiki, city-gate-enwiki, defensive-wall-enwiki

An inspection or tax barrier only works where traffic is forced single-file, and a walled city's gate passage is the ONE such chokepoint in the whole wall - set the station back and arrivals disperse into the street grid before ever reaching it, defeating its purpose. So the guard and inspection posts cluster at the gate opening, about 20 to 100 ft inside (the distance unsourced), flanking the road as it enters. Hakone is the worked example: the barrier's two guard stations - the menbansho, with the upper guard office, a rest room and a bath inside it, and the mukaibansho with its jail - stood within the fenced enclosure, the two gates about eighteen meters apart. That the two faced each other across the road, so that nobody reached the town beyond without passing between them, is this record's own reading and is on no page read. In a wengcheng the whole cluster reads most authentically inside the urn-courtyard between the two misaligned gates. Setting the station back a few hundred feet along the wall would be historically wrong.

Chinese customs practice is not the anchor. The Ming-Qing 鈔關 customs barriers stood at commodity centers and on the Grand Canal and strategic routes, not at a county seat's gates; lijin is a separate, later Qing tax. The gate-throat cluster rests on the Hakone sekisho arrangement (the two stations facing each other is a GUESS - no readable page supports it) and the chokepoint reasoning, not on Chinese customs practice.

The rule the map follows: every gate carries a guard house, an inspection station and its own gate tower. The guard house and the inspection station take OPPOSITE flanks of the opening and face each other across the road, so that arrivals pass between them - an arrangement this project chose and a GUESS, since no readable page says the two stations at a barrier stood opposite one another. They are seated about 105-135 ft inside the opening, at its inner mouth; what the research supports is only "at the gate rather than set back along the wall", and the distance itself rests on no source. All three stand SQUARE to the wall and to the patrol road they line - turned to the wall's tangent where each actually sits rather than to the gate's own, which on a shallow slope has already curved away - within about 6 degrees, and are then pulled in radially onto the patrol road's centerline so the road runs lengthwise through them. A gate's tower stands AT its gate, flanking the opening, and never out among the wall's own bastions: where one flank is blocked it takes the other flank of the same opening rather than walking off along the curtain.

The rule the map follows: gate furniture never overlaps. On a small tight ring the two buildings converge as they walk out from the opening, and the inspection station steps further out, about 66 ft at a time, until the two rectangles clear each other. Their keep-clear apron is about 36 ft, which is enough because the road's own corridor already fends the housing off one flank. A fire-watch tower keeps 15 ft of daylight from a graveyard, the same clearance its braced footing keeps from wells and buildings.
