Does the city wall close a full ring - and why so few gates?

A provincial seat's rampart is a closed defensive loop rather than the cost-minimized skirt a town throws around whatever ground it happens to have built on. It runs its own regular circuit and encloses deliberate open ground - drill ground, room to shelter the countryside when a column comes through. Defense concentrates in a few grand gates instead of many small openings, because every opening is a place the wall has to be held twice over: the sortie a European curtain makes through a sally port is made here from the wengcheng, the walled barbican court set between an outer and an inner gate. A European barbican's narrow neck keeps a mass of attackers from storming the gate, and by the same narrowness stops the defenders sallying out to meet them; the wengcheng is roomy enough to pass a body of troops either way, and serves as protected staging ground for defenders who mean to fight outside the wall. It is a court rather than a jar: weng is an urn and the name is the urn's, but the surviving wengcheng at Suzhou's Panmen is roughly square in plan, about 580 ft around inside, its wall 27 ft high on a base of dressed stone.

The nearest East Asian equivalents of a postern are the rear gate opposite the main approach (karamete-mon), the small walled-up postern (umon) and the water gate (shuimen) that a canal city has anyway. Each of the three is attested in itself. The karamete is a castle's rear gate, defined against the ote that is its main front. The umon is cut through the lower part of a stone wall or a moat face, kept impassable except when it is wanted, built so that it can be filled in easily, and meant among other things as the road by which the lord of the castle gets out - which is a sally port's own function. The shuimen is a defended opening in a Chinese city wall for a canal, and it is no small thing: Panmen's water gate runs 80 ft deep through two gate layers of stone arches, a grand gate in its own right, so what it shares with a sally port is only that it is not the main land approach. Setting the three of them against the European sally port as its nearest equivalents is this record's own comparison; no source makes it.

The rule the map follows: a provincial city's wall is a CLOSED ring with at least two gates, each a genuine gap in the rampart with a road running through it. The ring is regular, drawn as a defensive circuit rather than hugging the built-up ground, and the open ground inside it is left open on purpose. No map draws a postern or a sally gate.
