Towers along the city wall (mamian)

A Chinese city wall was studded with towers, the mamian: solid platforms built one after another against the wall's outer face, standing out from it so that a besieger at the foot of the wall between two of them could be struck from both sides. Set close together, in the eleventh-century scholar Shen Kuo's words, "the arrows and stones reach one another". On surviving walls they stand from about 50 m (~164 ft) apart at Pingyao to 120 m (~394 ft) at Xi'an, and a Tang army's archers began to shoot at sixty paces, about 90 m (~295 ft).

Along the wall the towers were straight-sided; at the corners a Song city could build them curved, on purpose. How many towers a wall carried followed the wars around it: in Ming Shanxi each great wave of wall-building came after a Mongol raid deep into the province.

  • The towers let the defenders shoot at besiegers from both flanks.
    The Chinese Wikipedia article on the wall tower says its projecting front and the windows in its sides make it easy to shoot at the besiegers from both flanks. An article on the defense works of the ancient Chinese city says the same of the mamian: in war they made it easy to strike the besieging enemy from two sides.
  • A tower's platform held more defenders at the height of the wall.
    A solid tower was filled in below, with a crenellated parapet round all four sides of its top, and it let more soldiers and defensive engines stand at the wall's height.
  • Shen Kuo paced out towers four zhang long and six or seven zhang apart.
    His Mengxi Bitan says that long towers let the defenders shoot back at attackers below the wall, and that close-set ones bring the arrows and stones of each within reach of the next. An enemy who came up to the foot of the wall had arrows and stones bearing down on him from all four sides.
  • Song writers set the towers at fixed intervals, from ten paces to a hundred.
    The Shoucheng Lu, a Song manual on holding a city, gives the old rule: a tower every sixty paces, standing out from the wall no less than two zhang, its breadth following the lie of the ground. A Song memoir of the capital, the Dongjing Meng Hua Lu, records one every hundred paces, and the Wujing zongyao sets them closer than either, at one every ten paces, about 16 m (~52 ft).
  • How far apart did the towers of surviving walls stand?
    From about 50 m (~164 ft) to 120 m (~394 ft). Xi'an's 98 towers stand 120 m (~394 ft) apart, each 12 m (~39 ft) out from the wall and 20 m (~66 ft) wide. The Chinese Wikipedia article on Pingyao's wall puts a tower every 50 m (~164 ft), but the same article counts 72 towers on a wall 6.4 km (~21,000 ft) around, which works out at about 89 m (~292 ft) apiece - the arithmetic is this project's.
  • How far did an archer on the wall reach?
    About 92 m (~302 ft) by the one figure read, the range at which men began to shoot. The Tongdian's drill for a Tang army in the field has the crossbowmen loose at 150 paces, the archers at 60, and both put down bow and crossbow for sword and staff within 20. In the old reckoning a pace was five of the builder's feet, and a Tang foot about 30.7 cm (~12 in), so the archers' sixty paces come to about 92 m (~302 ft) and the crossbowmen's 150 to about 230 m (~755 ft), by this project's arithmetic. The five-foot pace is given for the old reckoning, not for the Tang, so applying it to a Tang drill is this project's assumption. No page we read gives a range at which an arrow was sure to kill.
  • Was the sixty-pace spacing set by the bow's range?
    Perhaps. The Shoucheng Lu's sixty paces between towers is the Tang archers' opening range, so a wall towered to that rule has every point of its foot within that range of a tower on either side. Neither text names the other, and that the one number was chosen for the other is this project's reading. The one spacing a source ties to weapons is Xi'an's 120 m (~394 ft), said to stand exactly within the range of bows and firearms.
  • Were the towers along the wall straight-sided?
    The Song rule describes a tower whose two sides look straight down on the foot of the wall, and Xi'an's are 20 m (~66 ft) wide and 12 m (~39 ft) deep. The sources credit the tower's effect to its length and how far it projects; that a curved face would shoot worse along the wall is this project's reading.
  • A study of Song city building finds the corner towers made curved on purpose.
    The study, worked from the Northern Song military compendium Wujing zongyao, finds the dituan - the tower at a city's corner or at the turn of a barbican - arc-shaped, "in order to increase the difficulty of the enemy's attack and to reduce the area exposed to attack". Its author finds the form in no Han or Tang text and takes it to be a Song creation.
  • Europe built round towers for a like reason.
    The English Wikipedia article on fortified towers says round towers resist sappers and projectiles better than square ones. That Europe and Song China came to the curve separately is this project's inference from the Song study's dating; if so, a curved tower is no sign of European influence, and a round corner on a Chinese wall does not by itself mark an older piece of wall.
  • At Xi'an, three corner towers are square and the southwest one is round.
    The round one follows the older building that stood on the spot.
  • Accounts differ on how old Xi'an's round corner is.
    The Chinese Wikipedia article on the wall puts the older building in the Yuan; a popular-history piece puts the round platform in the imperial city of Sui-Tang Chang'an. The two agree that a later rebuild went round an older corner.
  • Were Japan's towers square too?
    This project takes Japan's yagura and tenshu as square or rectangular timber towers on stone bases, the one curve being the batter of the ishigaki wall beneath them. This is a GUESS: no page we read gives their plan.

How many towers a wall carried followed the wars around it. The clearest case read is Ming Shanxi, the province just inside the northern frontier.

  • In Ming Shanxi, each great wave of wall-building followed a Mongol raid.
    The province's cities walled and rewalled themselves in three waves, about 1449-1450, 1540-1543 and 1567-1570, each after a Mongol raid deep into Shanxi.
  • Ming Shanxi's walls of the 1540s were built to stand a siege.
    That wave's works were towers, corner towers, crenels, brick and stone facing and dredged moats, and in central Shanxi, which the raiders had ravaged three years running, the building rate reached 91 percent.
  • Pingyao added 94 brick towers in the wave of 1567-1570.
    The same wave gave its six gates drawbridges and outer gates.
  • Yuci, in Ming Shanxi, added 20 towers and 4 corner towers in 1541, in the second wave.
    They went up as the city faced its earthen wall in brick.
  • How many towers did a wall carry?
    The counts range widely: 9 on the four sides of the prefectural city of Guide, in Henan, 72 at Pingyao and 98 at Xi'an.
  • Pingyao's 72 towers are said to follow Confucius's 72 worthies.
    The Chinese Wikipedia article on its wall says its 72 towers and more than 3,000 crenels were designed after Confucius's three thousand disciples and seventy-two worthies, so a count could carry meaning as well as defense.
