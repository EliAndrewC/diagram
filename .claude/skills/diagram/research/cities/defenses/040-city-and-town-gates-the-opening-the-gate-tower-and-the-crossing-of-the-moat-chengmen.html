City and town gates: the opening, the gate tower and the crossing of the moat (chengmen)

A Chinese city gate was a passage through the wall with a gate tower built over it, and in front of it a drawbridge across the moat and a tower to work the bridge. The passages measured run about 16 to 20 ft clear, and the gate towers from about 46 by 33 ft at a county seat's main gate to about 85 by 43 ft at Chaozhou's. A town's gate was the city's made smaller: the size of a gate tower followed the scale of its wall.

In Japan a castle's moat was crossed by an earthen causeway or a timber bridge, chosen gate by gate, and a post town on a highway had no gate at all: banks on either side of the road marked where the town began.

  • A Chinese town's gates carried gate towers, as a city's did.
    Pingyao's six gates each carry their gate tower. Shanghai, walled in a hurry in 1553, had towers for defense built at its gates four years later, when the wall was also widened.
  • A gate tower grew with its wall.
    A historian of the Chinese city wall puts the rule in one line: the size of a gate tower is in proportion to the scale of its wall.
  • How large was a gate tower?
    Four have been measured. Pingyao's four main gate towers, at a county seat, are five bays wide, 14 m (~46 ft), and four bays deep, 10 m (~33 ft). Chaozhou's Guangji Gate tower stands on the wall with a ground story 25.8 m (~85 ft) wide and 13 m (~43 ft) deep. That Chaozhou's is a prefectural seat's tower is this project's reading, a GUESS: no page we read states the city's rank.
  • At the capital, a side gate's tower was smaller than the main gate's.
    Beijing's Yongding Gate, at the middle of the outer city's south wall, carries a tower five bays wide, 24 m (~79 ft), and three deep, 10.5 m (~34 ft). Zuo'an Gate, at the east end of the same wall, carries a tower three bays wide, 16 m (~52 ft), and one bay deep, 9 m (~30 ft), and the outer city's towers are all lower and smaller than the inner city's.
  • How wide was a city gate's passage?
    About 16 to 20 ft clear where it has been measured. Nanjing's Zhonghua Gate passes four tunnels in line, whose clear widths measure 5.35, 4.97, 4.82 and 4.80 m (~16-18 ft). Xi'an's are 6 m (~20 ft) both high and wide. A band of about 13 to 23 ft for a Ming provincial city is this project's generalization from those two cities, a GUESS; nothing we read is as narrow as 13 ft.
  • At Xi'an, the passage is a tunnel through the whole thickness of the wall.
    Xi'an's gate tunnels run 19.5 m (~64 ft) deep, through a wall 12 m (~39 ft) high and 15 to 18 m (~49-59 ft) wide at its base.
  • How wide was a county town's gate, and how large its tower?
    No page we read gives either figure. Since a gate tower followed the scale of its wall, a county gate was the city's gate made smaller, not a different thing.
  • How wide was the gate of a Japanese castle town?
    No page we read gives the clear width of a koraimon, the gate of an Edo castle town. That it was narrower than a Chinese city's passage is this project's reading, a GUESS.

Outside the gate, the road had to cross the moat, and the Chinese gate crossed it by a bridge that could be raised.

  • A complete Chinese city gate had a drawbridge before it, and a tower to work it.
    A complete city gate includes a drawbridge and the tower that works it. At Xi'an the drawbridge crossed the moat outside the outermost gate. In peacetime it lay down and carried people and carts, and when an enemy came the soldiers in the tower over that gate hauled it up with iron chains and ropes, and the moat could not be crossed. The tower took its name, zhalou, from the bridge it controlled.
  • A Ming drawbridge was timber, hung on iron chains and hemp rope.
    It was built of juniper or pagoda-tree wood and tied to the gate tower by iron rings and iron chains mixed with hemp rope.
  • County towns had drawbridges too.
    At Zhenhai every gate had its drawbridge as well as its barbican. Whether a gate had a barbican is at Did a town or city gate have a barbican, and how large was it?
  • Was there ever a fixed stone bridge at a Chinese city gate?
    None before modern times is on any page we read. At Xi'an the outer works and their towers were pulled down in the early years of the Republic of China, after 1912, so that fixed bridges could carry traffic over the moat.

A Japanese castle crossed its moat in one of two ways, and chose between them gate by gate.

  • A Japanese castle's moat was crossed by an earthen causeway or a timber bridge.
    The causeway, a dobashi, is not a bridge at all but a strip of ground left standing when the moat was dug. The choice was a trade: the causeway cannot be taken away in a hurry, which works against the defenders, while the timber bridge can, but large timber was very costly.
  • Timber bridges went to the rear and little-used gates, causeways to the gates the garrison meant to sally from.
    A timber bridge suited a gate that would be shut off entirely in need. An early-modern causeway was usually faced in stone and so wide that a person crossing it might not notice it was a bridge, and the main gate, built especially wide, was often reached by one.
  • A causeway could hold a water moat at two levels.
    Across a water moat, the water could be kept higher on one side of the causeway than on the other.
  • Edo's Sakurada Gate was known by its causeway.
    It appears on a map of Edo of about 1608 as the Sakurada causeway.
  • How long and how wide was a crossing?
    As long as the moat was wide; how wide the moats were is at City moats: how wide and deep, and whether they ring the wall (hori, chenghe). How wide a drawbridge, a causeway or a timber bridge was is on no page we read.

A Japanese post town, strung along a highway, marked its ends in a different way again.

  • A Japanese post town marked its entrances with banks, not a gate.
    At the entrances of the post towns on the main highways stood the mitsuke, and the stretch between the one on the Edo side and the one on the Kyoto side was, in effect, the town. Where a town kept a gate and a guard on its entrance instead, that is at Did a town on a road keep a barrier or inspection post?
  • At Kanagawa the banks stood about 8 ft high, with a fence along the top.
    Kanagawa's Edo-side mitsuke was a pair of banks either side of the road, each topped by a fence. In section each bank was a trapezoid about 4.2 m (~14 ft) across the base, about 60 cm (~2 ft) across the top and about 2.5 m (~8 ft) high, with a fence about 75 cm (~30 in) high on top.
  • Did the banks still matter by the end of the Edo period?
    Perhaps not much. The page that describes them reads them as a simple defense that also showed where the town began, and wonders whether they still mattered much in the late Edo period.
