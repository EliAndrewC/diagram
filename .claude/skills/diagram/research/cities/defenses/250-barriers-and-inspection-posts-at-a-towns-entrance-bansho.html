Barriers and inspection posts at a town's entrance (bansho)

A town kept its watch where the roads came in. It was there to keep the peace, above all at night, and to control who and what came in. A Chinese walled town's gates were themselves the post: they opened in the morning, shut in the evening and were locked at night, and after dark only a person holding an official tally went through. A Japanese town of the Edo period, before 1868, closed its streets with kido, gates each with a watch house where the gatekeeper lived, shut at night. The castle town of Kitsuki kept six such guard posts, bansho, at its entrances.

Beyond the town, travelers met barriers that inspected them or taxed their goods. The one this record knows best is the barrier at Hakone, rebuilt in 2007 to its late-Edo plan, two guard houses inside a fenced enclosure, its two gates about 18 m (~59 ft) apart. The domains kept their own barrier posts on their borders, and Ming and Qing China a handful of customs stations on its trade routes.

  • A Chinese town's gates opened in the morning, shut in the evening and were locked at night.
    A study of Chinese city defense says every city gate was kept to those hours.
  • At Shanghai, a county seat, the gates shut after six in the evening, and only an official tally let anyone through.
    Anyone who wanted to go in or out after closing had to carry an officially issued tally; slipping out without one was punished with the cangue.
  • A walled city's gates were where it controlled who and what came in, and, where trade routes passed through, where tolls were paid.
    City gates were built to be a point of controlled access for people, vehicles, goods and animals, and works beyond the wall kept caravans from going round the city, so that their tolls were paid at the gates.
  • If a city kept guard or inspection posts at a gate, where did they stand?
    At the gate's opening, flanking the road as it came in, is this project's reading, a GUESS: the gate is where traffic had to pass, and a post set back from it would meet travelers only after they had scattered into the streets. How far inside the gate they stood, no page we read gives; at Hakone the two gates were about 18 m (~59 ft) apart. Whether they stood in the court of a barbican is at Barbicans (wengcheng, masugata).
  • Below the county, the Chinese state kept patrol posts, the xunjiansi, though no page we read says where one stood.
    A xunjiansi was a post under the county, in the Yuan, Ming and Qing, headed, in the Qing at least, by an officer of the ninth grade, the lowest. In 1387 the Ming set up forty-two in Fujian against the pirates, by the encyclopedia's uncited count, and those of prefectures, departments and counties answered to the local official.
  • Did a Chinese county town keep an inspection building at its gate, apart from the gate itself?
    No page we read shows one.

A Japanese town kept its watch at its streets and entrances.

  • An Edo-period town closed its streets with kido, each with a watch house.
    In the early modern cities a kido stood at both ends of the street of each block, with a watch house attached where the kido keeper lived; the gates were first of all for keeping the peace, and were shut at night and in emergencies such as riots.
  • Some castle towns kept kido only at their entrances and where the samurai quarter met the townsmen's.
    Sendai and Matsuyama had no gate for each block, only at the entrances of the castle town and at the boundaries between the samurai and townsmen's quarters.
  • The castle town of Kitsuki kept six guard posts at its entrances.
    They watched the people and goods going in and out, closed their great doors at the hour and, as at a barrier station, allowed no coming and going late at night.
  • Beyond Kitsuki's main entrance post, old maps show only the road, with no houses.
    Outside the post at the town's front entrance on its highways, the old maps show no houses, only a single road.
  • At one of Kitsuki's posts, a small gate was opened at night for the quarter outside it.
    The post stood at the north foot of an earthen bridge, with the fish quarter outside it and outside the town's enclosure, and at night, though the post was shut, a small gate was opened for the people of the fish quarter.

A post town on a main highway marked its two ends with mitsuke instead; that is at City and town gates: the opening, the gate tower and the crossing of the moat (chengmen). The barriers recorded on the roads between towns were a domain's or, in China, the state's.

  • At the Hakone barrier, two guard houses stood inside a fenced enclosure, its gates about 18 m (~59 ft) apart.
    The menbansho held the upper guard office, the guards' duty room, a rest room and a bath; the mukaibansho held a guard post, a rest room and a jail; a stable, a crossroads watch post and a notice board stood with them, all fenced round.
  • At the rebuilt Hakone barrier, the two guard houses face each other.
    The barrier as rebuilt in 2007 follows the detailed records of a large-scale repair at the end of the Edo period. In it the foot soldiers' guard house, with its jail, stands across from the main guard house, where travelers were examined, beside the gate on the Edo side.
  • Did every traveler at Hakone pass between the two guard houses?
    This project reads both as standing within the 18 m (~59 ft) between the gates, with the road running between them, so that nobody went on without passing both. That is a reading of the rebuilt plan, a GUESS: neither page names the road.
  • Each domain kept its own barrier posts on its borders, the kuchidome bansho.
    They stood at the domain's borders and at key points of traffic, the domain's counterpart to the shogunate's barrier stations. They watched the travelers and goods going in and out and collected a duty on the goods, something like a customs duty.   • Were a domain's border posts open only from sunrise to sunset?
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on them says so, without a citation and for every domain alike.
  • Did a domain's border post stand at a town's own entrance?
    No page we read places one there, so this record treats them as the domain's posts, not a town's.
  • Ming and Qing China's customs stations were a handful on the trade routes, not a post at every town gate.
    The government set them at centers where goods were collected and at key water and land junctions, and by the Wanli reign only the eight most important were kept, seven of them along the Grand Canal.
  • At least one of the eight customs stations stood at a city gate, Yangzhou's.
    Yangzhou's was set at a gate of its new city, and another bore a gate's name, Chongwenmen.
  • The lijin tax was separate from the customs stations, and more than four centuries later.
    It was first levied in 1853, in the Yangzhou region, and approved for all of Jiangsu the next year, while the customs stations date from 1429.
