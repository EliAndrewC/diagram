Barbicans (wengcheng, masugata)

A barbican, the wengcheng, was a walled court built out in front of a Chinese city gate, as high and thick as the wall, with its own gate opened off to one side, so that attackers had to turn their siege engines aside rather than drive straight at the main gate. The Ming dynasty built them at its capitals and down to its county towns, and the one measured, the south barbican of Xi'an, was big enough to hold three thousand soldiers. The court's name and the surviving one at Suzhou are at City walls: a closed ring with few gates, and its shape (chengqiang).

Japan's castles guarded a gate the same way with the masugata, a square court with a gate at each end, where an attacker who forced the first gate was stopped by the second and fired on from every side. The widest surviving one at Edo castle is roughly 90 by 125 ft.

  • The military manual Wujing zongyao gives the barbican's form: round or square, as high as the wall, with one gate off to the side.
    The manual, the first work to describe the barbican, says a barbican outside the wall is round or square as the lie of the ground allows, its height and thickness equal to the wall's, with only one gate, opened to the left or the right as is convenient.
  • A barbican's gate was usually not in line with the gate it guarded.
    So besiegers had to turn their siege engines another way rather than drive straight in.
  • Barbicans were built widely in the Five Dynasties and the Northern Song, in the tenth and eleventh centuries.
    The Chinese Wikipedia article on the barbican says that among China's walled cities, the building of barbicans flourished in those two periods.
  • The Yuan built none at its capitals until its last years.
    To keep the Chinese from holding cities in revolt, the Yuan ordered city walls torn down everywhere, and neither of its capitals, Dadu and Shangdu, had a barbican until the risings at the end of the dynasty, when Emperor Shun hastily built them at Dadu.
  • There is no record of a barbican at the Southern Song capital, Lin'an, or at the Upper Capitals of the Liao dynasty and the Jin dynasty, the two empires that ruled north China beside the Song dynasty.
    The Chinese Wikipedia article on the barbican names these as places with no such record.
  • The Ming built barbicans even at county towns.
    It built them at its capitals, Nanjing, Fengyang and Beijing, and at local cities of prefecture, department and county rank, such as Xi'an, Guide (today's Shangqiu) and Pingyao.

The walled towns whose walls survive differ in which of their gates had a barbican.

  • At Pingyao, a county town, the barbicans recorded stand outside the east and west gates.
    Pingyao is the best preserved example of a Ming and Qing county town.Of its six gates, one each stands on the south and north and two each on the east and west, and the barbicans stand outside the east and west gates.
  • At Zhenhai, a county seat, each of the five gates left open had a barbican.
    Zhenhai's wall was the wall of a county seat. It set six land gates early in the Ming and blocked the north gate in 1415, leaving five, and outside them were set a barbican and a drawbridge; the Xiaonan gate's barbican was added in 1396.
  • At Shou county all four gates keep their barbicans.
    The wall has four gates, and their barbicans are still preserved.
  • How large was a barbican?
    The one measured is Xi'an's south barbican, 70 m (~230 ft) east to west and 50 m (~164 ft) north to south, 3,500 square meters (~37,700 sq ft), and in its day it could hold three thousand soldiers. How large a county town's barbican was, no page we read gives.
  • Did a city gate's guard and inspection posts stand inside the barbican court?
    A city watched and closed its gates, as Barriers and inspection posts at a town's entrance (bansho) tells. No page we read puts them there. Since the barbican's own gate stood off the line of the city gate, everyone entering crossed the court between the two, and that the posts stood in it is this project's reading, a GUESS. Where the record does place such posts is at Barriers and inspection posts at a town's entrance (bansho).

Japan's castles built the masugata in more than one form, and at more than one kind of gate.

  • A masugata was built inside the line of the enclosure or thrust out beyond it.
    The inner masugata makes a small square space on the inner side of the enclosure's entrance and builds a second gate; the outer thrusts a small square space out beyond the entrance and opens one more gate at its front.
  • A masugata's basic form was one gate in front and a gate with a tower over it behind.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on castle entrances takes a koraimon in front and a yaguramon behind to be the basic form, though some have only the rear gate and some only the front.
  • A castle's main gate was often built as a masugata.
    It was built heavily for defense, often with a large yaguramon forming a masugata with stone walls.
  • How large was a masugata?
    The Sakurada gate's is about 27 m (~89 ft) by 38 m (~125 ft), and is said to be the widest of the surviving masugata gates of Edo castle.
  • At Edo, masugata stood on the castle's outer works as well.
    Edo castle is said to have had 36 mitsuke, watched gates along its outer and inner moats, and Akasaka mitsuke, a gate on the outer moat, took the form of a masugata gate.
  • Did a Japanese town's own gate have a masugata?
    No page we read shows one: the pages that describe the masugata describe a castle's gates, and a Japanese town enclosed itself, if at all, with a moat and an earth bank round a castle town (How high and thick is a town's rampart?).
