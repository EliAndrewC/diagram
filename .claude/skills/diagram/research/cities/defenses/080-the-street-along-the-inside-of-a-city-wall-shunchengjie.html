The street along the inside of a city wall (shunchengjie)

A walled Chinese city ran a street along the inside of its rampart, the 順城街 (shunchengjie, "follow-the-wall street"). At Tongzhou, east of Beijing, the street of that name began as a patrol road ridden along the wall, and at Chang'an, the capital of the Tang dynasty, such streets ran along all four sides inside the outer wall, the narrowest of its main streets at about 20 to 25 m (~66-82 ft).

  • At Tongzhou the street had formed by the Ming dynasty, as a patrol road.
    The street was originally a patrol road along the wall, called the West Horse Road.
  • At Chang'an the wall-side streets were narrower than the avenues and wider than the streets inside the wards.
    The avenues that led to no gate ran 35 to 65 m (~115-213 ft) wide, the wall-side streets 20 to 25 m (~66-82 ft), and the streets inside the wards about 15 m (~49 ft).
  • Was the street kept clear of buildings?
    Its name says it was ridden to patrol the wall. That it was kept clear of buildings so that troops could be moved along the wall wherever they were wanted, its whole value being that it was unobstructed, is this project's reading, a GUESS: no page we read says houses were barred from it.

At Chang'an the street also ran between the city's walled wards and its rampart.

  • Every ward of Chang'an was walled on all four sides.
    The crisscrossing roads divided the outer city into 110 wards, each with a wall built around all four sides, its gates guarded by soldiers and a curfew enforced at night.
  • Did a ward's wall ever meet the city wall at Chang'an?
    Apparently not. With every ward walled on four sides and the wall-side street running along all four sides inside the outer wall, the wards beside the rampart were closed by their own walls, with that street between them and the rampart.
