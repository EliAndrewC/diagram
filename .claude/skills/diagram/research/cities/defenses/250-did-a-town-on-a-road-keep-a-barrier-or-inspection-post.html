Did a town on a road keep a barrier or inspection post?

Sources: sinoss-cao-city-defense (gates opened at dawn and locked at night), shanghai-xiancheng-chengqiang-zhwiki (a county town's closing hour and its pass), xunjiansi-zhwiki (the Chinese patrol post below the county), kuchidome-bansho-jawiki (the domain's own barrier posts), kitsuki-bansho (a small castle town's six entry posts), historist-kido (the town gate and its watch house)

The walled town: the gate is the post. A Chinese town's gates opened in the morning, shut in the evening and were locked at night. At Shanghai the gates shut after six in the evening, and anyone who wanted to go in or out after that had to carry an official tally; slipping out without one earned the cangue. The state's own posts below the county were the xunjiansi, each under a minor officer - the Ming set forty-two in Fujian alone against pirates, and those under a county answered to its magistrate; the page read does not say where one stood. A separate inspection building at a county town's gate is on no page read; the city's, with the Hakone barrier as its worked example, is under gate furniture at the throat.

The Japanese town: a gate and a watch house at each entry. An Edo-period town closed itself with kido, gates across its streets, each with a watch house where the gatekeeper lived; they kept the peace and were shut at night and in an emergency, and some castle towns had them only at the town's entrances and where the samurai quarter met the townsmen's. The small castle town of Kitsuki kept six guard posts (bansho) at its entrances. They watched the people and goods going in and out, shut their great doors at the hour and, like a barrier station, allowed no passage late at night; outside the main one on the highway there were no houses, only the road. One stood at the north foot of an earthen bridge, with the fish quarter outside it and outside the enclosure, and at night, though the post was shut, a small gate was opened for the people of the fish quarter. A post town marked its ends with banks rather than gates, under what stands at a walled town's gate.

The domain's border post is a different thing. Each domain set its own barrier posts, the kuchidome bansho, on its borders and at key points of traffic - its answer to the shogunate's barrier stations, watching travelers and goods and collecting a duty, open from sunrise to sunset. No page read places one at a town's own entrance, so this record treats them as the domain's posts, not a town's.

The rule the map follows: a town keeps its watch at its own entrances; which kind is a knob that follows the town's model. A walled town on the Chinese model draws no inspection building apart from its gate: the gate itself is the post, with a small guard room just inside it (the guard room is a GUESS: no page read shows one). An open town on the Japanese model draws, where each road enters it, a kido - a gate across the street - with a watch house beside it on the town side, and no houses beyond it on the road. The guard room and the watch house are drawn about 12 by 18 ft; that size is a GUESS, since no page read gives one. A border post is not drawn on a town map.
