Did a walled county town have a moat, and how wide was it?

Sources: sinoss-cao-city-defense (wall and moat as one word), pingyao-chengqiang-zhwiki (a narrow county moat), shanghai-xiancheng-chengqiang-zhwiki (a broad one), zhenhai-wall-zhwiki (one whose width changed side to side, and stopped at the sea), sogamae-jawiki (Sakai's moat), hori-jawiki (moated villages and temple towns)

Yes: in China the wall and its moat go together. A city wall was generally ringed outside by a moat, and the ordinary written word for a walled town, chengchi, means the wall and the moat together. Every county wall read here has one, and they differ widely in width. Pingyao's is 4 m (13 ft) wide and 4 m deep. Shanghai's was 6 zhang wide and 1 zhang 7 chi deep - about 63 ft and 18 ft at the 3.2 m zhang (this page's conversion); it was joined to the town's water gates for boats and to the river's tides. Zhenhai's was about 16 m (52 ft) wide on the east, 14.2 m (47 ft) on the south and 41.6 m (136 ft) on the west, 6.4 m (21 ft) deep throughout, and on the north, where the wall met the sea, there was none. In Japan, only the town that enclosed itself. Settlements ringed by a ditch were a Japanese form of long standing, and the temple towns of the civil-war era dug moats to defend themselves; the self-governing port of Sakai was enclosed on three sides by a moat about 10 m (33 ft) wide and 3 m (10 ft) deep, with gates (kido) at its entries. A Japanese county town under settled rule stood open (under was a county seat walled), and had no moat to draw.

The rule the map follows: a walled county town on the Chinese model always draws a moat outside its rampart. Its width is rolled between about 13 and 66 ft - Pingyao's to Shanghai's, a continuum - and may vary from side to side as Zhenhai's did; a side that a river, a lake or the sea already guards may go without. A town on the Japanese model that encloses itself draws a moat about 33 ft wide outside its bank, Sakai's; one that stands open draws none.
