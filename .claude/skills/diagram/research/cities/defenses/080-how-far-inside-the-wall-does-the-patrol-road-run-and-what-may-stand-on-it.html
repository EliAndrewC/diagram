How far inside the wall does the patrol road run, and what may stand on it?

Sources: tongzhou-xishuncheng-zhwiki (a Ming patrol road along a city wall), kepuchina-tang-changan and tang-changan-zhwiki (Chang'an's wall-side streets, measured); the area cost is this project's own measurement

A walled city ran a street along the inside of its rampart - the Chinese follow-the-wall street, 順城街. At Tongzhou, east of Beijing, the street of that name had formed by the Ming as a patrol road along the wall, called the West Horse Road; at Tang Chang'an such streets ran along all four sides inside the outer wall, narrow ones by that city's measure, 20 to 25 m wide, against 35 to 65 m for its avenues that led to no gate. The name says it was ridden to patrol the wall; that the lap was kept clear of buildings so that troops could be moved along the wall wherever they were wanted is this page's reading. The whole value of it is that it is unobstructed. Pulling the built ground in off the wall costs a city about one perimeter row of buildings, roughly eight percent of the ground it can build on, so a map that adds the patrol road makes that back somewhere - usually a small rise in density in one or two quarters - rather than quietly housing fewer inhabitants than the city is declared to hold.

The rule the map follows: a patrol road runs as a closed loop about 102 ft inside the rampart and about 20 ft wide - both figures GUESSES, since the one wall-side street measured is a capital's, 66 to 82 ft wide - and the quarters pack inside IT rather than up to the wall. Nothing may stand on its bed: no house, no ministry, no governor's compound, no temple, no field. What legitimately lines it is the wall's own furniture - the gate guard houses, the inspection stations, the mural towers - and a ward fence may cross it, gated. Housing is held off by a no-build corridor along the loop, half the road's width plus 63 ft, so that even a large or turned footprint's nearest corner stays well clear of the bed. Unlike an ordinary city street it is exempt from the rule that a street must be built up, its wall side being bare by design. A street or an alley running at it reaches its bed and ENDS there - neither stopping a sliver short in open ground nor poking a stub out the far side - while a genuine crossroads that carries well past is a different thing and is fine.
