Did a town or city gate have a barbican, and how large was it?

Sources: wengcheng-zhwiki (the barbican's form, and which cities of which periods built one), pingyao-chengqiang-zhwiki (a county town with barbicans on some gates only), zhenhai-wall-zhwiki (a county town with one at every gate), shouxian-wall-zhwiki (four gates, four barbicans), sohu-xian-wall-archive (Xi'an's south barbican, measured), koguchi-jawiki (the Japanese masugata), sakuradamon-jawiki (a masugata, measured), otemon-jawiki (the masugata as a castle's main gate)

What a barbican is. The wengcheng is a walled court thrown out before a city gate. The Song military compendium that first describes it says a barbican outside the wall is round or square as the ground allows, as high and thick as the wall itself, with its one gate opened off to one side; its gate is usually not in line with the gate it guards, so an attacker cannot drive straight through. What it was for, and the surviving court at Suzhou, are under does the city wall close a full ring.

How common: by period, and down to the county. Barbicans flourished from the Five Dynasties and the Northern Song; the Yuan had its walls torn down and built none at its capitals until its last years, and there is no record of one at the Southern Song capital. The Ming set them not only at its capitals but at the walls of prefectures, departments and counties, Pingyao among them. Not every gate had one. Pingyao has six gates, one each north and south and two each east and west, and the barbicans stand outside the east and west gates; at Zhenhai every gate had its barbican, one of them added some years after the rest; at Shou county all four gates keep theirs.

How large. Xi'an's south barbican, at one of the largest walls in China, is 70 m by 50 m (230 by 164 ft), and could hold 3,000 soldiers. How large a county town's barbican was is on no page read.

The Japanese masugata. Japan's answer at a castle gate is the masugata: a square space before the gate with two gates to it, so that whoever breaks the first is stopped by the second and fired on from every side. It is built inside the line or thrust out beyond it, and its basic form is a light roofed gate (a koraimon) in front and a gate with a tower over it (a yaguramon) behind, though some have only one of the two. A castle's main gate was usually built so. Edo castle's Sakurada gate, said to be the widest of the survivors, has a masugata about 27 by 38 m (89 by 125 ft). These are a castle's gates; an unwalled Japanese town had none to guard.

The rule the map follows: whether a gate has a barbican is a knob rolled per gate. A provincial city gives each main road gate a barbican; its court is rolled round or square and drawn between about 125 and 230 ft along the wall, from Edo's widest masugata to Xi'an's south barbican (calibrated liberty between the two measured courts), its own gate opened in its side wall rather than in line with the main gate. A walled county town on the Chinese model gives a barbican to some of its gates or to all of them, as Pingyao and Zhenhai differ, and draws it about 80 to 120 ft along the wall - a GUESS scaled down from the city's. A town on the Japanese model draws none; only a castle's gate draws a masugata.
