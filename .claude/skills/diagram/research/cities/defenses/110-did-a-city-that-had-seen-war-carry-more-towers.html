Did a city that had seen war carry more towers?

Sources: li-ga-2013-shanxi-walls (the raids and the waves of wall-building they drove in Ming Shanxi), thepaper-surviving-walls (the tower counts of Yuci and Guide), pingyao-chengqiang-zhwiki (Pingyao's count and the story behind it)

Yes, and the clearest case read is Ming Shanxi, the province inside the northern frontier. Its cities walled and rewalled themselves in three great waves, around 1449-1450, 1540-1543 and 1567-1570, and what drove each was a Mongol raid deep into the province. The works were the works of a city expecting a siege - enemy platforms (mamian), corner towers, crenellated parapets, a dredged moat - and in the 1540s wave the central basin, which the raiders had ravaged three years running, recorded a wall-building rate of 91 percent. Pingyao added 94 enemy platforms of brick in the 1567-1570 wave. Yuci faced its earthen wall in brick in 1541 and added 20 enemy platforms and 4 corner towers as it did. The counts that result vary widely: the prefectural city of Guide, in Henan, carries only 9 on its four sides, and a count may carry meaning as well as defense - Pingyao's 72 mamian are said to follow the 72 worthies among Confucius's disciples.

The rule the map follows: the three defense settings - SIEGE, GARRISON and PEACEFUL, set out at Wall towers - the mamian system and bowshot ranges - stand, and a city's setting follows the wars it has seen: a city that has been raided or besieged is towered densely, one long at peace sparsely. The direction is attested; the proportion - how many towers a given history buys - is this page's calibration, since no source read counts towers against sieges.
