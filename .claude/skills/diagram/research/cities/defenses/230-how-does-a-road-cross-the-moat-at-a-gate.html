The Chinese gate: a drawbridge. A complete Chinese city gate has, before it, a drawbridge and the tower that works it. At Xi'an the drawbridge crossed the moat outside the outermost gate; in peacetime it lay down and carried people and carts, and when an enemy came the soldiers in the tower over that gate hauled it up with iron chains and ropes, and the moat could not be crossed. The tower took its name from the bridge it controlled; in the early years of the Republic of China (after 1912) the outer works and their towers were pulled down so that fixed bridges could carry traffic over the moat. A Ming drawbridge was built of juniper or pagoda-tree timber and hung from the gate tower by iron rings and iron chains mixed with hemp rope. The county town had it too: at Zhenhai every gate had its drawbridge as well as its barbican. A fixed stone bridge at a city gate before modern times is on no page read.

The Japanese gate: earth or timber, by choice. At a Japanese castle the moat is crossed either by a dobashi - not a bridge at all but a strip of ground left standing when the moat was dug - or by a timber bridge. The choice was a trade: the causeway cannot be taken away in a hurry, which works against the defenders, while the timber bridge can, but large timber was very costly. So timber bridges tended to go to the rear gate and little-used gates that would be shut off entirely in need, and causeways to gates the garrison meant to sally from; an early-modern causeway was usually faced in stone and so wide that a person crossing it might not notice it was a bridge, and the main gate, built especially wide, was often reached by one. Across a water moat the causeway could hold the water higher on one side than on the other. Edo's Sakurada gate appears on a map of about 1608 as the Sakurada causeway.

How long and how wide. A crossing is as long as the moat is wide - about 66 ft at a provincial city (under what keeps the moat full) and 13 to 66 ft at a county town (under did a walled county town have a moat). How wide a drawbridge or a causeway was is on no page read.

The rule the map follows: every road gate crosses its moat, and the crossing is as long as the moat is wide. On the Chinese model it is a drawbridge in line with the outermost gate - before the barbican's own gate where there is one - drawn as a plank deck as wide as the gate passage, about 16 to 20 ft (a GUESS from the passage it serves), shown lowered. On the Japanese model a castle's main gate is reached by an earthen causeway, drawn as a strip of ground with stone faces about 30 to 40 ft wide (the width a GUESS; "very wide" is what the page says), and its rear gate by a timber bridge about as wide as the drawbridge; where a causeway crosses a wet moat the map may shade the water on its two sides differently. Which model a city follows is the setting's.
