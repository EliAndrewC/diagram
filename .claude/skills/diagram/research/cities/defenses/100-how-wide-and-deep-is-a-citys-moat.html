How wide and deep is a city's moat?

Sources: wujing-zongyao-wikisource (the Song compendium's rule: how far out, how wide, how deep), sohu-moat-history (the Ming treatise's floor for a moat's width and depth), chinese-units-enwiki (the Ming foot, to put the zhang in feet), kemingbaike-pingyao-gucheng (Pingyao's moat as rebuilt in 1370), han-changan-zhwiki and chinese-city-wall-enwiki (the Han capital's excavated moat, in two figures), zhengding-chengqiang-zhwiki (a prefectural seat's moat in zhang, undated), pingyao-chengqiang-zhwiki (Pingyao's moat, measured), thepaper-surviving-walls (the moats of Qufu, Kaifeng, Xi'an, Jingzhou, Xiangyang and Guide), xian-fortifications-enwiki (Xi'an's, measured), xiangyang-cheng-zhwiki (the widest moat read)

A Chinese walled city's moat took one of two forms. The common one is a dug ditch. The Northern Song's military compendium of 1044 digs it about thirty paces out from the wall and fixes no width: the width follows the ground, wide at the top and narrow at the bottom, dug down to the water, and the digging stops when arrows and thrown stones can hardly reach across. The Ming military treatise Wubei Zhi sets a floor: a city moat must be three zhang five chi wide, the wider the better, and one and a half or two zhang deep. On the Ming foot (chi) of 0.30 to 0.32 m, ten to the zhang, that is a moat at least about 35 ft across and 15 to 21 ft deep. Moats built in the period run below and above that floor. When the county town of Pingyao was rebuilt in 1370 its moat was one zhang deep and one wide, about 10 ft each way. The Western Han capital's excavated moat is 8 m wide and 3 m deep in one encyclopedia and 45 m wide and 4.5 m deep in another, about 26 ft or about 150 ft. Zhengding, a prefectural seat in the Ming, is given a moat over ten zhang wide and over two deep, about 100 ft by 20, in a sentence that names no period or survey. No gazetteer's figure for a larger city's moat could be read.

Measured today, on moats that survive and have often been restored, the ditch's width follows the size of the city it guards. Pingyao has a moat 4 m wide and 4 m deep, about 13 ft each way; Qufu's is about 3.3 m wide and deep and stands 7 to 10 m out from the wall. At the great cities the ditch is five to ten times as wide: Kaifeng's 16.66 m wide and 3.33 m deep, Xi'an's 18 m wide and 6 m deep, and Jingzhou's 30 m wide with 4 m of water. So the lesser ditches read are about 11 to 13 ft across, and the great cities' about 55 to 100 ft; the depth is 3 to 6 m, 10 to 20 ft, whatever the width. The two pages read disagree on Xi'an, the one giving 18 m by 6 m and the other 20 m by 10 m, and neither says which survey it follows.

The second form is a broad sheet of water rather than a ditch. Xiangyang's moat reaches 250 m at its widest, 130 to 250 m wide and only 2 to 3 m deep; Guide's - the old prefectural city at Shangqiu - lies 3.5 m outside the wall and runs from 25 m wide at its narrowest to 500 m at its widest, 1 to 5 m deep. These are lakes round a city more than trenches, and shallower than the dug ditch. What made these two so wide is on no page read; that it follows the ground a city stands on, low and wet beside a great river, is this page's GUESS. Both widths are present-day measurements, and no page read dates them: the one page puts Xiangyang's widest 250 m in the same sentence as the city's extension in the first years of the Ming without saying which moat it measures, and a page on Guide dates its wall and gates to the Ming and gives no date for the lake round them. No period figure for a moat of this form was found, so on the record it is attested only today.

The rule the map follows: the moat's width is a DEGREE set by the city's standing, calibrated to the period figures rather than to the moats measured today: about 10 ft at a county seat, as Pingyao's was in 1370, and at a provincial seat the Ming treatise's floor of about 35 ft, the width the map should draw there. The 55 to 100 ft of the great cities is measured on surviving moats, and the only older figures near it are Zhengding's undated ten zhang and a Han capital two encyclopedias measure differently, so a provincial seat's moat drawn 66 ft across rests on no period figure. That a county seat takes the lesser end and a provincial seat the greater is this page's calibration; the treatise sets one floor for every city. Its depth, 15 to 21 ft on the treatise, is not drawn. The broad water moat, shallow and hundreds of feet across, is attested only in present-day measurement, so the maps do not draw it.
