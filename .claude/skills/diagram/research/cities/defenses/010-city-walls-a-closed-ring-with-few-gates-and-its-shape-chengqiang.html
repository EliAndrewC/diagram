City walls: a closed ring with few gates, and its shape (chengqiang)

A walled Chinese city was enclosed all the way round by its wall (chengqiang), and the walls of important cities often took in far more ground than the city had built on. The wall was broken by only a handful of gates: a county city ordinarily opened four and a prefectural city eight, set on the four quarters. Before a gate there could stand a walled court, the barbican (wengcheng), and it was from there, not from small doors cut through the wall, that the defenders went out to fight.

In plan, the great Chinese walls were rectangles, and where the ground was rough they bent to follow it. Japanese castles set their walls at angles to the ground they stood on, and the outer enclosure of a whole castle town ran in a long loop over hill and shore. In both traditions a bigger wall was a bigger rectangle or a longer loop, not a rounder ring: the round walls the record found are a county town's and a clan's house. How high and thick a walled town's rampart stood is at How high and thick is a town's rampart, and what of it shows from above?, and the stone walls of a capital's castle at The castle in a capital: where it sits, how big it is, and its gates (shiro).

  • The walls of important cities took in far more ground than the city had built on.
    An encyclopedia of Chinese city walls gives the reasons: room for the city to grow, and timber and farmland secured in time of war. That the open ground inside also served as drill ground, or as room to shelter the countryside's inhabitants when an army came through, is this record's GUESS.
  • A county city ordinarily opened four gates, and a prefectural city eight.
    They were usually set on the four quarters, east, west, south and north, and the Song military compendium Wujing Zongyao prescribes four gates, two kept open and two shut.
  • Why were there so few gates?
    In this record's reading, because every gate is a place where the wall has to be held twice over, so defense was gathered into a few grand gates rather than spread over many small openings. It is a GUESS: the sources count the gates but do not give the reason.
  • The barbican court before a gate (wengcheng) was the defenders' staging ground for a sortie.
    A wengcheng is a small walled enclosure, semicircular or square, built outside a city gate to strengthen its defense. A specialist blog on Ming military technology describes it as roomy enough to pass many troops in or out at once, and as a protected staging ground for defenders who mean to fight outside the walls.
  • A European barbican's narrow neck stopped the defenders' sorties as well as the attackers' assault.
    The same blog contrasts the two: the neck of a European barbican keeps a mass of attackers from storming the gate, and by its narrowness also keeps the defenders from sallying out to meet them.
  • The wengcheng is named for a jar, but the surviving one at Suzhou is roughly square.
    Weng is a large jar with a small mouth and a wide belly, and the court takes its name from the jar's shape. The wengcheng between the two layers of Suzhou's Panmen land gate is roughly square in plan, about 177 m (~581 ft) round the inside, its wall 8.1 m (~27 ft) high on a base of dressed stone. Whether a town's gate had one as well, and how large, is at Did a town or city gate have a barbican, and how large was it?

A European curtain wall was pierced by sally ports, small doors from which the garrison slipped out to strike the besiegers. East Asian walls had three other kinds of opening that were not the main approach.

  • A Japanese castle's rear gate (karamete-mon) is named against its main front.
    A Japanese dictionary defines the karamete as a castle's rear gate, the opposite of the main front, the ote.
  • A Japanese castle's hidden postern (umon) was an escape route, kept blocked until it was wanted.
    A glossary of castle terms describes the umon as a gate quietly provided so the enemy would not notice it was an entrance, plain in form, cut as if hollowed out under the stone wall or the moat face, and built to be easy to fill in so that it could be kept impassable except when needed. It served, among other things, as the road by which the lord of the castle escaped.
  • A canal city's water gate (shuimen) was a grand gate in its own right.
    At Suzhou's Panmen the water gate stands beside the land gate, and is built of two layers of gate, inner and outer, 4.6 m (~15 ft) apart and 24.5 m (~80 ft) deep.
  • Were these three the East Asian equivalents of a sally port?
    Only in part, and the comparison is this record's own: no source we read makes it. The rear gate shares with a sally port that it is not the main approach; the umon's purpose, escape, is nearer a European postern's than a sally port's; and the water gate, doubled like a land gate, shares only that it is not the road in.

The shape of the ring is a separate matter. The Chinese tradition begins from a classical ideal of a square city.

  • The classical ideal capital is a square with three gates on each side.
    The Kaogongji, the ancient text on the crafts, lays out the capital nine li square, with three gates on each side, nine thoroughfares running each way, the ancestral temple on the left and the altar of the soil on the right, the court in front and the market behind.
  • Was the square city a picture of a square earth?
    Probably not. The cosmology usually given for it, round heaven and square earth, has no direct evidence behind it, a Chinese article on city walls argues, and the square was simply the easiest form to build.
  • The walls of Chinese capitals and great prefectural cities were rectangles.
    Beijing's and Xi'an's walls were built in the old style of a square or rectangle. Xi'an's is nearly rectangular, about 13.74 km (~8.5 miles) round and enclosing about 12 square km (~4.6 square miles). Suzhou's, on the Song city plan, is a rectangle long from north to south, with five gates, each a land gate and a water gate together, and a broad moat outside. Most imperial capitals and many important cities of the north had rectangular walls.
  • Where the ground was rough, a Chinese wall followed it.
    In rugged country the square was usually replaced by an irregular shape. Nanjing's Ming wall took a winding, free course set by the city's complicated ground, at the foot of a mountain with the river as its moat, and part of it stands on the south shore of Xuanwu Lake on the foundations of an older wall.
  • The government compounds inside a Chinese city (yamen) were laid out on an axis, in courtyards closed on four sides.
    A study of Song local yamen finds the central axis the backbone of the whole complex. The Huozhou department magistrate's yamen of the Qing dynasty faces south, its surviving buildings covering 18,700 square meters (~4.6 acres), with its gate tower, ceremonial gate, great hall and other buildings in a line on its axis; another surviving yamen has six courtyard groups and 85 rooms, laid out symmetrically.

Japanese castles took their angles from their ground and from the needs of flanking fire.

  • A Japanese castle's baileys (kuruwa) followed the ground.
    Castle layouts (nawabari) are classified by how their baileys are arranged. In the layout whose baileys run in a line (renkaku), the form almost always comes from dividing a ridge, a cape or a tongue-shaped terrace with ditches across it; in another, the inner bailey (honmaru) backs onto a lake, a mountain, a river or a cliff and is enclosed by other baileys on two or three sides.
  • A castle's rampart line was set with angles so its faces could be shot along from the flank.
    An angle in the line lets the defenders shoot arrows and fire guns into the enemy's flank, so that even a few weapons are used to effect.
  • Was a keep's stone base rectangular?
    No page we read gives the plan of the platform a keep (tenshu) stands on; that it was rectangular is this record's GUESS.
  • Nagoya castle's enclosures are rectangular and straight-lined.
    The castle's own guide calls them a simple composition of rectangles in straight lines, straighter and simpler than the complicated layouts of the castles of the Sengoku wars, which prized being hard to assault.
  • A castle town's outer enclosure (sogamae) ran in a long loop over the ground.
    At Odawara an earthen rampart and dry moat 9 km (~5.6 miles) long enclosed the whole town, from Mount Hachiman down to the seaward side. Its horseshoe shape, following the ground and neither square nor round, is this record's reading of that extent.

Why the corners survived in East Asia, when European builders turned to round towers, is a matter of how each tradition covered a corner's weakness.

  • In Europe, a square keep's corners were held to be its weak point.
    The corners of square keeps were in theory open to siege engines and to mining, and left dead ground the defenders could not shoot into, and the round design held military advantages over the square; but the weakness mattered little before the trebuchet and the crossbow came into use in the twelfth century. The source speaks of keeps; carrying it to a city wall's corners is this record's reading.
  • Chinese walls guarded their corners with corner towers.
    Large towers stood where two walls joined, well above the wall itself, giving the defenders a view over the city and the country round it. Along the straight runs between, projecting bastions (mamian) let the defenders shoot along the wall's face, as Wall towers - the mamian system and bowshot ranges sets out, and why those towers were square is at Why are the wall towers rectangular and never round?
  • Edo castle's outer enceinte had 26 gates with square gate courts (masugata).
    That is the count in the records of the shogunate's works office; the watch stations at Edo's castle gates were many more, 66 or 90 by various accounts.
  • Why did East Asia keep its corners and flank them, instead of rounding them?
    The classical ideal city was a square to begin with, and the square was the easiest wall to build. That rammed-earth formwork and stone facing (ishigaki) both want straight runs is this record's GUESS: no page we read says so.

The record found two round forms, and neither was a seat of government.

  • Shanghai's county wall was round.
    It was the town's first wall, raised in 1553 against the wokou pirates coming upriver, and its circular outline still shows on a map of 1933.
  • Why was Shanghai's wall round?
    A circle encloses the most ground for the length of its wall, so this record reads the round wall as a form of speed and economy, not of prestige. It is a GUESS: the page gives the date and the pirates, not the reason for the shape.
  • The round earthen houses of the Hakka (tulou) housed a whole clan, and most tulou are square.
    A tulou held one large clan of several generations, sometimes more than one, its rooms all of one size with none set above the rest; the square tulou is the commonest of these enclosed buildings. They are rural dwellings of the Hakka in the mountains of southeastern Fujian, built for four generations under one roof, dwelling and defense in one.
