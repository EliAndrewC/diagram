City defenses: the research behind the wall, gate and tower rules

The findings behind the wall, moat, gates and towers on these maps, the decisions they drove, and - where no generator draws the feature yet - the rules a map follows.

Does the city wall close a full ring - and why so few gates?

Sources: greatmingmilitary-fortification-gate (what the barbican's neck stops and what the wengcheng allows), suzhou-panmen-gov (the one surviving land-and-water gate, measured), karamete-kotobank (the rear gate and the front it is named against), touken-world-umon (the concealed postern and what it was for)

A provincial seat's rampart is a closed defensive loop rather than the cost-minimized skirt a town throws around whatever ground it happens to have built on. It runs its own regular circuit and encloses deliberate open ground - drill ground, room to shelter the countryside when a column comes through. Defense concentrates in a few grand gates instead of many small openings, because every opening is a place the wall has to be held twice over: the sortie a European curtain makes through a sally port is made here from the wengcheng, the walled barbican court set between an outer and an inner gate. A European barbican's narrow neck keeps a mass of attackers from storming the gate, and by the same narrowness stops the defenders sallying out to meet them; the wengcheng is roomy enough to pass a body of troops either way, and serves as protected staging ground for defenders who mean to fight outside the wall.13 It is a court rather than a jar: weng is an urn and the name is the urn's, but the surviving wengcheng at Suzhou's Panmen is roughly square in plan, about 580 ft around inside, its wall 27 ft high on a base of dressed stone.19

The nearest East Asian equivalents of a postern are the rear gate opposite the main approach (karamete-mon), the small walled-up postern (umon) and the water gate (shuimen) that a canal city has anyway. Each of the three is attested in itself. The karamete is a castle's rear gate, defined against the ote that is its main front.14 The umon is cut through the lower part of a stone wall or a moat face, kept impassable except when it is wanted, built so that it can be filled in easily, and meant among other things as the road by which the lord of the castle gets out - which is a sally port's own function.20 The shuimen is a defended opening in a Chinese city wall for a canal, and it is no small thing: Panmen's water gate runs 80 ft deep through two gate layers of stone arches, a grand gate in its own right, so what it shares with a sally port is only that it is not the main land approach.21 Setting the three of them against the European sally port as its nearest equivalents is this record's own comparison; no source makes it.

The rule the map follows: a provincial city's wall is a CLOSED ring with at least two gates, each a genuine gap in the rampart with a road running through it. The ring is regular, drawn as a defensive circuit rather than hugging the built-up ground, and the open ground inside it is left open on purpose. No map draws a postern or a sally gate.

What keeps the moat full?

Sources: xian-fortifications-enwiki (the one measured moat width read); the feeding below rests on this project's own reasoning about flow

A wet ditch has to be fed, and a trickle cannot hold one full against seepage and evaporation: the channel that supplies a moat carries something like what the moat itself holds. So a map that draws a full moat draws the watercourse that feeds it, running in from off the sheet, and draws it at the moat's own width rather than as a thread - a stream half a moat wide reads as a moat that must be nearly empty. A provincial seat's moat is taken here at about 66 ft across, which sits a little above the one measured example read: the ditch round Xi'an's Ming wall is 59 ft wide and 20 ft deep.15 Cities with moats commonly tapped them to water the farmland outside the wall, which a map may show and need not.

The rule the map follows: the moat is a closed ring pushed outward from the rampart, about 66 ft wide, with every point of the wall inside it. At least one watercourse reaches it from a map edge - the off-sheet source - and that feeder's width is within a quarter of the moat's. A stream may legitimately END at the moat instead of running off the far edge. Water draws as solid color with a lighter mid-current sheen and never with a dashed centerline: the dash is this sheet's mark for a road's lane, so putting one on water is a map drawing convention borrowed from the wrong vocabulary.

How wide is the opening a road passes through?

Sources: zhonghua-gate-enwiki (the four tunnels of the Nanjing gate, measured), xian-wall-zhwiki (the Xi'an tunnel, 6 m each way)

A gate NARROWS the road that passes through it; it never widens for it. A Ming provincial city's gate tunnel runs about 13-23 ft clear. Nanjing's Zhonghua gate passes four tunnels in line, whose clear widths measure 5.35, 4.97, 4.82 and 4.80 m - about 16 to 18 ft each.22 Xi'an's are 6 m in both height and width, about 20 ft, and 19.5 m deep, which is a passage rather than an opening.23 An Edo castle-town koraimon is narrower again, and no readable page gives its figure.16 So the opening is sized to pass the trunk road and no more.

The rule the map follows: a road gate is 30 ft of clear opening between masonry piers 15 ft across, so the gap in the rampart is 60 ft overall; the 30 ft is the width of the trunk road it passes. A water gate passes a 36 ft cargo canal with about 12 ft of pier each side, 60 ft clear. Every one of these is a length in real feet, so a town map drawn at one foot to the pixel is unaffected by the city's three. Shrinking the throat frees no ground for housing - it sits inside the wall gap and the patrol road's corridor - so no quarter has to be re-packed when it changes.

Gate structures - real footprints

Sources: xian-wall-zhwiki (98 mamian, 20 m wide, projecting 12 m;1 the wall 12 m high, base 15-18 m, top 12-14 m2)

  • What the research found. A gate guard duty room is a small 1-3 bay building (~15-35 ft); a gate inspection hall (sekisho/lijin bansho) ~25-45 ft; the wall's MAMIAN bastion (马面 - the projecting spur that carries the enemy-tower) is ~65x40 ft (Xi'an's are ~20 m wide projecting ~12 m),3 the tower building on it ~30-40 ft; a provincial GATE tower (城楼) ~52x30 ft; a corner tower ~50-66 ft (the gate-tower and corner-tower figures unsourced).

The rule the map follows: the buildings at a gate are drawn at their real sizes and not as fixed markers - a guard house about 34 by 20 ft, an inspection hall about 44 by 22 ft, a masonry gatepost 15 ft across by 24 ft along the opening, a gate tower about 52 by 30 ft, and the wall's own tower as a mamian bastion about 65 by 40 ft: a rectangle PROJECTING from the curtain, longer along the wall than deep, with the tower building set back on it. Only the mamian figure is attested. The provincial gate tower's is a GUESS, because no readable page gives one, and the guard house and the inspection hall are estimates from bay counts rather than measured buildings. Each is drawn at the legible upper end of its band and no further - a real value, never one inflated to read better; strokes keep their own legibility floor and the footprints take no license.

Gate furniture at the throat - barbican and tax-barrier practice

Sources: hakone-seki-jawiki (the 面番所 and 向番所 lists;4 that they stand opposite each other across the road is a GUESS - no readable page supports it),5 wengcheng-zhwiki (the barbican).6 chaoguan-zhwiki (the Ming-Qing customs barriers, which stood on the canals rather than at a county seat's gates)

  • What the research found (China first: wengcheng 甕城 barbican + lijin 厘金 / chaoguan 鈔關 tax barriers;9 Japan's Hakone/Arai sekisho as the best-preserved architectural proof). An inspection/tax barrier only works where traffic is forced single-file, and a walled city's gate passage is the ONE such chokepoint in the whole wall - set the station back and arrivals disperse into the street grid before ever reaching it, defeating its purpose. So guard + inspection cluster AT the gate opening, ~20-100 ft inside (the distance unsourced), flanking the road as it enters. Hakone is the worked example: just inside each gomon (gate) stood the Obansho, the papers-and-goods inspection office, and the ashigaru guardhouse, the two gates about eighteen meters apart. That the two faced each other across the road, so that nobody reached the town beyond without passing between them, is this record's own reading and is on no page read.8 In a wengcheng the whole cluster reads most authentically inside the urn-courtyard between the two misaligned gates.7 Setting the station back a few hundred feet along the wall would be historically wrong.

Chinese customs practice is not the anchor. The Ming-Qing 鈔關 customs barriers stood at commodity centers and on the Grand Canal and strategic routes, not at a county seat's gates;10 lijin is a separate, later Qing tax.28 The gate-throat cluster rests on the Hakone sekisho arrangement (the two stations facing each other is a GUESS - no readable page supports it)11 and the chokepoint reasoning, not on Chinese customs practice.

The rule the map follows: every gate carries a guard house, an inspection station and its own gate tower. The guard house and the inspection station take OPPOSITE flanks of the opening and face each other across the road, so that arrivals pass between them - an arrangement this project chose and a GUESS, since no readable page says the two stations at a barrier stood opposite one another. They are seated about 105-135 ft inside the opening, at its inner mouth; what the research supports is only "at the gate rather than set back along the wall", and the distance itself rests on no source. All three stand SQUARE to the wall and to the patrol road they line - turned to the wall's tangent where each actually sits rather than to the gate's own, which on a shallow slope has already curved away - within about 6 degrees, and are then pulled in radially onto the patrol road's centerline so the road runs lengthwise through them. A gate's tower stands AT its gate, flanking the opening, and never out among the wall's own bastions: where one flank is blocked it takes the other flank of the same opening rather than walking off along the curtain.

The rule the map follows: gate furniture never overlaps. On a small tight ring the two buildings converge as they walk out from the opening, and the inspection station steps further out, about 66 ft at a time, until the two rectangles clear each other. Their keep-clear apron is about 36 ft, which is enough because the road's own corridor already fends the housing off one flank. A fire-watch tower keeps 15 ft of daylight from a graveyard, the same clearance its braced footing keeps from wells and buildings.

Wall towers - the mamian system and bowshot ranges

Sources: xian-wall-zhwiki (the ~120 m mamian spacing, tied to bow and firearm range)

  • What the research found (China first: the mamian 马面 wall-tower system; Warring-States through Ming). Spacing the towers a bowshot apart IS the original design purpose of the projecting wall tower.29 Adjacent mamian bastions are spaced so their fields of flanking fire (侧射) overlap - an attacker at the curtain base between two towers is shot at from BOTH (Shen Kuo's 11th-c. Mengxi Bitan describes it as 矢石相及, "arrows and stones reach each other").30 The spacing that achieves ">= 2 towers everywhere" therefore equals the effective arrow range: put towers a bowshot apart and every curtain point has two within a bowshot. Real spacings bracket the effective ranges: Pingyao ~50-60 m (>= 2 everywhere, an aimed-lethal bowshot),31 Xi'an ~120 m (= 2 x 60 m; the crossfire covers the curtain but the base near a tower has only that one at full reach - the sparser, peacetime-affordable ring).12 Effective bow ranges: aimed-lethal ~60 m (a war arrow kills reliably), full war-bow reach ~100-150 m (harassing fire).32

The rule the map follows: how densely a city towers its wall is a per-city choice, because fortification tracked THREAT rather than a universal constant - a border city that has repelled sieges was towered as hard as it could afford, a city at peace for centuries was not. Three settings, each naming how far a tower's fire reaches and how many towers must cover every point of the curtain. A SIEGE city: two towers within 197 ft (60 m, the aimed-lethal bowshot), so bastions stand about a bowshot apart and the wall wears a dense necklace of them, which is the correct read for a border fortress. A GARRISON city, the default: two towers within 328 ft (100 m, full war-bow reach), a looser ring. A PEACEFUL city: one flanking tower within 197 ft everywhere, the midpoints between towers still covered by two, which comes out at roughly 394 ft (120 m) between towers. Only that last spacing is attested; the ranges the first two rest on are unsourced, and the dense siege ring is named for a spacing at Pingyao that no readable page gives. The SPACING is honest to whichever setting a city is given - what is a guess is the range figure the setting is calibrated against.

The rule the map follows: coverage is judged along the whole curtain, about every 54 ft, and a tower counts as reaching 36 ft past its recorded center, because an archer shoots from the bastion's parapet SPAN and not from a point. Four stretches are left out of that reckoning, each because it is already the most defended ground on the wall and no tower is seated there: within 390 ft of a gate, within 165 ft of a gate's guard buildings, within 135 ft of a water gate's opening - a grated passage flanked by its own two towers - and within 186 ft of a ward gate where a neighborhood fence meets the rampart, which is a manned chokepoint. Those exemptions MIRROR where towers are seated, deliberately: ground that will structurally never be towered must not be ground the coverage rule demands, or no map can ever satisfy it. Beneath all of it sits a coarse floor - at least six towers, and no bare stretch spanning more than about 75 degrees of the ring. The towers also keep a RHYTHM: no gap between two open-curtain bastions falls below seven tenths of that map's own median gap, the flanking pairs at gates and water gates excepted, because mamian stand at regular intervals and a doubled-up pair reads as a mistake. The three bad pairs measured 0.58 to 0.60 of the median, while every sound gap on every drawn city sits at 0.87 or above. And each tower is turned SQUARE to the wall where it stands, within about 15 degrees: a tower on a 30 degree stretch slants 30 degrees rather than sitting as an axis-aligned box.

Why are the wall towers rectangular and never round?

Sources: sinoss-cao-city-defense (what the wall's projections are for), jah-song-military-cities (the Song corner tower, curved on purpose), xian-wall-zhwiki (three square corners and one round one at Xi'an), qq-xian-southwest-corner (the other dating of that round corner)

Along the curtain, rectangular - and the reason is the defender's volume of fire. A mamian is a platform built one after another against the outer face of the wall, so placed that a besieger at the foot of it is struck from two sides at once,25 and the spurs stand about a bowshot apart so that no stretch of the wall's base is out of reach. Flat faces let archers line up along them and shoot straight down the wall, which a curved face does not.

The corner is the other case, and it goes the other way. A study of the Northern Song military compendium finds the dituan - the tower standing where the wall turns, or at the angle of a barbican - deliberately arc-shaped, made so "in order to increase the difficulty of the enemy's attack and to reduce the area exposed to attack", a form its author finds in no Han or Tang text and takes to be a Song creation.24 That is the reason Europe gives for its own round towers, reached separately, so a curved tower is not a European signature, and a round corner on a Chinese wall does not by itself say "an older piece of wall". Xi'an is the case that looked as though it did: three of its four corner towers are square and the southwest one is round, following the building that stood on the spot before it.26 Even there the century is disputed - the encyclopedia account puts the inherited round platform in the Yuan, a popular one in the imperial city of Sui-Tang Chang'an, and the two agree only that a later rebuild went around an older corner.27

Japanese yagura and tenshu are square or rectangular timber on stone bases, and the one thing Japan curves is the batter of the ishigaki wall rather than the towers standing on it. No readable page says so.17

The rule the map follows: every wall and gate tower draws as a RECTANGLE. The mamian spur is wider along the wall than deep, about 1.7 to 1; the tower building set on it, and the gate towers, run nearer square, so no ratio is forced there. The CORNER is where the drawn map and the record part company: the record finds a Song city's corner towers arc-shaped on purpose, at the turn of the wall and at a barbican's angle, and these maps square every corner along with the rest of the ring. No map draws a round tower.

How far inside the wall does the patrol road run, and what may stand on it?

Sources: none cited here - the follow-the-wall street rests on general reading; the area cost is this project's own measurement

A fortified city keeps a clear lap of ground inside its rampart so that troops can be moved along the wall to wherever they are wanted - the Chinese follow-the-wall street, 順城街 (this rests on general reading; no source is cited).18 The whole value of it is that it is unobstructed. Pulling the built ground in off the wall costs a city about one perimeter row of buildings, roughly eight percent of the ground it can build on, so a map that adds the patrol road makes that back somewhere - usually a small rise in density in one or two quarters - rather than quietly housing fewer inhabitants than the city is declared to hold.

The rule the map follows: a patrol road runs as a closed loop about 102 ft inside the rampart and about 20 ft wide, and the quarters pack inside IT rather than up to the wall. Nothing may stand on its bed: no house, no ministry, no governor's compound, no temple, no field. What legitimately lines it is the wall's own furniture - the gate guard houses, the inspection stations, the mural towers - and a ward fence may cross it, gated. Housing is held off by a no-build corridor along the loop, half the road's width plus 63 ft, so that even a large or turned footprint's nearest corner stays well clear of the bed. Unlike an ordinary city street it is exempt from the rule that a street must be built up, its wall side being bare by design. A street or an alley running at it reaches its bed and ENDS there - neither stopping a sliver short in open ground nor poking a stub out the far side - while a genuine crossroads that carries well past is a different thing and is fine.

Where does a neighborhood fence stop when it reaches the rampart?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-27; this project's measurement of the three drawn cities

The GM looked at a drawn city and said: "the neighborhood walls stick out the other side of the city walls" (2026-07-27). This is the wall member of a family the roads and the watercourses on these maps already had, and the one nobody had stated: where two linear features meet, ONE of them ends at the junction. A lane stops at the through-lane it reaches instead of poking a stub out the far side; a channel joins another at a T or a Y instead of running across it; a ward fence meeting the rampart is the same junction. Here the reason is physical rather than cartographic. The rampart is what SEALS the ward - the fence's ends abut solid wall so that the ward gate cannot simply be walked around - so a palisade continuing out through the rampart encloses nothing on the far side, and draws as two walls crossing at an intersection rather than as one wall closing against another.

Why a fence can overshoot invisibly. comfortably inside that tolerance. The overshoot existed only in the drawn INK, and it is small numbers all the way down: the rampart's stroke covers only its own half width, about 16 ft, while the fence is stroked with a ROUND cap that inks about 7 ft past its last recorded corner. Fifteen plus seven against sixteen left a tan nub outside the rampart - at three feet to the pixel, about 6 ft of palisade standing on the moat berm. So what is measured is the drawn TIP rather than the recorded corner, and both stroke widths are read from the drawing's own records, so that what is drawn and what is judged cannot drift apart. Interior corners are measured too: a fence that dives out through the wall and back mid-run is the same crossing, just further from its end.

The drawing does the work, so a map never has to aim. Getting this right by hand was hopeless - all three drawn cities were three to fifteen feet out at a city's scale and none of it was visible while authoring - so each fence end is snapped onto the wall's centerline before it is drawn, trimmed or extended ALONG ITS OWN LAST SEGMENT to where that line crosses the ring. That is the same rule a lane follows when it is extended to the through-lane it should meet: along its own axis, never diagonally to the nearest point, because a perpendicular snap kinks the last stretch of fence off the line it was drawn on. A last segment running parallel to the wall never meets it, and falls back to the nearest point on the ring. And an end further than 72 ft from the wall is left exactly where it was put: that is not a junction at all but a fence failing to reach the rampart, which is a real hole in the ward, and silently dragging it two hundred feet would hide it.

The rule the map follows: a ward fence ENDS where it meets the city wall - on the wall's centerline, snapped along its own last segment, with no ink of it crossing to the outside, and its interior corners held to the same rule. An end further than 72 ft from the rampart is left where it is and reported as a ward that does not close, never dragged onto the wall.
