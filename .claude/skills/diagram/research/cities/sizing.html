Sizing a city: the space budget and the population rule

The findings behind the size of a walled city's ring and the density of its quarters on these maps, the decisions they drove, and - where no generator draws the feature yet - the rules a map follows.

How big is a city's wall for the population inside it?

Sources: this project's measurements of the three drawn provincial cities; the GM's ruling of 2026-07-26; the setting's own budget notes for the caste table. The historical shares the budget is checked against - a county seat's street share, its unbuilt reserve and its civic share - are on the urban-fabric page.

Every settlement mode on these maps grows from a first principle: a village from the way water runs, a manor from a declared square footage. A walled city's is the space budget. The order matters more than it looks. Guessing a ring and then grinding placements until they fit produces cities that every aggregate measure accepts and a reader does not: the first Nagahara looked fully finished while about a sixth of its interior - five pockets, some of them seven acres apiece, about 24 acres in all - was open ground nothing had asked for. An aggregate treats open ground as "still fillable" rather than as unjustified, so the emptiness never registers as a fault. The order is therefore inverted: compute the ground the city needs, derive the ring from it, and hold the drawn map to that promise.

What the ground costs, per household. The costs are measured at the footprints the maps actually DRAW, legibility floors included, because budgeting true real-world square footage would undersize the wall for the map that has to be drawn inside it. A packed row household costs about 0.14 acre gross - Tango's healthy quarters run 0.09 to 0.17 acre - and a detached samurai house about 0.51 acre, measured at 0.60 acre in the samurai ward itself. Gross means the roof plus its share of eaves, alley and margin, which is why the ratio between the two is about 3.6 to 1 where the drawn footprints are closer. Both are calibrations against the drawn exhibits, not figures from a source, and the pair reproduces Tango's accepted ring to within a fifth of a percent, which is the whole argument for trusting them elsewhere. About two thirds of a city's samurai households live inside the wall; the rest hold estates outside it.

The civic program is a FLOOR, not a per-capita share. A seat of 2,000 inhabitants still carries its yamen, its six ministries, its temples, its theater and its gate furniture, so the civic ground is itemized rather than scaled: at Tango's measured compounds it comes to about 13 acres, near 9% of the interior. A county seat's civic ground historically ran about a tenth of the enclosure, in a band of 5 to 15% (the record was not checked for this figure; no source is cited). The temple part of that program is declared, not fixed: temple count is not a program floor, because a Fox city runs eight small precincts where an ordinary clan runs two great ones. Minami declares eight precincts at about 0.70 acre each plus an itemized uplift for its Inari ground, which puts its civic program at about 15.5% of the interior - the top of the historical band, and the honest signature of a city with four times the usual temples rather than a number to fudge.

What circulation and reserve take off the top. Streets, lanes and alleys are priced at 7% of the interior at the widths the maps draw, measured at 6.8% and 7.0% on the two shipped cities. The historical envelope is higher - roughly a tenth to a fifth of the walled ground, and normally a quarter to a third of the enclosure left deliberately unbuilt, both on the urban-fabric page - so the map-calibrated figure is a deliberate departure: the budget has to price what the map actually draws, and deep blocks with alley warrens sit at the sparse end of that envelope. Roominess is credited only where it is DECLARED and DRAWN as something: an in-wall agricultural district takes a further 15% of the interior, and a drill ground or a garden is entered by name. A city that wants the roomier historical character opts in legibly; it can never launder slack.

Then the ring is solved, not chosen. The wall is the ellipse-like polygon that encloses exactly the required interior - the twenty-sided ring the map actually draws, whose area is a few percent under the smooth ellipse through the same radii, so solving for the ellipse would make every wall systematically small. The radii are never picked by hand.

The rule the map follows: a walled city's ring is computed from its program BEFORE anything is drawn - the households' ground, the civic floor, the water and the declared extras, divided by what circulation and reserve take out - and the drawn ring is the polygon that encloses that area. The drawn interior then stays within +8% and -5% of the budget: more than 8% over is the empty-space fault, and the answer is a smaller ring or an itemized line that declares and draws the extra ground; more than 5% under means the program does not fit, and the answer is a larger ring or a smaller program. Under is the tighter bound because an undersized wall breaks the packing immediately. Both tolerances are calibrated against two drawn cities rather than derived from a source: the accepted Tango encloses about a quarter of a percent under its budget, and the rejected first Nagahara about 21% over.

Does the map draw a house for every household the figure promises?

Exactly one, and there is no allowance. A declared population used to be accepted anywhere within 7% of the figure, and a band like that is how a city ends up quietly smaller than the number on its own placard: Minami was signed off at 486 dwellings against a target of 520 and read as finished. The GM ruled on 2026-07-26:

if we have a target population with math indicating a target number of dwellings we must ALWAYS meet that number EXACTLY.

A declared population is a promise about what the map CONTAINS, so the band is gone. When the ground will not take the houses, grow the WALL - from the budget - and never trim the declared figure to fit the layout. That is the same instruction the sizing model already gives, since an undersized ring is a wall fault; removing the tolerance only closes the escape hatch of shrinking the population instead. Where the budget is genuinely under-counting, the budget is what gets corrected: Minami's temple-precinct line was pricing a precinct's hall compound rather than the ground the drawn precinct occupies, and both Minami and Nagahara carry a measured excess of laneway because the flat 7% circulation allowance does not cover what they actually draw. A filling pass that stalls short is a wall too small, which is a budget question and not a population question.

The rule the map follows: a city draws exactly one dwelling for every five inhabitants of its declared population, with no tolerance in either direction, and counts only the dwellings INSIDE the wall. The count includes every dwelling the figure covers - the adept-monk households beside each temple precinct, and, where a city has an agricultural district, its in-wall farmhouses (a local tally that omitted them overshot Tango by 15 houses). The filling pass asks one caste at a time for precisely the shortfall, smallest footprints first because those are what still fit once the quarters are full, and stops the moment that caste reaches the figure asked; no caste may pass its own band of plus or minus 30% on the way (filling smallest-first pushed Tango's servants to 159 against a ceiling of 156). No commoner household - laborer, servant, burakumin or merchant - stands outside the wall: only samurai country estates, farmhouses, the wharf shops and the gate-market shops are legitimately extramural, so a city can never meet its figure by spilling.

How densely is a quarter built, and what counts as empty ground?

Sources: this project's measurements of Tango and of the rejected draft of Nagahara; the GM's rulings of 2026-07-22 and 2026-07-23. The density contrast between a wealthy quarter and a laborer warren is on the urban-features page.

The measurement that decided it. Density is a SPATIAL property, and the proof is worth stating plainly: Tango (accepted) and the rejected draft of Nagahara had the same in-wall dwelling count - 521 against 525 - and the same median block density, about 2.2 dwellings per acre. A city-wide average, or a check that samples an anonymous grid, literally cannot tell a good city from a broken one. The only difference between them was WHERE the empty ground sat: Nagahara's east was dense, its northwest temple quarter was empty, and about 35 commoner dwellings had spilled outside the walls. So the map declares its quarters - each a named region with a role, residential, civic, mixed or reserve - and density is judged one quarter at a time.

The band, and why it is wide. A residential or mixed quarter holds between about 1.5 and 11 dwellings per acre. That is a sevenfold span, and it is deliberate: a commoner warren packs far denser than a samurai or official ward, since the wealthy quarter spreads into courts while the laborer rows pack tight and uniform. The numbers at the ends are calibrations against the drawn cities rather than historical measurements. A quarter's density is measured over its NON-civic ground - the quarter's area less the civic compounds standing in it - so that a government ward's yamen does not make the ward read as under-built.

Every open is claimed by something. A wall encloses what it must defend, and an oversized ring with a wide empty perimeter would not have been built. A city keeps plenty of open ground, but every deliberate open is CLAIMED by a feature: a right of way, a field, a working yard. Ground claimed by nothing, sitting on wall-protected premium land, would not have been left bare, and that is the fault the map has to be able to see.

Finding it takes footprints, not centers. The GM caught a bare pocket just inside Tango's north gate, roughly 690 by 285 ft, that the first test passed: it sampled a coarse grid and counted a cell as used if it lay within 360 ft of any building's CENTER, a radius so wide that a single house sanitized a disc 720 ft across, so only vast voids could ever register. The rebuilt test counts every claiming feature at its real FOOTPRINT - buildings, compounds and groves, field and ground polygons, wells, stable yards and shrine gates, the rights of way of roads, streets, alleys, the ring road and water, ward fences, the rampart with its patrol strip, the pond - marks any cell about 96 ft square that lies 60 ft clear of all of them as dead ground, and fails a contiguous dead cluster larger than about 0.83 acre inside the core. The threshold is a calibration and not a finding: the pocket that prompted it measured about 1.3 acres of dead core, while the largest legitimate opens anywhere else in the pool measured about 0.42 and 0.21 acre, so the line sits between them with roughly a factor of two of headroom each way.

What to do with a pocket, in preference order. Fill the ground - extend a quarter, or drop a neighborhood into it - or CLAIM it as deliberate working ground. The standing easy claim is a stable yard: beaten earth, hitching rails and ground litter, which keeps clear of roads, fields, water, the rampart and every footprint, and which reads to the eye as a real feature rather than as a check being satisfied. Extra animal ground is the natural claim where a pocket adjoins a gate or the stables, which is how Tango's north gate got one: the caravan traffic coming down from and departing toward the Phoenix border wants marshaling room there.

And a thin quarter is FILLED, not compensated for elsewhere. Nagahara's first remedy densified the east, which was already dense - that satisfies an average and fixes nothing. The honest fix was to build the empty northwest itself, as a pocket of commoner housing in front of the temple: a temple neighborhood was crowded, not quiet, because the approach to a great temple carried the shops and inns that served it (this rests on general reading; no source is cited). Both cities then read as fully built for the same reason rather than by two different arithmetics.

The one quarter that draws nothing (GM, 2026-07-22). A reserve quarter is normally drawn as what it is - a drill ground as bare earth with a dashed muster perimeter, a garden as green sward - so that its open ground reads as deliberate rather than accidental. An agricultural district is the exception: it draws nothing of its own. Its field combs, its in-wall farmhouses and its label already carry the visual, and the faint dashed boundary it used to add read as a stray dotted line cutting through the farmhouses and across the Imperial road above the burakumin neighborhood. The district is still declared, so the per-quarter judging is unaffected.

The rule the map follows: a walled city declares quarters that cover at least 85% of its interior, overlap by no more than 5% and spill no more than 3% outside the wall, each quarter naming its role - residential, civic, mixed or reserve - and a reserve quarter naming its kind as well (drill ground, garden, agricultural district). A drill ground is drawn as bare earth inside a dashed muster perimeter and a garden as green sward; an agricultural district draws no boundary of its own.

The rule the map follows: every residential or mixed quarter carries between about 1.5 and 11 dwellings per acre, measured over the quarter's ground less the civic compounds inside it. A purely residential quarter additionally holds no dead zone: its largest empty pocket stays under about 450 ft across from any dwelling, counting wells and civic compounds as ground served, so that a half-dense, half-empty quarter fails even when its average is in band. A mixed quarter is exempt from that, because it legitimately holds a civic forecourt.

The rule the map follows: a civic quarter may be as much as 70% open - a yamen or a temple precinct is mostly courtyard and garden - but it must actually HOLD its compounds; open and structureless reads as merely empty. Reserve ground of every kind together stays at or under a fifth of the interior: civic buildings alone are only a few percent of a county seat, the big open consumers are the drill ground and an under-built garden or farm, and a fifth is already generous against the quarter-to-a-third of unbuilt ground the historical record allows. Past that the ring encloses more open ground than a provincial seat justifies and the answer is a smaller ring; emptiness is never laundered by declaring it reserve.

The rule the map follows: no contiguous patch of the city's core larger than about 0.83 acre stands 60 ft clear of every claiming feature at once. Ground that would otherwise be bare is either built on or given to a drawn working feature - most often a stable yard of beaten earth with hitching rails - which is then a feature of the map with a name, not a gap.
