Why does an unwalled seat draw no fire tower?

Sources: jinya-kotobank (what an intendant's compound held, and the town that grew round it), jishinban-jawiki (the ladder and bell on the watch house, and the gear inside), hinomi-yagura-jawiki (the lookout raised on a watch house, and how far the towers spread), hyoshigi-jawiki (the night round and its call), tensuioke-jawiki (the stored water), dozo-jawiki (what makes a plastered storehouse fireproof), gogura-jawiki (the store the tax rice passed through), takayama-jinya-city (what survives on the ground of the one surviving provincial office); the GM's question of 2026-07-24. Where a claim below carries no note, it rests on this project's general reading and has not been checked against a page a reader can open.

Because it confirms the line the fire-tower question draws between the walled city and the open town, rather than breaking it. The GM asked on 2026-07-24 whether an unwalled tax-administration seat should carry a tower. Its real-world analog is the jin'ya or daikansho town, and the identification is the published one: the official residence in a district administered by a shogunal district governor or intendant went by the name daikan-jin'ya, its grounds held the intendant's own residence, the office for public business, the houses of the subordinate staff, the rice store and the levy store, and a judgment yard and jail; where a settlement grew up around such a compound and became a town it was called a jin'ya-machi, and often flourished as the central town of the territory administered. On this record's reading, that town's fire posture was defensive rather than observational, and the neighborhood watch house is where its apparatus stood. Many watch houses were fitted with a ladder on the roof - a small-scale fire tower, in the account's own words - and a hansho bell, and the firefighting gear was kept inside: the company standard, the fire hook, the hand pump, the buckets, ladders and well-buckets, so that when the bell was struck the town officers and the firefighting hands ran to the watch house, took the gear out, assembled and went to the fire together. Where a frame was raised on the watch house so that the watchman could look over the whole town, that raised platform IS the hinomi-yagura. The night round was walked calling "mind your locks, mind your fire" and striking clappers, though the bodies the readable account names for it are modern ones. Water was stored against fire in the tensuioke, the traditional cistern that held rainwater and was used mainly as urban firefighting water in the Edo period. The plastered kura is the fire-resistant form: storehouses are named for what they hold, a rice store among them, and are built to protect it from fire, damp and theft, their walls often 300 mm thick or more and their outer doors sometimes faced in earth and plaster, so that in the great fires of the Edo period there were many cases in which the fire never reached the inside. The tax rice had a store of its own, the gogura, one to a village or to several, a holding place for the rice on its way out and kept under strict guard. That losing the tax rice was the career-ending disaster, and that the storehouse was therefore fireproofed rather than the horizon watched, is this record's own reading of those facts. Two pieces of the posture rest on nothing readable: the local temple bell doubling as the alarm, and any of this standing at the daikansho OFFICE rather than at the town's watch post. Takayama Jin'ya, the Hida daikansho, is the one such office of more than fifty still standing, and no fire ever took it; what its ground keeps is the trace of a single water-supply pond, and a grain storehouse framed in Keicho-era hinoki that is among the foremost in the country. The freestanding tower itself is no late arrival: it was built up together with the firefighting system, beginning with Edo in the Edo period, and by the early Showa era towers stood in almost every region of the country. What that dates is the SPREAD, and the reading this record takes from it is its own: the institution starts in the shogunal capital and reaches the ordinary countryside last, so an unwalled country seat of the period we draw stands at the beginning of that spread rather than at its end.

So on an unwalled seat the fire story is carried instead by the kura on the intendant's compound, the headman's house and the merchants' houses; a small hansho bell-frame at the intendant's compound would be the one defensible extra glyph, and drawing nothing at all is equally accurate. Who keeps the watch where a tower does stand is the fire-tower question's, not this one's.
