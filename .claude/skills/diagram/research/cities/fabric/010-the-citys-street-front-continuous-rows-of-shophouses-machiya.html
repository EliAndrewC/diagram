The city's street front: continuous rows of shophouses (machiya)

In a Japanese city of the Edo period, before 1868, the commoners did not build houses standing apart in their own yards. The townsmen's houses stood along the street with no gaps between them, so that a shopping street was a continuous front of shophouses (machiya), pierced only by the gates and alleys that led to the row houses behind. A Kyoto shophouse commonly fronted about 2 ken, some 3.6 m (~12 ft), on a lot 10 to 12 ken, about 18 to 22 m (~59-72 ft), deep.

Behind the street, most of the townspeople and craftsmen rented rooms in row houses (nagaya) down the back lanes. The house standing alone in its walled yard belonged to the samurai.

  • Townsmen's houses stood side by side along the street, with no gaps between them.
    In a castle town, the townsmen's district had less ground to a household than the samurai district, and its houses stood built up along the highway with no gaps; unlike a farmhouse, whose main building stands deep inside a gated lot, the shophouses stood in a fairly even row facing the street. The same article shows the merchant houses of Seki, a post station on the Tokaido highway, standing eave to eave.
  • Adjoining shophouses raised a fire wall (udatsu) between them.
    Where shophouses stood adjoining and were built continuously, a fire wall came to be built between them, to stop a fire in the house next door from spreading across. The part of the roof raised a little between one house and its neighbor came to be called the udatsu, and in its full form the gable-end wall where it met the neighboring house was raised and given a small roof of its own. Each was a separate house built against the next.
  • Shophouse lots were narrow at the street and deep behind it.
    Many Kyoto shophouses are said to have fronted around 2 ken (~12 ft) and run 10 to 12 ken (~59-72 ft) back, and the Japanese Wikipedia article on the Kyoto shophouse describes its frontage as narrow and its depth as deep. The 2 ken is the ordinary Kyoto shop's; how frontage grew with wealth, and what a Chinese shop measured, is at How wide is a merchant's shophouse, and how does wealth change it?
  • Why were the lots so narrow?
    The popular answer is a tax. Toyotomi Hideyoshi, it is said, taxed houses by the width of their frontage, so townsmen built narrow and deep to save on it. The Japanese Wikipedia article on the Kyoto shophouse disputes this: in the Edo period, Kyoto's tax was first assessed as a total on each town, whatever its frontages, and the house-owning townsmen then divided it among themselves, so much a house. The continuous street front stands either way; it does not rest on the tax.
  • The back-lane row houses were single buildings divided into homes.
    In the dense cities of the Edo period, merchant houses of middling size and above kept their own shops on the main street, while almost all the other townspeople and craftsmen rented rooms in row houses on the back streets. A row house was one building divided along its length, each part a separate home, the homes sharing their walls.
  • A gate and an alley between two shops led to the row houses behind.
    The way in and out of the back row houses was a wooden gate and an alley set between two street-front shops.
  • The lanes between back row houses were about 3 to 6 shaku (~3-6 ft) wide, with a drain down the middle.
    A lane (roji) between one back tenement building and the next was about 3 to 6 shaku, about 0.9 to 1.8 m (~3-6 ft), wide. Drain boards ran down its middle over a small ditch 18 to 21 cm (~7-8 in) wide, which took both the waste water from the well-side and the rain off the row houses' roofs, and the lane widened where a shared well and water supply stood.
  • Samurai households lived in walled compounds, and their lowest foot soldiers in row houses.
    A samurai residence was enclosed by a hedge, a wall, a gate and the like. The lower foot soldiers (ashigaru) lived not in such a residence but in row houses of their own.
  • Did fire policy space the shophouses apart?
    Not on any page we read. After the Great Meireki Fire of 1657, the shogunate set aside open firebreak lots (hiyokechi) in Edo, and broad streets made for the same purpose, but these were a handful of sites - five burned-over lots after the fire, thirteen by the Kyoho era (1716-1736) - and the page on them names no rule spacing individual houses apart. From 1722, Edo ordered tile roofs and earth-plastered walls instead.
