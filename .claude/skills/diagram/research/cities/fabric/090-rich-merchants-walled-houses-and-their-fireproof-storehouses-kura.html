Rich merchants' walled houses and their fireproof storehouses (kura)

The richest merchants of a Japanese town before 1868 lived in great shop-houses at the street edge: the shop at the front, the living quarters behind it, a row of earthen storehouses at the back of the lot, and a wall round the rest. The storehouses were built of earth against fire, and in Kyoto custom put them at the back of the lot, never on the street; in Osaka they stood so thick along the backs of the lots that they served as a firebreak. Two such houses survive with their figures. One in Kyoto fronts its street for about 70 ft; one in the castle town of Matsusaka grew, lot by lot, to about 200 ft. One kept three storehouses, the other five.

A gatehouse with rooms to either side was not a merchant's to build at will: it was a mark of rank, laid down for warriors by their house rank; in the villages it was permitted only to farmers who held office, and in the towns it is found on the houses of town elders. Across a whole city the storehouses were far fewer than the houses: Osaka, probably the best guarded of Japan's cities against fire, had at least one storehouse to every ten houses.

  • The Sugimoto house in Kyoto, a kimono-cloth merchant's, is among the largest town houses left in the city.
    The Agency for Cultural Affairs describes it as a large-scale town house surviving in the city, and a Kyoto guide as a kimono-cloth dealer's house of the largest class.
  • How large is the Sugimoto site?
    The two pages that give it do not agree. A Kyoto guide gives a frontage of eleven and a half ken, about 70 ft, and a site of about 1,200 square meters (~12,900 square feet), more than half of it garden. A second page gives 30 m (~98 ft) of frontage by 52 m (~171 ft) of depth, which would make it about 1,560 square meters (~16,800 square feet).
  • The Hasegawa house in Matsusaka belonged to a cotton wholesaler with a shop in Edo.
    The Agency for Cultural Affairs counts the family among the leading merchants of the castle town.
  • The Hasegawa main house is about 99 ft long and 52 ft deep.
    The Agency gives it as 30.2 m (~99 ft) by 15.9 m (~52 ft).
  • The Hasegawa house grew by buying the lots beside it.
    After the main house was built, the family bought the lots on both sides of it one after another, until by the end of the Edo period its frontage on the street was about 34 ken, some 61 m (~200 ft).
  • How wide was a great merchant's frontage?
    From about 70 ft (eleven and a half ken; ~98 ft on the second Kyoto page's figure) to about 200 ft. The larger of the two stood in a castle town, not in the great city, so in these two houses at least the size followed the family's fortune rather than the rank of its town.
  • How many great merchant houses did a small town hold?
    No page we read counts them for a town of about 1,200 inhabitants.
  • Both houses face their streets.
    The Sugimoto house occupies a broad estate facing Ayanokoji street, and the Hasegawa site faces Uomachi street.
  • The Sugimoto house is a shop at the street front, with its living quarters behind and a high wall round the estate.
    The shop and the living quarters are joined by the entrance hall, the three storehouses stand in a row behind the main house, and a high wall runs round the estate.
  • In Kyoto a storehouse was never built on the street side.
    The custom was to put it at the back of the lot, the opposite of Edo, where the shop-house itself was built as an earthen storehouse.
  • The Sugimoto house's three storehouses stand in a hook toward the north of the main house.
    A Kyoto guide places them so; they are said to have survived the city's great fire of 1864.
  • The Sugimoto storehouses, all built in the Edo period, are squares of about 20, 16 and 14 ft.
    They are two-story earthen storehouses of about 38 square meters (~405 square feet), 24 square meters (~253 square feet) and 19 square meters (~205 square feet). All three were built by 1822, so the sizes are the Edo period's own.
  • The Hasegawa house has four storehouses at the back and a fifth at the front, beside the entrance.
    Passing through the entrance, the front storehouse is on the left; at the back stand four more side by side.
  • The Hasegawa lot runs back to the block's drain, which is also the ward's boundary.
    Behind the storehouses flows the drain the backs of the lots share, the boundary of the ward; the garden beyond it is land the family bought later.
  • The city's list of the Hasegawa house's protected buildings names a front wall and an inner gate, and no gatehouse.
    The city's list of what the designation covers names a front wall, an inner gate and inner wall, and a garden wall, among the main house and five storehouses.
  • How big was an ordinary storehouse?
    The commonest size an architect's survey of one village district found, 2.5 by 3 ken, about 15 by 18 ft, falls in the same range as the Kyoto house's (How big was a farm storehouse?). But the same survey says the district's houses came to own storehouses probably only from about the end of the shogunate, so the Edo period's sizes rest on the Kyoto house, not on it.
  • How far apart did a merchant's storehouses stand?
    Both houses set theirs in a row, but no page we read gives a distance between them or a rule for one.

The one count of storehouses for a whole premodern city that we found is Osaka's, from its great fire of 1724.

  • How many storehouses did Osaka have when it burned in 1724, in the Edo period?
    At least one to every ten houses. A one-sheet print issued after the city's fire of 1863 records that the fire of 1724 destroyed some 28,000 houses, 98,700 hearths and 2,800 earthen storehouses; a hearth in such a count is a household. That is about one storehouse to every ten houses, or to every 35 households.
  • A standard encyclopedia's account of Osaka's fire of 1724 gives smaller counts, but the same share of storehouses to houses.
    It gives 11,765 houses, 60,292 households and 1,097 earthen storehouses burned, over about two-thirds of the city. That is again about one storehouse to every ten or eleven houses, but one to every 55 households rather than 35.
  • Both Osaka counts are a floor: they count only the storehouses that burned.
    The print and the encyclopedia each give the storehouses the fire destroyed, not those that stood.
  • An article on Osaka's fires thinks the city probably the best guarded in Japan against fire.
    Its storehouses, set at the backs of the lots, stood thick along the ward boundaries as a kind of firebreak.
  • Did an ordinary provincial street have as many storehouses as Osaka?
    Probably fewer, since Osaka was probably the best guarded of cities. No page we read says how storehouses were shared along a street; our GUESS is that they belonged to its richer merchants, a few each, not to every house.
  • The one town ratio we found higher than Osaka's, Kitakata's one storehouse to every four households, is modern.
    At Kitakata, a goods-collection center and road town in the Edo period, some 2,600 storehouses are said to stand today, one to every four households, most still in use. Storehouses spread there from the 1800s for their use and their resistance to fire, but reached ordinary houses and farmhouses only after a great fire in 1880, in the Meiji period; another page counts about 4,000 across the modern city.

A gatehouse, like a surname, was a mark of rank that only a lord could allow; the surname's history shows whether such a grant could be bought.

  • A gatehouse with rooms to either side (nagayamon) was a mark of rank, allowed to few commoners.
    For a warrior's residence its form was laid down by the house rank of its daimyo or bannerman. In the villages it was permitted only to the upper farmers who served as village officials, and in the towns it appeared on the houses of town elders and the like.
  • A commoner's gatehouse had no guard post, and its side rooms held servants, sheds and stables.
    The rooms to either side of its doorway were men's rooms, attendants' rooms, sheds and stables.
  • The surname and the sword (myoji-taito) were a warrior privilege that a lord could grant some commoners.
    Those granted it were country samurai, headmen of special pedigree, town elders and purveyor merchants, and besides them filial sons and persons of special merit. It came in several forms, the surname only or the sword only, for one generation or in perpetuity, and a domain could grant it only within its own territory.
  • Granting the surname and sword was an instrument of rule.
    Licensing it was one of the ways the early modern state reached into the localities.
  • From the middle of the Edo period the domains granted the surname for money.
    In financial distress they issued it indiscriminately to peasants and townsmen in exchange for contributions, and a large contribution stood beside social merit and administrative service as a recognized ground for it. It remained the lord's favor even where money was the ground.
  • Could a merchant buy the right to a gatehouse the same way?
    No page we read says so: neither work on the surname says a wall or a gate could be had for money.
