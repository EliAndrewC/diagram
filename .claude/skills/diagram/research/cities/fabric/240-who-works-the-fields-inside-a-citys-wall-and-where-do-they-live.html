Who works the fields inside a city's wall, and where do they live?

Sources: the GM's setting notes for the provincial city's population; thepaper-city-fields (city dwellers farming inside Chinese city walls), kotobank-jokamachi (townsmen who farmed beside their trade)

The setting's answer: no one who lives there as a farmer. A provincial city's population counts only those living inside its walls; the fertile land around it is farmed by villagers counted under the rural tiers, and a city has no farmer households inside its walls at all (Who lives in a provincial city?). That is canon, and the record does not argue with it. It leaves open who works the ground that is farmed inside a wall, where a city has any (Does a city farm inside its walls?).

History's answer: the city's own households, beside their other work. For a long time Chinese city dwellers grew rice and vegetables inside the city, and even kept pigs and chickens; until two generations ago nearly every Chinese city had fields to be seen inside it. One historian of the Song, quoted in the same essay, puts it at its plainest: before the Northern Song, city residents mostly grew their own vegetables on the various idle plots inside the city. Japan has the same thing at its smaller scale. The castle towns gathered the warriors and the merchants and artisans and separated them from the farmers, but in the small provincial castle towns there were townsmen who farmed alongside their trade. Neither source makes these households farmers by status, which is the setting's own line: a laborer or a merchant who works a plot inside the wall is still a laborer or a merchant.

Villagers coming in through the gates to farm is not on record. It is what the canon implies for a city's in-wall fields, and it is plausible for a field near a gate, but no page read describes it.

What this means for the map. The in-wall field is worked from the city's own houses, so it is bordered by them - the ordinary houses of the quarter that faces it - and not by a ring of farmhouses, which would be farmer households living inside the wall. The ring the map draws now departs from both the canon and this record.

The rule the map follows: no farmhouse stands inside a city's wall. An in-wall field is edged by the city's own houses, laborers' and merchants' among them, facing it from the surrounding quarter, and its edge reads as worked because those houses line it (accurate for the city dwellers who farm; the canon for there being no farmer households). How densely they line it is a calibration against the drawn city.
