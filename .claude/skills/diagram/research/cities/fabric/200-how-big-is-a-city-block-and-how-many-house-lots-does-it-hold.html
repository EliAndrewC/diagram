How big is a city block, and how many house lots does it hold?

Sources: crd-heiankyo-block (the Heian block and the lot it was cut into), machiwari-jawiki (how that block was divided in practice), kotobank-jobosei (the older capitals' larger wards, cut into a block of near the same size), ginza-machiwari (the Edo block, its lots and its open middle), machiya-shoka-jawiki (the frontages a lot came in)

About 400 feet on a side, in both of the Japanese plans that give a figure. The ancient capital at Kyoto, Heian-kyo, was laid out in blocks of 40 jo by 40 jo, about 120 m - the unit was itself called a machi. The older capitals had larger wards, 90 jo and then 180 jo on a side, but each was cut by lanes into blocks of 45 jo from road center to road center, the same in both, so that the block stayed constant while the ward grew - a figure taken from road center to road center, so the ground inside the lanes was less than 45 jo. Centuries later the commoner quarter of Edo was laid out on a square of 60 ken in the Kyoto measure, again about 120 m. 120 m is about 395 ft, the same figure reached twice for two different cities.

What fills it. The Heian block was divided in principle into 32 house lots for lesser officials, each 5 jo by 10 jo (about 15 by 30 m, or 50 by 100 ft, some 136 tsubo), and some households lived on less. In practice the blocks were divided many ways, from a noble's mansion covering several blocks to commoner lots cut so as to face only the north-south streets. The Edo block was built differently: its lots ran 20 ken deep from each of the four streets, shops along the street and craftsmen's tenement rows down alleys behind them, and the middle was left as open ground, whose use is not certain. Twenty ken is a third of the block's side, about 40 m or 130 ft, which leaves the open middle a square of the same size - that last is the record's arithmetic from the page's figures.

How many lots, for the Edo form. A lot's frontage marked its holder's standing - in Kyoto from the middle of the Edo period, a split the page says was as marked as Edo's: 10 ken or more for a large house, 4 or 5 for an ordinary one, and 1.5 to 2 for a small one, whose holder might instead rent. On a 60-ken side, ordinary frontages give about twelve to fifteen lots a side, so a block holds some fifty street-front lots, fewer where a large house takes several frontages, with the tenement rows behind them uncounted. That count is the record's arithmetic, not a figure any page gives.

The Chinese provincial block is not on record. The capital's walled wards are (see Which districts get internal walls), but a size for the block of a prefectural or county city was not found. How the streets that bound these blocks join into a grid is How do a city's streets make a grid?.

The rule the map follows: a city's commoner block is about 400 ft on a side , the size both Japanese plans give (accurate). Its street frontages are cut into lots about 130 ft deep , most of them 25 to 30 ft wide , with a few large houses taking 60 ft or more ; tenement rows stand behind the street-front lots, and the block's middle may be left open. About fifty street-front lots to a block is the order to aim at, and a block's count is a calibration on that arithmetic rather than a sourced figure.
