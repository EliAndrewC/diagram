Did a Chinese city watch for fire the same way?

Sources: dongjing-menghualu-wikisource (the Song capital's fire-watch tower and the posts around it), chinanews-ancient-firefighting (the night watch and the volunteer companies of a Chinese county town), xiancheng-zhwiki (how usual a wall was at a county seat, age by age)

At the dense end, yes. Song Kaifeng set a fire-watch tower, the wanghuolou, on high ground and built it of brick, with men keeping lookout on top of it and more than a hundred soldiers quartered below with buckets, swabs, axes and saws, ladders, fire-forks, ropes and grapnels, and a patrol post of five soldiers about every three hundred paces through the wards and lanes besides. Below the capital an organized fire WATCH is attested, though not the tower. Ming government at the base level put a headman in charge of fire and theft: the county town of Wanping in Beijing was organized into wards and posts, each post with a head and three to five firemen under him, who kept watch by night in turn, striking clappers and ringing bells to remind the inhabitants to beware of fire and theft, and some places also set up a patrol post where the watch sheltered from rain and snow and where knives and spears, fire-hooks and buckets were kept. In 1743 the governor of Hunan instructed every prefecture and county to organize volunteer fire companies, registered on rolls, reported to the authorities and paid a fixed sum of silver each year. What no page read attests below the capital is the brick lookout TOWER itself.

A Chinese county seat of the later dynasties was usually walled: the Song had discouraged walling a seat away from the borders, but by the late Ming an estimated two-thirds and more of the country's county seats had walls, and by the end of the Qing the great majority of prefectural and county seats did. So a county seat of that age stands on the walled side of the line the fire-tower question draws, with our walled towns and cities.
