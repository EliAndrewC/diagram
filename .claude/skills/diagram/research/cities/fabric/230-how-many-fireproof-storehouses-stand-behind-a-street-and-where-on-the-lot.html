How many fireproof storehouses stand behind a street, and where on the lot?

Sources: machiya-shoka-jawiki (where on the lot a Kyoto storehouse stood), kyototuu-sugimoto (a great house's three storehouses, in figures), matsusaka-hasegawa-kanko (another's five, and where they stand), sekkei-sya-sayama-kura (the usual size of a village kura), iwasaki-kitakata (a storehouse town's ratio to its households), jtb-kitakata (when and why that town built so many)

Where on the lot: at the back, and sometimes one at the street as well. In Kyoto a storehouse was never built on the street side; the custom was to put it at the back of the lot, the opposite of Edo, where the shop-house itself was built as an earthen storehouse. The two great merchant houses of the question before this one bear that out, with a difference: the Kyoto house has all three of its storehouses behind the main house, in a hook toward its north side, and the castle-town house has four side by side at the back and a fifth at the front, beside the entrance. Both forms are attested, so the map rolls between them rather than choosing.

How big. The Kyoto house's storehouses are two-story, of about 38, 24 and 19 square meters - 400, 250 and 205 square feet, or squares of about 20, 16 and 14 ft. The commonest size a survey of one village district found, 2.5 by 3 ken, about 15 by 18 ft, falls in the same range (How big was a farm storehouse?).

How many. One great merchant house held three to five, as above. For a whole town the one ratio found is from Kitakata, a goods-collection center and road town in the Edo period, where some 2,600 storehouses are said to stand, one to every four households, most still in use. That is a ceiling rather than a norm: Kitakata is a town famous for its storehouses, which spread there from the 1800s for their use and their resistance to fire, and after a great fire in 1880 reached ordinary houses and farmhouses too; another page counts about 4,000 across the modern city. The ordinary pre-modern street lies between the two: its storehouses belong to its richer merchants, a few each, not to every house.

How far apart they stand is not on record. Both houses set theirs in a row, close together; no page read gives a distance between them or a rule for one.

The rule the map follows: a merchant's storehouses stand at the back of the lot, the side away from the street, in a row or a hook close together; in some estates one more stands at the street front beside the entrance - a knob rolled per estate between the Kyoto and the castle-town forms. Each is 14 to 20 ft square (accurate). A walled merchant estate carries three to five; a large unwalled merchant house one or two; an ordinary shop-house none. So a city's count is roughly four for each walled estate plus one or two for each large merchant house, and never more than one to every four households of the city (calibration between the attested house counts and the one town ratio).
