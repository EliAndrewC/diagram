How many fireproof storehouses stand behind a street, and where on the lot?

Sources: machiya-shoka-jawiki (where on the lot a Kyoto storehouse stood), kyototuu-sugimoto (a great house's three storehouses, in figures), matsusaka-hasegawa-kanko (another's five, and where they stand), sekkei-sya-sayama-kura (the usual size of a village kura), iwasaki-kitakata (a storehouse town's ratio to its households), jtb-kitakata (when and why that town built so many), anju-osaka-fires (a premodern city's storehouses counted against its houses), kotobank-myochiyake (the standard account of the same count),kotobank-kamado (a hearth in an old count is a household)

Where on the lot: at the back, and sometimes one at the street as well. In Kyoto a storehouse was never built on the street side; the custom was to put it at the back of the lot, the opposite of Edo, where the shop-house itself was built as an earthen storehouse. The two great merchant houses of the question before this one bear that out, with a difference: the Kyoto house has all three of its storehouses behind the main house, as the question before this one quotes its listing, in a hook toward the main house's north side, and the castle-town house has four side by side at the back and a fifth at the front, beside the entrance. Both forms are attested, so the map rolls between them rather than choosing.

How big. The Kyoto house's storehouses are two-story, of about 38, 24 and 19 square meters - 400, 250 and 205 square feet, or squares of about 20, 16 and 14 ft - and all three were built by 1822, so the sizes are the Edo period's own. The commonest size a survey of one village district found, 2.5 by 3 ken, about 15 by 18 ft, falls in the same range (How big was a farm storehouse?), but that survey says the district's houses came to own storehouses probably only from about the end of the shogunate, so the size rests on the Kyoto house, not on it.

How many. One great merchant house held three to five, as above. The one count for a city before modern times is Osaka's. A one-sheet print issued after the city's fire of 1863, 139 years on, records that the fire of 1724 destroyed some 28,000 houses, 98,700 hearths and 2,800 earthen storehouses; a hearth in such a count is a household. That is about one storehouse to every ten houses, or to every 35 households. The standard account of the same fire gives smaller figures, over about two-thirds of the city: 11,765 houses, 60,292 households and 1,097 earthen storehouses burned. That is again about one storehouse to every ten or eleven houses, but one to every 55 households, so the two agree on houses and differ on households. Each counts only the storehouses that burned, in the city the article thinks probably the best guarded against fire in Japan, where the storehouses at the backs of the lots stood thick along the ward boundaries as a kind of firebreak, so each is a floor for Osaka rather than all its storehouses. The one town ratio higher than that is modern: at Kitakata, a goods-collection center and road town in the Edo period, some 2,600 storehouses are said to stand today, one to every four households, most still in use. Storehouses spread there from the 1800s for their use and their resistance to fire, but reached ordinary houses and farmhouses only after a great fire in 1880; another page counts about 4,000 across the modern city. So the one-to-four ratio is a Meiji and later one, and the map does not use it. An ordinary provincial street most likely held fewer than Osaka, and here the record guesses, since no page read says how storehouses were shared along one: its storehouses belong to its richer merchants, a few each, not to every house.

How far apart they stand is not on record. Both houses set theirs in a row; no page read gives a distance between them or a rule for one.

The rule the map follows: a merchant's storehouses stand at the back of the lot, the side away from the street, in a row or a hook, close together (the spacing a guess, since none is on record); in some estates one more stands at the street front beside the entrance - a knob rolled per estate between the Kyoto and the castle-town forms. Each is 14 to 20 ft square (accurate). A walled merchant estate carries three to five; a large unwalled merchant house one or two; an ordinary shop-house none (a guess, as above). So a city's count is roughly four for each walled estate plus one or two for each large merchant house, and never more than about one to every ten houses of the city, or every 35 to 55 households (calibration between the attested house counts and Osaka's counts of 1724, the one premodern town figure, on which the print and the standard account agree for houses and differ for households; the modern Kitakata ratio of one to four is not used).
