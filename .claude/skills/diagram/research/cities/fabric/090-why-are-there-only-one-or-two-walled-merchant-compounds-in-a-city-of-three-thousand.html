Why are there only one or two walled merchant compounds in a city of three thousand?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-23; the GM's setting notes for a provincial city's merchant households; nagayamon-kotobank (the gate whose form followed its holder's rank and office), myoji-taito-kotobank (the surname granted to commoners, and what a domain took for it)

A city of about 3,000 holds a dozen or so very rich merchant families, and most of them could pay for a wall and a gate. One to three of them have one. The GM ruled on 2026-07-23 that a gated compound is not something wealth can buy: it is a right the daimyo explicitly grants to a particular family, of a piece with the other granted merchant privileges - an audience with the daimyo at the New Year's celebration, permission to carry a surname down the generations without being samurai. So the wall marks legal standing rather than money, and a very rich merchant without the grant keeps a large unwalled house like anyone else's, only bigger.

The rule has a real counterpart, and it runs through the GATE. The nagayamon - the gatehouse with a range of rooms to either side of the doorway - was what expressed a warrior residence's formal rank, and its form and structure were laid down by the house rank of the daimyo or bannerman who held it. In the villages it was permitted only to the upper stratum of farmers who served as village officials, and among townsmen to the town elder; on the commoner version there is no guard post, and the flanking rooms are men's rooms, attendants' rooms, sheds and stables, which is what a merchant compound's gate range would in fact hold.17 The companion privilege in the paragraph above is myoji-taito, the surname and the sword. It was a warrior privilege that was at times permitted to commoners too - country samurai, headmen of special pedigree, town elders and purveyor merchants, and besides them filial sons and persons of special merit - and it came in several forms, the surname only, the sword only, some for a single generation and some in perpetuity, and a domain could grant it only to those within its own territory.41 Licensing it was one of the ways the early modern state reached into the localities,42 which is the setting's own reasoning about privilege.

Where the setting departs from it. From the middle of the early modern period the domains, in financial distress, issued the qualification indiscriminately to peasants and townsmen in exchange for monetary contributions, and a large contribution stands beside social merit and administrative service as one of the recognized grounds for the grant.43 It remained the lord's grant and never became simply a purchase, which is the setting's structural point and holds; but "not something wealth can buy" is a deviation this setting takes from the later part of that record rather than a finding about it. What no page read does is attach the money to a WALL or a GATE: the gate sources tie those to rank and to office alone.

The count is rolled per city rather than chosen, one compound 30% of the time, two 40%, three 30%, from the map's own seed. A rolled count is what keeps two cities honestly different from each other.

The rule the map follows: a provincial city draws one to three walled merchant compounds, the number rolled 30/40/30 from the map's seed, each in the deep core of a block - well inside the rampart, clear of the moat, and off the temple neighborhood - rather than jammed against the wall. A compound's court is treated as a compound for clearance, not just the house inside it, so it overlaps no temple, ministry, house or other court. The rest of the very rich merchants take large houses. A town places its compounds by hand and rolls nothing.
