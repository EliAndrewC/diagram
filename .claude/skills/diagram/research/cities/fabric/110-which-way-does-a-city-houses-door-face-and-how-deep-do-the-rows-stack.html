Which way does a city house's door face, and how deep do the rows stack?

Sources: the GM's doctrine of 2026-07-18; townhouse-jawiki (the ridge-split back-lane pair and where its shared well and privy stood), machiya-shoka-jawiki (the one gate between two front shops, and how many rows a frontage bought), roji-jawiki (the width of the lane, and the widening at the shared well), nagaya-jawiki

A farmhouse faces south wherever it stands - its dooryard garden and its threshing ground want the sun, and that orientation is canon, so it is exempt everywhere. A city house has no sun constraint. What it must have instead is an entrance that opens onto ground a person can walk on - a street, a roji, a court - and never into the back wall of another house an eave-gap away. The pages read give every tenement unit an entrance of its own and put the alley's mouth between the street-front shops; the prohibition itself is the GM's doctrine, and no page read states it as a rule.

The depth rule follows from the door rule rather than standing beside it. Terrace rows come in back-to-back PAIRS with both fronts outward, and the form has a name: the back-lane tenement was mune-wari, ridge-split along the lane, one roof over two ranks that share a rear wall and open to opposite sides, each unit nine shaku by two ken with a four-and-a-half-mat room and a mat and a half of earth floor for the stove and the sink. A three-deep stack cannot occur, because the middle row would be walled on both faces and those households would have no way out to a lane; and it did not occur, because the whole block of tenements was entered through one wooden gate set between two front shops and along the lane behind it, and because what a plot's frontage bought was one row along the lane or two facing rows, never a third rank behind them - about three ken of shop frontage gave one side, about five gave both. The lane widens where the shared well stands, immediately inside the back of the merchant house or at the lane's middle, and that widening is where a shared well or water supply was put.

How wide is the lane, and how wide the map draws it. The roji between one back-lane building and the next was three to six shaku, 0.9 to 1.8 m - about 3 to 6 ft, a path so private that almost nobody but the residents of the buildings it served used it. That is the historical width, two to four times narrower than the 12 ft these maps draw. These maps draw the roji at 12 ft and continue to: at three feet to the pixel a true roji is one to two pixels wide, and a lane that thin neither reads as a way nor carries a caption, so the drawn width is a map drawing convention - the way is widened so that a reader can see there is one. The real gap between two eaves is 3 to 6 ft.

The rule the map follows: every city dwelling's door opens onto at least 7 real ft of clear ground, at every map scale - the line sits between the eave band, about 6 ft or less, which is for drainage rather than for entering, and the walkable band of about 10 ft. Rows stack at most two deep. In a street-fronting pair the front row faces the street and the row behind it faces the back lane, the ura-dana pattern; a house standing on its own takes whichever cardinal side is open. The farmhouse, facing south, is exempt. Town and city tiers both.
