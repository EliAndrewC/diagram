How do a city's streets make a grid?

Sources: kotobank-machiwari (Heian-kyo's and Edo's block sizes, the square block with its open core, and the rectangular block most castle towns used), kyoto-city-jobosei (the Heian block's fixed size and the width of the lanes between blocks), ginza-machidukuri-machiwari (Edo's square block in Ginza, and the lanes cut through it after the fire of 1657), jokamachi-jawiki (the trunk road lined without gaps, and bent into dog-legs), roji-jawiki (the bustle on the main streets alone); this project's calibration against the three drawn provincial cities

A planned Japanese town really was a grid of blocks, and the size of a block is known. The avenues and lanes of Heian-kyo, the old capital, cut it into blocks called machi, 40 jo (about 120 m, some 390 ft) square, a size fixed exclusive of the roads, with lanes about 12 m (some 40 ft) wide between them. Edo's commoner quarter kept the square: a block 60 ken (again about 120 m) on a side, lots 20 ken deep along each of its streets, and an open ground called the kaishochi, 20 ken square, left at its center - and a ward (a machi, in the other sense of the word) was not a block but the two sides of one street facing each other. Most castle towns did not take the square, though. Outside Edo, Sunpu, Nagoya and a few others, blocks were rectangles with no open core; in 1591 Hideyoshi ran a north-south lane down the middle of the blocks around Kyoto's center, making its squares into rectangles, and after Edo's great fire of 1657 two new lanes were cut through the Ginza blocks. The trunk road of a castle town was lined with houses on both sides with no gaps between them, and its roads were bent into right-angle dog-legs and stopped in dead ends to lengthen the way to the castle. The street frontage was where the life of the quarter was: in Edo's commoner quarter the alleys behind were no place of bustle, and the main streets alone carried it. How a Chinese county seat's trunk streets run - a cross or a T, most often deflected - is on the question of a county seat's street share.

So a block's shape is two attested forms, a KNOB rather than a choice: the square of about 390 ft with an open core, the rarer form, and the long rectangle with no open core, the usual one. A map rolls between them per settlement, weighted toward the rectangle, and either keeps its blocks near the historical size - about 390 ft on the side the lots front, with the lots about 130 ft (20 ken) deep. The square's open core is drawn as open ground or threaded by a lane, as Kyoto's and Ginza's were.

A quarter's grid is a few crossing streets - two running north-south against two running east-west, nine blocks, is a good unit - and then a fill dense enough that the buildings themselves line the streets. Density is what makes a grid read: the failure is a sparse fill that leaves a stretch of street with nothing along it, so a quarter is packed to its real per-caste counts rather than to a comfortable spacing. None of the tolerances below is a historical finding. Each is the threshold at which a drawn street stops reading as a street, calibrated against the three drawn cities; the one with a historical yardstick, how far a building may stand off a street, is looser than the Edo lot of about 130 ft deep that it would otherwise follow.

The rule the map follows - lining: no larger street runs a stretch longer than about 420 ft with its nearest building more than about 175 ft off it, because roadside land on a commercial or residential through-street is the prime ground of the city and a wide bare margin reads as an oversight. Narrow alleys threading a block interior are exempt, and so is the government avenue, whose frontage is the spaced ministry compounds rather than shops and houses. Two streets are never drawn on the same line - a doubled corridor pushes the buildings out of fronting range.

The rule the map follows - joining: the streets are one connected network wired to the Imperial road, and two lanes count as joined only where their paved beds actually touch: a lane that stops even a roadbed short of the next one is a separate network, so each quarter's road-facing street is run out until it meets the road. A ward gate does not break a run - the centerline goes on under it. Lanes never come almost together without meeting: a pair closing to within about 6 to 90 ft without crossing, and a lane overshooting a crossing by a sliver of about 9 to 150 ft , both read as mistakes - so lanes either cross and run on, or end cleanly at the junction, and the two outermost streets of a grid end at their shared corner rather than each running past the other. Two lanes of any kind heading straight at each other - within about 37 degrees of aligned, less than about 240 ft apart, with clear ground between - simply connect; a gap that a building, a ward fence or a wall genuinely blocks is no fault, because there the lane is correctly routing around something.

The rule the map follows - drawing: where two lanes cross, the wider is drawn over the narrower - the Imperial road over the city streets it crosses, a street over an alley - and a junction is drawn as a crossroads: every lane's dark curb goes down before any lane's paved bed, so the beds merge into one continuous junction instead of one lane's curb ruling a line across the other's mouth. Walls are drawn over the lanes: a road, street or alley that meets the city rampart or an internal ward fence passes under it, and breaks it only at a gate, where the wall itself carries a real gap so the roadway shows through the opening. These are map drawing conventions - how the sheet reads, not how the ground was built.
