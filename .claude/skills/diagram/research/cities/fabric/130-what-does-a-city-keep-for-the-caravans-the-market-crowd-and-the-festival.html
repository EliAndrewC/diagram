What does a city keep for the caravans, the market crowd and the festival?

Sources: the town tier's own entries for a market center's amenities; tokaido-qa-mlit (what a post station was obliged to keep standing), sukego-kotobank (where the animals came from when that was not enough); this project's calibration against the three drawn provincial cities

A provincial city is a bigger version of the market center a town already is, so the town's civic furniture ports up rather than changing in kind: fireproof kura behind the merchant houses, market-day lodging, a theater stage in a temple precinct. Three town rules do not port up. A city has no farmer plurality and no farmers living inside it; it has temples rather than a monastery with a clan patron; and its wall is a regular defensive ring rather than the irregular run a town's arc may take.

What a city adds is scale at the gate. A wagon train arriving at or passing through a city brings many dozens of draft animals - oxen and packhorses - with their guards, porters and drivers, and the order of magnitude is documented from the Japanese post road: each Tokaido station was first made to keep thirty-six horses standing, and from 1638 a hundred post horses and a hundred porters, the requirement rising as the traffic did.

The contrast the map's gate cluster is drawn from - that a town inn can absorb that into a small stable yard and a city cannot - is this record's own, and the readable evidence cuts against it. Those post stations were themselves towns, a hundred horses standing was routinely not enough, and the answer was never a bigger yard: the neighboring villages supplied men and horses in support, at two men and two horses per hundred koku of assessed yield, and the ring they were drawn from began at two or three ri around the station and was widened past ten. So the bottleneck is real and documented; what is unsupported is that a city meets it worse than a town does.

The rule the map follows: a provincial city draws at least five merchant storehouses. Just inside each gate stands a caravan cluster - a cheap lodging house, a prominent inn and a large stables, all within about 1,020 ft of the gate - with open ground around the stables for tying up and penning, which means no more than four dwellings within about 225 ft of it; the fencing itself is not drawn. A city carries a theater stage, adjacent to a temple as a town's is, and its viewing ground is wider than a town's, about 555 ft across . The counts and the distances are calibrated against the three drawn provincial cities rather than derived from a source.
