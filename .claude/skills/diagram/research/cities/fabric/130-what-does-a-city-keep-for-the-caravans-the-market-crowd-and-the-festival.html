A provincial city is a bigger version of the market center a town already is, so the town's civic furniture ports up rather than changing in kind: fireproof kura behind the merchant houses, market-day lodging, a theater stage in a temple precinct. Three town rules do not port up, by this record's own reading of the town tier's entries (no source read states them for a city). A city has no farmer plurality and no farmers living inside it; it has temples rather than a monastery with a clan patron; and its wall is a regular defensive ring rather than the irregular run a town's arc may take.

What a city adds is scale at the gate. A wagon train arriving at or passing through a city brings, as a guess, many dozens of draft animals - oxen and packhorses - with their guards, porters and drivers (no source read counts the animals of one train or names oxen among them), and the nearest documented order of magnitude is not one train but what a Japanese post station had to keep standing for the traffic: each Tokaido station was first made to keep thirty-six horses standing, and from 1638 a hundred post horses and a hundred porters, with the neighboring villages made to supply the shortfall when even that was not enough.

The contrast the map's gate cluster is drawn from - that a town inn can absorb that into a small stable yard and a city cannot - is this record's own, and the readable evidence cuts against it. Those post stations were themselves towns (the towns question linked below), a hundred horses standing was not always enough, and the answer the sources record is not a bigger yard: the neighboring villages supplied men and horses in support, at two men and two horses per hundred koku of assessed yield, and the ring they were drawn from began at two or three ri around the station and was widened past ten. So the bottleneck is real and documented; what is unsupported is that a city meets it worse than a town does.
