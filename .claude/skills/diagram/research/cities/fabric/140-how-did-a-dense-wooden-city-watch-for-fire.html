How did a dense wooden city watch for fire?

From a tower, and only where the roofs touch. A premodern Japanese city burned constantly - Edo was called the City of Fires, and the Meireki fire of 1657, the worst in the city's history, claimed up to 107,000 lives - and the institution these maps draw is the fire-watch tower, the hinomi-yagura (火の見櫓): a raised lookout platform, kept for spotting a fire early and calling out the brigade, carrying an alarm bell at its top, the hansho (半鐘), which the watchman struck in a cadence that told the quarter how near the fire was. The terraces the first entry on this page describes are what make the tower necessary: packed wooden rows burn together, so the fire furniture on these maps is not decorative.

Why a walled town and a city carry one, and an open town does not. Two reasons reinforce each other. Fire is catastrophic where the roofs touch, and that is the packed intramural warren; an open road-town strung along a highway, with fields running off every edge, has natural breaks - the road itself, the gaps between holdings, the open ground all round - so a standing tower does not earn its cost. And the rampart that keeps raiders out keeps a blaze in, so the watch over the rooftops is wanted exactly where there is a wall.

Two neighboring questions test that line from either side: why an unwalled seat draws no fire tower, which turns out to confirm it, and whether a Chinese city watched for fire the same way, which agrees at the dense end.

Whose watch it is - a deviation, taken deliberately. The famous townsman fire brigades, the machi-bikeshi "iroha" companies, are a peace-era institution of the Tokugawa century: the South Town Magistrate Ooka Tadasuke issued the ordinance that organized them in 1718, and by 1720 there were forty-seven iroha companies covering the ground west of the Sumida River and sixteen more for Honjo and Fukagawa east of it, one company to about twenty blocks, manned at first by ordinary townsmen and paid for by the blocks, but placed under the town magistrate's command. So the difference our setting takes is narrower than the phrase "townsman brigade" suggests, because command sat with the magistrate in the historical case too; what changes is who climbs the tower. In a setting pitched earlier and more martial the watch is the magistrate's own people - their yoriki, doshin and ashigaru - rather than townsmen enrolled block by block.

Why more than one tower spreads out. The watch was parceled out by neighborhood, though not one tower to each block-group: in the Kyoho era one fire tower was provided to every ten machi, and a machi without one had a fire ladder on the roof of its own watch hut, some of them with a bell as well, while the shogunate's standing fire watch kept fire-brigade mansions at several places in the castle town, each carrying a tower with watchmen on it day and night, and the townsmen set a watch house in every machi and a gate watch at the machi boundaries. So the watch STATION was per-machi and the tower was one to about ten of them, which is the same figure the capitals page gives for Edo. The map spreads its towers out on its own reading that a tower's useful watch is its own visual neighborhood of rooftops. A floor on the distance between towers is not enough by itself: two towers a generous gap apart can still both stand on one side of center and leave the city's largest rooftop mass with no tower amid it at all. So the dispersal rule is written in two parts, a floor between towers and a requirement that each stand amid the district it is nearest tower for.

Two things about the drawn tower are map drawing conventions rather than history. A single tower stands in for a quarter's whole fire watch, where a real city kept many. And the glyph is an open braced frame with a red bell, so that it reads apart from the solid guard towers on the rampart: a fire tower stands among the roofs it watches, not on the wall. The open legs are history rather than convention - a townsmen's tower was not allowed to have its legs boarded in - and the bracing and the bell's red are the convention; how tall such a tower was, and that it stood beside a ward gate, is the urban features' question.

The rule the map follows: a provincial city draws at least two fire towers and a walled town one; an open town draws none. Each stands in a commoner quarter, never the samurai ward, and on ground with sightlines - a cleared block or an open seam between building clusters - which is why the towers go down last, onto the seams the finished map already has. Where there is more than one they disperse: no two stand within one watch radius of each other, about 690 ft , and each stands amid the district it watches - every commoner dwelling inside the walls belongs to its nearest tower, and a tower sits within the larger of 0.9 of that district's radius and one watch radius of the district's center of mass, rather than at its edge with an unwatched quadrant behind it. In practice that is one tower amid each major commoner quarter, three for a city with laborer, servant and merchant quarters to cover. A tower keeps at least 15 ft of daylight all round its frame, and the same clear of every wellhead, because an open braced tower's footing and ladder stand off its neighbors' eaves and must never block the court the quarter draws its water from.
