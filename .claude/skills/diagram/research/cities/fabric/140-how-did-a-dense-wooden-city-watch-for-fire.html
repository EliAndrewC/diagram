How did a dense wooden city watch for fire?

From a tower, and only where the roofs touch. A premodern Japanese city burned constantly - Edo was called the City of Fires, and the Meireki fire of 1657, the worst in the city's history, claimed up to 107,000 lives26 - and the institution these maps draw is the fire-watch tower, the hinomi-yagura (火の見櫓): a raised lookout platform, kept for spotting a fire early and calling out the brigade, carrying an alarm bell at its top, the hansho (半鐘),27 which the watchman struck in a cadence that told the quarter how near the fire was.28 The terraces the first entry on this page describes are what make the tower necessary: packed wooden rows burn together, so the fire furniture on these maps is not decorative.

Why a walled town and a city carry one, and an open town does not. Two reasons reinforce each other. Fire is catastrophic where the roofs touch, and that is the packed intramural warren; an open road-town strung along a highway, with fields running off every edge, has natural breaks - the road itself, the gaps between holdings, the open ground all round - so a standing tower does not earn its cost.29 And the rampart that keeps raiders out keeps a blaze in, so the watch over the rooftops is wanted exactly where there is a wall.65

The unwalled seat is the confirming case, not an exception. The GM asked on 2026-07-24 whether an unwalled tax-administration seat should carry a tower. Its real-world analog is the jin'ya or daikansho town, and the identification is the published one: the official residence in a district administered by a shogunal district governor or intendant went by the name daikan-jin'ya, its grounds held the intendant's own residence, the office for public business, the houses of the subordinate staff, the rice store and the levy store, and a judgment yard and gaol; where a settlement grew up around such a compound and became a town it was called a jin'ya-machi, and often flourished as the central town of the territory administered.40 That town's fire posture was defensive rather than observational, and the neighborhood watch house is where its apparatus stood. Many watch houses were fitted with a ladder on the roof - a small-scale fire tower, in the account's own words - and a hansho bell, and the firefighting gear was kept inside: the company standard, the fire hook, the hand pump, the buckets, ladders and well-buckets, so that when the bell was struck the town officers and the firefighting hands ran to the watch house, took the gear out, assembled and went to the fire together.30 Where a frame was raised on the watch house so that the watchman could look over the whole town, that raised platform IS the hinomi-yagura.48 The night round was walked calling "mind your locks, mind your fire" and striking clappers, though the bodies the readable account names for it are modern ones.49 Water was stored against fire in the tensuioke, the traditional cistern that held rainwater and was used mainly as urban firefighting water in the Edo period.50 The plastered kura is the fire-resistant form: storehouses are named for what they hold, a rice store among them, and are built to protect it from fire, damp and theft, their walls often 300 mm thick or more and their outer doors faced in earth and plaster, so that in the great fires of the Edo period there were many cases in which the fire never reached the inside.51 The tax rice had a store of its own, the gogura, one to a village or to several, a holding place for the rice on its way out and kept under strict guard.52 That losing the tax rice was the career-ending disaster, and that the storehouse was therefore fireproofed rather than the horizon watched, is this record's own reading of those facts. Two pieces of the posture rest on nothing readable: the local temple bell doubling as the alarm, and any of this standing at the daikansho OFFICE rather than at the town's watch post.53 Takayama Jin'ya, the Hida daikansho, is the one such office of more than fifty still standing, and it is standing because no fire ever took it; what its ground keeps is the trace of a single water-supply pond, and a grain storehouse framed in Keicho-era hinoki that is among the foremost in the country.31 The freestanding tower itself is no late arrival: it was built up together with the firefighting system, beginning with Edo in the Edo period, and by the early Showa era towers stood in almost every region of the country.32 What that dates is the SPREAD, and the reading this record takes from it is its own: the institution starts in the shogunal capital, the densest wooden city there was, and reaches the ordinary countryside last, so an unwalled country seat of the period we draw stands at the beginning of that spread rather than at its end.

China agrees at the dense end. Song Kaifeng set a fire-watch tower, the wanghuolou, on high ground and built it of brick, with men keeping lookout on top of it and more than a hundred soldiers quartered below with buckets, swabs, axes and saws, ladders, fire-forks, ropes and grapnels, and a patrol post of five soldiers about every three hundred paces through the wards and lanes besides.33 Below the capital an organized fire WATCH is attested, though not the tower. Ming government at the base level put a headman in charge of fire and theft: the county town of Wanping in Beijing was organized into wards and posts, each post with a head and three to five firemen under him, who kept watch by night in turn, striking clappers and ringing bells to remind the inhabitants to beware of fire and theft, and some places also set up a patrol post where the watch sheltered from rain and snow and where knives and spears, fire-hooks and buckets were kept. In 1743 the governor of Hunan instructed every prefecture and county to organize volunteer fire companies, registered on rolls, reported to the authorities and paid a fixed sum of silver each year.34 What no page read attests below the capital is the brick lookout TOWER itself. A Chinese county seat was walled in any case, so it falls on the near side of the same line.

Whose watch it is - a deviation, taken deliberately. The famous townsman fire brigades, the machi-bikeshi "iroha" companies, are a peace-era institution of the Tokugawa century: the South Town Magistrate Ooka Tadasuke issued the ordinance that organized them in 1718, and by 1720 there were forty-seven iroha companies covering the ground west of the Sumida and sixteen more for Honjo and Fukagawa east of it, one company to about twenty blocks, manned by the blocks and paid for by them but placed under the town magistrate's command.35 So the difference our setting takes is narrower than the phrase "townsman brigade" suggests, because command sat with the magistrate in the historical case too; what changes is who climbs the tower.64 In a setting pitched earlier and more martial the watch is the magistrate's own people - their yoriki, doshin and ashigaru - rather than townsmen enrolled block by block. On an unwalled seat the fire story is carried instead by the kura on the magistrate's compound, the headman's house and the merchants' houses; a small hansho bell-frame at the magistrate's compound would be the one defensible extra glyph, and drawing nothing at all is equally accurate.

Why more than one tower spreads out. The watch was parceled out by neighborhood, though not one tower to each block-group: in the Kyoho era one fire tower was provided to every ten machi, and a machi without one had a ladder and a bell fitted on the roof of its own watch hut, while the shogunate's standing fire watch kept fire-brigade mansions at several places in the castle town, each carrying a tower with watchmen on it day and night, and the townsmen set a watch house in every machi and a gate watch at the machi boundaries.36 So the watch STATION was per-machi and the tower was one to about ten of them, which is the same figure the capitals page gives for Edo. Towers spread out because a tower's useful watch is its own visual neighborhood of rooftops. A floor on the distance between towers is not enough by itself: two towers about 1,020 ft apart, a generous gap, but both northwest of center, leave the city's largest rooftop mass - a northeast laborer warren - with no tower amid it at all. So the dispersal rule is written in two parts, a floor between towers and a requirement that each stand amid the district it is nearest tower for.

Two things about the drawn tower are map drawing conventions rather than history. A single tower stands in for a quarter's whole fire watch, where a real city kept many. And the glyph is an open braced frame with a red bell, so that it reads apart from the solid guard towers on the rampart: a fire tower is a civilian structure in the interior watching the roofs, not a work of the wall.

The rule the map follows: a provincial city draws at least two fire towers and a walled town one; an open town draws none. Each stands in a commoner quarter, never the samurai ward, and on ground with sightlines - a cleared block or an open seam between building clusters - which is why the towers go down last, onto the seams the finished map already has. Where there is more than one they disperse: no two stand within one watch radius of each other, about 690 ft , and each stands amid the district it watches - every commoner dwelling inside the walls belongs to its nearest tower, and a tower sits within the larger of 0.9 of that district's radius and one watch radius of the district's center of mass, rather than at its edge with an unwatched quadrant behind it. In practice that is one tower amid each major commoner quarter, three for a city with laborer, servant and merchant quarters to cover. A tower keeps at least 15 ft of daylight all round its frame, and the same clear of every wellhead, because an open braced tower's footing and ladder stand off its neighbors' eaves and must never block the court the quarter draws its water from.
