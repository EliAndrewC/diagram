City streets and blocks: the grid, the block and how wide the streets are (machiwari)

A planned Japanese city was laid out as a grid of streets around blocks of a known size. The blocks of Heian-kyo, the old capital at Kyoto, were about 120 m (~394 ft) square, and the commoner quarter of Edo, laid out many centuries later, kept a square of about the same size, with its house lots along the streets and open ground at the middle. Most castle towns cut their blocks as long rectangles instead.

The streets between the blocks were narrow beside the avenues of the ancient capitals. Heian-kyo's central avenue was over 80 m (~262 ft) wide, while an Edo-period highway ran about 13 to 15 m (~43-49 ft), and the Tokaido kept that width through Edo itself.

  • Heian-kyo's blocks were 40 jo, about 120 m (~394 ft), on a side, not counting the roads.
    The avenues and lanes of the old capital cut it into square blocks, each called a machi, fixed at 40 jo a side exclusive of the roads, with lanes about 12 m (~39 ft) wide between them.
  • The older capitals had larger wards, but cut them into blocks of about the same size.
    Fujiwara-kyo's wards, as inferred from its law code, were 90 jo, about 270 m (~886 ft), on a side, and Heijo-kyo's twice that, 180 jo. Both were cut by lanes into blocks 45 jo from road center to road center, so the block stayed the same while the ward grew, and the ground inside the lanes was less than 45 jo.
  • A Heian block was divided, in principle, into 32 house lots.
    A lesser official's lot was 5 by 10 jo, about 15 m (~49 ft) by 30 m (~98 ft), some 136 tsubo, and some households lived on less. In practice the blocks were divided many ways, from a noble's mansion covering several blocks to commoners' lots cut so as to face only the north-south streets.

Edo's commoner quarter, laid out from 1603, took up the square again, and its blocks are the best described.

  • Edo's commoner quarter kept the square block, about 120 m (~394 ft) a side, with open ground at its center.
    Its plan took as its unit a square 60 ken on a side in the Kyoto measure, with lots 20 ken deep along each of its streets and an open ground, the kaishochi, 20 ken square, left at the middle. Twenty ken is a third of the side, about 40 m (~131 ft), so the open middle is a square of the same size - this page's arithmetic from those figures.
  • In Edo a ward was a street, not a block.
    A town, a machi in the other sense of the word, was the two sides of one street facing each other across it. Who ran a ward is at What is a ward, who runs it, and how many does a city have?.
  • On an Edo block, shops fronted the street and tenement rows stood down alleys behind them.
    Shops stood in a row on the street side of a lot, and down the alley behind them stood the nagaya, the craftsmen's tenement rows, on both sides; what the open middle was used for is not clear. The page describes the quarter laid out from Nihonbashi to Kyobashi, not every part of Edo.
  • How many house lots did an Edo block hold?
    Some fifty along its streets. A lot's frontage marked its holder's standing - in Kyoto from the middle of the Edo period, as in Edo: 10 ken or more for a large house, 4 or 5 for an ordinary one, and 1.5 to 2 for a small one, whose holder might instead rent. On a 60 ken side, ordinary frontages give about twelve to fifteen lots a side, so a block held some fifty street-front lots, fewer where a large house took several frontages, with the tenement rows behind them uncounted. The count is this page's arithmetic, not a figure from a source.

Most castle towns did not follow Edo's square, and even the square blocks were later cut up.

  • Most castle towns used a long rectangular block with no open core.
    Square blocks were limited, outside Edo, to a few towns such as Sunpu and Nagoya; the great majority of castle towns laid out rectangular blocks that kept no open ground.
  • Square blocks were later cut into rectangles by new lanes.
    In 1591 Toyotomi Hideyoshi ran a north-south lane through the middle of the blocks around Kyoto's center, remaking its squares as rectangles, and after the Meireki fire of 1657 two new lanes were cut through the Ginza blocks of Edo.
  • A castle town lined its trunk road with houses and bent it into dog-legs.
    Houses stood along both sides of the trunk road with no gaps between them, making the castle hard to see, and the roads were bent into right-angle dog-legs and stopped in dead ends to lengthen the way to the castle.
  • In Edo, a quarter's bustle was on its main streets, not its alleys.
    The alleys of Edo's commoner quarter were no place of bustle; the main streets alone carried it. What a back alley was like underfoot is at What was a back alley like underfoot?, and how wide a town's street and its lanes were at How wide is a town's street, its side lane and its back alley?.

Chinese cities are recorded differently: their capitals' walled wards are well described, their provincial cities' blocks are not.

    No page we read gives it. The capital's walled wards are on record (at Which districts get internal walls), but not the block of a provincial city, nor how many lots it held. How a county seat's trunk streets ran is at A county seat's street share, open reserve and civic share.

How wide the streets were varied far more than the blocks, from the processional avenues of the ancient capitals to the highways of the Edo period.

  • The ancient capitals' central avenues were 73 to 84 m (~240-276 ft) wide, and Chang'an's 147 m (~482 ft).
    Heijo-kyo's Suzaku avenue was 73 m (~240 ft) wide, and Heian-kyo's 82 m (~269 ft) by the Engishiki, or 84.0 m (~276 ft) by a later estimate, 70.2 m (~230 ft) of it roadway inside the side ditches. The Suzaku avenue of Tang Chang'an was 147 m (~482 ft) wide.
  • An Edo-period highway was about 7 to 8 ken, 13 to 15 m (~43-49 ft), wide, and so was the Tokaido through Edo.
    Ginza-dori, which carried the Tokaido through the city, was about that width too.
  • Early in the Edo period the Tokaido's width was set at 5 ken, about 9.1 m (~30 ft).
    In the second month of 1604 Okubo Nagayasu and others were ordered to make the highway 5 ken wide, with hackberry trees planted along it and a marker at every ri. At the 1.82 m (~6 ft) ken, 5 ken is 29.8 ft.
