The Imperial road through a city, and the shops that line it

Where a highway entered a Japanese castle town, both sides of its trunk road were built up with houses and no gaps between them, and a post station lined its road with inns, cheap lodgings, teahouses and shops. The teahouses that served travelers, first opened out in the hills, moved in to the edges of the post towns. Chinese walled cities treated the street between two gates differently: most of them broke it so that no street ran straight through from one gate to the other.

  • A castle town built up both sides of its trunk road, leaving no gaps.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on castle towns says that once the road entered the castle town, houses were set along both sides of the trunk road with no gaps between them - its own reason being to make the castle hard to see from the road.
  • A post station lined its road with inns, cheap lodgings, teahouses and shops.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on post stations says inns, cheap lodgings where the guest paid only for firewood, teahouses and shops stood in a row along its road. It describes a post station, not a city's through-road.
  • The travelers' teahouses moved in from the hills to the edges of the post towns.
    A Japanese encyclopedia says the teahouses that served travelers on the highways of the Edo period were at first opened in the hills and other places away from the post towns, as resting places on the way, and gradually moved in as far as the edge of the post town, where they were called tateba teahouses.

Chinese walled cities, laid out by a different tradition, had model street plans for the road between their gates, and most departed from them.

  • The model Chinese city joined its gates with a cross or a T of main streets.
    A study of the forms of Chinese walled cities gives as the most regular plan a cross of two streets joining the four gates, and as the next a T, where the street running north from the south gate meets the east-west thoroughfare.
  • Most Chinese city plans avoided a street running straight from one gate to the opposite one.
    The same study finds that the majority of plans show a clear preference for avoiding an uninterrupted thoroughfare from gate to gate, deflecting the main street instead.
