What is a ward, who runs it, and how many does a city have?

Sources: kotobank-edo-machinanushi (the headman, how many wards he held and where he lived), kotobank-machiyakunin (the monthly duty, the ward's costs and the city's town elders), kotobank-machikido (the ward's gate), kotobank-jobosei (the ancient capital's ward heads), lijia-zhwiki (the Ming unit of 110 households)

The ward is the street community, not a walled district. In Edo the unit was the machi, a ward of townsmen's houses along a street, and it was closed at night by its own wooden gate, the kido, set up for security in each of the city's wards. Who runs it. The ward's day-to-day business fell to its property owners in turn: the house-owning townsmen, or the agents who managed property for its landlords, took the ward's administrative work month by month. Its running costs were levied on the landowners and house-owners, and paid for, among much else, the festival, the headman's fee, the upkeep of the time bell, dredging, a share of the water-supply works and the fire brigade's standard. Above the ward stood the headman (nanushi). He was not one to a ward - for reasons of cost one headman usually governed two or three to a dozen and more wards, and there are said to have been two hundred and some of them for all of Edo. He handled the business of his wards from his own house, under the city's town elders; the post was hereditary, he was barred from any other trade, and the wards paid him a salary. As a rule he lived in one of the wards he governed. Over the whole city Edo had three town elders, of three hereditary houses, not chosen by the townsmen.

The older and the Chinese forms. The ancient Japanese capitals, built on the Chinese model, put one ward head over each ward and one superior over every four, to check the households, watch for wrongdoing and press the labor levies. The Ming registered households in units of 110 neighboring households, a li of ten groups of ten, the remaining ten being the heads. On that unit a city of 600 households would make five or six - the record's arithmetic, since the page does not say how the unit was applied inside a city's walls.

How many wards a provincial city has is not on record. What the record does fix is the shape: a ward is a street's worth of houses between gates, several wards share a headman who lives among them, and there is no wall round any of them.

The rule the map follows: a city's commoner ground is divided into wards, each the houses along one block-length of street, about 400 ft , with a ward gate across the street at each end and none at the ward's back (accurate). One headman's house stands for every few wards, inside one of them, a larger house among the others and not a separate office. The number of wards follows from the city's street length rather than from a sourced count; for a city of about 600 households, a dozen or two is the order the drawn blocks give, and that is a guess.
