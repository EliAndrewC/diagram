Where do the shops stand, and where does the poor housing go?

Sources: nagaya-jawiki; the first entry on this page for the street-front shop and the back-lane tenement; the GM's setting notes for the merchant and laborer wealth bands; this project's calibration against the three drawn provincial cities

The pattern is the one the first entry already quotes: the middling and better merchant houses kept an independent shop on the main street, while the rest of the townsfolk and the craftsmen rented in the back lanes. So a street grid cuts a quarter into blocks, the businesses line the paved streets, and the residential majority jams the deep interior, reached by alleys rather than by streets; that those alleys were unpaved gravel or plank rests on general reading and on no page read. The more mercantile the quarter, the more streets it wants.

The two commoner quarters are drawn as opposites on purpose. Merchant housing is varied and roomy - the very rich behind compound walls, the rich in large houses, the rest in small ones - while the laborers' is uniform, small and jammed, with a clear minority of wealthier master laborers taking the prime back-street lots. The wealth bands themselves are the setting's own, not a finding of ours: of a provincial city's merchant households about 8% are very rich, 12% rich, and the remaining 80% poor or middling, and about one laborer household in eight is a master laborer.

The rule the map follows - frontage: almost every shop and merchant building stands within about 255 ft of a street, and most laborer and burakumin dwellings do not - they pack the block cores, off the street, which is where the alleys reach them. The exception is the larger master-laborer home, drawn at 6 to 20% of the laborer stock (a generous band around the setting's one-in-eight, because the big homes are limited to the frontage there is room for) and placed on back-street frontage with room around it. Samurai may front their streets; a merchant building counts as a business and as a dwelling at once. Merchant homes are spread: their median gap to the nearest neighbor is at least 1.3 times the laborers', so a merchant quarter is not cut into shallow strips by too many streets - the walled compounds need deep block cores.

The rule the map follows - alleys: an alley is held to a street's rules - it never runs over a building and never crosses the wall or the moat - and it earns its length by uniquely serving dwellings: each dwelling is credited only to the lane nearest it, at about one uniquely served dwelling per 90 ft of the alley's own length. That fails a lane running off into a half-empty corner and a redundant lane laid beside one that already serves the block, both. The rule of thumb is one lane per block: a block interior wants a single spine, not a cross-arm the spine already reaches, and never an alley shadowing a street a few tens of feet away. An alley continuing past its last building out to a ward gate is providing gate access rather than block service, so that terminal stretch is not counted against it. And no large cluster of dwellings - more than about thirty - may sit with no street or alley within about 285 ft : every deep block, including any space-filling band between two quarters, is laced with alleys so that each dwelling can be reached.
