City wards and the gates that shut them at night (machi, kido)

A Japanese city of the Edo period housed its townsmen in wards (machi), each the houses facing each other across one street, run by its own house-owners and shut at night by its own wooden gate, the kido, set up for its security. The gates stood open from about 4 in the morning to about 10 at night, and each was kept by two watchmen whom the ward paid and who lived in a small hut built against it.

China had walled its wards once, in the great capitals of the Tang, and under the Song dynasty the walls came down as shops opened on the street. Later Chinese cities reached the answer Edo did: Ming and Qing Beijing hung more than a thousand palings across its streets and lanes, opened early and shut late. On this project's reading of these pages, in both traditions a city at night was a mesh of barred gates at the mouths of its streets and lanes, not a set of fenced wards.

A ward was a community as much as a place, with its own officers and its own purse.

  • A ward was the two sides of one street.
    In Kyoto, from the war of the Northern and Southern Courts in the fourteenth century on, the houses facing each other across a street made one ward, and that two-sided ward became the townsmen's unit of life, and out of the ruins of the Onin war it took form everywhere. The size of the block a ward's houses stood on is at City streets and blocks: the grid, the block and how wide the streets are (machiwari).
  • The ward's house-owners ran it, month by month.
    The house-owning townsmen, or the agents who managed property for its landlords, took the ward's administrative work in turn each month.
  • The ward paid its own costs, its gate among them.
    Its running costs were levied on the landowners and house-owners and paid for, among much else, the festival, the headman's fee, the upkeep of the time bell, dredging, a share of the water-supply works and the fire brigade's standard. The gate too was kept up out of the ward's levy, and the ward paid its keepers.
  • In Edo a headman governed several wards, from his own house.
    The headman (nanushi) was not one to a ward: for reasons of cost one usually governed two or three to a dozen and more, and there are said to have been two hundred and some in all of Edo. He did his wards' business at his own house, under the city's town elders. As a rule he lived in one of the wards he governed.
  • The headman's post was hereditary, and his wards paid him.
    He could take up no other trade, and the wards paid him a salary.
  • Edo had three town elders over the whole city.
    They came from three hereditary houses and were not chosen by the townsmen.
  • We found no count of a provincial city's wards.
    The dictionaries and encyclopedias we read give Edo's headmen, but none counts the wards of a provincial castle town or a Chinese prefectural city.

The ward's gate stood across its street, and a city's order at night rested on it.

  • A ward gate stood on the street, at the boundary between one block and the next.
    So the dictionaries define the Edo ward gate.
  • Castle towns had ward gates too, shut at night.
    Each town division had its fences and gates, shut at night and kept by gatekeepers.
  • A large town set a gate to every block, a small one a gate to several.
    The gates stood one to a block in the large towns and one to several blocks in the small and middle-sized towns.
  • How wide was a ward gate?
    About 12 to 15 ft. One encyclopedia gives an Edo gate as double doors hung between posts about 2 ken (~12 ft) apart, with a fence or board fence at either side running to the road's edge; another gives a large central gate 2.5 ken (~15 ft) wide as the way from block to block. Both measures are of great cities, the one of Edo and the other from an entry on towns such as Edo, Kyoto and Osaka, and none measures a provincial town's gate.
  • The gate stood open by day and shut at about ten at night.
    By day it was shut only to cut off traffic when a criminal fled into the block or a quarrel broke out. At the hour of yotsu, about 10 pm, the keeper shut it, and after that people passed by the wickets to its left and right, once the keepers had questioned them.
  • Two keepers lived at each gate, in a hut of about 6 by 9 ft.
    The rule was that a keeper lived in and had no wife or children, and the hut was fixed at 6 by 9 shaku (~6 by 9 ft), its eaves 1 jo (~10 ft) high.
  • A ward had a gate at each end of its main street, the keepers' hut at one and the ward's watch house at the other.
    Both were built against the gates, one at each end, and each ward bore their running cost. How many watch houses a city kept is at How many watch houses and guard boxes did a city keep?, and a town's night watch at Did a town bar its street at night, and who kept the watch?.
  • Edo's alleys had gates of their own, shut at six in the evening.
    The gate at an alley's mouth (roji-kido) was opened at daybreak and shut at about 6 in the evening, its key held by the tenements' landlord or by the month's duty-holder.
  • The gates had a large part in keeping Edo's order.
    Shutting the gates at key points kept criminals from fleeing and stopped a riot spreading to other districts.
  • How many gates divided Edo?
    Many thousands, by one English essay, which describes Edo's Low City as divided by many thousands of internal checkpoints, all shut at night.

A castle town also held the samurai's districts and the temples', and the pages we read describe walls round the castle and round single estates, not round whole districts.

  • In a typical castle town, the samurai lived in several districts, with townsmen's strips between.
    A typical pattern put the townsmen in wards forming narrow strips that separated different groups of samurai, usually along the major thoroughfares, where their services were most convenient for all.
  • Each samurai estate had its own wall.
    A samurai residence was one enclosed by hedges, walls and gates, in a castle town whose ground was divided into warrior land and townsmen's land. In Edo each daimyo estate was surrounded by a perimeter wall broken by just one or two gates.
  • Was a whole samurai district ever walled?
    No page we read describes a wall round a samurai district rather than round its estates; that none stood is this project's reading of those walls being the houses' own.
  • Many castle towns gathered their temples in a line at the outer edge, for defense.
    The temple quarter (teramachi) was placed together and in a continuous line at the key points of a town's outer edge, for its military value in an emergency, and before the Edo period to form a defensive line against attack.
  • Was each temple precinct walled?
    That each temple compound in the quarter was its own walled enclosure is a GUESS: no page we read speaks of each precinct's wall.
  • Was the town before a great temple's gate walled?
    The temple-gate town (monzenmachi) grew along both sides of the pilgrimage road, its inns and shops serving pilgrims and sightseers, and in Edo and many castle towns the town gates shut at night. That a temple-gate town had such gates and no wall, a wall choking the trade it lived on, is this project's reading.
  • A different kind of temple town was ringed by a moat and rampart.
    The same reference sets the temple-gate town apart from the jinaicho, a town built round a temple, its monks' and followers' houses laid out in planned blocks and the whole ringed by a moat and earthen rampart for defense.
  • Were the castle, the walled compounds and the outer earthwork a city's only interior walls?
    They are the only ones the pages we read describe, but none sets out which enclosures a city did and did not wall.

Chinese cities had walled their wards a thousand years before, and the first Japanese capitals were built on their model; then China took the walls down.

  • The walled ward was a feature of the great early capitals.
    Tang Chang'an, the Silla royal capital, Japan's Heian-kyo and the Western Xia's Xingqing were all capitals built on the ward system.
  • The ancient Japanese capitals put a head over each ward.
    Built on the Chinese model, they set one ward head over each ward and one superior over every four, to check the households, watch for wrongdoing and press the taxes and labor levies.
  • How many walled wards did Tang Chang'an have?
    About 110, by the English encyclopedia's count, beside two markets.
  • Chang'an's wards were large, and walled about 10 ft high.
    The smallest covered 68 acres and the largest 233, and the walls round each averaged 9 to 10 ft high.
  • Chang'an kept a curfew, signaled by drum and gong.
    Its markets opened at noon to 300 strokes on a drum and closed before dusk to 300 beats on a gong, the curfew's signal, and only at one festival was the nightly curfew lifted so that people could leave their wards.
  • The Song dynasty took the ward walls down.
    With trade growing, shops could line the streets of residential areas rather than stand behind ward walls. The Song abolished the Tang's ward-and-market system and its night curfew, the ward walls were torn down, and the ward system was gone after the Song and Yuan.
  • Ming and Qing Beijing barred its streets with palings instead.
    In 1488 a Ming emperor ordered palings (zhalan) set up across the great streets and small lanes of the capital and its suburbs, opened early and shut late, and the inner city alone came to have more than a thousand. Each paling was manned by three foot soldiers. The commercial street Dashilan is named for the gate at its lane's entrance.
  • Ming China registered its households in units of 110.
    A li was ten groups of ten households, the remaining ten being the heads of the groups. The page does not say how the unit was applied inside a city's walls.
  • Qing law punished anyone abroad in the night watches.
    The Great Qing Code's night-curfew article gives thirty blows of the light bamboo for being out after the first watch's bell fell silent and before the fifth watch's sounded, and fifty in the second to fourth watches, roughly 9 pm to 3 am; the provincial cities and towns took a degree less.
  • Did Beijing's Bell Tower and Drum Tower signal when its gates shut?
    For Beijing's city gates, yes: they were shut each night at the setting of the watch and opened at its lightening, when the gates heard the bell and drum of the Bell and Drum Towers. Whether the lane palings shut on the same signal is not on the page we read.
