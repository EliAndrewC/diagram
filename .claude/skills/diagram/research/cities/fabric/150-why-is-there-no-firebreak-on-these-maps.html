Why is there no firebreak on these maps?

Sources: tokyo-archives-hirokoji (what the cleared strips were for and what filled them); this project's two attempts at drawing one and the decision to draw neither; the second entry on this page for what the Edo breaks actually were, hiyokechi-jawiki

The other historical answer to urban fire was the firebreak - the hiyokechi (火除地), and the widened hirokoji (広小路): an open strip of ground kept against the spread of fire. That the strip, being far too valuable to leave idle, filled up with removable stalls and theater booths and so doubled as the market and amusement ground, until Edo's hirokoji had become its entertainment districts, is what the city archive's own account says: most of the wider streets called hirokoji were laid out about the city as fire-clearance open ground, no permanent building could stand on one, and at busy bridgeheads and before the gates of well-visited temples and shrines they filled with movable stall-shops trading goods, with markets, with street performance and with reed-screened booths staging entertainments, so that many of them grew into pleasure quarters. The second entry on this page carries the scale at which those breaks were cut: whole blocks and broad streets, opened after a disaster, never gaps between individual houses.

Two ways of drawing one were tried, and both were dropped. A DRAWN plaza - a shaded clearing placed before the dwellings pack in - makes the housing flow around an invented clearing, and the result reads as a designed plaza, which is the opposite of what a firebreak is: the ABSENCE of building. A LABEL-ONLY marking - an annotation dropped onto a gap the finished map happens to leave - only names existing dead space; it adds a word rather than a feature, and getting the gaps and the label orientations to read well costs real effort for no gain. So a firebreak is not a feature of these maps, and the towers carry the fire story by themselves. That is an omission on the sheet, not a claim that these cities had no firebreaks.

The rule the map follows: no map draws a firebreak, and none marks a gap as one. A future map that genuinely needs one - a wide ceremonial fire-plaza that is itself the subject of the drawing - builds it as a real, deliberately placed feature with its own glyph; the marked-gap approach is not revived.
