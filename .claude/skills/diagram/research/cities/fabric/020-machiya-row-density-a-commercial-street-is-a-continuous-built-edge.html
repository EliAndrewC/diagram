Machiya row density: a commercial street is a continuous built edge

Sources: kyototuu-unagi (frontage ~2 ken, depth 10-12 ken),5 kyomachiya-jawiki, hiyokechi-jawiki (breaks at block scale after 1657).6 See the frontage-tax note in the entry above, udatsu-jawiki, machiya-shoka-jawiki, edo-no-kaji-jawiki

The question (GM, 2026-08-11), looking at Shiro Daika's north gate market: "Is that the correct amount of space between gate market buildings? No objection, they just look more spaced out than I expected."

What the research says. The Japanese urban shophouse (machiya) was built to the street and to its neighbors. Frontage was the taxed and traded dimension, so lots were narrow and deep:10 a Kyoto kyo-machiya typically fronted 2-3 ken12 (about 12-18 ft) on a lot running 60 ft or more back,11 and the fronts abutted, sharing party walls and a continuous eave line down the block.57 The street read as a WALL of shopfronts pierced by gateways and alley mouths, not as a row of detached boxes.58 Post-fire policy in Edo cut firebreaks (hiyokechi) and broad avenues THROUGH the fabric13 and pushed tile roofing,59 but it never spaced individual machiya apart - the breaks were at block scale, and the block itself stayed solid.14

The decision. Shops in a market row stand shoulder to shoulder along the street they front, and a row may front a street that bends.

The deliberate departure from literal reality. True machiya stood adjoining, their gable walls touching - a gap of ZERO (the shared wall proper being the nagaya's).73 We land at a median of 18 ft on the capital's north and south gate markets (9 ft on the crowded southwest strip, 48 ft on the loose roadside market at the east gate, which is a different thing and should stay loose). An 18 ft gap is enough that a reader can still count individual shops and see which way each one faces, which is the whole job of the drawing. The relative sizes stay honest; only the mortar joint is drawn wider than life.
