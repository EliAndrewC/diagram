How big is a rich merchant's walled house, and how is it laid out?

Sources: bunka-sugimoto-omoya (the Kyoto house, its plan and its wall), kyototuu-sugimoto (its site, frontage and storehouses in figures), jtb-sugimoto (its site's frontage and depth), bunka-hasegawa-omoya (the castle-town house and how it grew), matsusaka-hasegawa-bunka (its frontage at its largest, and its storehouses and walls), matsusaka-hasegawa-kanko (the storehouses' places and the lot's back edge)

Two surviving houses give the size. The Sugimoto house in Kyoto, a kimono-cloth merchant's, is among the largest town houses left in the city. Its frontage on the street is eleven and a half ken, its site about 1,200 square meters (13,000 square feet), more than half of it garden. A second page gives the site as 30 m of frontage by 52 m deep, about 100 by 170 ft, which would make it nearer 1,560 square meters; the two pages do not agree. The Hasegawa house in Matsusaka, a castle town, belonged to one of its leading merchants, a cotton wholesaler with a shop in Edo; its main house is 30.2 by 15.9 m, about 100 by 52 ft. It began as one lot, and the family bought the lots on both sides one after another, until by the end of the Edo period its frontage on the street was about 34 ken, some 61 m or 200 ft. So the frontage runs from about 70 ft (eleven and a half ken; 100 ft on the second Kyoto page's figure) to 200 ft, and the larger of the two stood in a castle town, not the great city: the size follows the family's fortune, not the settlement's rank.

The plan: a shop-house on the street, storehouses at the back. Neither house stands back from the street inside a walled court: the Sugimoto house puts its shop at the street front, as the description that follows shows, and no page read on the Hasegawa house places it behind a court. The Sugimoto main house is a shop at the street front joined to the living quarters behind it by the entrance hall, the three storehouses stand in a row behind the main house, and a high wall runs round the estate. Its storehouses are two-story earthen kura of about 38, 24 and 19 square meters (400, 250 and 205 square feet), set in a hook toward the north side of the main house. At the Hasegawa house one storehouse stands at the front, beside the entrance, and four more stand side by side at the back of the lot. Behind them runs the back-to-back drain that is also the ward's boundary. Its protected buildings include a front wall and an inner gate. A great merchant's house is therefore three to five kura, a wall round the sides and back of the lot and along its street front where the shop does not fill it, and the house itself as the street face; its gate is a gate in that wall or the shop's own entrance. The gatehouse with flanking rooms (nagayamon) was a mark of rank granted to few townsmen (Why are there only one or two walled merchant compounds); the Hasegawa house's list of protected buildings names none, and no page read on the Sugimoto house mentions one.

How many such houses a town of 1,200 held is not on record. Where the wall may stand is Where may a merchant's compound wall stand?, and the storehouses of the street at large are How many fireproof storehouses stand behind a street?.

The rule the map follows: a rich merchant's walled house fronts a commercial street: its main house, about 100 ft along the street and 50 ft deep , stands at the street edge with the shop at the front, and the lot runs back about 170 ft to the back of the block. Its frontage is rolled per estate between about 70 and 200 ft , a degree along a continuum (accurate at both ends). A wall runs round the sides and back of the lot; three to five kura stand at the back in a row or a hook, and in some estates one more stands at the front beside the entrance - a knob between the two attested plans. There is no gatehouse and no court set back from the street.
