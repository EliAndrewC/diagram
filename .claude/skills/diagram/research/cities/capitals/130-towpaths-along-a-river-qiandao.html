Towpaths along a river (qiandao)

A towpath is the path along a river or canal bank from which teams hauled boats upstream. In China it is the qiandao, and the best known runs beside the Eastern Zhejiang Canal at Shaoxing, raised as an earth embankment in 815 and close to a hundred li long. Part of it stands not on the bank at all but out in the water, a causeway of stone slabs on stone piers.

A towpath is not a road. Water carried bulk goods far more cheaply than carts or porters, so the river was itself the trunk route, and the path on its bank served the boats. Of the three ways a river city's waterside might carry - a road beside the river, a towpath and a landing's quay - the towpath and the quay are the ones the sources show.

  • Water carried bulk goods far more cheaply than land.
    Over long distances a Chinese junk may have had a nine- or tenfold cost advantage over human and animal carriage, and in Japan the tribute rice sent to Edo from every region went by river boat rather than overland.
  • Did a trunk road run beside a navigable river?
    The cost of carriage suggests not: a highway shadowing a navigable river would carry what the boats already carried more cheaply, so roads ran where the water did not. That a road beside such a river was redundant is this project's reasoning from the cost gap, not a source's statement.
  • On the Tokaido, the Oi River had no bridge and no ferry.
    In the Edo period no bridge was built over the Oi and no ferry was allowed, so travelers were carried across by porters. The defense of Edo is the usual explanation; the river's great volume and swift current, and the profits the crossing trade brought the river offices and post towns, are argued as well. That a road met a river to cross it, not to follow it, is this project's generalization from this case.
  • Shaoxing's towpath was raised in 815.
    A museum account says Meng Jian, the surveillance commissioner of Eastern Zhejiang, raised it as an earth embankment in 815, and the Chinese encyclopedia gives its whole length as close to a hundred li, some 50 km (~31 miles), though it calls Meng Jian's work of 815 a large reconstruction of an older path.
  • A towpath ran either on the bank or out in the water.
    Shaoxing's towpath takes two forms, one on the bank of the canal and one out in the watercourse, parallel to the bank. The Chinese encyclopedia divides the second again, into a solid causeway and a deck on stone piers.
  • How high above the water did the stone-pier towpath stand?
    A news report puts each of its stone slabs, laid on the piers, about 0.5 m (~20 in) above the water. No other page we could read gives the height.
  • Why did a river need a towpath?
    A boat going downstream drifts with the current, and one going upstream has to be pulled; the towpath is where the haulers walked. It served the boats rather than competing with them, and so ran on one bank and only as far as the boats went. This is this project's reading of what a towpath was for; no page we read sets it out.
  • A river landing, the kashi, was a place, not a route.
    A Japanese dictionary defines the kashi as the bank of a river, and in particular the place where cargo was loaded onto and taken off boats, and adds that administratively a kashi was a town or a village. Its quay served the landing's docks, storehouses and brokers rather than carrying through traffic, which is this project's reading. The wharf itself is at The wharf's working face.
