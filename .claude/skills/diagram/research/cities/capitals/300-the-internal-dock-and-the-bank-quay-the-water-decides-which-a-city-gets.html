The internal dock and the bank quay: the WATER decides which a city gets

Sources: horikawa-nagoya-jawiki (the Horikawa cut from the castle to the sea); "Edo dug nine funairi-bori" is this page's reading, on no page read, kawagoe-gokashi-jawiki, smtrc-kawagoe-shuun, kashi-jawiki, kotobank-kashi, chuoku-nihonbashi-kashi, mizu-no-bunka-32-yanagawa, nippon-com-matsue-horikawa, hanzheng-jie-zhwiki, ndl-crd-hikifune, coastalwiki-siltation

The question (GM 2026-08-09): Nagahara and Minami let barges float INTO the city - water gate, dock basin. Shiro Daika unloads everything at a riverbank quay, and so far only grain. Would a city of this size have an internal dock? Is not drawing one unusual, or did some cities have them and some not?

Both forms are THE standard form - of two different kinds of water. Which one a city gets reads off its river, not off its size or rank.

China first. Canal-country cities let the boats in: Suzhou's Pan Gate is the paired land-and-water gate - one arch for the road, one for the barges89 - opening the wall between the Grand Canal, the moat, and the internal canal grid. That is exactly the form the two sibling cities already draw (the Suzhou pattern: canal communicates with the moat, the moat's river junction is the single navigation mouth). But it belongs to FLAT DELTA ground where the water stands at grade and near-still - in canal country the canal is the street grid.184 A Chinese city on a live river keeps its wharf ON the bank instead: boats moor along the shore and the goods go up the landing into a bankside commercial district.183

Japan drew the same split and named both halves. The funairi-bori (boat-entry canal) is the internal-dock form - Edo dug nine of them,65 Nagoya cut the Horikawa from the castle to the sea,66 Matsue and Yanagawa are laced with them - and each sits on delta or lakeside ground: Yanagawa on the Chikugo's silt beside a tidal sea, its channels drawn from above a tide barrier, Matsue's channels spreading out from its castle moat into the town.182 The river towns' form is the kashi (bank landing): a quay on the riverbank worked by its wholesalers, often specialized by cargo (rice kashi, salt kashi, the small-boat kashi), with the warehouses, wholesalers and markets forming a commercial settlement along the bank.181 Kawagoe's trade ran through its Shingashi-river landings, the outer port that replaced the older Irima-river landings, which a belt of low wet ground had set off from the castle town.180

Why a live river refuses the basin. (1) Current and seasonal stage: a still-water basin cut into a flowing bank silts up and must be dredged, and stands open to the river's rise.210 (2) A towpath river is a HAULED river130 - barges are dragged upstream along the bank, which is bank work; a basin serves boats that arrive under pole or sail on still water. (3) At a siege-grade wall the moat is a military work: Nagahara and Minami's moats communicate with their rivers at grade, so a barge slips river -> moat -> canal through the water gate, but Shiro Daika's ring stands off the bank behind two SLUICED leats whose whole job is to control the level. Opening that plumbing to navigation would surrender the control that makes the wet moat work.

Verdict: not an omission, and not a size threshold. Still, at-grade water (delta, lake, canal country) gets the water gate + dock basin; a live current past the wall gets the kashi quay + towpath. Shiro Daika's diagonal towpath reach takes the second, correctly.

What the quay still owes. A kashi was never only grain - the landing IS the commercial hub, and the warehouse rows, brokers, and markets belong on the bank top.209 The wharf-chain doctrine already plans exactly this (wharf -> domain granary -> brokers' row -> entertainment district), so the grain-only look is not the design.
