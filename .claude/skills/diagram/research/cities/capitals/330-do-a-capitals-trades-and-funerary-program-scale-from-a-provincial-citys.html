Do a capital's trades and funerary program scale from a provincial city's?

Sources: sento-jawiki (600+ sento in the Bunka era), shichiya-kotobank (the Edo pawnshop count, twice over), kasoba-jawiki (temple-precinct cremation), dongjing-menghualu-rujia (Kaifeng's patrol stations). imado-yaki-jawiki, kyoyaki-jawiki, wugou-song-cremation-rujia, hinomi-yagura-kotobank

The GM asked it plainly: "would there be the same number and size of kiln works in a capital city as in a provincial city? Same question for dye works, the common burial ground, etc." The answer, from the record: almost nothing is "same number and size," and the four features scale in four different ways. What follows is the WHY for the first two classes; the other two - what grows faster than the city and what stays one to a seat - are the next question, and the features whose form changes instead of their count, with the official kilns, are the one after.

The four scaling classes, and why each feature lands where it does.

  1. LINEAR - per-capita services multiply, they do not grow. Bathhouses are the cleanest case in the whole record: Edo in the Bunka era ran over 600 sento for ~1.1M residents, one per ~2,000, and each was an ordinary shophouse with a fuel yard. A capital of 12,400 therefore gets six, all the same size - on this record's reading of the count, the bath did not become grander, there were simply more of them. Pawnbroking is the same shape at a startling ratio (Edo: 2,700+ pawnshops, one per ~400 residents - pawnbroking was the commoner's bank; the count is the recorded one and the ratio is this record's own arithmetic against an Edo population taken from elsewhere), which our maps DISCLOSE as a representative draw: we draw two or three with their pledge-kura courts and imply the rest in the shop rows. Fire towers are linear in built AREA rather than population, because the rule in both records is a spacing, not a head count: Song Kaifeng posted fire-watch towers on high ground and a military patrol station every 300 paces; Edo ran roughly one per ten blocks.

  2. SUBLINEAR - works CONSOLIDATE. A kiln is a quarter, not a shop: some fifty kiln households at Imado outside Edo, kiln houses concentrated at Awataguchi on Kyoto's eastern side, with others scattered to the east and north - firing clustered where (this record's reading, not the pages') the clay and the fuel came in, and the rest scattered. So a capital's second works belongs BESIDE the first, sharing the pit. Cremation splits the two traditions: Southern Song Lin'an ran its cremation through crematoria by the dozen - several dozen at the least, sixteen of them once demolished and fourteen ordered restored - while Edo in 1667 moved the precinct crematoria of some twenty Asakusa and Shitaya temples together to one bakufu-designated site - our capital follows the Japanese form: ONE cremation ground, sized up, not four. Burial grounds likewise accreted AREA faster than SITES.

Where the record is thinnest - each of these is a guess: the oil-press count (an unsourced impression that Osaka's oil-guild concentration makes it sublinear - no page read says so - but no count survives) and the capital kiln count of exactly two.

The one place our canon and the research disagree, and the reconciliation. Our maps want 2-4 graveyards, and the research says a capital carries 3-5 - but our temple doctrine gives EVERY temple a parish ground, and a capital has seven or eight temples. The two rules are counting different things: the research's "burial grounds" are the community's grounds (parish + extramural common), while our maps also draw each temple's own precinct plot. A capital therefore shows more cemeteries than the band, legitimately.
