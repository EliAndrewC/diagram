How a capital differs from a provincial city: what multiplies, what changes form, and what only a capital has

In the setting a domain capital is, in its trade, a provincial city made larger, so most of what a provincial city has, a capital simply has more of. Edo shows the pattern: over 600 public bathhouses in the early nineteenth century, in the Edo period, for a million or more inhabitants. But not everything grows in step. Works that need a site - kilns, crematoria - gathered into one quarter rather than multiplying. Permanent theaters and schools gathered in the capital far beyond its share of the population. A few things stayed one to a seat, whatever its size.

A capital also changes the form of some things rather than their number - the dyers become a street, the ruling house's tombs a precinct - and it holds a few things a provincial city has no version of at all: the daimyo's court and senior retainers, the Emperor's magistrate, the head temples of the domain's religious orders.

What reverses in a capital

  • A castle town's senior retainers lived close to the castle, in walled houses.
    In a castle town, basically, the higher a retainer's status the closer to the castle their house stood, and the karo, a daimyo's senior retainers, basically kept their houses near the castle. The upper samurai built houses enclosed by hedges and walls inside the middle moat, near the castle, with the foot soldiers' row houses around the outer edge.
  • The setting staffs its capital with the domain's senior officials.
    The daimyo, the councilors, the domain ministers and their deputies, the high-rank clerks, the castle guards and the daimyo's household retainers are the capital's samurai. They average about 35 koku a year against about 15 in a provincial city, so a capital's samurai are senior-heavy; what that does to the mix of their houses is at The capital's samurai are SENIOR-heavy.
  • A capital has no governor: its ruler is the daimyo.
    The setting puts a provincial governor, with six provincial ministers, in each provincial city; the capital is the daimyo's own seat.
  • A city's farmers live outside it.
    The setting's cities stand on or beside fertile land that is farmed, but the farmers who work it live in the villages and hamlets of the surrounding county, not in the city, and this holds for provincial cities and the capital alike.

What simply multiplies

  • Edo had over 600 bathhouses, about one to every 2,000 inhabitants.
    In the Bunka era, 1804-1818, Edo, with some 1,000,000 to 1,200,000 inhabitants, is said to have had more than 600 bathhouses (sento). The ratio is this page's arithmetic. No page we read says what kind of building an Edo bathhouse was, or where it kept its fuel. How many a smaller place keeps is at How many bathhouses does a place keep?
  • Edo's pawnbrokers' guild counted 2,731 shops, about one to every 400 inhabitants.
    Pawnbroking was the common household's short-term lending. Two published encyclopedias count Edo's pawnshops independently - 2,700 in the eighteenth century, 2,731 in the guild in 1723 - with 628 in Kyoto in 1699 and some 2,400 in Osaka in the nineteenth century. The one-to-400 ratio is this page's arithmetic, against the population the bathhouse count gives.
  • Fire watch was set by spacing, so it grew with a city's area, not its people.
    Song Kaifeng kept a military patrol post every 300 paces in each ward and fire-watch towers on high ground. Edo, in the Kyoho era (1716-1736), set up one fire-watch tower to every ten blocks, and a block without one put a fire-watch ladder on its watch house.
  • A castle town's trade wards were named for their trades.
    Merchants and craftsmen were divided up by trade and resettled in the castle town by compulsion, and place names such as Gofuku-machi (the drapers) and Kaji-machi (the smiths) survive from it. What gathers into one place

Works that need a site of their own - clay and fuel, a fire, a burial field - do not multiply with the city the way shops do.

  • Potters gathered into a kiln quarter.
    Some fifty households made Imado ware outside Edo at the end of the Edo period, and Kyoto's kiln houses were concentrated at Awataguchi, with others scattered to the east and north of the city. The pages give the concentration and the counts; that the kilns clustered where the clay and the fuel came in is this page's reading.   • Edo gathered its temple crematoria onto one site.
    By about 1650 almost every temple in Edo's Asakusa and Shitaya districts had a crematory in its precinct, and in 1667 those of some twenty temples were moved together to one site the shogunate designated, near the Kozukappara execution ground. Cremation grounds are at Cremation grounds and bone mounds (kasoba).
  • Southern Song Lin'an kept several dozen crematoria.
    Lin'an, the Southern Song capital, had at least several dozen; when sixteen were demolished, the emperor ordered fourteen of them restored.
  • Did a growing city's burial grounds grow in area rather than in number?
    No page we could read says. The one Japanese page found describes burials laid over one another in a ground of fixed extent, which is re-use rather than growth.

What gathers in the capital beyond its size

  • Song Kaifeng had more than fifty roofed theaters in its pleasure quarters.
    Kaifeng's pleasure quarters (wazi) held more than fifty roofed theaters (goulan), large and small, the largest holding several thousand. Hangzhou, the later Song capital, had no fewer than seventeen pleasure quarters by one memoir's count and twenty-three in the other's list, and its North Wa alone held thirteen theaters.
  • Where did a Song provincial city's players perform?
    No page we read says; that they made do with touring players on temple ground is a GUESS.
  • Edo licensed exactly three kabuki theaters, while villages kept stages of their own.
    Edo's many early playhouses were cut to four and, from 1714, to three licensed houses, the Nakamura-za, Ichimura-za and Morita-za. Many villages kept a permanent stage in the precinct of the village shrine, and Japan's oldest surviving playhouse, the Kanamaruza, stands in the town before the gate of the Kotohira shrine. By opposite means, Song China and Japan both put the roofed playhouses in the great city; that concentration marks a capital is this page's reading, not a rule a source states.   • Almost every domain founded a school (hanko) on its own land.
    At their height there were 255 domain schools, founded in almost every domain, on domain land at the domain's expense, and martial arts were encouraged in them beside letters; Aizu's Nisshinkan and Choshu's Meirinkan are among the best known. What stays one to a seat

  • The Song throne ordered a pauper cemetery (louzeyuan) in every sizable place.
    The emperor Huizong, on his minister Cai Jing's proposal, founded the louzeyuan in 1104 to bury unclaimed and destitute remains on government land, and every later dynasty kept it. The Song's own history orders them added, following each county, in every walled town, stockade, garrison post or market of a thousand households or more that had a supervising official - which this page reads as one to a seat, and one to any sizable place below it. The forms these grounds took are at Burial grounds of towns and cities, and paupers' grounds (yizhong).
  • Did Japan set a pauper ground one to a seat?
    No page we read describes one. The nearest is an execution ground: Kozukappara, Edo's northern execution ground, saw more than 200,000 put to death, their bodies left under a covering of earth, and early in the nineteenth century it became a great cremation and burial ground for the north of Edo.

    At Iwatsuki, a domain's castle town, the lord placed the first bronze bell in 1671 as the bell of Iwatsuki castle. How many bells a capital keeps is at How many time bells does a capital keep?

What changes its form

  • Dyers gathered in a dyers' ward (Konya-machi) on running water.
    Yamatokoriyama's dyers' ward has a channel down the middle of its street, flowing out of the castle moat, in which dyed cloth and thread were rinsed. Edo's Kanda Konya-cho was named for the indigo dyers standing eave to eave there from the early Edo period, and its bolts of cloth bleaching in rows on fine days, like banners, were a famous sight.
  • Did the dyers' ward stand downstream of the town?
    No page we read says so; that it did is a GUESS.
  • The Qing's official weaving offices stood in three cities, each with its own wares.
    The Jiangning weaving office, set up by the Qing government at present-day Nanjing, and the offices of Suzhou and Hangzhou were together the Three Jiangnan Weaving Offices; Jiangning made the court's red satins and gold brocade, Hangzhou its silks, and Suzhou its blue cotton cloth.
  • A daimyo house's tombs became a precinct of their own.
    Zuihoden, the mausoleum of Date Masamune, founder of the Sendai domain, stands on Kyogamine, across a meander of the Hirose river from the site of Sendai castle's inner bailey, with the mausolea of the later Date lords Tadamune and Tsunamune and other Date mausolea around it, on a wooded hill whose trees escaped felling. A great house also kept a family temple in the town, as the Tokugawa kept Kan'eiji and Zojoji. A city's temples are at How many temples a city keeps, and its temple quarter (teramachi).
  • Where was a senior retainer's lineage buried?
    No page we read says.
  • Beijing's glazed-tile works stood at the city's edge, enlarged for the palace.
    The Liulichang was one of the five great works of the Ming Ministry of Works. An official kiln firing glazed tile had stood there since the Yuan, and it was enlarged when the Ming built the inner city and its palaces; it reached from the inner-city moat to the site of today's Beijing Normal University, and fired bricks, tiles and vessels for the inner court. That bulky building ceramic followed the building site is this page's reading.
  • The Nabeshima moved their domain kiln to a guarded valley.
    The Nabeshima domain moved its kiln to Okawachiyama and set up a barrier, under strict control, to keep its techniques and its craftsmen from leaving. An official kiln thus took one of two forms: a building-tile works at the capital's edge, or a fine-ware kiln sent away to a guarded site.

What only a capital has

Eight things in the setting's capital have no smaller version in a provincial city.

  • Only a capital holds the Imperial Magistrate's compound.
    In the setting's own notes, every domain has one Imperial Magistrate, appointed from outside the domain and stationed at its capital, in a walled court and residence that is an outside power's ground: How big is the Imperial Magistrate's compound?
  • Only a capital holds the compounds of the ruling house's lineages.
    The setting's cosmopolitan lineages - the great houses of a domain not tied to one province - are based at its capital: How big is a lineage's compound in the capital?
  • The setting's capital draws the domain's retired samurai elders.
    The setting makes the capital the domain's schooling-and-retirement magnet, which raises its count of resident samurai. The samurai families of the county towns send their children to the provincial city or the domain capital for schooling, and their elders retire there.
  • The domain's religious orders have their sovereign temples and grand abbots in the capital.
    Every domain capital has at least two grand abbots, and a religious order's Grand Abbot serves in its sovereign temple in the capital, the head house of the whole order in the domain.
  • Some capitals have a witch hunter's office.
    In the setting's history an emperor once ordered a witch hunter placed in every domain capital, and some of those orders were later rescinded.
  • The domain school, the Emperor's granaries and the wharf granaries stand only in a capital.
    Each is a section of its own: The domain school is the hanko, The Emperor's granaries are separate and The wharf is the collecting end.
