How many time bells does a capital keep, and where do they hang?

Sources: iwatsuki-tokinokane-jawiki (a castle's one bell and its ringers), matsushiro-shoro (what a domain's bell did), tokinokane-list-jawiki (one bell to a castle town across Japan), kokucho-tokinokane-jawiki (Edo's bell moved out of the castle, and the ring of bells that relayed it), xian-gulou-zhwiki and xian-zhonglou-zhwiki (the Chinese pair of separate towers)

Every walled seat on these maps draws one bell-and-drum tower. The question here is whether a capital, the largest seat, should draw more.

A castle town kept one time bell, and it was the domain's. At Iwatsuki the fourth lord set up a bronze bell in 1671 "as Iwatsuki castle's bell", at one spot in the town; in 1687 four low-ranking men rang it, and it was struck twelve times a day. At Matsushiro the domain's rule was a great bell struck every two hours, day and night, by which retainers and townspeople alike told the time, and which also gave the alarm for fire; the first lord set it up in 1624 together with a fire lookout. The list of Japan's surviving time bells gives one to a place - Morioka, Hanamaki, Iwatsuki, Kawagoe, Osaka's town bell - and several only for Edo.

Edo is the exception, and a metropolis. Its first time bell hung inside the castle; in 1626 it was moved out into the town because it disturbed the shogun's quarters, and the castle used a drum for its own time signal from then on. The town bell rang by the castle drum, and the other bells rang after it. That relay served the shogun's own city, not a domain's seat; nothing read gives a second bell to any domain's castle town.

China's larger form is two towers, not two of the same. Xi'an built a bell tower in 1384, standing opposite the drum tower. The drum tower, built in 1380, stands west of the bell tower, and the two reported the hours, one by drum and one by bell.

What this means for the map. One signal to a seat is accurate for a capital of the size these maps draw: a domain's castle town kept one time bell, and the multiplied bells of Edo belong to the shogun's city, far larger than the seats these maps draw (a comparison this project makes, not one a source here states). The castle's own drum is inside the castle and is not drawn, by the blank-castle rule. The one real alternative is the Chinese pair - a separate drum tower and bell tower facing each other along the main street - which is a knob: a capital draws either the single combined tower or the pair, rolled from the map's seed.

The rule the map follows: a capital draws exactly one time signal station at its main crossing - either the single combined bell-and-drum tower every walled seat draws (about 36 ft square), or, as a per-capital roll, a drum tower and a bell tower of that size facing each other along the main street, one on each side of the crossing. It never draws a second station elsewhere in the city.
