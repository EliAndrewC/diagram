Rice storehouses and the rice brokers in a capital (kura, fudasashi)

A domain's tax rice was paid in to the lord's granary, and the lord paid their samurai stipends out of it, in rice. So a domain's capital was the collecting and paying end of the rice trade. The selling end lay elsewhere: in Japan the domains sold their tax rice at Osaka, from warehouses there, to rice brokers at auction. In the setting, the stipends of a capital's 800 or so paid samurai come to about 28,000 koku of rice a year, and the Emperor keeps granaries in each domain capital as well.

The shogun's own capital, Edo, shows what such a store looked like. The shogunate's granary there, the Asakusa Okura, was built out into the Sumida River in 1620, on about 30 acres, with eight boat channels cut into it along some 2,000 ft of bank. In the block in front of it stood the rice brokers, the fudasashi, a guild of 109 merchant houses who cashed the samurai's stipend rice for them and lent against it.

  • How much of a domain's rice was paid out as stipends, and how much sold?
    Under the stipend system a retainer drew from the domain's granary the tax rice the domain had collected, and was paid a stipend in rice by the lord. A domain also sold tax rice, at Osaka. The rice the domains sent to Osaka between the 1690s and the 1820s is estimated at 30 to 45 percent of a domain's tax revenue, so the larger part was spent somewhere other than Osaka. In the Hagi domain's budget of 1754 the retainers' stipend rice was the largest item, at 32 percent, against 22 percent shipped to Osaka. Other domains and years differed.
  • A kurayashiki was a lord's warehouse at the market, not a store in the lord's own capital.
    The domains' kurayashiki at Osaka combined a warehouse with a residence; from them the tax rice was auctioned to brokers, who were given rice bills, notes promising rice, which were traded in their turn. Most stood near Nakanoshima and the Tosabori and Edobori rivers, for the boats. Osaka is said to have had 80 of them in the 1670s and 125 in the 1840s. So this project does not use the word for a store in a lord's own capital: it names the selling end, and the capital was the collecting end.
  • The shogunate kept the rice it paid out at a granary on the river, the Asakusa Okura.
    It was built in 1620 by cutting down a hill and filling in the Sumida River, to hold the rice the shogunate gathered from its own lands and elsewhere. The tax rice came in by ship and was paid out as stipend rice to the shogun's own retainers. Its ground ran about 625 m (~2,051 ft) along the river, with eight boat channels, on about 12 ha (~30 acres) in all.
  • Asakusa's storehouses stood in rows facing the boat channels, and the boats unloaded into them.
    The channels were numbered from upstream, and the rice storehouses stood in rows facing them; the rice sent by boat from the shogun's lands was unloaded there. The district in front of them took its name, Kuramae, "before the storehouses", from the granary.
  • Were the riverside storehouses built up against floods?
    No page we read says. The pages on the Asakusa granary describe its channels and its storehouses, and none speaks of a raised floor, a stone-faced bank or flood.
  • The shogunate kept its reserve rice, against war and famine, inside castles.
    This castle rice was stored in the shogun's own castles and those of the shogun's hereditary vassal lords, and it passed with the castle to the next lord, as the castle's arms did. So Edo kept its two kinds of rice apart: the reserve in the castles, the rice it paid out on the river at Asakusa.
  • The shogun's intendant's office at Takayama kept its rice storehouse inside its own grounds.
    The shogun's intendant's office at Takayama, which ran the province of Hida, kept in its grounds a storehouse that held the tax rice of the villages around it.

The setting adds a store Japan did not have: the Emperor's.

  • In the setting, the Emperor's tax grain is stored and spent in each domain capital.
    Most of what is collected in Imperial taxes never travels to the Imperial capital; it is stored in the Imperial granaries of each domain capital and used there, among other things for the stipends of the Emperor's own staff. The Imperial Magistrate oversees them, with granary staff of their own.
  • Why are the Emperor's granaries kept apart from the daimyo's castle?
    The setting's notes put the Emperor's granaries in each domain capital but do not say where in it they stand, and describe none kept inside the daimyo's castle. What follows is this project's reasoning. The Emperor's granaries are a working store that pays stipends, like the Asakusa granary, not a war reserve like the castle rice. And they face a different threat: an invading neighbor would be unlikely to attack the Emperor's granaries, so they need protection from brigands, not from an army laying siege. A stout wall and a watch suffice, and they stand outside the castle as a modest walled compound of their own.

Edo's rice brokers were merchants, and they grew out of the granary's front.

  • The rice brokers began as the shops outside the granary where samurai waited for their rice.
    The shogun's retainers, waiting to be called for their stipend rice, rested at the tea stalls in front of the Asakusa granary and at the shopfronts of the rice dealers they sold to, and those shops came to receive and cash the rice for them. They took a commission for receiving, carrying and selling it, and lent at high interest against the stipend to come.
  • The brokers' row stood on the town side of the granary, the houses eaves to eaves.
    On the town side of the granary, the brokers and the rice wholesalers, great merchants, stood eaves to eaves.
  • The block in front of the granary also kept an open firebreak.
    Beside the brokers' houses, the block kept a firebreak, an open ground left clear. The page does not say where in the block it lay.
  • Edo's brokers formed a guild of 109 houses, bound to the place.
    By 1724 the guild's 109 houses were in three groups of 31, 47 and 31. The members agreed that a house taking up a share in the guild would have its address at Kuramae.
  • The guild posted the Kuramae rice price in front of its shops.
    Each of its three groups sent five officers a month, who were bound to check the Kuramae rice price and post it in front of the shops.
  • The brokers grew rich as lenders and middlemen.
    They stored a lord's rice for a fee and traded it for coin or receipts, lent money, and became wealthy and powerful.
  • The brokers were patrons of the kabuki theater and the pleasure district.
    Like the rest of Edo's townspeople they were frequent patrons of the kabuki theater and the Yoshiwara pleasure district. Where such a quarter stood is at its own section.
  • How many rice brokers would a domain capital keep?
    No page we read gives a count for a daimyo's castle town. Edo's 109 houses served the shogun's own retainers, and a capital in the setting pays about 800 samurai, so its brokers would be far fewer.

In the setting a samurai ministry, not a merchant, takes a share of the rice-to-coin exchange - which raises the question of who keeps the brokers' row.

  • In the setting, the Ministry of Retainers takes a cut of the exchange of the capital's stipend rice for coin.
    The ministry administers the paying of stipends: the choice of rice or coin, the reckoning by rank and the payment-day logistics. Its budget credits it with about 1,600 koku a year from the rice-to-coin exchange on the capital's 28,000 koku of stipends.
  • Who keeps the brokers' row in the setting: merchants, or the Ministry of Retainers?
    Merchants, in this project's reading. The ministry's cut covers the paying of stipends only; the contracts, the clearing of accounts, the lending against next year's stipend and a samurai household's own trading lie outside it, and merchants have grown rich on them as the fudasashi did in Edo. So the row stays a merchant street, and its brokers are the theaters' patrons, as they were in Edo.
