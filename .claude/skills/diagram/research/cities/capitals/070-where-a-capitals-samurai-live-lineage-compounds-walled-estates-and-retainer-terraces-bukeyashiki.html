Where a capital's samurai live: lineage compounds, walled estates and retainer terraces (bukeyashiki)

In a Japanese castle town of the Edo period, the ground a samurai lived on followed their standing. The shogun granted the lords' estates in Edo on a scale by their holdings, a retainer of 1,000 koku at Fukui held a walled plot of about 1,000 tsubo, some 36,000 square feet, and the lowest retainers lived in row houses beyond the samurai residences - at Shibata, eight households under one roof.

The setting's domain capital, a castle town the size of Hikone's (Domain capitals: the daimyo's castle town (jokamachi)), is staffed by the domain's senior samurai: seven in ten of its working samurai, against about a quarter in a provincial city. The heads of the ruling house's great lineages serve there too, on its council.

  • The setting's capital is staffed with the domain's senior samurai.
    Its budget notes put the daimyo, the councilors, the domain ministers and their deputies, the high-rank clerks, the castle guards and household retainers of the daimyo's retinue, and junior officials in training in the capital. Its campaign notes add that senior ranks cluster heavily in the domain capital.
  • A capital posting is prestigious even when the job is menial, which is why the capital is so rank-heavy.
    The setting's budget notes give that as the reason.
  • The daimyo's close family hold high rank even without an office.
    No samurai may be more than three ranks below their immediate family, so the daimyo's adult children, siblings and parents are at least Rank 9 even when they hold no office.
  • The chancellors of the domain's great lineages sit on its council, the House Chancellery.
    The setting's budget notes count about six of them, of Rank 11, just below the daimyo.
  • Seven in ten of a capital's working samurai are of Rank 5 or above - the reverse of a provincial city.
    Summed from the rank table of the setting's budget notes, the capital's 800 working samurai and a provincial city's 225 divide as below.

Historical castle towns show what each kind of samurai dwelling was, from the lowest retainers' row houses to a great retainer house's estate.

  • The lowest retainers lived in row houses: at Shibata, eight households under one roof.
    The surviving row house of the Shibata domain's foot soldiers, an Important Cultural Property, joins eight dwellings in one building, each an entrance, two rooms of six tatami mats, a room of three and a kitchen, and it is 43.6 m (~143 ft) long and 7.3 m (~24 ft) deep - about 18 ft of frontage and 430 square feet to a household.
  • The setting's ashigaru are peasants, not the lowest samurai.
    Historically, these row houses were built for the domain's lowest retainers, whom the domain held strictly apart from full samurai status. In the setting, ashigaru are peasants, not samurai - a departure from historical Japan it makes on purpose - and they are about a tenth of the farmers of the countryside, licensed to carry arms unsupervised once proven and unlicensed until then. The foot soldiers' quarter of a castle town therefore has no counterpart under that name in the setting's capital.
  • A mid-ranking retainer's main house at Matsue was about 67 tsubo.
    The surviving samurai residence at Matsue, lived in by a succession of the domain's retainers of about 500 to 1,000 koku, has a main house of about 67 tsubo, some 2,400 square feet.
  • A 1,000-koku retainer at Fukui held a walled plot of about 1,000 tsubo.
    The Suganuma family's plan of 1839 shows a site of 28 by 32.5 ken, about 51 m (~167 ft) by 59 m (~194 ft), some 1,000 tsubo, about 36,000 square feet (~0.8 acre). How a domain graded its retainers' plots by stipend, and a provincial city's samurai lots, are at Samurai house lots and houses by rank (bukeyashiki)
  • A senior retainer's estate was walled, with a rowhouse gate.
    The estates of the senior-retainer class had earthen walls, a gate built into a row house and a formal entrance.
  • The shogunate granted the lords' estates in Edo by the size of each lord's holding in koku.
    Under the rule of 1738, a lord of 10,000 to 20,000 koku was given 2,500 tsubo, one of 50,000 to 60,000 koku 5,000, and one of 100,000 to 150,000 koku 7,000 - about 89,000, 178,000 and 249,000 square feet (~2, 4 and 6 acres) - though many estates were larger and the rule was not strictly applied.
  • Did a larger holding buy ground for a larger household?
    In this project's reading, yes: the lower retainers of a lord's household lived in row houses built inside the estate's walls, and at Fukui a 1,000-koku retainer's street walls were row houses where the house's servants lived, so the ground a larger holding bought held more of the lord's people.

In this project's reading, a historical domain's nearest counterpart to the setting's lineages was its elder houses, the great retainer families whose estates were the largest a retainer held.

  • The Kaga domain's greatest retainer house held an estate of over 10,000 tsubo.
    At Kanazawa, each of the Kaga domain's eight elder houses held 10,000 koku or more, the Honda the greatest of them at 50,000. The Honda estate, where the house's own retainers came to serve and its affairs were run, covered over 10,000 tsubo, some 356,000 square feet (~8 acres).
  • How big were the estates of a mid-sized domain's elder houses?
    No page we read gives them, so between the 1,000-koku plot and the Honda estate the record has no figure for the great houses of a domain the size of Hikone, the setting's capital.
  • Where did a great house's estate stand?
    Two sitings are recorded. In the castle town, the higher a retainer's standing, the nearer the castle their estate stood. But a castle's third bailey could also hold senior retainers' residences, and at Fukuoka great retainers' estates stood in part of the third bailey from the early Edo period to its end. The castle's baileys are at Inside the castle: the lord's palace, the council and the baileys (goten, honmaru).
