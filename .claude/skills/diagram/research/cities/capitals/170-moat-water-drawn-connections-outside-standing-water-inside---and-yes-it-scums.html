Moat water: drawn connections outside, standing water inside - and yes, it scums

Sources: xian-moat-zhwiki (a permanently watered moat 20 x 10 m);, hori-jawiki, kojodan-mizubori, shirobito-906-mizubori, takamatsu-my-kagawa, imabarijo-blog, roving-bridge-enwiki, kotobank-kawazarae

The questions (GM 2026-08-09): does the inner (castle) moat need to connect to anything? How is the water kept in, and kept from stagnating? And why was the CITY moat not simply connected to the river, as Minami's and Nagahara's are?

The city moat: river-fed flow-through, and the connection is drawn. Japanese wet moats filled from whatever water stood nearest - rivers and streams, lakes, even the sea (Takamatsu's moat is seawater and its fish are ocean fish; Imabari's exchanges tidewater with the sea) - and the water came from wherever it could be had: welling up from below, let out of the castle, or deliberately drawn in. Minami and Nagahara back onto their rivers, so their moat FEET are the connection and nothing extra needs drawing. Shiro Daika's ring stands ~600 ft off its bank, so the connection is a pair of engineered channels. Two things decide their form. The tap is not at the river's CLOSEST approach (southeast) but upstream: with the land falling NE -> SW, water entering at the low southeast corner could not climb the east arc, and the whole northern ring would be a dead arm. And they are moat-width, as TANGO's are - the other stand-off closed ring takes 66 ft feeder and outfall channels, so the moat reads as flowing through. The form is river-to-river, the loop the sibling cities close at their moat feet: a 66 ft sluiced feeder taps the river's upper reach (downstream of the aqueduct's intake) and runs monotonically down-map - every segment west and south, the declared NE -> SW bearing - into the ring's east arc, and the 66 ft drain leaves the southeast arc and rejoins the river just below its bend, approaching swept DOWNSTREAM, onto ground the planar fall genuinely puts below the arc. The towpath crosses the drain's mouth on a plank deck, as a real towpath was bridged where a side channel interrupted it.

The castle's inner moat: standing water, and that is period-accurate. An inner moat dug below the water table holds groundwater and rain; water wells up where the moat is dug, and castles sectioned their moats with earthen weirs into pools at different levels (mitochigai). There is no underground aqueduct feeding it - a besieged castle wants water that cannot be cut off, which is exactly what groundwater is. Keeping it IN is the easy half (the moat floor sits below the table; earthen moats hold water the way any pond does). Keeping it CLEAN mostly did not happen: duckweed, algae and pond scum on a quiet moat are the historical look - Edo's surviving moats bloom green to this day - managed only by occasional dredging (the same sarae a canal got) and (in this page's reading, on no page read) by the carp kept in them. So the inner moat connects to NOTHING, and a GM describing it as still, green-skinned water with fat carp under the lilies is being accurate, not unflattering.
