The brokers' row is MERCHANT, and the ministry's cut is narrower than it looks

Sources: asakusa-kuramae (the brokers as lenders and middlemen), kotobank-asakusa-okura (the block in front of the granary), fudasashi-jawiki (where the trade came from, its guild and its shops), the setting's own budget notes "Domain ministry budgets"

GM ruling, 2026-08-08. The brokers' row is a merchant quarter, not a Ministry of Retainers annex - and the reason is a distinction the budget line does not spell out on its face.

The setting's own budget notes credit the Ministry of Retainers with "~1,600 (rice/coin arbitrage on capital stipend throughput of ~28,000)." That figure covers the PAYING of stipends only - the denomination decisions, the rice-versus-coin split, the payment-day logistics that the ministry administers. It is not a general franchise over rice finance. Everything else - the contracts, the clearinghouse business, the lending against next year's stipend, the arbitrage a samurai household does on its own account - sits outside it, and the merchant class has grown rich on that in Rokugan exactly as the fudasashi did in Edo.

So the Edo causal chain survives intact, and with it the reason the entertainment district belongs beside the granary: the brokers' money is what builds the theaters.

Who kept the row, and what it looked like. In Edo the block in front of the shogunate's granary was called Kuramae, "before the storehouses", and on its town side the brokers and the rice wholesalers - great merchants - stood eaves to eaves along the street. The same block kept a firebreak, an open ground left clear. The trade grew out of the granary's front itself: samurai waiting to be called for their rice rested at the tea stalls and at the rice dealers' shopfronts outside it, and those shops began to collect and sell the rice for them. They took a commission for receiving, carrying and selling it, and lent at high interest against the stipend to come. By 1724 they were a licensed guild of 109 houses in three street groups of 31, 47 and 31, bound to post the day's rice price in front of their shops, and they agreed among themselves that a house joining the guild would have its address at Kuramae. So the row was a street of big merchant houses, each its own shop with the price board at its front, facing the granary across the street.

How long a row. Edo's 109 houses served the shogunate's own retainers, a population far beyond a domain's; the setting's budget notes put the capital's paid samurai at about 800. No source read gives a count of brokers for a domain seat, so the length of our row is a GUESS: a short street of six to twelve frontages.

Why this matters beyond one row of buildings. It resolves an apparent conflict between setting canon and the historical model without weakening either, and the resolution is a general one: a ministry line item in the setting's own budget notes prices the ministry's own administrative function, not the whole economic activity that function touches. The same reading applies to any other ministry's "informal income" line.

The rule the map follows: the brokers' row is drawn as merchant frontage, never as state ground: one street of large merchant houses on the town side of the domain granary, facing it across the street, with the wealth band skewed high; an open firebreak is kept clear in front of the granary's storehouses; the row runs six to twelve frontages (a guess); and the entertainment district stands beside it.
