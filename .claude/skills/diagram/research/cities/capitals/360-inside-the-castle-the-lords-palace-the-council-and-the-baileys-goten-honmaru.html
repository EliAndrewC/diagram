Inside the castle: the lord's palace, the council and the baileys (goten, honmaru)

A castle of the Edo period was a set of walled baileys, and its heart, the innermost bailey (honmaru), held the lord's palace (goten): a public front that was the domain's seat of government and a private back where the lord and the lord's family lived, with the keep and the turrets built beside them as the castle needed. The outer baileys held senior retainers' residences, stables and storehouses, and the castle kept a reserve of rice against war and famine.

Kakegawa's palace, the seat of a mid-sized domain, was some seven buildings of about twenty rooms, holding the domain's ceremonies, the lord's official residence and the offices that ran the domain. A domain's council of senior retainers (karo) worked in the domain's office, which a castle-holding domain kept in its castle, in a room called the goyobeya.

  • Did the senior retainers live inside the castle or beside it?
    Both are recorded. The senior retainers (karo) basically kept their mansions near the castle, and a castle's outer bailey could hold senior retainers' residences. At Fukuoka, estates of the great retainers stood in part of the castle's third bailey from the early Edo period to its end. Where a capital's great houses stand is at Where a capital's samurai live: lineage compounds, walled estates and retainer terraces (bukeyashiki)
  • The castle's reserve rice passed with the castle to each new lord, as its arms did.
    The castle rice was kept inside the castle against war and famine, was of the same nature as the arms that belonged to the castle, and was handed on when the lord changed.
  • The shogunate kept castle rice at 68 places across the country.
    Under a system of 1685, 442,000 koku of it was stockpiled at 68 places, among them the shogunate's own castles - Edo, Osaka, Nijo and Sunpu - the castles of the fudai domains, and post stations on the Tokaido.
  • Did a domain keep its rice in one place or two?
    Two, for two purposes. Besides the reserve inside the castle, the tribute rice a domain collected went to a granary of its own: Kumamoto's, at Kawashiri, was the collecting point for the domain's tribute rice and also served as a naval port. Retainers were paid in stipend rice from their lord's stores; that the stipends came from the port granary in particular is this project's reading.

The palace itself is the part of a castle the record knows best, room by room.

  • A palace was both the seat of government and the lord's residence.
    Its public front (omote) was where the lord and the retainers governed, and its private back (oku) where the lord and the lord's family lived.
  • A palace did not have to stand in the innermost bailey.
    Where the ground kept the honmaru small, the palace was sometimes built in the wider second or third bailey. Kakegawa's first stood in the honmaru and was moved to the second bailey after the great earthquake of 1707.
  • As its work of government grew, a castle sometimes moved its offices out of the innermost bailey to the second or third.
    In the Edo period, as a castle's work of government grew and the honmaru became cramped, some castles moved those functions out to the second or third bailey - still inside the castle's walls.
  • Kakegawa's palace, the seat of a domain of 50,000 koku, survives nearly whole.
    It is the second-bailey palace of the Ota family, rebuilt after an earthquake of 1854, and it is rare in keeping not only its great hall and its reception rooms but its lesser rooms and its offices.
  • Each rank came into Kakegawa's palace by its own door.
    The lord and the senior retainers came in by the formal porch (shikidai), other samurai to the east of it, and the foot soldiers (ashigaru) by an earth-floored entrance on the north.
  • Kakegawa's rooms fall in three parts.
    A hall-and-audience part held the formal porch, the great hall, a consultation room, the audience chamber and the goyobeya, the room where the senior retainers worked; a small-audience-hall part held the small audience hall, its anteroom, a long hearth room and two small rooms; and an offices wing held rooms for the steward, the chief inspector, the provisions office and the other officials.
  • At Kawagoe, the lord lived in the second bailey until the residence there burned.
    The lord's residence stood in the second bailey until it burned, and the bailey held the armaments office as well.
  • Did larger domains build larger palaces?
    Kawagoe's honmaru palace, built in 1848 when the domain stood at 170,000 koku, had sixteen buildings to Kakegawa's seven, covering 1,025 tsubo, about 3,400 square meters (~36,600 sq ft), a great entrance and carriage porch 19 by 5 ken, and a great hall of 36 mats; 165 tsubo of it survive. Hiroshima's honmaru palace took about 16,530 square meters (~4.1 acres) of ground (The castle in a capital).
  • The shogun's palace at Nijo shows the scale at the top.
    Its entrance guardroom alone covers 1,048 square meters (~11,281 sq ft), the formal porch 332 (~3,574 sq ft), the great audience hall 784 (~8,439 sq ft), and its two further reception halls, the kuro-shoin and the shiro-shoin, 569 (~6,125 sq ft) and 318 (~3,423 sq ft).

The record places the council and the offices castle by castle, from a small domain's to the shogun's.

  • A castle-holding domain kept its government office in its castle.
    A domain's government office (hancho) was set in the castle where the daimyo held one, and in a mansion in the territory where the daimyo was not permitted a castle.
  • The senior retainers decided in council, and worked in a room called the goyobeya.
    They assembled on fixed days and settled important matters after joint deliberation, and the room where they carried out their duties in each domain's office was called the goyobeya.
  • At Kakegawa, a mid-sized domain, the council's room and the domain's offices were under the palace's roofs.
    Kakegawa castle's second-bailey palace was the domain's administrative office, and the council's consultation room and goyobeya survive in it beside the offices wing.
  • A small domain's fortified residence (jin'ya) kept the lord's residence and the offices in one compound.
    Ringed by earthen ramparts and a moat, without high stone walls, it laid out the residence, the offices, the retainers' quarters, storehouses and a drill ground within.
  • The shogunate's senior councilors worked inside Edo castle, but its judicial council sat outside it.
    The shogun's great elder (tairo), senior councilors (roju) and junior elders (wakadoshiyori) governed from the goyobeya inside Edo castle. The Hyojosho, the shogunate's court for important matters - the town magistrate, the temple-and-shrine magistrate, the finance commissioner and one senior councilor - stood outside the castle at Tatsunokuchi, in present Marunouchi.

In China's imperial capitals, the ruler's advisers and the ministries of state were placed apart.

  • In Ming China, the Grand Secretariat worked beside the palace's Meridian Gate.
    The Grand Secretariat (neige), the emperor's inner secretariat, worked from the Wenyuan Ge, east of the palace's Meridian Gate.
  • Did the offices of government stand inside the castle or outside it?
    The ruler's advisers worked close to the ruler in both traditions: the council in the lord's palace in Japan, the Grand Secretariat by the palace's Meridian Gate in China. The offices differed. A Japanese domain kept its offices in the palace as well, as Kakegawa's offices wing shows, or moved them to an outer bailey still within the walls; China's Six Ministries stood outside the palace, on its approach (Offices of the six ministries (liubu)).
