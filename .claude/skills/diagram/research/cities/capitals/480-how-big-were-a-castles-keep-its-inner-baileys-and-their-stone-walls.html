How big were a castle's keep, its inner baileys and their stone walls?

Sources: tenshu-jawiki (castles with no keep), marugame-castle-jawiki (the smallest surviving keep; a tall tiered wall), kakegawa-castle-jawiki (a small domain's keep), hiroshima-castle-numbers (a great domain's castle in figures), nijo-castle-jawiki (a castle and its honmaru measured), kawagoe-castle-jawiki (a domain castle's whole area), osaka-ishigaki-govonline (the tallest wall), shirobito-1213-kobai (how a wall leaned)

The capital's castle is drawn as its outer wall, its moat and its gates, with nothing inside. These are the sizes of what that blank stands for - the figures a castle's own sheet would be drawn to.

The keep: most castles had one, and not all. Some castles built the keep's stone base and never the keep (Tsu, Akashi, Ako); some had no base and used a three-story turret in its place (Yonezawa, Yamagata, Mito); and some had neither keep nor stand-in (Kagoshima, Hitoyoshi). The three measured here run from 15 to 27 m tall, on plans of 200 to 350 square meters: Marugame's, the smallest keep that survives, is 15 m tall; Kakegawa's, a wooden keep, covers 209 square meters (about 2,250 sq ft, some 47 ft square) and rises 16.18 m; Hiroshima's, the keep of a great domain as rebuilt, covers about 354 square meters on its ground floor (about 3,800 sq ft, some 62 ft square) and stands 26.6 m, 39 m with its stone base.

The inner baileys: about two hectares for the innermost. Nijo's honmaru is about 150 m square (about 2.25 ha), inside a castle of about 500 by 400 m. Hiroshima's upper honmaru is 21,705 square meters (about 2.2 ha), of which the palace took about 16,530. The whole castle is another matter: Kawagoe's grounds were about 50,000 tsubo (about 16.5 ha), later enlarged to some 99,000 tsubo (about 32.6 ha), while Hiroshima's ran about a kilometer each way, 900,000 square meters. Hiroshima's outer and middle moats were about 20 m wide, its inner moat 30 to 104 m.

The stone walls: a leaning face, as high as the site allowed. The first stone walls were low, and most rose vertically; the lords who built under Toyotomi Hideyoshi laid theirs at about 45 to 50 degrees, and after 1600 the lean grew steeper; the typical "fan batter" wall rose at about 45 degrees for two-thirds of its height and curved over the rest. The tallest is Osaka's honmaru east wall, 34 m from its buried footing and 24 to 25 m above the water; Marugame stacked four tiers to 60 m in all, its third-bailey wall alone 22 m at its highest. Those are the famous extremes. The height of an ordinary domain seat's wall is on no page read.

Should the castle be drawn inside, then? Not on the city map. The figures above are enough to draw a keep and an honmaru to scale, but the GM's ruling keeps the castle blank until the city map and the castle's own sheet can be kept in step, and nothing here changes that argument. What the figures do settle is the sheet: its keep is one of the four forms above, rolled per castle, and when it has one it stands 15 to 27 m on a base of 200 to 350 square meters, in an honmaru of about two hectares.

A number this puts in question. Nijo's whole castle (about 20 ha) and Kawagoe's (16.5 to 32.6 ha) are both smaller than the 50 ha the capital's land budget allows for its castle, worked out in its own entry; Hiroshima's 90 ha is inside it. That allowance is weighed there, not here.
