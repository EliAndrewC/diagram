How big is a domain school's ground, and what stands on it?

Sources: bunbu-gakko-jawiki (the smallest whole school, and one with no Confucian shrine), meirinkan-jawiki (one school's ground before and after its move), hagi-meirinkan-guide (the surviving martial hall, the shrine gate and the pond), nisshinkan-jawiki (a school beside its castle), kodokan-mito-jawiki (a school inside its castle), kodokan-ibaraki-about (the largest school's program, and the next largest grounds)

The domain school is drawn as one walled compound with nothing inside it. What history put inside, and how much ground it took, is below.

The ground runs from a third of a hectare to ten. Matsushiro's school had 3,089 square meters in all (934 tsubo, about 0.3 ha). Choshu's Meirinkan began on 940 tsubo inside the castle's third bailey and in 1849 moved into the castle town onto 15,184 tsubo (about 5 ha), with a drill ground of 3,020 tsubo. Aizu's Nisshinkan stood on about 120 by 60 ken (about 220 by 110 m, 2.4 ha). Mito's Kodokan was the largest in the country at about 32,000 tsubo (about 10.5 ha), ahead of Fukuyama's at 23,704 tsubo, Kanazawa's at 18,256 and the second Meirinkan at 15,184. That is a continuum, not a set of forms.

What every school read had: a hall of letters and halls for the martial arts, one per art. Matsushiro's list is the whole of a small school - a hall of letters with east and west classrooms, a book store, separate halls for the sword, jujutsu, the spear and archery, an office and a guardhouse. The Meirinkan's surviving martial hall is one building 37.8 by 10.8 m, a wooden-floored sword hall of 39 mats at one end and an earth-floored spear hall of 54 mats at the other.

What only some had: a shrine to Confucius. The first Meirinkan was laid out around its Confucian hall, the Taiseiden, with the sword, spear and gunnery halls forming the school's outer ring, and the second kept a walled shrine to Confucius with its own gate; Mito set its Confucian shrine beside a Shinto one. Matsushiro's building list has no shrine at all. So the shrine is a knob, not a rule.

What only the great schools had. Mito's ground also held a medical school, an observatory, a riding ground, a drill ground, an archery ground and a gunnery ground, with the hall of letters north of the main hall and the martial hall south of it; the Nisshinkan had a swimming place and an observatory, and the Meirinkan a pond where pupils swam and rode in the water.

Where it stood: three places, all attested. Inside the castle - the Kodokan was built in Mito castle's third bailey on ground that had held senior retainers' residences, and the first Meirinkan stood in Hagi's third bailey; beside the castle - the Nisshinkan was built next to Wakamatsu castle on its west; or in the town - the second Meirinkan in the castle town at Emukai, and Matsushiro's school on the ground of a converted official residence in Kiyosu-machi.

What this means for the map. The compound the capital draws is about 400 by 260 ft, about 1 ha (some 3,000 tsubo): inside the attested band, below the Nisshinkan and far below Mito, above Matsushiro - a mid-sized school. That a median domain would build one of about this size is this project's inference (a guess): the five schools read are not a sample by domain size. It stays accurate. Its interior stays blank, by the same rule as the castle's, so the program above - and the knob between a school with a Confucian shrine and one without - belongs to the school's own sheet, not to the city map. The siting is a knob too, but its inside-the-castle form cannot be drawn while the castle is blank, the same collision the castle's inventory records for the lineage compounds; the map draws the two forms outside the castle.

The rule the map follows: a capital's domain school is one walled compound of about 1 ha, standing either beside the castle or in the town; the choice between the two is rolled per capital from the map's seed. Nothing is drawn inside it.
