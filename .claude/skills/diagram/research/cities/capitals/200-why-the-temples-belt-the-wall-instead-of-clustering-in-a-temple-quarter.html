Why the temples belt the wall instead of clustering in a temple quarter

Sources: bodaiji-jawiki (the lineage temple), teramachi-jawiki (temples gathered at the town's edge), jokamachi-jawiki (the temple quarter on the town's outer ring), kotobank-teramachi (one town's temple quarter after another, with what each was for), wakayama-teramachi-nwn (how a temple became a fort), miyazu-teramachi (the defensive reading, and why it is not the whole story), mano-2016-takada-teramachi (a scholar's doubt)

The question (GM 2026-08-09): why seven temples strewn through the city rather than two main temples plus smaller ones in a temple neighborhood?

The structure IS "two main + the rest" - the two sovereign temples (Benten, Jurojin) are the capital's great complexes, flanking the government axis at equal offsets, and the five modest halls are the "everything else", among them the lineages' family temples. What differs from the provincial pattern is WHERE the rest stand: at the town's edge, as a temple quarter (teramachi).

The edge is the attested castle-town place for them. Many castle towns gathered their temples and graveyards at the outer edge of the town; the temple quarter stood on the castle town's outer ring. One town after another shows it: Fukuchiyama gathered its temples as the town's northern guard, Shibata set its temples outside the river and moat on its southeast edge, at the town's entrance, and Yonago gave a stretch of sand hills northwest of the castle to temples as the town's line of defense.

Why the edge: the standard answer is defense, and it is contested. The usual reading is that a temple quarter was a ready-made defensive line and a place to mass troops: the reference work's entry for Tokushima says that is what placing one in a castle town is generally held to have been for, and its entry for Matsue calls that town's long line of temples a defensive position against an enemy from the east or south, or a muster point. The concrete argument is that a temple's tile-roofed buildings stood inside stone or earthen walls, so in an emergency each became a fort - and a temple could build or mend stone walls without the shogunate's leave. But Miyazu's own city history notes that temple quarters elsewhere hold the lord's family temple, or lie beside the pleasure quarter or the port, so their function varied, and a scholar of Takada's temple quarter, while granting that the defensive reading is nearly the settled view, thinks it may be a product of the Tokugawa peace, since Takada's plan left the highway nearly undefended. That the graveyards and groves also served as a firebreak is this page's guess.

What the edge looked like: three forms. One group at one edge - Fukuchiyama's on the north, Miyazu's twelve temples in the southwest; a long line down one side - Matsue's temples strung north to south along one street; or several groups - Kanazawa had three temple quarters. Not every town had one at all: Hikone's street named Teramachi was lined with samurai houses, with only three temples to its north. No page read shows temples ringing the whole of a town's edge.

What this means for the map. The edge is accurate, and so is the reason the map gives for it, as the standard reading rather than a settled fact. A capital has enough temples to spend them on its edge, where a provincial city clusters its few into a lane (Nagahara's temple neighborhood) because they serve street life. But the capital's rim, drawn as a belt along the whole inner face of the wall, is more than the history shows: the attested forms are a knob among one group at one edge, a line along one side, and several groups.

The rule the map follows: a capital's modest temples stand at the town's edge, just inside the wall, in one of three forms rolled from the map's seed - one group against one stretch of wall, a line along one side, or several smaller groups along it. The two sovereign temples stay on the government axis.
