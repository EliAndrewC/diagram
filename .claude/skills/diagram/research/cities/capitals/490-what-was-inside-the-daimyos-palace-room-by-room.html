What was inside the daimyo's palace, room by room?

Sources: shirobito-612-goten (what a palace was for, and where in the castle it stood), kakegawajo-goten (a median domain's palace: its buildings, rooms and entrances), kakegawa-goten-bunka (the same palace's rooms, part by part), goyobeya-kotobank (whose room the goyobeya was), kawagoe-castle-jawiki (a larger domain's palace), nijo-castle-jawiki (the shogun's halls measured)

The palace - the goten - is the part of the castle this record says the most about and the map draws the least of: it is where the council meets, and it is inside the blank castle.

Two roles, two sides. A palace was at once the seat of government and the lord's residence; its public side was where the lord and his retainers governed, its private side where the lord and his family lived. It did not have to stand in the honmaru: where the ground kept the honmaru small, the palace was often built in the wider second or third bailey. Kakegawa's first stood in the honmaru and moved to the second bailey after the earthquake of 1707.

A median domain's palace: seven buildings, about twenty rooms. Kakegawa's palace, the second-bailey palace of a 50,000-koku domain, is one of the very few that survive whole, down to its offices. It did three things - the domain's ceremonies and audiences, the lord's official residence, and the offices that ran the domain - in seven buildings of about twenty rooms, and each rank came in by its own door: the lord and the senior councilors by the formal porch, other samurai to the east of it, foot soldiers by an earth-floored entrance on the north. Its rooms fall in three parts: a hall-and-audience part, with the formal porch, the great hall, a consultation room, the audience chamber and the room where the senior councilors worked; a small audience hall with the lord's own rooms; and an offices wing, with rooms for the steward, the chief inspector, the provisions office and the other officials.

Larger domains, larger palaces. Kawagoe's honmaru palace, built in 1848 when the domain stood at 170,000 koku, had sixteen buildings covering 1,025 tsubo (about 3,400 square meters), a great entrance and carriage porch 19 by 5 ken, and a great hall of 36 mats; 165 tsubo of it survive. Hiroshima's honmaru palace took about 16,530 square meters of ground (its figures). The shogun's palace at Nijo shows the scale at the top: its entrance guardroom alone covers 1,048 square meters, the formal porch 332, the great audience hall 784 and the two audience chambers 569 and 318.

What this means for the map. Nothing changes on the city map: the palace is inside the castle and the castle is blank. The record now holds what the castle's own sheet needs - a palace of the median domain's size, seven buildings and about twenty rooms in three parts, public in front and private behind, in whichever bailey the sheet puts it - and it confirms, room by room, that the council's chamber is a room of the palace and not a building of its own.
