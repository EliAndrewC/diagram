Domain capitals: the daimyo's castle town (jokamachi)

A domain capital is the seat of a daimyo, and in Japan it took the form of the castle town (jokamachi), a city that grew up around its lord's residential castle. Its samurai lived in quarters around the castle, the higher a retainer's rank the nearer the castle, and its merchants and artisans were settled by trade in wards of their own. Hikone, the castle town of the Ii house, counted some 15,000 townspeople in 1695, in the Edo period, with perhaps 20,000 warriors besides.

Imperial China had no town of this kind, because it had no resident hereditary military aristocracy to house: a Chinese prefectural seat was governed from a yamen, the magistrate's office compound, by officials who came and went. So this project's domain capitals take their form from the Japanese castle town, with Chinese practice settling what the Japanese leaves open - the reverse of the order the rest of the record follows. The setting's capitals are also far more commercial than Hikone: about 12,400 inhabitants, of whom about 13% are samurai.

  • The castle town was a Japanese institution, with no Chinese counterpart.
    A Chinese prefectural seat had its yamen, and sometimes a walled inner citadel, but no castle keep (tenshu), no rings of samurai houses graded by rank and no rim of temples (teramachi) - because imperial China had no resident hereditary military aristocracy to house. This comparison is the record's own: no page we read sets the two kinds of city side by side.
  • China's local officials were rotating appointees who lived in the yamen.
    An official and his immediate family typically lived in a residence attached to the yamen; he served in one post for a short term, usually three years, and could not serve in his home province or one next to it.

  • A castle town's samurai lived nearer the castle the higher their rank.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on castle towns says of the samurai quarters that, basically, the higher a retainer's status, the closer to the castle their house stood - a rule it gives for castle towns in general, not for one domain's.
  • Merchants and artisans were settled by trade into wards of their own.
    Villages nearby were moved into the castle town, and merchants and artisans were divided by occupation and resettled by force. Place-names such as Gofukumachi, the dry-goods ward, and Kajimachi, the smiths' ward, survive from it.
  • Hikone counted 15,371 townspeople in 53 wards in 1695, in the Edo period.
    Hikone was the seat of the Ii house, whose domain was valued at 290,000 koku after 1615. Its census of 1695 counted 15,371 townspeople, living in 53 separate wards, and counted townspeople only: warriors were not in it.
  • How many warriors did Hikone hold?
    About 20,000 at the end of the seventeenth century, by one estimate. Warriors, samurai and foot soldiers (ashigaru) alike, were normally exempt from the census counts, so their precise number is hard to know. On that estimate they were more than half of Hikone's roughly 35,000 inhabitants - this page's arithmetic.
  • The setting's capital is far more commercial than Hikone - a deviation.
    The setting's median domain capital has about 12,400 inhabitants, about 13% of them samurai - some 1,560. That leaves about 10,800 who are not samurai, some 70% of Hikone's townspeople, while its samurai share is under a quarter of Hikone's. The census is history; the capital's figures are the setting's own.
  • Does a clan lay out its capital its own way?
    Not in the setting's notes, which describe each clan's temperament, wealth and politics and give no clan's capital a layout of its own. The history points the same way: the zoning by rank and by trade described above is given for castle towns in general.
