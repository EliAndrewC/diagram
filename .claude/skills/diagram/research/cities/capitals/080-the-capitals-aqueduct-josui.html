The capital's aqueduct (josui)

Edo, the shogun's capital, stood largely on land reclaimed from the sea, where a dug well gave salty water, so the shogunate set out at once to bring fresh water in. It did so by aqueducts, the josui. The Tamagawa Josui took its water from the Tama River at Hamura and carried it about 43 km (~27 miles) in an open channel dug in the earth to the Yotsuya Okido, the gate where the Koshu highway entered Edo. There the water went underground, into wooden and stone pipes, and so was distributed into the city. The gate was the boundary between the two systems: an open channel outside, buried pipes inside.

The water ran by gravity alone. The channel fell very gently the whole way from Hamura to Yotsuya, and where Edo's other aqueduct, the Kanda Josui, had to cross a river it was carried over in a flume. We found no East Asian aqueduct that stood on arches as Rome's did.

  • The Tamagawa Josui began at a weir on a river, the Kanda Josui at a spring.
    The Tamagawa Josui's intake at Hamura was the Nagewatashi weir, built of cedar logs, bundles of branches and gravel so that it could be easily removed. The older Kanda Josui rose instead from Inokashira Pond, in what is now Kichijoji, a pond once called "the pond of seven wells" for its seven spring outlets.
  • At what angle did an intake leave its river?
    An intake's angle to its river governs how much silt it draws in, as modern engineering has it; that reading, and whether anything older set the angle, is at Which way does an offtake leave a river. No page we read on the Edo josui gives the angle of theirs.
  • Outside the city, the channel was an open cut in the earth.
    The Tamagawa's upstream course from Hamura is largely an open channel cut through the Musashino plateau, a plain earth cut with no retaining work on its banks. All of it, the whole way to the Okido, was open to the sky.
  • The channel fell about 21 cm (~8 in) in every 100 m (~328 ft).
    Hamura stands 126 m (~413 ft) above the sea, and the fall to Yotsuya was just 92 m (~302 ft), so the channel could drop only about 21 cm (~8 in) every 100 m (~328 ft) over its 42.74 km (~26 miles).
  • The channel's long course followed a ridge.
    The Tamagawa's main line was laid along a ridge of the Musashino plateau, and its large branch channels mostly along the higher ground between the rivers. Holding to the high ground let so long a channel keep its gentle, even fall, which is why its course winds. That is this project's reading of the route.
  • Where the Kanda Josui had to cross a river, it went over in a wooden flume.
    Where the Kanda Josui met the Kanda River at Ochanomizu, a flume, the kakehi, carried it over the river, and the view was one of the famous sights of Edo. The nearby bridge, Suidobashi ("aqueduct bridge"), is said to take its name from the early Edo period, when it straddled the wooden pipe, and Hiroshige drew it, with the Kanda Josui, in his One Hundred Famous Views of Edo.
  • A Song-dynasty Chinese capital also carried its water over a river in a wooden trough.
    Twelfth-century Kaifeng carried its Jinshui stream over the Bian river on a wooden trough and brought it into the city through the Northwest Water Gate, walled in on both sides, to feed the pools of the palace garden.
  • How did an open channel meet a city wall?
    At a gate. Kaifeng's stream went in through a water gate in its wall, as Water gates and sluices (shuimen) describes. The Tamagawa never went in: it ended at a gate of the city, the Yotsuya Okido, and its water went on in pipes.
  • Did East Asia build arched aqueducts like Rome's?
    None that we found. Every page we read sets the Roman arcade on one side and open earth channels, buried wooden and stone pipes and canals on the other, but none states the absence as such.
  • What was an East Asian aqueduct built of?
    On our reading of the systems above, three things: a channel at ground level, a buried pipe, and a flume bridge where a river had to be crossed.
  • The channel ended at the city gate, at a water-watch station.
    The Tamagawa ended at the old Yotsuya Okido, where a water-watch station stood, and from there the water went to every part of the city in buried stone and wooden pipes. Whether the open channel also emptied into a settling tank at the gate is on no page we read.
  • The aqueduct let its surplus water out into a river.
    A spillway called a yosuibaki let water out of the Tamagawa to regulate its flow, into the valley south of the Yotsuya Okido where the Shibuya River ran. The water-watch station at the Okido, which regulated how much water went on into the city, also had the role of discharging the surplus into the Shibuya River.
  • Inside the city, the water ran in buried pipes of stone and wood.
    From the Yotsuya Okido the pipes were buried underground, and since wood was easy to come by, wood was used for the pipes, which were called mokuhi.
  • Ship's carpenters helped make the wooden pipes watertight.
    The pipes had to be leak-tight, and ship's carpenters and the carpenters who built underground storerooms brought that skill to them.
  • How long were the buried pipes?
    A Meiji-era record gives the Kanda Josui 1.56 km (~1 mile) of open channel and 67 km (~42 miles) of stone and wooden conduits, and the Tamagawa 43 km (~27 miles) of open channel and 84.7 km (~53 miles) of conduits. Early in the eighteenth century the whole of Edo's waterworks had a total pipe length of 150 km (~93 miles).
  • How did a resident get the aqueduct's water?
    From a well near home. The pipes ran to the daimyo and samurai residences and to the row houses of the townsmen in half of Edo's districts, and wells were installed in the row houses, so no one had far to go for water. That a row house's well drew from the aqueduct's pipes, rather than from the ground water beneath it, is this project's reading of that passage.
  • How many aqueduct wells were there?
    One count puts the aqueduct wells, the josui-ido, across Edo and Tokyo at 8,000.
  • We guess the water was buried to keep a crowded wooden city's streets clear and its water clean.
    No page we read says. This project's reading, a GUESS, is that a dense wooden city needed its streets clear, and an open channel threaded through them would have been both an obstruction and open to fouling.
