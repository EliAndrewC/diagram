The capital's aqueduct (josui)

Edo, the shogun's capital, drank from aqueducts, the josui. The Tamagawa Josui took its water from the Tama River at Hamura and carried it about 43 km (~27 miles) in an open channel dug in the earth to the Yotsuya Okido, a gate at the edge of the city. There the water passed a water-watch station and went underground, into wooden and stone pipes laid beneath the streets that fed the wells of the city. The gate was the boundary between the two systems: an open channel outside, buried pipes inside.

The water ran by gravity alone. From Hamura to Yotsuya the channel fell only about 92 m (~302 ft) in 43 km (~27 miles), and where it had to cross another river it was carried over in a wooden flume. We found no East Asian aqueduct that stood on arches as Rome's did.

  • A josui began at a weir on a river or at a spring.
    The Tamagawa Josui's intake at Hamura was the Nagewatashi weir, built of cedar logs, bundles of branches and gravel so that it could be easily removed. The older Kanda Josui rose instead from the spring-fed Inokashira Pond, in what is now Kichijoji.

    No page we read on the Edo josui gives it. Which way a channel leaves a river, and why, is at Which way does an offtake leave a river.
  • Outside the city, the channel was an open cut in the earth.
    The Tamagawa's upstream course from Hamura is largely an open channel cut through the Musashino plateau, a plain earth cut with no retaining work on its banks. All of its roughly 43 km (~27 miles) to the Okido was open to the sky, and only below the water-watch station did it run in pipes.
  • The channel fell about 21 cm (~8 in) in every 100 m (~328 ft).
    Hamura stands 126 m (~413 ft) above the sea, and the fall to Yotsuya was just 92 m (~302 ft), so the channel could drop only about 21 cm (~8 in) every 100 m (~328 ft) over its 42.74 km (~26 miles).
  • The channel's long course followed a ridge.
    The Tamagawa's main line was laid along a ridge of the Musashino plateau, and its large branch channels mostly along the higher ground between the rivers. That the winding holds the channel on its gentle fall across long country, rather than wandering, is this project's reading of the route.
  • Where the channel had to cross a river, it went over in a wooden flume.
    Where the Kanda Josui met the Kanda River at Ochanomizu, a flume, the kakehi, carried it over the river, and the view was one of the famous sights of Edo. The nearby bridge, Suidobashi ("aqueduct bridge"), is said to take its name from the early Edo period, when it straddled the wooden pipe, and Hiroshige drew it, with the Kanda Josui, in his One Hundred Famous Views of Edo.
  • A Song-dynasty Chinese capital did the same.
    Twelfth-century Kaifeng carried its Jinshui stream over the Bian river on a wooden trough and brought it into the city through the Northwest Water Gate, walled in on both sides, to feed the pools of the palace garden. Where an open channel passes a city wall it goes through a water gate, as the water gate describes; where it ends at the wall, it ends at a gate of the city, as the Tamagawa did.
  • Did East Asia build arched aqueducts like Rome's?
    None that we found. Every page we read sets the Roman arcade on one side and open earth channels, buried wooden and stone pipes and canals on the other, but none states the absence as such. On our reading of the systems above, an East Asian aqueduct was built of three things: a channel at ground level, a buried pipe, and a flume bridge where a river had to be crossed.
  • The channel ended at the city gate, at a water-watch station.
    The Tamagawa ended at the old Yotsuya Okido, where a water-watch station stood, and from there the water went to every part of the city in buried stone and wooden pipes. Whether the open channel also emptied into a settling tank at the gate is on no page we read.
  • The aqueduct's surplus water went to a river, not back into the city.
    A spillway called a yosuibaki let water out of the Tamagawa to regulate its flow, into the valley south of the Yotsuya Okido where the Shibuya River ran; the aqueduct had the role of discharging its surplus into the Shibuya River.
  • Inside the city, the water ran in buried pipes of stone and wood.
    From the Yotsuya Okido the pipes were buried underground, and since wood was easy to come by, wood was used for the pipes, which were called mokuhi. They had to be leak-tight, and it was ship's carpenters and the carpenters of underground storage who brought that skill to them.
  • How long were the buried pipes?
    A Meiji-era record gives the Kanda Josui 1.56 km (~1 mile) of open channel and 67 km (~42 miles) of stone and wooden conduits, and the Tamagawa 43 km (~27 miles) of open channel and 84.7 km (~53 miles) of conduits. No page we read gives a total for all of Edo's buried mains.
  • What a resident saw of the aqueduct inside the city was its wells.
    The pipes ran to the daimyo and samurai residences and to the row houses of the townsmen in half of Edo's districts, and wells were installed in the row houses, so no one had far to go for water. One count puts the aqueduct wells, the josui-ido, across Edo and Tokyo at 8,000. That a row house's well drew from the aqueduct's pipes, rather than from the ground water beneath it, is this project's reading of the first passage.
  • Why was the water buried inside the city?
    No page we read says. This project's reading, a GUESS, is that a dense wooden city needed its streets clear, and an open channel threaded through them would have been both an obstruction and open to fouling.
