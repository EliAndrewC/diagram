The wharf is the COLLECTING end, and "kurayashiki" is the wrong word for it

Sources: osaka-kurayashiki, asakusa-kuramae, kurayashiki-jawiki, kotobank-horokusei

The word first. A kurayashiki (蔵屋敷) is a daimyo's warehouse-and-residence at the market - Osaka, around Nakanoshima - where tax rice was auctioned to brokers against tradeable rice bills. By the 1670s eighty domains reportedly kept kurayashiki warehouse compounds there, and by the 1840s 125. That is the SELLING end, and a domain capital is not it. Calling Shiro Daika's waterfront a kurayashiki district would be wrong twice: wrong word, and wrong economic role.

What a domain capital actually holds. Peasants pay tax rice up to the domain granary, and the domain pays its samurai their stipends out of it (kuramai, the rice stipend). So the capital is the collecting-and-disbursing end: rice arrives from the provinces, much of it goes straight back out as stipends (how much is a GUESS - no page read gives the share), and the surplus ships downriver to the market.

The model to copy is Asakusa Okura / Kuramae. Edo's rice brokers sat in the section of Asakusa literally named Kuramae (蔵前), "before the storehouses" - by its name, in front of the shogunate's own rice granaries on the Sumida River, which the page cited here does not itself describe (no source on the Asakusa granaries has been read yet). There sat the fudasashi - brokers who warehoused rice for a fee, converted it to cash, and lent money, becoming wealthy; they were frequent patrons of the kabuki theater and the Yoshiwara pleasure district.

That gives the capital a chain of three features linked by one mechanism, which is the sort of thing that makes a map read as a real place:

river wharf -> the domain granary -> the brokers' row in front of it -> the entertainment district next door (next door is a GUESS: the brokers patronized it, but the page read does not place it beside them)

Where a licensed pleasure quarter really stood, and why the setting's capital draws an entertainment district rather than one, is its own question.

The divergence from Edo, which was the GM's call. In Edo the arbitrage between rice and coin made chonin brokers rich. In L7R, the setting's own budget notes hand that income to the Ministry of Retainers - "~1,600 (rice/coin arbitrage on capital stipend throughput of ~28,000)" - a samurai ministry, not a merchant street. A brokers' row that is a ministry annex rather than a commercial quarter weakens the Edo causal chain from broker wealth to the entertainment district; the two can coexist (the ministry takes its cut of the rice-to-coin exchange; merchant brokers still trade), but which one the map depicts changes what is drawn there. The GM ruled it merchant - see "The brokers' row is MERCHANT" below.

The Emperor's granaries are separate, and their siting is not in the sources. The setting's own budget notes give the Imperial Magistrate a ~450-koku line for "local Imperial granary supervision," with "separate granary staff, materials, and operations," so they are distinct stores under a foreign authority - but nothing in the sources says where a capital would put them. Adjacent to the Imperial Magistrate's compound, or with their own water access, are both plausible, and the choice between them is a knob - see "The Emperor's granaries are separate" below.
