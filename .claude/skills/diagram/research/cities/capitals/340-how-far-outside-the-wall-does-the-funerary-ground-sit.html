How far outside the wall does the funerary ground sit?

Sources: yoro-sosoryo-sol (the ancient code: no burial near the capital or beside roads, no distance), louzeyuan-zhwiki (the 1104 edict), suzugamori-jawiki, kozukappara-jawiki, rokudo-chinnoji-jawiki, fuso-jawiki, toribeno-jawiki, injoji-jawiki, souda-kyoto-enmado, jokanji-jawiki, senbon-dori-jawiki, kyototuu-rendaino, yoritomo-rokudo

The GM's objection: a common burial ground, cremation ground and pauper's ossuary drawn 900-1,260 ft outside the wall "makes the map harder to read," and they asked for the distance only if research supports it. It does not: the record says the constraints are satisfied far closer.

What actually set the distance was LAND QUALITY and the ROAD, not a setback. The only explicit siting criterion anywhere in the record is Chinese and says nothing about distance: Cai Jing's edict of 1104, which established a louzeyuan (public pauper cemetery) in every prefecture and county, directs officials to "choose high, open, barren ground" (translated from the Chinese by this project; original: 「選在高曠不毛之地」). A spread of one to ten li (~1,800-18,100 ft) is given for Zhejiang county gazetteers and rests on no page read, and a spread that wide would not be a rule being obeyed: it is where each county's first patch of worthless ground happened to lie. A capital whose bad ground begins 300 ft past the moat is fully compliant. The second driver is that a corpse is carried on foot: every attested site straddles a highway at a gate - Kozukappara on the Ōshū/Nikkō Kaidō, Suzugamori on the Tōkaidō, Rendaino at the head of Senbon-dōri, Toribeno at the Rokudō crossroads.

Ritual pollution is a BINARY, not a gradient. The requirement is outside, full stop. Japan's ancient Yōrō code forbade burying the dead near the capital or beside the roads, and gave no distance for either. And Kyoto shows it: Injō-ji (Senbon Enmadō), the gate-temple "at the entrance of Rendaino," stands at the ground's mouth and is described as the place that leads through to the other world. The Rokudō crossroads does the same job for Toribeno at ~1,334 ft. Pollution buys zero extra feet past the wall.

Fire buys almost nothing either. Edo burned its dead inside the city, and no premodern rule sets a pyre's distance from anything that burns; the only such figures are present-day codes, which the maps do not use. That finding is its own question.

The three features belong together, and tighter than we drew them. At Edo's north gate one patch astride one road holds the Kozukappara execution and burial ground, Enmei-ji at 287 ft, Ekō-in at 258 ft (which maintains the site's muen-zuka - the literal pauper's mound), and Jōkan-ji the "throw-in temple" at 1,527 ft. Kyoto's Toribeno was ONE field doing all three jobs - exposure, cremation and burial - entered through one gate-temple at the Rokudō crossroads, and Rendaino likewise a single ground entered at Injō-ji. The Chinese louzeyuan is a fenced compound with a resident caretaker monk, and Chinese cremation was institutionally a Buddhist-temple business, so the crematory sat at the temple that ran the pauper ground.

The shape of the record. In each case this page could read, the funerary complex's ENTRANCE is at or just past the wall and the field extends outward for another 1,000-4,000 ft. A small compact feature drawn at 900-1,260 ft puts at the NEAR end what history puts at the FAR end - which is why such a feature reads unmotivated.
