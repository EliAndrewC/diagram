How far outside the wall does the funerary ground sit?

Sources: yoro-sosoryo-sol (the ancient code: no burial near the capital or beside roads, no distance), louzeyuan-zhwiki (the 1104 edict), suzugamori-jawiki, kozukappara-jawiki, rokudo-chinnoji-jawiki, fuso-jawiki, toribeno-jawiki, injoji-jawiki, souda-kyoto-enmado, jokanji-jawiki, senbon-dori-jawiki, kyototuu-rendaino, yoritomo-rokudo

The GM's objection: a common burial ground, cremation ground and pauper's ossuary drawn 900-1,260 ft outside the wall "makes the map harder to read," and they asked for the distance only if research supports it. It does not: the record says the constraints are satisfied far closer.

What actually set the distance was LAND QUALITY and the ROAD, not a setback. The only explicit siting criterion anywhere in the record is Chinese and says nothing about distance: the louzeyuan (public pauper cemetery), proposed in 1104 by Cai Jing to bury the unclaimed dead on official land, was generally "sited on high, open, barren ground" (translated; original: 「選在高曠不毛之地」). A spread of one to ten li (~1,800-18,100 ft) is given for Zhejiang county gazetteers and rests on no page read, and a spread that wide would not be a rule being obeyed: it is where each county's first patch of worthless ground happened to lie. A capital whose bad ground begins 300 ft past the moat is fully compliant. The second driver is that a corpse is carried on foot: every attested site lies on a highway out of the city - Kozukappara on the Nikkō Kaidō, Suzugamori on the Tōkaidō, Rendaino at the head of Senbon-dōri, Toribeno at the Rokudō crossroads.

Ritual pollution is a BINARY, not a gradient. The requirement is outside, full stop. Japan's ancient Yōrō code forbade burying the dead near the capital or beside the roads, and gave no distance for either. And Kyoto shows it: Injō-ji (Senbon Enmadō), the gate-temple "at the entrance of Rendaino," stands at the ground's mouth and is described as the place that leads through to the other world. The Rokudō crossroads does the same job for Toribeno, ~1,334 ft from the ground by this project's own measurement. Pollution buys zero extra feet past the wall.

Fire buys almost nothing either. Edo burned its dead in temple precincts inside the city, and later at one ground on its edge, and no premodern rule sets a pyre's distance from anything that burns; the only such figures are present-day codes, which the maps do not use. That finding is at Cremation grounds and bone mounds (kasoba).

The three features belong together, and sit close together. At Edo's north gate one patch on one road holds the Kozukappara execution ground, and on one split set of grounds beside it the temple founded to bury its dead: Enmei-ji, within which the execution site lies, and Ekō-in on the land adjoining it. Jōkan-ji, the "throw-in temple" where the dead were buried as if thrown in, stands near the Yoshiwara pleasure quarter (1,527 ft from it); no page read places it on the same road. Kyoto's Toribeno was a ground of both exposure burial and cremation, entered at the Rokudō crossroads, and Rendaino likewise a ground entered at Injō-ji; whether Toribeno's dead were also interred on the same field is not on a page this record read. The Chinese louzeyuan is a fenced compound kept by Buddhist monks who lived on it. Whether a crematory stood there is a GUESS: the page read says nothing of cremation.

The shape of the record. In each case this page could read, the funerary complex's ENTRANCE is at or just past the wall and the field extends outward for another 1,000-4,000 ft. A small compact feature drawn at 900-1,260 ft puts at the NEAR end what history puts at the FAR end - which is why such a feature reads unmotivated.
