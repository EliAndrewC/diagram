Does a pyre need a clear distance from anything that burns?

Sources: kasoba-jawiki (Edo's cremation in temple precincts), bernstein-fire-and-earth (the Senju cremation grounds; the May 1873 proposal and the stench), ifc-307-bonfire (a present-day bonfire setback), manzello-2019-firebrand, nwcg-spotting (present-day wildfire spotting)

The funerary ground's own question, how far outside the wall it sits, finds that land and the road set the distance and that pollution adds nothing past the wall. This one asks whether FIRE adds anything.

Fire buys almost nothing, and this is the finding that settles it. Edo cremated its dead in temple precincts inside the city - by about 1650 almost every temple in Asakusa and Shitaya had a crematory - and in 1667 moved them together to one ground by the Kozukappara execution ground, with a cluster of kasō-dera at Senju, where they burned for over two centuries. Neither recorded move of the crematoria was for fire: the 1667 move was for their smoke, and when they were proposed for relocation past the shubiki line in May 1873, the reason on the record is the stench, not sparks. The real practice was CONTAINMENT, not distance: a stone hearth inside a walled kasoba (Varanasi burns its dead on open pyres in the heart of its old city every day, on a stone-stepped ghat at the river).

No premodern rule sets a distance. None found sets one between a pyre and anything that burns: the searches turned up distance rules for a cremation ground only from the Meiji government onward, after 1868. The only figure for a fire of this size is a present-day code's: 50 ft for a bonfire (IFC §307.4.1). Wildfire ember-spotting figures are modern too, and do not fit this fire: they come from big fires lofting brands in a convection column, and even there only a burning firebrand starts a new fire more than ~60 m from the main front.

The decision: the maps take no fire distance from either. A pyre stands in its walled yard with no clearance ring drawn around it, and a wet moat ends the fire argument at its outer face. The funerary ground's minimum distance past the moat is a drawing convention, so that the ground reads as outside, not a fire setback.
