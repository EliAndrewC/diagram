How often is a sluice OPEN? The duty cycle, and why one glyph suffices

Sources: changan-zhitu-jingqu (a Yuan canal's sluice rules: permit, turn, season, the spate rest), kotobank-bansui (delivery in turns in a drought, medieval Japan), jsidre-bansui-2009 (the same drought regime in present-day districts; the moat gate's duty cycle itself is this page's reading, on no page read), kiyosu-haisui-schedule (a present-day rice season's dates), guangdong-tongzhi-14

The question (GM 2026-08-09): were sluices closed by default and opened only when more water was needed, or did something else set the schedule? This decides whether the map needs separate open and closed glyphs.

The moat pair runs THROTTLED, not open-or-shut. A wet moat loses water continuously to seepage and evaporation, and standing water goes foul - which is why this map declares through-flow at all (feeder in at the east leat, around the ring, out at the southwest). So the resting state of BOTH boards is partly raised: a small steady trickle in, the same trickle out, and the operator's normal work is TRIMMING the opening a few inches, not opening or closing the gate. Neither board sits fully open or fully shut in ordinary times.

What moves a board is an EVENT, not a schedule:

  • River in spate - the intake winds DOWN hard: storm water carries the silt load that chokes a leat, and the moat must not surge. A Yuan-dynasty canal rested the same way when its river rose in the summer spate.
  • Drought - the outfall shuts to hold what the ring has.
  • Dredging and repair - the one time the pair works fully opposed: intake shut, outfall full open, and the ring draws down for the spade men. Moats silt and were periodically dredged; this is the maintenance drawdown.
  • Siege - both shut, holding maximum water.

Irrigation sluices are the scheduled kind, and the schedule is old. The field gates on the village and city maps DO run a calendar. The water rules of the Jing canal in Shaanxi, set down under the Yuan in the 14th century, open a field sluice only when the office has issued a permit for that sluice's share, run the water at the appointed time, and shut the sluice the moment the watering is done, handing the water on to the next sluice up the line. The same canal ran for a season - water let in from the first day of the tenth month, stopped in the seventh - and was repaired in the eighth and ninth. In Japan, delivery in turns when drought tightened the supply (bansui) is recorded from the medieval period, its rules written down by the late middle ages; present-day districts still switch to it only in a drought. What no premodern page read gives is the rice season's own dates: a spring opening and an autumn shutoff appear only on a present-day district's schedule, so the maps draw a gate that has a season but name no month for it. The distinction is the JOB: a supply gate holds a LEVEL (trim continuously), a field gate delivers an ALLOTMENT (open on your day, shut on your neighbor's).

Consequence for the glyph: one closed-board form, with the word, is the honest drawing. At rest neither moat gate is "open" or "closed" - both are throttled a few inches, and a partly-raised board is sub-pixel at 3 ft/px. Separate open/closed glyphs would assert a binary state the real duty cycle does not have; the state worth showing on a map is an EVENT (a drawdown, a siege), which is a caption's job on the day it matters, not a permanent glyph.
