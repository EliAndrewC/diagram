Why do East Asian walls keep their corners, and where were the round ones?

Sources: keep-enwiki, chinese-city-wall-enwiki, edo-36-mitsuke-jawiki, kaogongji-wikisource, shanghai-xiancheng-zhwiki, fujian-tulou-enwiki, fujian-tulou-zhwiki

The wall-geometry question finds that a bigger East Asian wall is a bigger rectangle or a longer terrain loop, never a rounder ring, and decides the map's shapes from that. This one asks why the corner survived, and looks at the two round forms the record does hold.

  • The military logic, and why East Asia never needed the circle. A corner is the weak point of a curtain (dead ground at the angle, open to siege engines and mining), and Europe's round keeps held military advantages over square corners - though the record calls the corner's weakness theoretical before the trebuchet and the crossbow. East Asia cured the same problem by FLANKING instead: Chinese walls carry mamian ("horse-face") bastions and corner towers on straight curtains, Japanese walls carry masugata gate courts (Edo's outer works alone had 26) - because hangtu rammed-earth formwork and ishigaki masonry both want straight runs, and the classical ideal capital was a square to begin with. Same problem, different cure, and the cure is why big East Asian walls stay angular.
  • The attested circles, and why neither is our case. Shanghai's 1553 county wall was genuinely round - raised against the wokou pirates coming upriver, and a circle encloses the most area per foot of wall, so this page reads the round wall as the SPEED-AND-ECONOMY form, not a prestige form. The Hakka tulou are round communal fortress-HOUSES (a clan under one roof, wedge apartments with no hierarchy and no weak corner) - and even tulou are more commonly rectangular. The tulou is a communal, defensive form of the mountain districts, and Shanghai's ring a town wall raised against pirates; neither is how a daimyo's seat or a governor's city announces itself.
