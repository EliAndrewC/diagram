Where a canal passes the city wall, what is the water gate, and how many does a city have?

Sources: panmen-zhwiki (Pan Gate's water gate, its sluice and palisade), suzhou-panmen-gov (its measured depth), dongjing-menghualu-juan1 (Kaifeng's water gates and their night grille), dongshuiguan-nanjing-zhwiki (a river-wide water gate at Nanjing), pingjiang-tu (Suzhou's gates in land-and-water pairs)

A water gate is a passage through the wall's thickness for the water and the boats on it, closed at night and in war. Where the canal first reaches the river is a separate question, answered in whether a city's canal opens its own mouth on the river; this one is where the canal crosses the wall.

Its form. Suzhou's Pan Gate keeps the one complete example. Its water gate stands on the south side of the land gate and, like it, is doubled: an inner and an outer gate, the passage 24 m deep, with a sluice gate and a wooden palisade set front and back and a hidden passage between the two gates leading up to the gate tower. The two gates stand 4.6 m apart, the whole 24.5 m deep, and between them the channel is lined with stone revetments on both sides. Twelfth-century Kaifeng closed its East Water Gate, which spanned the Bian river, with iron-clad lattice gates let down to the water at night like a sluice, and on each bank beside it a gate let people through on foot.

Its width follows the water. Where a whole river enters, the gate can be a row of openings: Nanjing's East Water Gate, where the Qinhuai enters the city, was built three tiers high with eleven vaulted openings in each; the lowest carried the water, and the upper two stored troops and grain. A canal is drawn through a single channel, a guess: no page read counts the openings of Pan Gate's water passage. The width of Pan Gate's water passage in meters is on no page read. A floating boom or a chain across the water at the gate is not described on any page read either; the closing gear read is the lowered grille, the sluice gate and the palisade.

How many: two patterns are attested.

  • Every gate paired (the Suzhou pattern). On the 1229 plan of Suzhou each of the city's five gates is drawn with a land gate and a water gate together - the canal grid reaches every gate.
  • One per watercourse (the Kaifeng pattern). Kaifeng's outer wall had a water gate where each river crossed it, each named in the text with the river it carries: two on the Cai river, standing beside the Chenzhou and Dailou land gates; the East and West Water Gates for the Bian; the Northeast for the Wuzhang river and the Northwest for the Jinshui - six in all by this page's count, two named beside a land gate and the rest listed apart from any land gate.

The rule the map follows: wherever a canal crosses a city wall the map draws a water gate - a passage through the wall's thickness as wide as the canal, a grille across the water at its outer and inner faces, and the channel between faced in stone. A river crossing the wall is drawn as a row of arched openings rather than one. How many a city carries is a knob rolled from the map's seed per settlement: either every land gate has a water gate beside it and the canals are laid to reach them, or a water gate stands only where a watercourse crosses the wall, beside a land gate when one is near and alone when not. The passage's width is drawn equal to the canal's, a guess, since no measured width was read.
