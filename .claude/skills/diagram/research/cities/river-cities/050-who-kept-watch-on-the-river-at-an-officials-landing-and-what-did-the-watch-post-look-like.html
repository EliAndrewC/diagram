Who kept watch on the river at an official's landing, and what did the watch post look like?

Sources: funabansho-jawiki (what a boat watch post was for and where it stood), koto-nakagawa-funabansho (one watch post's size, fence, arms and river-edge hut, and who manned it), l7r-tariffs (the setting taxes goods where they are sold, not where they pass)

What it was for. In the Edo period, domains with a coastline set up boat watch posts (funabansho) at the landings of busy boat routes and at other key points, to inspect passing boats and to collect taxes, among other duties. The best recorded is the shogunate's Nakagawa post, set up in 1661 at the mouth of the Onagi river where it meets the Naka: a checkpoint for river boats, examining the people and goods entering and leaving Edo.

What it looked like. The Nakagawa post was a compound a little over 26 by 17 ken, about 155 by 100 ft. In an Edo-period illustration of it, a wooden palisade runs around it, ten spears are kept there, and a watch hut stands at the river's edge. The bannerman who held the duty did not normally attend but sent retainers, and the clearance seems to have become a formality over time. That post guarded the approach to the shogun's city. No page read describes a watch kept at a local office's own landing, or gives its size.

In this setting. The domains' boat watch posts described above inspected passing boats and collected taxes. This setting taxes goods where they are sold, not where they pass through, so a river watch here inspects and records who and what comes to the landing and takes no toll from boats passing by - a deviation from the domains' posts.

What the map draws. The river watch at an official's landing is the Nakagawa post's river-edge hut, not the whole fenced post: a small watch hut at the head of the landing, manned from the office. The fenced compound of 155 by 100 ft belongs to a checkpoint on a capital's approach. The hut's size and its place at a local office's landing are a guess.
