Which way does an offtake leave a river, and why?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-24; watersa-junction-angle (what a wider junction angle does to the separation zone and to the bed shear where one channel enters another), ignou-silt-control (how an offtake's alignment governs the sediment it draws, and where on a bend it belongs); general hydraulic reading, for which no source is cited, xian-wall-zhwiki, hori-jawiki, kotobank-hori, shanghai-xiancheng-chengqiang-zhwiki

The two ends of a river-fed moat answer differently, and the asymmetry is itself the signature of a worked waterway. At the outlet, where the moat returns its water, the junction angle is a primary control on how one channel merges into another: a numerical study of a confluence set in a river BEND found that widening the angle at which the side channel enters enlarges the zone of separated flow inside the junction, and raises the greatest bed shear stress there by 59% between 30 and 115 degrees. So a small, downstream-pointing angle merges smoothly, while a large one drives the entering jet across the receiving channel. That the water behind such a mouth then goes slack, and that the far bank is attacked, is this record's own extension of that result rather than a finding in it - the study is of a bend, and reports neither - and the moat's two feet are drawn from the extension all the same. Natural tributaries curve to join pointing downstream, and engineered drainage returns are cut to do the same (this rests on general reading; no source is cited). At the inlet, the same reasoning points the other way. A flow-aligned intake is the smooth-entry answer, but the sediment rides in the slow bottom layer, which is the layer a diversion takes preferentially, and how much of it comes in is a function of the offtake's alignment: a channel leaving a parent CANAL is turned toward the square, 60 to 80 degrees to the parent's centerline in the direction of flow, in order to keep the excess sediment out. A silted moat is a maintenance failure rather than a hydraulic one. Off a RIVER, though, the rule runs the other way: a canal taking off at 90 degrees to the main flow is "the most objectionable orientation of the regulator", and 30 to 45 degrees to the river's flow is what is normally recommended, so that the flow lines curve into the regulator instead of striking it. The near-square offtake therefore belongs to a distributary leaving a canal and not to a headworks on a river. What the two cases share is the siting and the skimming: the intake belongs on the OUTER, concave bank of a bend and toward its downstream end, because a bend sweeps its bottom layers toward the inside, and a raised inlet sill with a skimmer wall at the mouth keeps the bedload and the floating matter out so that what enters is the cleaner upper water. These maps draw the moat inlet near square - about 10 degrees upstream of it - which is the canal rule; on the river rule the mouth would lie at 30 to 45 degrees to the current. And a moat wants STAGE - the height of the water surface - rather than throughput: its job is to stay full, not to pass water, so it never needs the momentum-capturing mouth an irrigation canal would want (this rests on general reading; no source is cited). Two cautions go with all of this: what the engineering literature appears to hold, on no page that could be read, is that a diversion draws its LEAST sediment at an intermediate angle rather than at the square, and that the often-quoted 90 to 120 degree rule for a headworks is measured from the WEIR's axis rather than from the river's own line. Neither has moved what the map draws.

The decision (the GM, 2026-07-24). The two junction arms met the river as parallel right angles, and the GM asked whether they should not angle with the flow. They were parallel only because each open end of the moat was projected onto the river by dropping a perpendicular - an artifact of how the arc was closed, never a decision anyone made. The answer splits by end, in the way the hydrology above splits: the outlet now sweeps about 22 degrees off square, pointing downstream, and the inlet tilts only about 10 degrees, upstream of square, which is near enough to a right angle to keep its sediment out. Never the reverse: an inlet swept downstream is the one shape the map must not draw. The two angles themselves are a guess - they are the smallest tilts that read as deliberate at this scale, not figures taken from a source - while the directions they point rest on the reading above.

The far side of the ring circulates slowly, and that is accepted as drawn. The two junctions sit only about 800 ft apart along the river, so the head driving water around the ring is tiny and the arc furthest from them barely moves. The alternative - separating the junctions far enough along the bank to drive a real circulation - was not taken, and the reason is that the sluggish version is the historical one: a river moat was kept full by the river's own stage far more than it was ever flushed through, so partial stagnation is period-accurate rather than a defect. The cost of accepting it is one observable thing: a reader who thinks about the flow will see that the far arc is still water.

The rule the map follows: where a moat's open ends run onto a river, the UPSTREAM end is the inlet and the DOWNSTREAM end is the outlet, and the two meet the river at different angles. The outlet's junction sits clearly downstream of the square, by about 22 degrees of the arm's length; the inlet sits square or a little upstream of square, by about 10 degrees, and never swept downstream. The map's river is drawn from its source toward its mouth, so that which end is upstream is a fact about the drawing and not a guess.
