Did a city keep a fish and produce market at its landing, and what did it look like?

Sources: chuo-uoichiba-ezumen (the plans of Edo's fish market at its landing), zakoba-jawiki (Osaka's fish market on its canal bank), kotobank-zakoba (Osaka's three markets as separate sites), tenma-aomono-jawiki (Osaka's produce market on its riverbank), kotobank-uogashi (fish-market landings across the country)

Yes - fresh food was sold where the boats brought it in, and a great city kept its fish and its produce at different landings. The core of Edo's fish market was the landing ground on the north bank of the Nihonbashi river, because it was the handiest place to land the catch. Osaka's fish market, the Zakoba, stood on the east bank of the upper Hyakkenbori canal, near the river mouth where the city's two great waterways divide, which favored bringing the fish in; it met three times a day, morning, noon and evening. It was one of Osaka's three great markets, with the Dojima rice market and the Tenma produce market, and the river fish had their own market again at the Kyobashi bridgehead. The Tenma produce market stood along the Okawa river.

What it looked like. A plan of Edo's fish market from 1842 records, along the landing from the Nihonbashi bridge to the Edobashi, twelve piers at the bank's edge; behind them, back sheds along the bank for holding the catch and front sheds for selling, fifty in all; twenty-five aqueduct wells; and 126 display stalls and shopfronts pushing out into the street, many of the stalls only 3 shaku (about 3 ft) across. The fish were laid out and sold on ita-bune, flat boards about 70 cm by 150 cm; the plan does not show the boards, and that the narrow stalls were crowded with them is the ward's own inference.

Both cities are far larger than a provincial seat, but the form was not theirs alone: a landing holding a fish market that wholesaled to the trade was found all over the country. Nothing read says how a smaller city's market was laid out.

The rule the map follows: a city on navigable water draws a fish market at its landing: a short row of piers at the bank, a line of storage sheds along the bank behind them, a row of selling sheds and open stalls facing the street behind those, and a well among them. A produce market is a separate site at another stretch of the bank. The market's size at a provincial city is a guess, scaled down from Edo's; its form is attested.
