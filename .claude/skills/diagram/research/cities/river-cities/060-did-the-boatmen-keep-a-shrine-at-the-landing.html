Did the boatmen keep a shrine at the landing?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-09-28; funadama-jawiki (where the boat's guardian is kept, and its shrines on land), kotobank-funadama (the encyclopedias on where in the boat it is kept), katsushika-suijin (the water god kept by the river boatmen of one district, and the dates it gives), kotobank-suitengu (the Suitengu faith before the modern period), sumidagawa-jinja-jawiki (the Sumida river's water-god shrine and the river trades)

The boat's own guardian lives in the boat. The funadama, the guardian spirit of a boat, is kept aboard: dolls, copper coins, human hair, the five grains, dice and the like are placed at the foot of the boat's post, in a place called mori or tsutsu. The encyclopedias differ on where in the boat that is - one says it is in many cases a hole cut in the bow where the mast is stood, another the middle of the boat, and in the sailing-ship era the foot of the mast - and some places only paste on a talisman from a shrine or temple. It is sometimes also given a shrine on land, and those who chiefly worship it are fishermen and shipwrights.

What river boatmen kept ashore was a water god. In Katsushika, now a ward of Tokyo, nearly every village worshipped a water god (suijin). At Niijuku its festival was led by the boatmen of the boats carrying night soil and sand, who took their washed boats out onto the Naka river for it, hung with curtains, with music and food aboard; at Koyano a water-god shrine called Suitengu was kept by the boatmen of the night-soil boats that plied the Ayase river. It formerly stood on the bank where the Ayase and the old Ayase rivers part, and was later moved twice, the second time into the precinct of an Inari shrine; at its festival the boatmen moored their boats near the bank of the Ayase and spent the day drinking in the precinct. The ward history dates what it records only to the 20th century: its earliest time for its festivals is the early Showa era, when they were held with spirit in many villages, and it names a votive plaque of 1928; it gives the Suitengu's first site only as "formerly", and dates nothing before the Showa era.

Before the modern period, the water gods are there but the boatmen are not. The Suitengu faith is older: the lords of Kurume revered their Suitengu for its powers against water disasters and for safe birth, and in 1818 enshrined it at their residence in Edo, where its faith spread to the neighbors. One encyclopedia calls the Kurume shrine a guardian of boats, but in the present tense and with no date for that role. On the Sumida river, the water-god shrine was the guardian of the river and drew the faith of those who worked on it, water-transport operators and boat-hire houses among them - again with no date for that faith. Nothing read places boatmen keeping a water-god shrine before the modern period.

What the map draws. The GM ruled on 2026-09-28 that the maps draw nothing attested only in modern practice. The boatmen's water-god shrine is known only from an undated 20th-century record of custom, so the maps do not draw it: the landing's small shrine is the boat's guardian given a shrine on land. It is not recorded standing at a landing itself, nor with a size; a small shrine at the head of the landing is a guess.
