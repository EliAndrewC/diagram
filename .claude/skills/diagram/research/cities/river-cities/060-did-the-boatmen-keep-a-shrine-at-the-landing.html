Did the boatmen keep a shrine at the landing?

Sources: funadama-jawiki (where the boat's guardian is kept, and its shrines on land), kotobank-funadama (the encyclopedias on where in the boat it is kept), katsushika-suijin (the water god kept by the river boatmen of one district)

The boat's own guardian lives in the boat. The funadama, the guardian spirit of a boat, is kept aboard: dolls, copper coins, human hair, the five grains, dice and the like are placed at the foot of the boat's post, in a place called mori or tsutsu. The encyclopedias differ on where in the boat that is - one says it is in many cases a hole cut in the bow where the mast is stood, another the middle of the boat, at the foot of the mast - and some places only paste on a talisman from a shrine or temple. It is sometimes also given a shrine on land, and those who chiefly worship it are fishermen and shipwrights.

What river boatmen kept ashore was a water god. In Katsushika, now a ward of Tokyo, nearly every village worshipped a water god (suijin). At Niijuku its festival was led by the boatmen of the boats carrying night soil and sand, who took their washed boats out onto the Naka river for it, hung with curtains, with music and food aboard; at Koyano a Suitengu shrine was kept by the boatmen of the night-soil boats that plied the Ayase river.

What the map draws. Both forms are attested - a shrine on land to the boats' guardian, and a water-god shrine kept by the boatmen - and they are one small shrine on the map; which one a landing keeps is a knob, rolled from the map's seed, and the name follows it. Neither is recorded standing at the landing itself, nor with a size; a small shrine at the head of the landing is a guess.
