Does an official's compound have its own stepped landing, or a pier?

Sources: gangi-hiroshima-jawiki (Hiroshima's public and private stepped landings, and why the samurai quarters had none with a gate)

Steps, as a rule. Why a river landing is a flight of steps cut into a faced bank, and a pier the exception for a bank that shelves too gently, is answered at "The wharf's working face". The same is recorded where the water rises and falls most: in coastal ports with a large tidal range, floating piers are used now, but in old times the bank was made into steps, the gangi.

Whose steps. On Hiroshima's six rivers there are said to be some 300 to 400 stepped landings, the public ones used for landing river cargo and for river traffic, and small private ones besides. In the early-modern period, stepped landings were built where boats berthed and goods came ashore, above all in the townsmen's quarters, and served too for drawing water and for laundry; the page does not call those private. A private landing reached through a back gate tends to be found in the townsmen's and merchants' quarters; in the samurai quarters the residences were built back from the revetment and walled, so it is thought there were no stepped landings with a gate there. The private landings are dated to the modern period: some were built for private houses after the domains' boat licenses, which had regulated river traffic, were abolished with the domains themselves, and those that survive on the Kyobashi River, the back-gate kind among them, were almost all built by private owners from the Meiji period on, which is why they come in so many kinds. Nothing read places a townsman's or merchant's private landing, with a back gate or without, before the modern period. No page read describes an intendant's or other official's compound with a landing of its own.

What the map draws. The GM ruled on 2026-09-28 that the maps draw nothing attested only in modern practice, so they do not draw the merchant's private landing behind a back gate: a landing in the townsmen's quarters is the early-modern one, a flight of steps on the bank where boats berth and goods come ashore, not one reached through a house's own gate. An official's compound on a river keeps its wall back from the bank, with the bank street between, and its landing is a flight of steps cut into the revetment across that street, reached from a gate in the wall - never a back gate opening onto the steps. That the landing is the compound's own rather than a public one is a guess. A pier springs from the revetment only where the bank shelves.
