The river watch post at a landing (funabansho)

In the Edo period, domains with a coastline kept boat watch posts at the landings of busy boat routes and at other key points, to inspect the boats that passed and to collect taxes. The best recorded is the shogunate's Nakagawa post on the river approach to Edo, which examined the people and goods entering and leaving the city by water. It was a fenced compound some 150 ft long, with a watch hut at the water.

  • Coastal domains kept boat watch posts at busy landings to inspect boats and collect taxes.
    The Japanese encyclopedia article on the boat watch post describes it as an office set up at the landings of routes boats often traveled and at other key points, for inspecting passing boats and collecting taxes, among other duties.
  • The shogunate's Nakagawa post guarded the river approach to Edo.
    It was set up in 1661 on the north bank at the mouth of the Onagi river, where it meets the Naka, as a checkpoint for river boats, examining the people and goods entering and leaving the city.
  • The Nakagawa post was a compound of about 155 by 100 ft.
    An Edo-period gazetteer records it as a little over 26 by 17 ken.
  • An Edo-period illustration shows a palisade round the post and a watch hut at the river's edge.
    In the illustration of the Nakagawa post in an Edo guidebook, a wooden palisade runs around it, ten spears are kept there, and a watch hut stands at the edge of the Onagi river.
  • The bannermen who held the Nakagawa duty sent their retainers to man it.
    The duty was held by bannermen of the shogun, who did not normally attend the post themselves but sent their retainers.
  • Did the Nakagawa post's inspection become a formality?
    It seems so: the account of the post by Koto ward, the Tokyo ward where it stood, says the clearance procedure appears to have become, little by little, a formality.
  • No account we read describes a watch kept at an intendant's own landing.
    The posts above were checkpoints that domains and the shogunate kept on major routes, and the Nakagawa post guarded the approach to the shogun's city. No page we read describes a watch kept at an intendant's or other local office's own landing, or gives the size of one.
