Cities on rivers

Many of the walled cities of China, and the castle towns of Japan, stood beside a river: Pingyao on the Fen, Xiangyang on the Han, Okayama on the Asahi. The river filled their moats, guarded the side of the city it ran along, and moored their boats. It was also their danger. The Yellow River's bed stands above the city of Kaifeng, and in 1642 the Ming army turned the river on the city to drown the rebels besieging it, killing most of the city's inhabitants.

In this page's reading such a city stood on one bank, with the river as its defense on that side and a dug moat around its landward faces. Water and boats came inside the walls through a water gate, and the wharf lay outside the gate that faced the river. How a canal passes the wall is at the water gate, and which way a channel leaves the river at the angle of an offtake.

  • Did most provincial cities sit on a river?
    This page reads it so, but no page we read gives a proportion; Pingyao and Xiangyang are instances.
  • Did a great river run through a city's walls?
    In this page's reading it did not: where a river does cross a city, it is a lesser one passing through water gates, as Nanjing's inner Qinhuai River leaves the city by its West Water Gate.
  • Did a river city stand on one bank, with the river as its moat on that side?
    In this page's reading it did. Xiangyang stands on the south bank of the Han, with "water on three sides, a mountain on the fourth". Chongqing, a hill city on a peninsula, was walled to "rely on the rivers as the moat".
  • Did a river city dig a moat as well?
    In this page's reading the dug moat covered the faces the river did not. Pingyao, on the east bank of the Fen, has a moat 4 m (~13 ft) wide and deep just outside its walls, and Xiangyang's moat reaches 250 m (~820 ft) at its widest.
  • Was a city's moat fed from its river?
    At Shanghai it was: the county seat's moat joined the tides of the Huangpu River, bringing fresh water to the city and carrying foul water away.
  • This page guesses that the river's current flushed a city's moat.
    That a moat took the river in at its upstream end and let it out downstream, so that the current flushed it, is this page's reasoning, a GUESS: no page we read describes a moat working this way.
  • In Japan, Okayama's castle used the branches of the Asahi River as its moats.
    The channels were laid out to protect the castle's north and east sides, and the castle town stretched north to south between them.
  • How did water and boats enter a city's walls?
    In this page's reading, usually by a water gate that admitted a canal. Suzhou's Pan Gate is a paired gate, one opening for the road and one for boats, its water gate closed by a sluice and a wooden grating and joining the city's inner canals and its moat to the Grand Canal.
  • No page we read puts a dock basin inside a city's water gate.
    The pages on Suzhou's Pan Gate and its city wall describe the paired gates and the canals, and no basin inside the wall.
  • Did a river city's wharf stand outside the gate that faced the river?
    At Shanghai the wharf district, the Shiliupu, lay between the county seat's Little East Gate and the Huangpu, with several thousand sand junks moored south of it. What the wharf held - jetties, warehouses and a market - is this page's reading. It is also, in this page's reading, the river city's form of the district outside a city gate (guanxiang), which this page guesses was a gate market; that district is at the ground outside a city gate.

A river beside a city was also its danger, as the Yellow River was to Kaifeng.

  • The Yellow River's bed stands higher than the city of Kaifeng.
    A study of Kaifeng's floods shows the river's elevation above the city's.
  • How often did the Yellow River flood Kaifeng?
    About forty times in three thousand years, by the count of a study of the city's floods. By another count it flooded more than 300 times from the Southern Song to the late Qing, five major floods devastating the city between 1375 and 1416.
  • The flood that drowned Kaifeng in 1642 was let loose on purpose.
    While rebels besieged the city, the Ming army turned the Yellow River on the city to drown them.
  • How many died in Kaifeng's 1642 flood?
    Most of its inhabitants. Wikipedia's article on the flood gives 300,000 of the city's 378,000 residents dead of the flood and of the famine and plague after it. Its article on Kaifeng gives a different figure: only 30,000 of the city's 370,000 remained.
