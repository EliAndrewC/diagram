Before modern engineering, did anyone set the angle at which a channel leaves a river?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-09-28; hamura-tamagawa-josui (an Edo-period intake, and why its site was chosen)

What the period shows is where an intake goes, not the angle it leaves at. The Tamagawa aqueduct, which carried water to Edo, was built in 1653. Its intake at Hamura is thought to have been put there because the ground is high and because the Tama river strikes the ridge of the Kusabana hills on its south bank, so that the water gathers toward the opposite bank. At its weir today, gabions, frames and a throw-across weir guide the river's natural flow into the intake; the city's page does not say that the weir is as it was in the Edo period.

No angle was found before the modern period. Nothing read from before the Meiji Restoration in Japan, or before the end of the Qing in China, gives the angle at which a canal, a moat or an intake leaves or rejoins a river; every angle this record holds for such a junction comes from modern engineering, at "Which way does an offtake leave a river".

What the map draws. The GM ruled on 2026-09-28 that the maps draw nothing attested only in modern practice. The two tilts at a moat's river junctions rest on modern engineering alone, so the maps do not draw them: both arms meet the river square, which is itself a guess, since no period angle was found either way. The one period form found is the intake's site, where the river's water gathers toward the bank it leaves from.
