City canals: how wide, what lines their banks, and where they meet the river (horikawa)

A city canal was a channel dug through a town to bring boats to its doors and storehouses. They ranged from Kyoto's Takase canal, about 8 m (~26 ft) wide, to Osaka's Dotonbori, four to eight times as wide in 1787. Its banks were faced in stone, and along them stood either the backs of houses with steps down to the water or a landing lined with wholesalers' storehouses.

Where a canal met the river it took one of two forms. At Suzhou the city's canals reached the moat, and through it the outside waters, by the water gates in the wall; at Osaka the canals were cut straight off the rivers. How a canal passes the wall is at the water gate.

  • Kyoto's Takase canal was about 8 m (~26 ft) wide and 30 cm (~12 in) deep.
    It was cut between 1611 and 1614, an entirely artificial channel.
  • How many of its boats did the Takase canal hold abreast?
    About four. Its boats averaged 13 m (~43 ft) long and 2 m (~7 ft) wide, so its 8 m (~26 ft) is about four boats wide, by this page's arithmetic.
  • In the Edo period, Osaka's Dotonbori was 20 ken across at its east end and 34 ken at its west.
    A document of 1787 gives the two figures, about 36 m (~119 ft) and 62 m (~203 ft).
  • Today the Dotonbori is about 30 m (~98 ft) wide in its eastern half and 50 m (~164 ft) in its western.
    The city of Osaka gives the two figures for its two sections.
  • Suzhou's canals were faced in stone on both banks.
    They were for the most part cut by hand, both banks faced with solid stone revetments, some with wooden or stone railings along the top.
  • Where Edo's outer moat ran past the castle, its two banks differed.
    The moat served also as a drain and a canal. It was walled in stone on the castle side, while its other bank was a goods landing, the Shirobe landing.

What stood along a canal's banks took two forms, one recorded in a Chinese city and one at Japanese landings.

  • In Suzhou, houses backed onto the canal, with stone steps down to the water.
    A house fronted on a street and backed on the canal, and at its back a water landing had stone steps down to the water, for boats, for drawing water and for washing.
  • At a Japanese landing (kashi), wholesalers and their storehouses lined the bank.
    From the Edo period on, merchants with wholesale houses and their storehouses (kura) gathered at a landing and made it one commercial settlement. The landing's working face is at the wharf's working face.
  • The Takase canal had loading basins cut off its side.
    They were for loading and unloading goods and for turning boats: seven between Nijo and Shijo avenues (Kyoto's Second and Fourth) at first, nine by the end of the seventeenth century, and seven again by the middle of the eighteenth. The larger boat basins inside a city are at the internal dock.

Where a canal met the river, the record shows two forms.

  • Suzhou's canals reached the outside waters through the moat and its water gates.
    The water gate of the Pan Gate joins the city's inner canals and its moat to the Grand Canal. A city plan of 1229 shows a broad moat outside the wall, joined to the canals inside it through the water gates, its western stretch part of the Grand Canal.
  • Osaka's canals opened straight off its rivers.
    The Nishi-Yokobori, dug in 1600 by a timber merchant who was a town elder, branched from the Tosabori River just above a bridge and ran south into the Dotonbori.
  • Osaka's oldest canal began as its castle's outer moat.
    The Higashi-Yokobori, dug in 1585 on Toyotomi Hideyoshi's order as the castle's western outer moat, branches south from the upper Tosabori River. It was dug for a Japanese castle, not beside a walled Chinese city's gate.
  • Could a walled city open a gate straight onto its river?
    A water gate, by the English definition, is a fortified gate leading directly from a castle or town wall onto a quay, a river side or a harbor, so a walled city could open onto its river without the moat between.
