Most provincial cities sit on a river

Sources: kaifeng-flood-1642-enwiki (300,000 of 378,000 killed), kaifeng-pmc7048742 (~40 floods in 3,000 years), pan-gate-enwiki (the paired land-and-water gate), shanghai-xiancheng-chengqiang-zhwiki, panmen-zhwiki, xiangyang-cheng-zhwiki, pingyao-enwiki, shiliupu-zhwiki

  • The history (China first, Japan agreeing). MOST provincial cities, in this page's reading, sit on a river (Tango, on an off-map stream, is the exception). The trunk river NEVER runs through the walls - the one great city that let a river in, Kaifeng, silted its bed meters above the plain and was flooded about forty times in three millennia - more than 300 floodings by another count, five major ones in 1375-1416 - the 1642 flood alone killing ~300k of 378k. Instead the city stands ON one bank: the RIVER is the water defense on its flank, and the dug MOAT covers the three landward faces, tapping the river upstream and returning downstream so the current flushes it (Xiangyang's Han-river face + dug moat; Pingyao on the Fen; the Asahi's branches at Okayama "utilized to create moats"; Shanghai's county-seat moat connected to the Huangpu's tides). The ONE way water legitimately enters the walls is a water gate (shuimen) admitting a navigable CANAL, not the river - Suzhou's Pan Gate, a paired land-and-water gate with a sluiced arch, feeding the city's inner canals + a dock basin. The riverfront wharf (jetties, warehouses, market) sits OUTSIDE the water-side gate, the river-city version of the gate market (guan-xiang).

The rule the map follows: a river city's map shows a trunk river running off the sheet at both ends and drawn heavier than anything else on it, the dug moat included - the river IS the stronger defense on that flank, and the map should not say otherwise. The moat is an OPEN arc rather than a closed ring: it covers the three landward faces and both of its ends run onto the river, which closes the water ring itself, so every point of the wall stands behind either the moat or the river. A navigable canal - lighter than the moat, heavier than a field ditch - leads from the water through a grated water gate in the wall to a dock basin inside; the wall is drawn with a matching gap at the water gate and carries no tower on that vertex. Planked jetties spring from the near bank, each rooted at the bank with its tip in the near half of the water: a finger, never a bar spanning the river. Buildings keep out of the canal's water, and the canal must actually reach the dock - connected like a street reaching a road, with no visible gap. The wharf itself - jetties, stores, market - stands outside the water-side gate, which is the river city's version of the gate market. A river city has no Imperial spine through it: its highway passes off the sheet, so what the map owes instead is a road net that leaves the view in at least two directions. And the dead cross the river: the moat's water set-back leaves no dry landward fringe to put them on, so the funerary ground sits on the far bank, downstream of the city and clear of the bridge road.

The rule the map follows: three rules a river city shares with every other city on these maps. A gate's two posts stand to either side of the opening and square to the wall's LOCAL tangent, so the posts of an east-west gate stand north and south of it rather than upright on the page. A samurai country estate faces the capital, whichever direction that is from this particular city, and stands clear of the highway out of the gate. And a burial or cremation ground sits beside the farmland, never on a paddy body or on its ditches. One more thing the tier carries as the setting's own canon, from the GM's notes: a city holds a temple to each of its clan's two patron fortunes, and those are the temples that get labeled - the warrior fortune may be added where there is a samurai quarter, and everything else is a small wayside shrine, however thematic a larger dedication would be.
