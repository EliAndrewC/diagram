Wharves and landings: piers, quays and stepped landings (kashi, gangi)

Where a city met its river boats, the working face, in this record's reading, was often not a pier but the bank itself, faced and cut into flights of steps down into the water: the stepped landing the Japanese called a gangi, and one of the two forms of the Chinese wharf, the matou. A river's level rises and falls with the seasons, and a moored boat's side rises and falls with it; steps meet the boat at whatever height the water stands, where a quay wall would not. A long pier earned its place by reaching out to water still deep at low tide.

In Japan the river landing, the kashi, was more than its steps. Wholesalers and their storehouses gathered there into a commercial settlement, and a landing was governed as a town or a village in its own right. Some cities kept their boats at the bank and others let them in: Hankou, on a flowing river, kept its wharves along the bank, while Edo and Nagoya dug canals that brought the boats in. Which a city got seems to have followed its water, in this record's reading, a GUESS.

  • A stepped landing was cut into the slope of a faced riverbank.
    A Japanese encyclopedia describes Hiroshima's stepped landings as a stepped harbor facility and revetment built on the slope of the river bank, and another calls the gangi a stepped structure at a boat landing, found at many landings before modern times because it let people climb up and down and handle cargo.
  • When did stepped landings turn from timber to stone?
    Early in the Edo period, it is thought. Timber standing in the water rotted easily, so stone is thought to have come into use early in the Edo period.
  • The Chinese wharf took two forms, a solid pier or a flight of stairs.
    A Chinese encyclopedia defines the matou as a long solid pier (a mole) running from the bank out into the water, or merely a row of stairs running from the shore down into it.
  • A Chinese water town kept stone landings too.
    Zhouzhuang, a water town of crisscrossing waterways under Suzhou, still has its stone river landings along with its old stone arch bridges.
  • The steps answered a river that rose and fell.
    The water rose and fell with the tides and with the river's flow, and the side of a moored boat with it, so a gangplank was laid onto the steps, which, unlike a quay wall, met the boat at any level. A deck at one fixed height would, by this record's reasoning, stand too high to reach in the dry season and awash in the wet. A river rises in its flood season every year, though no page we read says by how much.
  • The floating pier is today's answer to the same problem.
    At coastal ports with a large tidal range, floating piers are used now so that boats can come alongside easily; in former times the bank was made into a flight of steps. A floating dock is a platform held up on pontoons, much used in marinas.
  • The pleasure pier was a nineteenth-century addition to the working pier.
    Working piers were built to handle passengers and cargo onto and off ships and canal boats; the first pleasure piers, for strolling out over the sea, were built in Britain early in the nineteenth century.
  • Where did a river landing want a pier?
    Where the bank shelved too gently for a loaded boat to come alongside, by this record's reasoning, a GUESS. Long piers are found where the tidal range is large, reaching out to deep water at low tide; no page we read ties a pier to a river bank's slope or to a cargo. So a landing on a steep faced bank would need few piers or none, and one on a shallow, shelving reach several.
  • Did the kashi build the same stepped bank?
    In this record's reading, a GUESS, they did. Hiroshima's stepped landings stood on its rivers, and the stepped landing was common at landings before modern times, but the pages on the kashi say what it did and nothing of how its bank was built.

What a landing needed depended on the boats that came to it and what they carried.

  • How long were the river boats?
    From about 9 m (~31 ft) to 27 m (~89 ft). The takasebune, Japan's long, flat river boat, was about 9 m (~31 ft) long in a record of the Heian period and 22 m (~73 ft) in a technical record of the Edo period. The boats on Kyoto's Takase canal were fifteen-koku boats, the small end of the type, while the two-hundred-koku boats of the Tone River ran to some 27 m (~89 ft).
  • A landing's traffic included the tax rice.
    New landings were set up under the domain lords to carry tax rice and other goods from the provinces to Edo and Osaka. Where a capital stored its rice is at Rice storehouses and the rice brokers in a capital (kura, fudasashi), and how a village's freight reached the water at Where does a village's freight go?
  • Timber came downriver afloat and was kept in the water.
    At Edo all timber was floated, worked and stored in the water, in a timber pond, and the timber-yard place names across Japan come from timber carried by sea or floated straight down the river from where it was cut. So timber, in this record's reading, a GUESS, wanted no pier.
  • A port that landed stone would want at least one pier, a GUESS.
    Stone is a heavy cargo lifted out of a boat, and no page we read speaks of stone landed at a river port.

In Japan a landing was a settlement in its own right, and often what made a town.

  • Wholesalers and their storehouses gathered at a landing.
    From the Edo period on, merchants with wholesale houses and their storehouses gathered at a landing, forming a single commercial settlement. At its center stood the landing's wholesaler or a shipping wholesaler, who took fees for loading and unloading boats and storing cargo.
  • A landing was its boat moorings and an unloading ground, the kashiba.
    A landing had its boat moorings and its unloading ground, together sometimes called the kashiba.
  • Landings grew where routes met.
    They flourished at river junctions and forks, at crossings with the main highways, and in castle towns and the gate towns of temples and shrines.
  • A landing was governed as a town or a village.
    It had the same town or village officers as any other, and the landing's wholesaler often held that office as well.
  • Boatmen, porters and innkeepers lived at a landing.
    Its residents were boat owners, the boatmen and crews they hired, and the porters and day laborers who loaded and unloaded the cargo, with tea houses and inns for shippers, their agents and travelers. The watch over the river at an official's landing is at The river watch post at a landing (funabansho), and the boatmen's shrine at The boatmen's shrine at the landing (funadama, suijin)
  • Many landings were markets as well.
    Because goods lay over there in quantity, a market formed and merchants gathered, and many landings came to work as markets. A city's fish and produce market at its landing is at Fish and produce markets at the landing (uoichiba).
  • At Edo's Nihonbashi the landings were named for their cargo or their boats.
    Both banks there prospered as the rice landing, the small-boat landing, the salt landing and others.
  • Why did Kawagoe's port move to another river?
    The sources give the old port's trouble, not the reason for the move. Kawagoe's outer port was at first a group of landings on the Iruma River, set off from the castle town by a belt of low wet ground that made heavy goods hard to carry; five landings on the upper Shingashi River later served as its outer port.
  • The word kashi first meant a piece of a boat's gear.
    It came to mean the mooring post fixed at a landing, and from that the landing where boats were unloaded.

Who could have a landing of their own is recorded on the rivers of Hiroshima.

  • How many stepped landings line Hiroshima's six rivers?
    Some 300 to 400. Besides the public ones, used for landing river cargo and for river traffic, the count takes in small private ones.
  • Hiroshima's early modern landings stood where boats berthed and goods came ashore, above all in the townsmen's quarters.
    They served too for drawing water and for laundry. The encyclopedia article on Hiroshima's stepped landings does not call them private.
  • Did a samurai residence have its own landing through a back gate?
    It is thought not. A private landing reached through a back gate tends to be found in the townsmen's and merchants' quarters; in the samurai quarters the residences were built back from the revetment and walled, so it is thought there were no landings with a gate there.
  • Did merchants have landings of their own before 1868?
    No page we read places one there. The private landings are dated to the modern period: some were built for private houses after the boat licenses that had regulated river traffic were abolished with the domains, and those that survive on the Kyobashi River, the back-gate kind among them, were almost all built by their owners from the Meiji period on, which is why they come in so many kinds.
  • Did an official's compound keep a landing of its own?
    No page we read describes one; the only word on samurai residences is that they had no gated landing.

Some cities did not unload at the bank at all, but let the boats in.

  • Suzhou's city gates each had a water gate beside the land gate.
    An old map of the city, the Pingjiang map, draws all five of its gates with a land gate and a water gate together. The water gates themselves are at Water gates and sluices (shuimen), and the canals inside a city at City canals (horikawa).
  • Edo and Nagoya dug canals to bring boats in to their castles (funairi-bori).
    Edo dug ten of them around 1612 to 1615 to land the stone for its castle walls, and Nagoya cut its Horikawa canal from Ise Bay to the castle, it is often said in 1610, to carry the castle's building materials.
  • Yanagawa and Matsue are laced with still-water channels.
    Yanagawa stands on the silt of the Chikugo River beside a tidal sea, its channels drawn from above a tide barrier, and Matsue's waterways spread out from its castle moat.
  • Hankou, on a flowing river, kept its wharves along the bank.
    At Hankou the wharves stood in rows along the north bank of the Han River's new channel, joined into a long street of shops and government offices.
  • Did a city's water decide whether its boats came inside?
    In this record's reading, a GUESS, it did. The boat-entry canals we read of belong to cities on delta or lakeside ground, though only Yanagawa's page says so, and in canal country the canal is the street grid, while the river cities kept their wharves on the bank; no page we read states the contrast as a rule.
  • A dock basin dug off a flowing river silts up.
    A harbor basin silts up and must be dredged regularly, as the river's silty water exchanges with the basin's. That such a basin also stands open to the river's flood is this record's reasoning.
  • On a river, boats going upstream were hauled from the bank.
    Going upstream was very heavy labor, and unless the current was very gentle, river transport could not have existed without towing, the boat drawn by a long rope from the embankment. The path the haulers walked is at Towpaths along a river (qiandao). That a dock basin serves boats arriving under pole or sail on still water is this record's reasoning.
