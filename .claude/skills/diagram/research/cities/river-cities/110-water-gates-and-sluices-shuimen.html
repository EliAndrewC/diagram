Water gates and sluices (shuimen)

A water gate was a passage through the thickness of a city wall for a canal or a river and the boats on it, shut at night. At Suzhou's Pan Gate it stands beside the land gate, a doubled passage about 24 m (~79 ft) deep closed by a sluice gate and a wooden palisade, and twelfth-century Kaifeng let iron-clad lattice gates down to the water over its East Water Gate at night. A city had a water gate beside every land gate, as Suzhou did, or one wherever a river crossed its wall, as Kaifeng did, six of them there.

A sluice is a board set across a channel to control how much water passes. Where a moat drew from a river and drained back to it, its gates held its level; how they were worked from day to day is this record's reading, a GUESS, on no page we read. The sluices that let water onto fields ran by rule: on a fourteenth-century Chinese canal each opened only on a permit and in its turn, and in Japan water was handed out by turns in a drought from the medieval period.

  • Suzhou's Pan Gate closed its water passage with a sluice gate and a wooden palisade.
    Its water gate stands on the south side of the land gate and, like it, is doubled, an inner gate and an outer. The passage is 24 m (~79 ft) deep, with a sluice gate and a palisade set front and back, and a hidden passage between the two gates leads up to the gate tower. The city's government gives the two gates as 4.6 m (~15 ft) apart and the whole as 24.5 m (~80 ft) deep, with revetments built along both sides of the channel between them.
  • Kaifeng let iron-clad lattice gates down over its water gates at night.
    Its East Water Gate, the water gate of the Bian river's lower course, spanned the river. At night its iron-clad lattice gates were let down to the water like a sluice, and a gate on each bank beside it let people through on foot.
  • Where a whole river entered a city, its water gate could be a row of arches.
    Nanjing's East Water Gate, where the Qinhuai river enters the city, was built three tiers high with eleven vaulted openings in each. The lowest tier carried the water, and the upper two hid troops and stored grain. The article dates the level-control sluice in the lowest tier to the late Qing dynasty, so the gate's earlier closing gear is not described.
  • How wide was a water gate's passage?
    No page we read gives it. Pan Gate's depth and the spacing of its two gates are published, but not the width of its water passage, nor how many openings it has.
  • Was a boom or a chain laid across the water at a gate?
    None of the pages we read on the water gates of Suzhou, Kaifeng and Nanjing describes one. The closing gear they describe is the lowered grille, the sluice gate and the palisade.

How many water gates a city had followed one of two patterns.

  • At Suzhou every city gate had a water gate beside it.
    On the plan of Suzhou carved in stone in 1229, each of the city's five gates is drawn with a land gate and a water gate together, so the city's canals reached every gate.
  • How many water gates did Kaifeng's outer wall have?
    Six, by this record's count, one wherever a river crossed the wall. The twelfth-century account of the city names each with the river it carries: two on the Cai river, beside the Chenzhou and Dailou land gates; the East and West Water Gates on the Bian; the Northeast on the Wuzhang river; and the Northwest on the Jinshui. Two stand beside a land gate, and the rest are listed apart from any.

A sluice (suimon) is the other gate on a city's water: a board across a channel, where a moat draws on a river and lets out again, or where a field channel leaves a canal.

  • How was a sluice board raised?
    In this record's reading, a GUESS from the gate's form, the board rides in grooves cut in its two posts. It is lifted by hand on a small gate, or by a windlass on a larger one, from a timber crossbeam spanning the posts above the water, the keeper standing on the beam or the bank. None of the Japanese encyclopedia articles we read on the water gate and the sluice gate describes the mechanism.
  • Was a moat's sluice kept open or shut?
    Neither, in this record's reading, a GUESS: no page we read describes how a moat's gates were worked from day to day. A wet moat loses water all the time to seepage and evaporation, and standing water fouls. So a moat fed from a river and drained back to it would keep both its boards partly raised, a small steady flow in and the same out, and its keeper's ordinary work would be trimming the opening by a few inches.
  • Was a moat's intake closed when the river was in spate?
    In this record's reading, a GUESS, it was wound down hard: storm water carries the silt that chokes a feeder channel, and a moat must not surge. A canal of the Yuan dynasty in Shaanxi was rested the same way when its river rose in the summer spate.
  • Were a moat's gates shut in a drought or a siege?
    In this record's reading, a GUESS: in a drought the outfall was shut to hold what water the moat had, and under siege both were shut, to hold as much as it could.
  • Moats silted up and were dredged again and again.
    The gazetteer of Guangdong records Guangzhou's moat silting up over the years and dredged by successive officials, between 1041 and 1048 and again in 1210. For a dredging, this record's reading, a GUESS, is that the moat's two gates worked against each other - the intake shut, the outfall wide open - to draw the water down for the diggers.

The sluices that let water onto fields did a different job: a moat's gates held a level, while a field's sluice delivered each field its share, and it ran on a calendar.

  • On a fourteenth-century Chinese canal, a field sluice opened only on a permit and in its turn.
    The water rules of the Jing canal in Shaanxi, written down under the Yuan dynasty, let a sluice open only when the office had issued its clerk a permit. The water ran at the appointed time, and the sluice was shut as soon as the watering was done, the water handed on to the sluices above it. The work's preface is dated 1342, and its rules cite an order of 1274.
  • The Jing canal ran for a season and was repaired out of it.
    Water was let in from the first day of the tenth month and stopped in the seventh, and the canal and its weir were repaired in the eighth and ninth months, by the lunar calendar. The canal watered wheat, hemp and millet, not rice.
  • In Japan, water was handed out by turns in a drought (bansui) from the medieval period.
    Two Japanese reference works describe it as a way of sharing irrigation water in a drought, the fields drawing water in a fixed order, kept as unwritten custom until the late medieval period, when its rules came to be written down. Irrigation districts today still switch to it only in a drought.
  • In what months did a rice field's intake open and shut?
    No page from before 1868 that we read gives the months. One district's present-day schedule opens its water on 21 April and shuts it on 28 September, while the records we read of turn-by-turn delivery and of water disputes before 1868 give no opening or closing month.
