The wharf's working face: piers, quays and stepped landings

Sources: kashi-jawiki (the river-port kashi with its warehouses; gangi as landing stairs), gangi-kowan-jawiki, floating-dock-enwiki, takasebune-jawiki, kotobank-takasebune, pier-enwiki, gangi-hiroshima-jawiki, xunqi-zhwiki, matou-zhwiki

The GM's question (2026-08-11): is three piers the right number for six granaries and three warehouses, and is there something between a pier and a boardwalk - a dock - that the map should be drawing? ("My guess is no on a boardwalk, because I associate that with tourism rather than an actual wharf. But some marinas have docks and piers both. I don't know how this would have worked.")

The boardwalk instinct is right, and for a better reason than association. A boardwalk is a promenade - a surface for walking along the water for its own sake. Nothing in a pre-modern working river port is built for that. Every surface at a wharf exists to get cargo off a hull and into a store as few times handled as possible.

The finding, China first (Song/Ming river ports), Japan as the tiebreaker. The dominant form is not the pier at all: it is the revetted quay face - the bank itself cut back, faced with stone or timber cribbing, and fitted with stepped landings at intervals. The Chinese matou (碼頭) is, in one of its two forms, a stepped landing (stone, in this page's reading) set into a faced bank; the Japanese riverside kashi (河岸) district uses the same arrangement, with the stepped landing called gangi (雁木) and the kura standing directly behind the bank street.

The reason is hydrology, and it is decisive: a river's level moves through the year by many feet. A fixed-height deck is at the right height for a few weeks and wrong the rest of the time - too high to reach in the dry season, awash in the wet. A flight of steps down a faced bank is at the right height at EVERY level, because the boat simply lies against a different tread. That is why the stepped quay is the norm on rivers and the projecting pier is the exception, and it inverts the intuition a modern marina gives you.

So what is the pier FOR? Reach, when the bank shelves too gently for a loaded hull to come alongside - the pier walks the working face out to where the water is deep enough. A port with a steep faced bank needs few or none; a port on a shallow shelving reach needs several.

What this means for the count. The question "is three piers enough for nine store buildings" has the wrong shape: the granaries and warehouses are not each served by a pier. The bank is the unloading face, continuous along the frontage, and its capacity is measured in feet of usable mooring, not in piers. A takasebune river barge ran from about 30 ft in the Heian record to 73 ft in an Edo technical record, and the two-hundred-koku boats of the Tone ran to some 89 ft, so a few hundred feet of faced bank moors four or five of the middling sort abreast of the stores they are filling - which is ample for six granaries and three warehouses, whose turnover is seasonal and dominated by the tax-rice harvest. Three piers on top of that is not too few. It may be slightly too many for a bank this steep, but they are within the band, and a port that also handles timber and stone wants at least one.

Decision for the map. Keep the three piers. The thing that actually does most of the work is the faced quay edge along the granary frontage, with stepped landings at intervals, and mooring posts. That is a real, drawable, map-visible feature - a line of revetment along the bank with two or three step flights notched into it. No boardwalk, no floating dock (a moored floating stage is a modern marina's answer to the same level problem, and the steps are the pre-modern one).

What the map draws. The faced edge is a stone band with a coursing line, stepped landings notched into it (four treads each, descending toward the water), and mooring posts along the top. On Shiro Daika it runs the granary frontage, following the river's own line rather than pinned, so a re-routed river carries the wharf with it. The quay is drawn as a line along the bank (like the towpath), not as a building, and a jetty may spring FROM the faced bank, which is the whole point of the pier - reach, starting at the revetment.
