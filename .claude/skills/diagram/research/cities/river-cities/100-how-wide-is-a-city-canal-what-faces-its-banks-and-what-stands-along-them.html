How wide is a city canal, what faces its banks, and what stands along them?

Sources: takasegawa-kyoto-jawiki (the narrow canal's width and depth), kyoto-city-takasegawa (its boats and its side basins), kotobank-dotonbori (a wide canal's Edo-period width), osaka-city-dotonbori (the same canal today), sohu-pingjiang-suzhou (Suzhou's stone banks and house steps), guidetokyo-edo-horiwari (Edo's stone-faced castle side and the landing opposite), kashi-jawiki (the wholesalers and storehouses of a landing)

A canal inside a city is drawn only where the setting has one: in the setting, transport canals belong to the Lion Clan, who follow the Chinese precedent, and appear elsewhere only when the GM asks for one, never by default (where a village's freight goes). Where a map does carry one, three things decide how it looks.

Its width runs along a wide range, set by what it carries. At the narrow end is Kyoto's Takase canal, cut between 1611 and 1614: an entirely artificial channel about 8 m (26 ft) wide and normally about 30 cm deep, worked by boats 2 m wide and 13 m long on average - so the channel is about four boats wide. At the wide end is Osaka's Dotonbori, which a document of 1787 gives as 20 ken across at its east end and 34 at its west, about 36 and 62 m (120 and 200 ft); today it runs about 30 m in its eastern half and 50 m in its western. Which job sets which width is a guess from the two figures, not a finding: no page read says what the Dotonbori carried, so a narrow barge cut to the townspeople's doors is drawn at the first figure and a wide freight channel at the second.

Its banks are faced. Suzhou's in-city rivers were mostly cut by hand, and both banks are faced with solid stone revetments, some with wooden or stone railings along the top. Where the water runs past a castle the two banks differ: Edo's outer moat, which also served as a drain and a canal, was walled in stone on the castle side, while its other bank was a goods landing, the Shirobe landing.

What stands along it takes one of two forms.

  • House backs with water steps (the Suzhou form). A house fronts on a street and backs on the canal, and at its back a water landing has stone steps down to the water, for boats, for drawing water and for washing.
  • An open landing with storehouses behind (the kashi form). Wholesalers and their kura gather at the bank and make it one commercial settlement, with the working face described in the wharf's working face.

A canal may also carry side basins cut off it for loading. The Takase had seven between Nijo and Shijo avenues (Kyoto's Second and Fourth) at first, nine by the end of the seventeenth century and seven again by the middle of the eighteenth; the larger boat-entry canals are the internal dock.

The rule the map follows: a city canal is drawn between about 25 and 200 ft wide - toward the narrow end for a barge cut feeding the town, toward the wide end for a channel that carries the city's through freight. That is a degree along a range, chosen for the canal's job, not a roll. Both banks are drawn faced in stone; where a bank is the castle's, it is the castle's wall. What lines a canal reach is a knob rolled from the map's seed per settlement: either house backs with small stepped water landings at intervals, or an open landing with a row of storehouses behind it.
