Offices of the six ministries (liubu)

In this setting every level of government, from the Empire down to a province, works through six ministries - Rites, Revenue, Retainers, War, Works and Justice; that is the setting's own. Imperial China had six ministries too, the liubu, and how they were housed depended on the level of government. In a local yamen they were six rooms off the court before the great hall. Only at the imperial capital did each become a compound of its own, the six lining the approach to the palace, outside its walls.

The Japanese pages we read give a domain no ministries by that name. At Nagoya the lord's palace, inside the castle, was both the lord's residence and the domain's chief government office, and some of its offices moved out into the town when they outgrew it. The council of senior retainers that advised the lord stayed inside, in the palace (Inside the castle: the lord's palace, the council and the baileys (goten, honmaru)).

  • In a local yamen, the six offices were rooms, not compounds of their own.
    In a local yamen the six offices stood between the ceremonial gate and the great hall: personnel, revenue and rites on the east, war, justice and works on the west, generally two bays to each. The county yamen of Neixiang sets them out the same way, the six in front of its great hall.
  • Pingyao's county yamen had over two hundred rooms.
    Six rooms were a small share of a county yamen. Pingyao's county yamen covers about 26,000 square meters (~279,900 sq ft, ~6.4 acres), 203 m (~666 ft) long and 131 m (~430 ft) wide, with over two hundred rooms across its courts.
  • At a governor-general's yamen the six offices were still rooms, three bays to each.
    The Zhili governor-general's yamen at Baoding is the highest in rank of the yamen that survive. Its six offices stand in front of the great hall, nine bays on each side: Personnel, Revenue and Rites on the east, War, Justice and Works on the west, three bays to each office. A writer who compared it with the county yamen found its plan broadly the same, one fixed and standardized pattern.
  • Were a higher seat's six offices larger than a county's?
    A little, on the one comparison we have: three bays to each office at the governor-general's yamen against generally two at a county's, half as many bays again, and rooms of the yamen at both. The pages give no bay's width or depth, so how much more floor that was is not known. How a whole yamen's size went with its rank is with the governor's compound (The provincial government's seat: the governor's compound and where it stands (yamen)).
  • The great hall was five bays wide at every rank.
    The great halls of the Zhili governor-general's yamen, of the Huozhou department magistrate's yamen and of the Neixiang county magistrate's yamen are each five bays wide.
  • At the Ming capital the ministries became compounds of their own, built one at a time.
    The Ministry of Rites was begun first, in 1430, its layout copied from the Ministry of Rites at Nanjing, the earlier Ming capital, and the ministries of Personnel, Revenue, Works, Justice and War were built after it, one after another.
  • The capital's ministries stood outside the palace, on either side of its approach.
    In Beijing they were built on both sides of the Chengtian gate, today's Tiananmen, flanking the Corridor of a Thousand Steps, the ceremonial approach outside it.
  • At the capital, five ministries stood east of the approach and Justice west of it.
    East of the Daqing gate stood Personnel, Revenue and Rites, and further east, across a street, War and Works. Justice, in the Qing, stood west of the gate, beside the Censorate and the Court of Judicial Review - with the other judicial offices rather than with its fellow ministries.
  • What was inside a ministry's compound?
    The Ming and Qing Ministry of Revenue worked through fourteen bureaus, one for each province, while in the Qing the things it managed - the mint, the three treasuries, the granaries - were offices of their own under it rather than rooms in it. The Ministry of Rites kept a great storehouse of its own.
  • No page we read gives the size of a capital ministry's compound.
    The pages we found place and date the compounds but give none of their dimensions, and a provincial ministry office has no historical counterpart to measure, since a province's six offices were rooms in its yamen.

In Japan, the evidence comes from the castle towns, the seats of the domains' governments, and from the shogunate's own offices.

  • Nagoya's palace was both its lord's residence and the domain's government office.
    Nagoya's second-bailey palace served as the lord's residence and as the administrative center of the domain's government.
  • At Matsumoto the county and town offices were moved out into the town.
    The second bailey's palace was only about two-thirds the size of the main bailey's, so the county office and the town office were moved to Rokku town, and the conference room for the lord and the village headmen to Agetsuchi town.
  • Was the office of a larger city larger? Not among the shogunate's offices.
    Edo was, early in the eighteenth century, the world's largest city, of about a million inhabitants. Its two town magistracies (The town magistracy and its constables (machi-bugyo, doshin)) are given at about 2,630 and 2,720 tsubo, roughly 9,000 square meters (~97,000 sq ft) each. The Nagasaki magistrate's Tateyama office was 3,278.5 tsubo, about 10,800 square meters (~116,700 sq ft), about twice its West office. The Takayama jin'ya, from which an intendant governed the province of Hida, has a protected site of 9,807 square meters (~105,600 sq ft) without the plaza in front of it. This project reads the three as one size: the office that governed the largest city was no larger than the others.
  • No page we read gives the size of a domain's own government offices.
    The searches for a domain's town magistracy returned the shogunate's offices and no domain's.
  • Did the offices move out of the ruler's seat as the government grew?
    This project reads it so. In China the six are rooms of the local yamen and compounds outside the palace at the capital; in Japan the domain's offices began in the palace and some moved into the town as they outgrew it. Read together, the two traditions keep the ruler's own hall inside and move the offices out as they grow - one institution at different sizes, not unrelated conventions.
