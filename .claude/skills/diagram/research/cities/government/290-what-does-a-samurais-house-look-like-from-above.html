What does a samurai's house look like from above - its gate, its wall or hedge, its garden?

Sources: kotobank-bukeyashiki (the enclosures and gates of a provincial town); bukeyashiki-wiki and jta-nagayamon (the senior house's wall and gate); nagayamon-jawiki, matsue-bukeyashiki-about, matsue-bukeyashiki and bunka-akasaka-bukeyashikimon (what the long-house gate held, and its size); fukui-bushi-jutaku (the servants' range on the street); semboku-kakunodate-en, semboku-bukeyashiki and jta-aoyagi-kakunodate (a small town's fences, gardens and what stood inside the gate); chiran-kagoshima-kankou (the stone wall and hedge); touken-bukeyashiki-madori (a kitchen's place); jta-ashigaru-kaga (the foot soldier's garden)

Every lot is enclosed; what encloses it follows rank. In a provincial castle town a samurai house was closed by an earthen wall, a board fence or a hedge, and entered by a long-house gate or one of two simpler gates. The senior retainers' houses had the earthen wall and the long-house gate (nagayamon), and at Kanazawa the long-house gate was allowed only from the middle rank up. Lower down the forms part: Kakunodate's houses stood behind a black board fence taller than a man or a hedgerow, while a low-ranking family there had no formal gate at all - two posts and a brushwood fence; Chiran's lanes run between stone walls with clipped hedges behind them; and the foot soldiers' plots at Kanazawa were hedged. Those are distinct forms, each attested, so the enclosure below the senior rank is a knob.

The long-house gate is a building. It was the front-gate form of an upper samurai's house, with a gatekeeper's room and servants' rooms on either side of the passage; Matsue's housed its gatekeeper and menservants, whose lookout windows faced the street. A large one in Edo, with a guardroom attached, is 21.8 by 4.7 m (about 72 by 15 ft) and two stories. On a big plot the whole street side can be such ranges: a 1,000-koku Fukui retainer's street front was the wall of the long houses his servants lived in, with the front gate in it. Where the household's servants live is the servant-housing question, and where the gatekeepers sat is the buildings page's question on the gatekeepers.

Inside the gate. Kakunodate's houses keep a front garden between the fence and the house; Matsue's has a plain hill-style garden. Through the Aoyagi house's gate stand a roofed well and a rice storehouse before the main entrance, and outside it a mounting stone and horse-tie stones. The well itself is Does a samurai's house have its own well?; the stable, the bath and the vegetable garden are the buildings page's own questions. A foot soldier's plot was split into the house and a garden, where the household grew vegetables and fruit trees.

The kitchen. In the Kuchiba house at Hagi the kitchen juts out to the east beside the living room; Matsue's stove, bath, miso room and privy are rooms of the house. No page read names a separate kitchen yard; that the kitchen wing and its service ground sit to the side and rear of the house, away from the gate, is a guess.

The rule the map follows: every samurai lot is enclosed. A senior house is walled with earth and entered by a long-house gate on its street side, a building about 15 ft deep that doubles as the gatekeeper's and servants' quarters; a junior house is closed by a board fence or a hedge, with a plain gate, and which of the two a settlement's junior houses carry is rolled per settlement, a stone wall topped by a hedge being a regional third form; the lowest are hedged or fenced with brushwood and have no formal gate. Inside, the house stands back from the gate behind a front garden, with the well and a storehouse near the gate, and its kitchen wing to the side and rear (that siting a guess).
