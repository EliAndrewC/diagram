The town magistracy and its constables (machi-bugyo, doshin)

A city in both traditions kept an office of government for its own streets, below the governor's or the lord's. A Chinese prefectural city usually held a county yamen as well as the prefect's, and a Japanese town magistrate (machi-bugyo) ran the administration and justice of a city's town quarters. Each of Edo's two magistracies stood on about 2.2 acres, where the magistrate lived as well as worked, with 25 officers (yoriki) and 100 constables (doshin); the officers and constables lived apart from it, on plots of their own in one group residence.

In this setting a city is policed by its Ministry of Justice, which oversees street policing, magistrates, courts and jails.

  • In the late Qing a prefectural city usually held a county yamen as well as the prefect's.
    A prefecture commonly had one county attached to its seat, and within the one city stood both a prefectural yamen and a county yamen, each with its own institutions, such as its school and Confucian temple.
  • A large prefectural or provincial city could hold two or three county yamen.
    The larger prefectural and provincial cities often had two or even three attached counties.
  • In Japan the town magistrate had charge of a city's town quarters, under the shogunate and the domains alike.
    The post was in charge of the administration and justice of the town quarters (machikata), and the domains set it up as well as the shogunate.
  • Edo's two magistracies stood just inside two of the castle's gates.
    The North magistracy moved inside the Tokiwabashi gate in 1631, and the South inside the Kajibashi gate in 1698.
  • How big was an Edo magistracy?
    About 2,630 tsubo for the North and about 2,720 for the South - roughly 9,000 square meters (~97,000 sq ft, ~2.2 acres) each. The figures are from a blog that repeats them without naming its source.
  • The magistrate lived in the magistracy, with 25 officers and 100 constables to each.
    The town magistrate's official residence was inside the magistracy, and each of the two, North and South, had 25 yoriki and 100 doshin.
  • Edo's North magistracy was office on the east, residence on the west, and court in the south-east.
    The South was built nearly the same. The officers' and constables' rooms were in the north-east, and the court, with its white-sand yard, interrogation rooms and holding cell, took the widest quarter, the south-east.
  • The officers and constables lived not in the magistracy but in a group residence at Hatchobori.
    Their residences were placed together at Hatchobori, on ground the shogunate granted to the corps as one block.
  • How big was an officer's or a constable's plot?
    About 300 tsubo, some 991 square meters (~10,670 sq ft), for an officer, and about 100 tsubo, some 331 square meters (~3,560 sq ft), for a constable.
  • The Hatchobori ground was about 40,000 tsubo, divided for 50 officers and 200 constables.
    That is some 132,000 square meters (~1,423,000 sq ft, ~33 acres) around 1850, granted in one lot and divided into plots for 50 yoriki and 200 doshin - the staff of both magistracies.
  • The officers and constables let part or all of their plots to tenants.
    They rented out part of the residence, or all of it, and took the rent.

The setting's own offices of justice differ from both traditions: it names no town magistrate, and gives a city's policing to its Ministry of Justice.

  • In this setting the Ministry of Justice polices a city.
    It oversees street policing, bandit patrols, magistrates, courts, jails and the rest of the civil and criminal justice systems. So this project reads a provincial city's town magistracy as its Ministry of Justice office.
  • The Emperor keeps a magistrate in each domain capital and a few officers in each provincial city.
    One Imperial Magistrate is stationed in every domain, at the domain capital, and a small station of Imperial yoriki in each provincial city audits the tariffs at its gates and reports to that magistrate. The magistrate's compound is at The Imperial Magistrate's compound in a capital.
  • Clan and Family magistrates hear the disputes between their own houses and families.
    A dispute between two samurai of one house is that house's Ministry of Justice's; a Family magistrate oversees the Family's tax collection and hears disputes between its houses, and a Clan magistrate disputes between the clan's families.
