Which way does a governor's compound face, and does an avenue lead to its gate?

Sources: qing-yamen-tushuo (the yamen at the city's middle, its gate to the south), bjd-qing-yamen (a governor-general's yamen facing south), sohu-wuzhou-yamen (the screen wall across the street), kokuga-jawiki (the avenue south from a provincial office's gate); this project's measurement of the drawn provincial cities

It faces south, in both traditions. A Chinese yamen usually stood at the city's middle, "governing from the middle", whence the saying that a yamen's gate opens to the south, its buildings strung on one north-south axis; the Zhili governor-general's yamen sits to the north facing south, and the main hall of a Japanese provincial office was usually built toward the north of its district, facing south.

The approach, though, took two forms. At the Japanese provincial office of Shimotsuke, walled on a one-cho plot with a gate in the middle of each side, a 9 m (30 ft) avenue ran south from the south gate, the other offices thought to lie along its west and south; in Suō an avenue called the Suzaku-oji, after the capital's own, ran north-south through the middle of the office's district. The Ming and Qing yamen was approached the other way: its screen wall stood facing the gate ACROSS the street, and its memorial arch stood outside it, by the street - the gate fronting a street that ran past it, not an avenue that ran up to it. The drawn provincial cities follow neither on facing: Minami's and Nagahara's compounds open north and Tango's west.

The rule the map follows: the governor's compound opens its gate to the south. Its approach is a KNOB rolled per city from the seed: an axial avenue about 30 ft wide running south from the gate, the ministries along it; or a cross street running east-west past the gate, a screen wall on its far side facing the gate. The three drawn provincial cities are frozen exhibits and keep their north- and west-facing gates; a generated city does not copy them.
