Where are a castle town's archery range and riding ground, and how long are they?

Sources: kotobank-baba (the riding ground in every castle town, its usual shape, and real ones placed); baba-jawiki (in the samurai district); kotobank-oimawashi (Kumamoto's and Sendai's); takadanobaba-jawiki (the shogun's, the largest); kotobank-yaba (the archery range's size and places); toshiya-jawiki (the domains' archery practice halls); mancheng-zhwiki (the Chinese garrison's arrow lanes)

The riding ground. An early-modern castle town always kept riding grounds, the baba, inside the castle and outside it; the one outside stood in the samurai district, for the samurai living there. It was banked with earth and fenced, and long and narrow - often about 100 ken long and 10 wide (about 600 by 60 ft) - because riders ran straight down it shooting from the saddle. The real ones sit by the lord: Ako's ran 2.5 cho (about 900 ft) north from the moat at the castle's main gate along a river embankment, 5 ken (30 ft) wide, with a small viewing stand of 3 by 2.5 ken below the bank at its middle and a three-horse tie-rail; Kumamoto's ran south from the lord's Hanabata residence, banked on its east side; Sendai kept three, one below the castle, where riding, mounted archery and a ball game on horseback were practiced; and Daishoji's lay beside two senior retainers' houses. The shogun's at Takada in Edo, 6 cho by some 30 ken (about 2,100 by 180 ft), is the upper bound, not a domain's.

The archery range. A samurai house's archery range was fixed at 33 bow-lengths long (about 76 m, 250 ft) and one wide (about 2.3 m), with an earth butt, the azuchi, at its end to hold the target; it was set inside the castle, inside a residence, or out in the thinly built outskirts. Several great domains also built covered practice halls for the long-shot contest. A Chinese garrison quarter had its arrow lanes and bow rooms inside it.

The rule the map follows: a castle town or provincial city draws one riding ground in its samurai ground near the castle or the governor's compound - a banked, fenced strip about 600 by 60 ft, or where it runs along a moat or river bank up to about 900 by 30 ft - with a small viewing stand at the middle of one long side; a capital may draw more than one. An archery range, about 250 by 8 ft with a butt at one end, is drawn where it stands outside a compound: inside the castle or a residence it is part of that compound's own sheet, and on the city map it stands in the thinly built edge of the town, which of the two a settlement shows being rolled per settlement.
