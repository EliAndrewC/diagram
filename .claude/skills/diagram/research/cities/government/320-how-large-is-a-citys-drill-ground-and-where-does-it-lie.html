How large is a city's drill ground, and where does it lie?

Sources: chongming-yanwuchang (a county seat's drill grounds, outside a gate and inside a corner); fuzhou-nanjiaochang-zhwiki (a provincial capital's, at its south gate); xian-mancheng-zhwiki (a garrison's own ground inside the walls)

Every seat had one. A Chinese walled seat of county rank or above generally kept a drill ground, the jiaochang. Chongming's, founded in 1586, lay outside the east gate on about a li of ground, walled with earth, a three-bay review hall at the middle of its north end with a rear hall and a tea hall, a commander's platform, and a gate on the south. Its ground is reckoned at 37.5 mu, about 6 acres. Fuzhou's, a provincial capital's, lay by the south gate and is given both as four li round and as over thirty mu (about 5 acres) of low ground - figures that do not agree, a four-li circuit holding far more than thirty mu.

Outside a gate, or inside a corner. The two places are both attested. Chongming kept its great ground outside the east gate and a small drill ground inside the city's southeast corner as well; Xi'an's garrison drilled on a ground inside the walls, on the site of an old prince's palace. A Japanese castle town trained on its riding and archery grounds instead (the archery range and riding ground), and training as a practice is martial training; who drills there is the garrison.

The rule the map follows: a provincial city or county seat draws one drill ground of about 5 to 6 acres, closed by an earth wall with a gate on one side and a small review hall with its platform on the opposite side facing across the ground. Whether it lies outside a gate or in a corner inside the wall is rolled per settlement; inside the wall it counts against the reserve ground's cap (how densely a quarter is built), and a city that places it outside may still keep a small practice ground inside a corner. The acreage is a calibration from two cases.
