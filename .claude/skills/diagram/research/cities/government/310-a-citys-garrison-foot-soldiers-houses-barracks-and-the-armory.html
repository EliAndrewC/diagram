A city's garrison: foot soldiers' houses, barracks and the armory

A city's standing force - the men who held its gates and drilled for war - had to be housed, and its weapons and powder kept. A Chinese city put its troops in barracks, either in a walled garrison quarter of their own or beside the city's drill ground. A Japanese castle town lodged its foot soldiers (ashigaru) by unit in blocks of small row-houses at the town's edge, outside the samurai's own ward.

The setting's cities house their garrisons in barracks, and its foot soldiers are peasants rather than samurai. A domain kept its armory inside its castle, and at least one domain its powder store too. Where a garrison drilled is at Drill grounds, archery ranges and riding grounds (jiaochang, yaba, baba), and martial training as a practice at Martial training is an urban institution.

  • The setting's cities house their garrisons in barracks, several to a city.
    In the setting a city's garrison lives in barracks spread through its districts: a captain keeps a small house next to one of the district barracks, and when a garrison outgrew its barracks the overflow was quartered in merchants' homes until more were built.
  • The setting's foot soldiers are peasants, not samurai.
    That is the setting's deviation from historical Japan, where the ashigaru were the lowest stratum of the samurai class.

A Chinese city of the Ming and Qing housed its standing troops in one of two ways.

  • A Qing banner garrison lived in a walled quarter of its own.
    The banner garrisons, the Qing's hereditary troops, lived with their families in a separate quarter built for them, in the southern provinces most often in one corner of the provincial capital. Its houses were laid out by banner in the pattern of army barracks, and it held its own bow rooms and arrow lanes, firearms depot, grain store, horse pens, drill ground and review hall.
  • Xi'an's garrison quarter took more than a quarter of the walled city.
    It covered about 3 square kilometers (~1.2 sq mi) in the city's northeast corner, against the north and east walls, and its troops drilled on fixed days each month on a ground inside it, on the site of an old prince's palace.
  • Other cities quartered their troops at the drill ground.
    Fuzhou's gazetteer has barracks built on the east and west of its drill ground at the south gate early in the Wanli reign (from 1573), to house troops called in against the pirates of the coast, and Chongming's 440 drilled troops of 1885 were stationed in five units at its drill ground.

A Japanese castle town of the Edo period housed its foot soldiers apart from the samurai they served under.

  • A castle town's foot soldiers lived at its edge, outside the samurai's ward.
    The upper samurai's houses stood near the castle, and the lower samurai such as the foot soldiers lived around the outside of them, often beyond the townspeople's quarter. Street names that survive today, such as Ban-cho, Yumino-cho and Teppo-cho - the guard's, the bowmen's and the gunners' streets - often mark where they lived.
  • Their blocks were often set at the town's entrances, for defense.
    The foot soldiers' houses and row-houses were often built together at the entrances to the castle town, from a defensive point of view.
  • Hikone ringed its whole castle town with its foot soldiers' houses.
    They stood one after another on the town's outermost edge, as if encircling it, to defend the castle and the town, and counted about 700 households at the end of the Edo period.
  • Most foot soldiers lived in shared row-houses of about two rooms.
    They received no house of their own but lived together in row-houses, two dwellings to a building or several in a row, each dwelling a narrow earth floor and about two rooms, normally without even the alcove of a formal room.
  • A surviving row-house at Shibata held eight households under one roof, 143 by 24 ft.
    The Shibata domain's foot soldiers' row-house of 1842, an Important Cultural Property, joins eight dwellings in one thatched building 43.6 m (~143 ft) long, 7.3 m (~24 ft) deep, about 18 ft of frontage to each household.
  • A few domains gave their foot soldiers detached houses with gardens.
    Hikone did, and so did Kaga, which the Japan Tourism Agency calls rare at a time when most domains housed their foot soldiers in row-houses.
  • In Kaga the foot soldiers' plots stood about ten to a row.
    Each plot, for one family, was about 165 or 230 m² (~1,776 or ~2,476 sq ft), hedged and divided between the house and a garden.
  • Was a foot soldier's plot walled or hedged?
    The enclosure marked rank at the top: in Kaga a samurai's house was walled with earth, a samurai of middle rank and above was allowed a long-house gate, and a senior retainer's house had earthen walls and a formal entrance. Below that, a dictionary of samurai houses gives earthen walls, board fences and hedges as the enclosures of a provincial castle town without tying any to a rank, and Kaga hedged its foot soldiers' plots. No page we read says the lowest rank were forbidden a wall.

The gates and the weapons were kept by the castle and its guard.

  • A castle town's gates were held by standing guard companies.
    At Fukui each company head commanded 20 men and the gate was watched day and night; the head of the company holding the Kaga-guchi gate had their official residence beside it.
  • A domain's armory stood inside its castle.
    What stood there is at Inside the castle: the lord's palace, the council and the baileys. Matsushiro's stone powder store seems to have stood in the west of its second bailey.
  • A Chinese garrison quarter kept its own armory.
    A firearms depot was among the buildings inside it.
  • Was powder ever stored outside a city?
    The one magazine found in the sources that did is the shogun's powder store of the 1750s, set on the Koshu highway, a military road for the defense of Edo, on about 62,000 m² (~15 acres) and guarded day and night. That it stood beyond the city's edge is this project's reading of where it was. It was a capital's, not a province's.
