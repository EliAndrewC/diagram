Servants in a samurai household: where they sleep and how they were hired (hokonin)

A samurai household's komono, its private servants and one kind of hokonin, lived in, inside their master's plot, and did the household's odd jobs. They were not samurai but of peasant or townsman origin, hired for wages on contracts of a fixed term.

Where they slept followed the size of the house. On a large house the buildings along the street were long ranges (nagaya) in which the servants lived, with the front gate cut through one of them, so that the servants who lived there kept the gate and watched who came and went; a smaller house, with few live-in servants, had a wall in place of the ranges. A Chinese courtyard house kept its servants in the same way, in the row whose back was its street wall.

  • A samurai household's servants lived inside their master's plot.
    A Japanese encyclopedia describes the komono, a samurai household's private servants also called genan, as living in, and at the Matsue samurai residence the long-house gate was the dwelling of the gatekeeper and of the chugen, the household's menservants. No page we read puts a servant in a street-fronting house of their own in the samurai quarter; that they never lived in one is a GUESS from that silence.

We find three arrangements, given here from the largest household to the smallest: long ranges along the street, a gate with rooms in it, and rooms under the main roof behind a plain wall. The order is this project's, a GUESS: no page we read ranks them.

  • On a large house, the servants lived in the long ranges along the street.
    The Fukui domain archive reads the 1839 plan of the Suganuma, a family of 1,000 koku, on a plot of 28 by 32.5 ken (~167 x 194 ft), as showing that the walls facing the street were long ranges in which the servants lived. The great lords, too, built ranges round their residences to house their retainers.
  • The front gate was cut through a range, and its rooms housed servants (nagayamon).
    A Japanese dictionary defines the long-house gate as a range at the front of a samurai residence, housing retainers and menservants, part of it opened to make a gate, its roof running on unbroken from the range's - a range with a gate cut in it, not a gate with rooms attached. Beside the passage were servants' quarters and a stable. At Matsue the gate held a gatekeeper's room and rooms for the chugen, with lookout windows onto the street, and a gate could also serve as a storeroom. That the lookout windows were barred is a GUESS, on no page we read.
  • The gate's guard rooms, one at each side, took a form set by the house's rank.
    A guard room projecting at each side of the gate was the long-house gate's mark, and its form and structure were fixed by the rank of the daimyo's or hatamoto's house.
  • How big was a range?
    It varied with the house. The one surviving range we found described is single-story. The Honda family's gate at Okazaki, now without its two wings, was once 120 m (~394 ft) along its front. An ordinary retainer's range is taken here as about 15 ft deep by 70 to 80 ft long, a GUESS: no page we read measures one. That a range stood on the inside of the boundary line is a GUESS too, on no page we read.
  • Was a middling household's gate all that was left of its range?
    This project reads it so, a GUESS. The long-house gate began as a gate opened in the ranges a lord built round a residence, and where a household's range was short, it is read here as surviving only as the gatehouse. No page we read says a short range was cut back to its gate.
  • A smaller house had a wall instead of a range.
    In Edo, a samurai house with few live-in retainers and servants kept a simpler enclosure, a wall in place of the ranges that ringed a daimyo's residence. No page we read gives the stipend below which a house had no range.
  • A provincial castle town needed few ranges, because every samurai had a plot of their own.
    In a provincial castle town every samurai was granted a plot, so there was no need to build many ranges inside it.
  • Where did a small household's servants sleep?
    Under the main roof, in this project's reading. The nando, a storeroom, was also used as a bedroom, and a small house's few servants are taken to have slept there and in service rooms off the kitchen. That is a GUESS: no page we read says where a small house's servants slept.
  • Even a chief retainer's great house kept its servants' rooms under its own roof.
    Aizu's reconstructed samurai residence has 38 rooms, from the hall where the lord was received through the domain's office rooms, the inner rooms, the kitchen and the bathroom. It is the house of Saigo Tanomo, a chief retainer (karo) of the domain, rebuilt in 1975 from a bird's-eye view of the house lost to fire in the Boshin War, the civil war of 1868-69 that brought down the shogunate. Its rooms fall into four groups - the lord's reception suite, the retainers' office and guard rooms, the family's rooms, and the rooms of the maids and servants - and a range on one side of the front gate held retainers' dwellings.

The servants came from outside the samurai class, and a household found them through its own connections or through a broker.

  • Most were the younger sons of peasants, on contracts of a year or half a year.
    The wakato, one kind of these servants, were basically of peasant or townsman origin, and most served on terms of a year, half a year or a day, for a yearly wage of about 2 ryo 2 bu. Most were the second and younger sons of peasants, though in great cities such as Edo, with many samurai residences, professional servants who went from residence to residence were often hired.
  • The number of servants a household had to keep was fixed by its assessed rice yield (kokudaka).
    A Japanese encyclopedia says the number a household had to retain was fixed by its assessed yield, and that they were levied from the civilian population of the castle town and elsewhere, so a castle town's servants are taken here to have come in part from its own townsmen's quarter, this project's reading of that passage.
  • Servants changed households on two fixed days a year (degawari).
    The changeover days were the 5th day of the 3rd month and the 5th of the 9th, moved there from the 2nd days of the 2nd and 8th months by a shogunal decree that one reference work dates to 1669 and another to 1668.
  • A household with no connections hired through a placement broker.
    Where a household had no connections and was short of hands, placement agents supplied its staff, and in the Edo period hiring a chugen from a broker only when needed became common. Over the period, samurai-household service became wage work by peasants and townsmen employed through these brokers, a trade that appeared with the growth of the cities.

China shows the same arrangement from its own evidence: a household's servants lived inside its compound, in the row that made its street wall.

  • A large Chinese courtyard house was walled all round, with no windows to the street.
    Its walls were tall and blank, to keep the house private.
  • Servants lived in the south row, whose windowless back was the street wall (daozuofang).
    The south row's doors and windows all faced north into the court, so it was dim and served as guest rooms or servants' rooms: the family school at its east end, the privy at its west, and servants in the rooms between. Its back wall faced the lane and generally had no windows.
  • The rear row usually housed the owner's daughters (houzhaofang).
    The rear row stood in the innermost court, near the compound's boundary, and the owner's daughters usually lived there.
  • How were a Chinese county office's staff housed?
    Their duty stations were inside the office's wall; where they lived, no page we found says. At the Neixiang county office the first court held a guest hall, a dining hall and the jail on its west side, and on its east the yamen god's temple, the earth-god shrine and the courtyard of the Three Corps (sanban yuan), but no clerks' lodging. The runners (yayi) had a duty station inside the office, the banfang. No page we found names a lodging for a county office's clerks. That the runners' station was a makeshift shed of no fixed place, and that they went home to houses scattered through the town, is a GUESS, on no page we read.
  • Did Japan and China house a household's servants the same way?
    As far as each tradition's own evidence goes, yes. In both, a household's servants lived in the range or row along its boundary, or inside the house, and in neither did any page we read show a rank of small uniform dwellings for servants inside an elite quarter.
