Servant housing in the samurai ward - servants are drawn as WALLS, not as houses

Sources: buke-hokonin-wiki, matsue-bukeyashiki, fukui-bushi-jutaku, jta-nagayamon, nagayamon-jawiki, nando-jawiki, aizu-bukeyashiki-jawiki

What the research found

Japan (whose look the maps take, so it decides the drawn form). Domestic servants of a samurai household - genan/gejo, chugen, komono - lived INSIDE their master's walled plot, and at Matsue the gate range itself housed the gatekeeper and the chugen. No page read puts them in a street-fronting house of their own in the buke-chi; that they never lived in one is a GUESS from that silence. Who they were and how they were hired is the question after this one; wherever a castle town recruited them from, their housing sat inside samurai walls. Three plan positions, in descending order of household size:

  1. The perimeter nagaya - a long, narrow, single-story range running along the INSIDE of the street boundary, forming the plot wall itself (that it was single-story, and on the inside of the boundary line, is a GUESS, on no page read). The Fukui archive's reading of the Suganuma (菅沼) plan (1,000 koku, 1839: a 28 x 32.5 ken plot, ~167 x 194 ft) says that the street-facing walls were nagaya and the like, in which the servants lived.
  2. The nagayamon gate rooms - where the range is short it survives only as the gatehouse (this record's reading of the gate's origin in the perimeter range; no page read says a short range was cut back to its gate): servants' quarters and stable, a gatekeeper's room, a chugen room, a storeroom, sometimes a projecting guard box, with barred lookout windows onto the street (the tourism agency's Kanazawa page names the servants' quarters and the stable, and the Matsue pages the gatekeeper's and chugen rooms and their lookout windows, saying nothing of bars; the storeroom, the guard box and "barred" are a GUESS). Measured examples, on no page this record has read (a GUESS until one is), run ~15 ft deep by 70-80 ft long (Omura Yahei, Kishu (Kii province), 12 ken x 2.5 ken = 76 x 15 ft; the Tokyo Important Cultural Property (ICP) gate 72 x 15 ft). It is a barracks range with a hole cut in it, not a gate with rooms attached.
  3. Rooms off the kitchen - below roughly 100-300 koku there is no room for a range at all, and the servants sleep in nando and service rooms under the main roof (the page attests the nando as a sleeping room; the koku threshold, the service rooms off the kitchen and the servants sleeping there are a GUESS, on no page read). Even at the top this persists for women: Aizu's reconstructed samurai residence has 38 rooms, from the lord's reception hall through the domain's office rooms, the inner rooms and the kitchen, all under one roof. That the maids and servants had their own group of rooms among them, and the house's owner, are on no quote here: a GUESS until one is read.

Where a castle town DID build ranks of small uniform houses, and for whom, is a question of its own (where the foot soldiers lived).

China was checked second, with its own evidence (the Chinese evidence).

The two traditions therefore give the same drawing rule from opposite directions, which is as strong as this kind of finding gets: service accommodation is part of the boundary or the interior of the compound it serves, and a rank of small uniform dwellings inside an elite quarter is the one thing neither tradition produces.

The decision it drove

A servant dwelling inside a samurai ward is not a freestanding house. It is service accommodation BOUND to the samurai household it serves - drawn on that household's street edge as a long, thin range (the nagayamon proportion, ~15 ft deep), or not drawn at all for the smallest households, whose servants sleep under the master's roof.

The count is not what matters: the setting's own budget notes give a provincial city 120 servant families, of which 72 are attached to samurai households (30 to wealthy samurai at the setting's property rating 2, 42 indentured to non-wealthy samurai at property rating 1) against 60 samurai families - about one servant household per junior samurai household and two per senior. They are property-holding families in the GM's economic model, so they are real households that must be housed, counted, and taxed. What matters is the ARRANGEMENT: a rank of detached servant houses unattached to any samurai household reads as "more commoner houses in the samurai neighborhood" (the GM's words) - exactly what the fence exists to exclude.

Disclosed departures

  • Our in-city samurai houses are UNWALLED (our city plans put the walled estates outside the rampart). Both traditions above put the servants inside a compound WALL that our city plans do not draw. The nagaya range is what resolves this without reversing the GM's settled doctrine: historically the range IS the wall, so drawing the range alone - a long thin building hard on the street line - carries the same read at the city map's scale without introducing walled compounds into the ward.
