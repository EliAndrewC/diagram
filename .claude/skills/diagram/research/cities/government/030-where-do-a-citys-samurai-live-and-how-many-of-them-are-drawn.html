Where do a city's samurai live, and how many of them are drawn?

Sources: l7r-median-domain (the setting's samurai share of a provincial city), kotobank-jokamachi (how much of a historical castle town was samurai ground and samurai population, and where each rank lived), kotobank-bukeyashiki (plots granted by rank, and how they were enclosed)

The setting's count. A provincial city is about a tenth samurai - some 300 of 3,000, which is about 60 households - and the setting's rank table splits that resident cohort roughly a quarter senior to three quarters junior. Most of them live in the samurai neighborhood as individual houses; the governor's compound and the walled country estates outside the rampart hold the rest.

A deliberate departure from the historical castle town. A Japanese castle town was mostly samurai: the greater part of its ground was samurai ground, often over half of the whole town, and when the domains were abolished in 1871 its samurai families were the greater part of its population. The setting's tenth is a DEVIATION from that: this city's samurai neighborhood is one quarter of a mostly commoner city, not most of the town.

Rank shapes the neighborhood. A samurai did not choose a plot: the domain granted one by status and stipend, and a samurai moved to a plot befitting their status on each promotion or transfer. The upper retainers held the plots in or near the castle's enclosures, with the middle and lower retainers around them. So a quarter of houses all one size is not a castle town's, and a token cluster of a couple of dozen houses is not this cohort. How large a plot was at each rank is the question of how big a samurai's house lot is, by rank. Historically every plot was enclosed, by an earthen wall, a board fence or a hedge; that the city's samurai houses here stand unwalled, and only estates outside the rampart are walled, is the setting's own plan and a DEVIATION.

The rule the map follows: the samurai neighborhood is sized to the cohort - at least about two thirds of the households the city's own declared population implies are drawn as samurai houses. They VARY by rank: a minority of large senior houses among many small junior ones, at least three of the large and a clear majority of the small, interleaved through the rows so the large ones front streets prominently. Unlike the poor, samurai LINE their streets - at least a third of the houses stand within about 270 ft of a lane, the deeper lots sitting behind them. NONE of them is walled: a walled samurai estate stands OUTSIDE the rampart, and the only walled samurai compound within the city is the governor's. The reverse holds too. No free-standing samurai house stands outside the wall, because the only extramural samurai residences are those walled estates, and an unwalled house beyond the rampart is defenseless and anchored to nothing. The two-thirds floor, the three large houses, the interleaving and the third within 270 ft of a lane are a calibration against the drawn exhibits, not findings.
