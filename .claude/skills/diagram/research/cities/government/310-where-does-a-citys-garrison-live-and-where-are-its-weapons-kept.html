Where does a city's garrison live, and where are its weapons kept?

Sources: l7r-toshi-ranbo-barracks (the setting's city barracks); mancheng-zhwiki and xian-mancheng-zhwiki (the walled garrison quarter); fuzhou-nanjiaochang-zhwiki and chongming-yanwuchang (troops quartered at the drill ground); jokamachi-jawiki and fukui-bushi-jutaku (a castle town's units and its gate guard); matsushiro-castle-jawiki and rehouse-meidaimae-enshogura (where the powder was kept)

The setting has barracks. In the GM's notes a city's garrison lives in barracks, more than one, spread through the city's districts: a captain keeps a small house next to one of the district barracks, and when a garrison outgrew its barracks the overflow was quartered in merchants' homes until more were built. That is canon, and it settles the question a castle town would have answered differently: a Japanese domain had no barracks, its foot soldiers living by unit in their own blocks at the town's edge (where the foot soldiers live), which is why castle towns keep street names such as Ban-cho, Yumino-cho and Teppo-cho - the guard's, the bowmen's and the gunners' streets. Where the garrison drills and trains is under martial training.

Two ways a standing force was housed in a Chinese city. The Qing's banner garrisons lived in a separate quarter of their own, in the south most often in one corner of the provincial capital, its houses laid out as barracks by banner, holding their own bow rooms and arrow lanes, an armory, a grain store, horse pens, a drill ground and a review hall. Xi'an's took more than a quarter of the walled city, and its troops drilled on fixed days each month on a ground inside it. The other way puts the troops at the drill ground: Fuzhou's gazetteer has barracks built on the east and west of its drill ground at the south gate, and Chongming's 440 drilled troops of 1885 were stationed in five units at its drill ground. Both are attested and both are distinct forms, so the siting of a city's barracks is a knob.

The gate guard. A castle town's gates were held by standing guard companies: at Fukui each company head commanded 20 men, the gate was watched day and night, and the head of the company holding the Kaga-guchi gate had his official residence beside it.

The weapons. The domain's armory stands inside the castle (what stands inside the castle); Matsushiro's stone powder store seems to have stood in its second bailey. A garrison quarter kept its own armory. The one magazine found in the sources that stood outside a city is the powder store set on the Koshu highway, a military road for the defense of Edo, on about 62,000 m2 (15 acres) and guarded day and night by its own constables - a capital's, not a province's.

The rule the map follows: a provincial city draws barracks for its garrison. Where they stand is rolled per settlement between a walled garrison quarter in one corner of the city, laid out in rows of barracks with its own drill ground, stables and armory, and barracks flanking the drill ground at a gate; a city large enough to have districts may instead spread them one to a district, as the setting's own city does. Each gate carries its guard post, with the guard commander's house beside the gate. The armory and the powder store stand inside the castle or the governor's compound and are not drawn in the city; a capital may add a powder magazine outside the wall on a main road.
