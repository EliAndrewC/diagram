The samurai quarter: where a city's samurai live, and how many (bukechi)

In a Japanese castle town of the Edo period a samurai did not choose where to live. Once samurai were made a class apart from farmers they were gathered into the castle towns, and the domain granted each one a plot by status and stipend. For military reasons the upper retainers lived in or near the castle's enclosures and the middle and lower ones around them, and the samurai's ground was laid out apart from the townsmen's. Samurai ground took up the greater part of the town.

This setting's provincial city departs from that, a deviation from the historical castle town: it is about a tenth samurai, so its samurai quarter is one district of a mostly commoner city, not most of the town.

  • Once samurai were made a class apart from farmers, they were gathered to live in the castle towns.
    The Nipponica encyclopedia dates the samurai house's own style to the Edo period, after the separation of warriors from farmers gathered them to live together in the castle towns and build their residences there.
  • Most of a castle town's ground was samurai ground, often half of it or more.
    The greater part of a castle town was taken up by samurai ground, which often took up 50% or more of the whole town.
  • When the domains were abolished, the former samurai were most of a castle town's population.
    The Nipponica encyclopedia says so of the abolition of the domains; no count from the Edo period itself is on the page.
  • The domain granted each samurai a plot by status and stipend, and moved them on promotion.
    Samurai residences were granted by the shogunate or the domain according to status and stipend, and since the residence was official housing, a samurai moved to one befitting their status at each promotion or transfer. How large a plot was at each rank is at Samurai house lots and houses by rank (bukeyashiki)
  • The higher a samurai's rank, the nearer the castle they lived.
    Basically, the higher a retainer's status, the nearer the castle their residence stood. Each rank had its own residential district, the upper and middle ranks often in or beside the castle's enclosures.
  • The low ranks, such as foot soldiers, lived at the town's edge, often outside the townsmen's district.
    The lower ranks were often placed at the town's edge, and the foot soldiers and other low-ranking men often outside the townsmen's district, where place names such as Teppōchō (gunners' ward) still mark them. The group residences of the foot soldiers stood on the outskirts with the temples and shrines.
  • Samurai ground and townsmen's ground were laid out as separate districts.
    A castle town's plan was required to separate the samurai from the other townspeople, and the townsmen's ground, the district of merchants and artisans, lay outside the samurai town.
  • Townsmen's wards often ran in narrow strips between groups of samurai, along the main streets.
    The strips lay along the major thoroughfares, where the townsmen's services were easiest for everyone to reach.
  • The temple town stood at the castle town's rim.
    Its spacious temples stood in rows on the outer perimeter and had a part in the town's defense.
  • A provincial castle town's samurai plots were enclosed by earthen walls, board fences or hedges.
    The dictionary says this of the provincial castle town. Whether a whole samurai district was walled, or only gated, is at City wards and the gates that shut them at night (machi, kido).
  • Where a castle town was enclosed, the enclosure could take in the samurai with the town.
    Funai's outer enclosure, of moat and earthwork, took in the samurai residences and the townsmen's district together. At Hagi the high-ranking retainers lived inside the castle's third bailey, made into one enclosure, and the middle and lower retainers lived with the townsmen inside an outer one.

Not every domain gathered all its samurai into its castle town. Some kept many of them on the land.

  • Samurai who lived in the country often had residences scattered through the farming villages.
    The Nipponica encyclopedia says so of samurai who lived in the countryside.
  • How many of Satsuma's samurai lived in its castle town?
    About one in nine, by a count of 1639, early in the Edo period. Satsuma could not house all its retainers in the Kagoshima castle town, so it settled them in 113 outer districts, and in 1639 it counted 1,015 castle-town samurai against 8,202 rural samurai. The one in nine is this project's arithmetic, and the page does not say whether the castle-town count leaves out the upper retainers.
  • Satsuma's rural samurai farmed to support themselves in peacetime.
    The rural samurai, the gōshi, farmed in peacetime and were mobilized under their district's steward when fighting broke out.
  • Some Sendai retainers built their residences out in the fiefs they held.
    Sendai paid its retainers in land, and under one form of grant the holder built a residence, with houses for their own retainers and foot soldiers, inside the fief at their own expense.
  • How many of one town's samurai households lived inside its enclosure, and how many outside it?
    No page we read gives a share. Nor do the sources settle whether the low ranks' quarters at the town's edge stood inside an enclosure: Hagi's outer enclosure took in its middle and lower retainers, while the foot soldiers' group residences stood on the outskirts. Leaving aside the low ranks at the town's edge, the samurai these sources place outside a castle town lived out in the country, not in a ring just beyond its wall.

China zoned its cities too, in an earlier age.

  • Under China's old ward system (lifang), a city's merchants could trade only in the market, and only by day.
    The government limited both the time and the place of trade within the city. How the walls of China's wards came down is at City wards and the gates that shut them at night (machi, kido).

The setting's society differs from the castle town's in ways that change who lives in a city's samurai quarter.

  • A provincial city in this setting is about a tenth samurai.
    A median provincial city is around 10% samurai, and a median domain capital about 13%, because the capital gathers the domain's samurai families. In a city of 3,000 that is some 300 samurai, this project's arithmetic. The capital's samurai are at Where a capital's samurai live: lineage compounds, walled estates and retainer terraces (bukeyashiki).
  • In this setting no samurai live in the villages.
    County towns are the lowest level at which samurai live, and a samurai family keeps at most a country estate near some farmland, never counted part of the village. So the domain's rural samurai are already counted in its county towns, and Satsuma's outer-district samurai and Sendai's fief-holders have no counterpart outside a provincial city's wall.
  • In this setting ashigaru are peasants, not samurai.
    That departs from historical Japan, where they were the samurai class's lowest stratum, so the historical foot soldiers' quarters at the town's edge have no samurai counterpart.
  • The low ranks at a castle town's edge were not only foot soldiers.
    The Nipponica encyclopedia places "the lower ranks, such as foot soldiers" at the town's edge, a wider group than the foot soldiers alone, so the setting's low-ranking samurai other than its ashigaru still have a historical counterpart there.
