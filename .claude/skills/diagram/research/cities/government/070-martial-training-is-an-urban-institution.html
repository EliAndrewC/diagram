Martial training is an URBAN institution

Sources: hanko-jawiki (255 schools, martial arts (武芸) and the training hall (演武場)), edo-three-dojos-jawiki (the three dojos' founding dates, 1773/1822/1826), genbukan-jawiki (the disciple count is contested, ~3,000 against 6,000+, and no readable page gives one), kenjutsu-jawiki, edo-enwiki, wuxue-zhwiki, kotobank-machidojo

What the research found. The standalone dojo with a resident sensei and enrolled students is an urban institution in both anchor cultures. In Edo Japan formal martial instruction lived in the castle town: the domain school (hanko) and its martial hall (bugeijo) were built in the domain's own territory (藩地) for the domain's own retainers, and by late Edo nearly every one of the ~260 domains had one. Private commercial halls - machi-dojo, run by low-income bushi and eventually by commoners teaching for a living - are a late phenomenon and a metropolitan one: the boom runs roughly 1830-1860 - a range on no page read, drawn from the three great halls' founding dates of 1773, 1822 and 1826 and from late-Edo growth - and its famous examples (the Three Great Dojo of Edo; Chiba Shusaku's Genbukan, founded at Nihonbashi in 1822 and later moved to Kanda Otamagaike) sit in a city of a million. The Chinese anchor is starker: military students were enrolled at prefectural and county schools while the examinations ran at provincial, metropolitan and palace level, but there was no dense network of private martial academies at a county seat - drill happened at garrison grounds.

What the research did NOT find. The GM asked whether "roughly one martial establishment per ~100 resident samurai" had a historical basis: "I don't know where the 1 per resident samurai number came from since I don't recall that offhand, so do you know whether there's a historical basis for that?" There is none.

The attested content is narrower and supports a different shape: one state institution per castle town (the hanko pattern, which is a program item rather than a count that scales) and a deliberately thin private tail (because the machi-dojo boom needed a metropolis). So a capital carries one private hall per ~200 samurai, and gains the domain school instead.

The arithmetic is kept only as a rough plausibility read, never as an authority: where it disagrees with the number the map actually draws, the map wins. On that read a county town comes to 0.2 and rounds to none, a provincial city to about 3, a domain capital to about 15 and the Imperial capital to about 400, of which three or four would be famous. Note that it is a ratio on the SAMURAI count and not on the population, a hall serving samurai and nobody else; the samurai share of each tier already carries the difference in density between them.

The countryside is deliberately not counted (the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25). A province holds about 550 samurai in all - roughly 300 in its city, 120 across its six county towns, 90 on country estates - but only the city cohort is large enough to support an institution: the rural samurai either ride in or make do with the magistracy's practice ground. Asking separately what the countryside supports would double-count, because a provincial city's size already scales with the countryside that feeds it.

Two departures are disclosed. The training compound is drawn as its wall plus three interior features with the rest implied, the way the governor's compound is; a real martial hall also had a changing room, an armory and a viewing platform. And state violet against private buff is a MAP DRAWING CONVENTION rather than a historical difference - nothing about a domain school's construction differed from a private hall's, and the color is carrying an ownership distinction the map could not otherwise show.

The rule the map follows: a provincial city is the first tier that trains INDOORS. It keeps exactly ONE state martial hall - a state institution is a program item, not a count that scales - and rolls its private halls at one per full 200 resident samurai plus a chance of one more for the remainder, floored at one, which puts a city of about 3,000 at one or two of them. A county town draws a practice ground and no hall at all. Both kinds stand in or against the samurai neighborhood, a hall serving samurai and nobody else, and the state hall is its own compound rather than a wing of the governor's - standing near the government it answers to, but not inside it.
