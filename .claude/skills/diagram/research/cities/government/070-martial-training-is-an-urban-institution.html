Martial training is an URBAN institution

Sources: hanko-jawiki (255 schools, martial arts (武芸) and the training hall (演武場)), edo-three-dojos-jawiki (the three dojos' founding dates, 1773/1822/1826), genbukan-jawiki (the disciple count is contested, ~3,000 against 6,000+, and no readable page gives one), kenjutsu-jawiki, edo-enwiki, wuxue-zhwiki, kotobank-machidojo, han-abolition-enwiki (how many domains there were)

What the research found. A private dojo is a privately run hall of enrolled pupils paying a monthly fee. What follows is why formal martial training, private or state, belongs to the towns in both anchor cultures. In Edo Japan it lived with the domain's government: the domain school (hanko), where martial arts were taught after letters, with a drill hall of its own in some domains, was built in the domain's own territory (藩地) for the domain's own retainers, and from the Horeki era (1751-1764) on nearly every domain came to have one - of the 261 domains still standing when they were abolished in 1871. Private commercial halls - machi-dojo - are taken here as a late phenomenon and a metropolitan one, from the spread of the training they served: commoners could not learn swordsmanship until the middle of the Edo period, and from the Tenpo era (1830-1844) on it spread until several thousand styles had come into being, led by three great halls in Edo. That the spread ran through privately run, fee-charging halls, and that the boom ended roughly 1860, are on no page read. The the three great halls themselves were founded in 1773, 1822 and 1826; its famous examples (the Three Great Dojo of Edo; Chiba Shusaku's Genbukan, founded at Nihonbashi in 1822 and later moved to Kanda Otamagaike) sit in a city of a million. The Chinese anchor culture is starker: an order of 1637 required the prefectural, departmental and county schools all to enroll military students. That the examinations then ran at provincial, metropolitan and palace level, that there was no dense network of private martial academies at a county seat, and that drill happened at garrison grounds are a guess, on no page read.

What the research did NOT find. The GM asked whether "roughly one martial establishment per ~100 resident samurai" had a historical basis: "I don't know where the 1 per resident samurai number came from since I don't recall that offhand, so do you know whether there's a historical basis for that?" There is none.

The attested content is narrower and supports a different shape: one state institution per castle town (the hanko pattern, which is a program item rather than a count that scales) and a deliberately thin private tail (because the machi-dojo boom needed a metropolis). So a city carries its one state hall and a thin private tail of about one hall per 200 samurai.

As a rough plausibility read only, never as an authority - where it disagrees with the number the map actually draws, the map wins - one hall per 200 samurai puts a county town at 0.1, which rounds to none, a provincial city at about 1.5, a domain capital at about 7 and the Imperial capital at about 200, of which three or four would be famous. Note that it is a ratio on the SAMURAI count and not on the population, because the map draws its halls as serving samurai - a map decision, since some domain schools were later opened to other residents and commoners came to train at private halls; the samurai share of each tier already carries the difference in density between them.

The countryside is deliberately not counted (the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25). A province holds about 550 samurai in all - roughly 300 in its city, 120 across its six county towns, 90 on country estates - but only the city cohort is large enough to support an institution: the map has the rural samurai either ride in or make do with the magistracy's practice ground, a guess no page read speaks to. Asking separately what the countryside supports would double-count, because a provincial city's size already scales with the countryside that feeds it.

Two departures are disclosed. The training compound is drawn as its wall plus three interior features with the rest implied, the way the governor's compound is; a real martial hall also had a changing room, an armory and a viewing platform. And state violet against private buff is a MAP DRAWING CONVENTION rather than a historical difference - no page read shows a domain school built any differently from a private hall, and the color is carrying an ownership distinction the map could not otherwise show.

The rule the map follows: a provincial city is the first tier that trains INDOORS. It keeps exactly ONE state martial hall - a state institution is a program item, not a count that scales - and rolls its private halls at one per full 200 resident samurai plus a chance of one more for the remainder, floored at one, which puts a city of about 3,000 at one or two of them. A county town draws a practice ground and no hall at all. Both kinds stand in or against the samurai neighborhood, the map drawing its halls as serving samurai, and the state hall is its own compound rather than a wing of the governor's - standing near the government it answers to, but not inside it.
