What stands inside a governor's compound, and in what order?

Sources: qing-yamen-tushuo (the Qing rule for a yamen's parts, and how it scales by rank), bjd-qing-yamen (a governor-general's and a prefect's yamen, court by court), sohu-wuzhou-yamen (the six rooms and the shrines), kokuga-jawiki (the Japanese provincial office's halls), takayama-jinya-jawiki (the surviving provincial office's parts)

The parts are the county magistracy's, and so is the order; what a governor's seat adds is more of them. The Qing rule for every official's yamen named the business halls (a great hall and a second hall), the main and ceremonial gates outside them, the living quarters, and the clerks' rooms - a senior official having the full set, a junior one a reduced set in order. Beside those a yamen held an archive for its documents and could not do without its granary and storehouses, its armory and its jail, and most had the official's residence attached.

In order, from the street: at the Zhili governor-general's yamen a screen wall, flagpoles and the guards' rooms stood outside the gate; inside, on one axis, came the gate, the ceremonial gate, the admonition arch, the great hall, the second hall, the inner-residence gate, the governor's study and inner office, the family's quarters and a rear storehouse - five courts in all, the inner gate the line between the front court of business and the residence behind. Its great hall was 22 m by 10 m (about 72 by 33 ft). The six clerks' rooms flanked the path from the ceremonial gate to the great hall, three on the east and three on the west, generally two bays each, with rooms for dispatch, accounts and stores beside them where the business called for them; and a yamen kept its own shrines, to the yamen god and the jail god among them. What the larger seats added were side routes: the Huai'an prefectural yamen ran in three parallel routes, the business halls and the clerks' rooms on the middle one, a police and military office on the west.

The Japanese provincial office kept the same front-to-back sense in a different shape: its main hall stood to the north facing south, with side halls east and west and a front and rear hall around a court, and the one surviving Edo-period provincial office, at Takayama, is a gate and gatehouse, an office range rebuilt in 1816 with its entrance, examination room, office and great hall, and rows of storehouses.

The rule the map follows: on the city map the governor's compound stays walls and a gate with its interior implied. Drawn as its own sheet, it takes the county magistracy's program (office in front, residence behind) and enlarges it by addition rather than by swelling the halls: a front court with the gatehouse and the guards, the office hall with the courtroom as its room and the clerks' rooms flanking the court before it, a second office hall behind, then the residence; the archive, the storehouses and the granary on a side route; the shrine; and the jail (where a city keeps its jail). The number of courts is a degree, from the county's two to the five of a governor-general's seat.
