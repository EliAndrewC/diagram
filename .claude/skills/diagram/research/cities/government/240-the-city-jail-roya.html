The city jail (roya)

A city jail held the accused while their cases were heard, and China and Japan kept it in different places. In China the jail was part of the governor's or magistrate's compound, the yamen: every prefecture, department and county had one, standing to the southwest of the great hall, outside the ceremonial gate. In a Japanese castle town of the Edo period the jail was a compound of its own, like the Chōshū domain's two jails at Hagi. Why a jail was for remand rather than punishment is at Cells are remand, not punishment.

In this setting a city's jails are run by its Ministry of Justice, which also oversees street policing, magistrates and the courts.

  • Every Chinese prefecture, department and county kept a jail, and from the prefecture up a jail office ran it.
    From the early Ming every prefecture, department and county had a jail; the prefectures and the levels above them had an office to manage it, while a department or county appointed no official of its own for the purpose.
  • A Chinese yamen's jail stood southwest of the great hall, outside the ceremonial gate, and was called the South Jail.
    The jails of local yamen were all set there, and so were called the South Jail.
  • By Qing statute a jail held an inner jail for the condemned and an outer one for lesser convicts, with the women apart.
    The outer jail held those sentenced to penal servitude, exile and below, and women were kept in a room of their own, the women's jail.
  • In practice the department and county magistrates ran their jails in four grades, with a walled women's jail beside the outer one.
    Department and county jails were divided into a light jail, an outer jail, an inner or heavy jail and a dark jail, with a women's jail besides, set beside the outer jail behind a courtyard wall of its own. The magistrates themselves ran these jails, on the basis of the code and its regulations.
  • A small county's jail could be just two rooms.
    A county gazetteer of the Kangxi reign (1662-1722) gives its jail as two rooms, a heavy and a light, the heavy one inside the ceremonial gate and the light one beside the county hall.

A Japanese castle town set its jail apart from the offices that sent prisoners to it.

  • The Chōshū domain's jail at Hagi was two compounds facing each other across a road: one for samurai, one for everyone else.
    The Noyama jail, the upper jail, held those of samurai status, and the Iwakura jail, the lower jail, everyone else.
  • The Hagi jails were built on the sites of two confiscated residences.
    The residences were confiscated, and the sites were later rebuilt as jails.
  • The upper jail at Hagi had twelve single cells, six facing six, with an inspection hut and an execution ground.
    The article gives no layout for the lower jail.
  • The shogunate's largest jail, Edo's Tenmachō, filled a moated block of its own of about 8,850 square meters (~95,000 sq ft, ~2.2 acres).
    It was the largest of the shogunate's jails, which also stood at its magistrates' and intendants' offices elsewhere, with temporary jails in Edo's own courts. It covered 2,677 tsubo, about 8,850 square meters (~95,000 sq ft), and was enclosed on three sides by moat and embankment.
  • Edo's North town magistracy kept a temporary holding cell among its court rooms.
    The cell stood in the quarter given to lawsuits and trials, with the court, the interrogation rooms and the station of the jail constables. The magistracy itself is at The town magistracy and its constables (machi-bugyo, doshin).
