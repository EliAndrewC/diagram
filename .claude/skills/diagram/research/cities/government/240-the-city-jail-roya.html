The city jail (roya)

A city jail held prisoners until their cases were done: the accused awaiting judgment, and the convicted awaiting their punishment. China and Japan kept it in different places. In Qing China the jail was part of the governor's or magistrate's compound, the yamen, and from the early Ming every seat of government down to the county had one. In Edo-period Japan a domain's or the shogunate's main jail could be a compound of its own, like the Chōshū domain's two jails at Hagi. Why a jail was for remand rather than punishment is at Cells are remand, not punishment.

In this setting a city's jails are run by its Ministry of Justice, which also oversees street policing, magistrates and the courts.

  • Under the Ming dynasty every Chinese prefecture, department and county kept a jail, and from the prefecture up a jail office ran it.
    From the early Ming every prefecture, department and county had a jail, and the prefectures and the levels above them had an office to manage it.
  • A department's or county's jail was run by its magistrate directly.
    A department or county appointed no official of its own to manage its jail. The department and county magistrates ran these jails themselves, on the basis of the code and its regulations.
  • A Qing yamen's jail stood southwest of its great hall, outside the ceremonial gate, and was called the South Jail.
    The jails of local yamen were all set there, and so were called the South Jail.
  • By Qing statute a jail held an inner jail for the condemned and an outer one for lesser convicts, with the women apart.
    The outer jail held those sentenced to penal servitude, exile and below, and women were kept in a room of their own, the women's jail.
  • Department and county jails were divided into four grades, with a walled women's jail beside the outer one.
    The four were a light jail, an outer jail, an inner or heavy jail and a dark jail, and the women's jail stood beside the outer jail behind a courtyard wall of its own.
  • One county's jail was just two rooms, a heavy and a light.
    A county gazetteer of the Kangxi reign (1662-1722) gives its jail as two rooms, the heavy one inside the ceremonial gate and the light one beside the county hall, unlike the South Jail, outside the gate.

In Japan the main jail could stand apart from the offices that sent prisoners to it, though those offices might keep a holding cell of their own.

  • The Chōshū domain's jail at Hagi was two compounds facing each other across a road: one for samurai, one for everyone else.
    The Noyama jail, the upper jail, held those of samurai status, and the Iwakura jail, the lower jail, everyone else.
  • The Hagi jails were built on the sites of two confiscated residences.
    The residences were confiscated, and the sites were later rebuilt as jails.
  • The upper jail at Hagi, for samurai, had twelve single cells, six facing six, with an inspection hut and an execution ground.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on the Noyama jail gives this layout for the upper jail, and none for the lower one.
  • The shogunate's largest jail, Edo's Tenmachō, filled a moated block of its own of about 8,850 square meters (~95,000 sq ft, ~2.2 acres).
    It was the largest of the shogunate's jails. It covered 2,677 tsubo, about 8,850 square meters (~95,000 sq ft), and was enclosed on three sides by moat and embankment.
  • The shogunate also kept jails at its magistrates' and intendants' offices, and temporary jails at its courts in Edo.
    Besides Tenmachō there were jails at the magistrates' and intendants' offices of Kyoto, Osaka, Nagasaki and elsewhere, and temporary jails at Edo's high court, its town magistracy and its finance magistracy.
  • Edo's North town magistracy kept a temporary holding cell among its court rooms.
    The cell stood in the quarter given to lawsuits and trials, with the court, the interrogation rooms and the station of the jail constables. The magistracy itself is at The town magistracy and its constables (machi-bugyo, doshin).
