River cities: the research behind the river, moat-junction and wharf rules

The findings behind the river, the moat's junctions with it and the wharf on these maps, the decisions they drove, and - where no generator draws the feature yet - the rules a map follows.

Most provincial cities sit on a river

Sources: kaifeng-flood-1642-enwiki (300,000 of 378,000 killed), kaifeng-pmc7048742 (~40 floods in 3,000 years), pan-gate-enwiki (the paired land-and-water gate; its sluice is a GUESS - no readable page supports it)

  • The history (China first, Japan agreeing). MOST provincial cities sit on a river (Tango, on an off-map stream, is the exception). The trunk river NEVER runs through the walls - the one great city that let a river in, Kaifeng, silted its bed meters above the plain1 and was flooded about forty times in three millennia9 - more than 300 floodings by another count, five major ones in 1375-141610 - the 1642 flood alone killing ~300k of 378k.2 Instead the city stands ON one bank: the RIVER is the water defense on its flank, and the dug MOAT covers the three landward faces, tapping the river upstream and returning downstream so the current flushes it (Xiangyang's Han-river face + dug moat; Pingyao on the Fen; the Asahi's branches at Okayama "utilized to create moats"3; Shanghai's county-seat moat "connected to the Huangpu"20). The ONE way water legitimately enters the walls is a water gate (shuimen) admitting a navigable CANAL, not the river4 - Suzhou's Pan Gate, a paired land-and-water gate5 with a sluiced arch,6 feeding the city's inner canals7 + a dock basin21. The riverfront wharf (jetties, warehouses, market) sits OUTSIDE the water-side gate, the river-city version of the gate market (guan-xiang8).

The rule the map follows: a river city's map shows a trunk river running off the sheet at both ends and drawn heavier than anything else on it, the dug moat included - the river IS the stronger defense on that flank, and the map should not say otherwise. The moat is an OPEN arc rather than a closed ring: it covers the three landward faces and both of its ends run onto the river, which closes the water ring itself, so every point of the wall stands behind either the moat or the river. A navigable canal - lighter than the moat, heavier than a field ditch - leads from the water through a grated water gate in the wall to a dock basin inside; the wall is drawn with a matching gap at the water gate and carries no tower on that vertex. Planked jetties spring from the near bank, each rooted at the bank with its tip in the near half of the water: a finger, never a bar spanning the river. Buildings keep out of the canal's water, and the canal must actually reach the dock - connected like a street reaching a road, with no visible gap. The wharf itself - jetties, stores, market - stands outside the water-side gate, which is the river city's version of the gate market. A river city has no Imperial spine through it: its highway passes off the sheet, so what the map owes instead is a road net that leaves the view in at least two directions. And the dead cross the river: the moat's water set-back leaves no dry landward fringe to put them on, so the funerary ground sits on the far bank, downstream of the city and clear of the bridge road.

The rule the map follows: three rules a river city shares with every other city on these maps. A gate's two posts stand to either side of the opening and square to the wall's LOCAL tangent, so the posts of an east-west gate stand north and south of it rather than upright on the page. A samurai country estate faces the capital, whichever direction that is from this particular city, and stands clear of the highway out of the gate. And a burial or cremation ground sits beside the farmland, never on a paddy body or on its ditches. One more thing the tier carries as the setting's own canon, from the GM's notes: a city holds a temple to each of its clan's two patron fortunes, and those are the temples that get labeled - the warrior fortune may be added where there is a samurai quarter, and everything else is a small wayside shrine, however thematic a larger dedication would be.

Which way does an offtake leave a river, and why?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-24; watersa-junction-angle (what a wider junction angle does to the separation zone and to the bed shear where one channel enters another), ignou-silt-control (how an offtake's alignment governs the sediment it draws, and where on a bend it belongs); general hydraulic reading, for which no source is yet cited

The two ends of a river-fed moat answer differently, and the asymmetry is itself the signature of a worked waterway. At the outlet, where the moat returns its water, the junction angle is a primary control on how one channel merges into another: a numerical study of a confluence set in a river BEND found that widening the angle at which the side channel enters enlarges the zone of separated flow inside the junction, and raises the greatest bed shear stress there by 59% between 30 and 115 degrees.13 So a small, downstream-pointing angle merges smoothly, while a large one drives the entering jet across the receiving channel. That the water behind such a mouth then goes slack, and that the far bank is attacked, is this record's own extension of that result rather than a finding in it - the study is of a bend, and reports neither - and the moat's two feet are drawn from the extension all the same. Natural tributaries curve to join pointing downstream, and engineered drainage returns are cut to do the same (this rests on general reading; no source is cited).14 At the inlet, the same reasoning points the other way. A flow-aligned intake is the smooth-entry answer, but the sediment rides in the slow bottom layer, which is the layer a diversion takes preferentially, and how much of it comes in is a function of the offtake's alignment: a channel leaving a parent CANAL is turned toward the square, 60 to 80 degrees to the parent's centerline in the direction of flow, in order to keep the excess sediment out.15 A silted moat is a maintenance failure rather than a hydraulic one. Off a RIVER, though, the rule runs the other way: a canal taking off at 90 degrees to the main flow is "the most objectionable orientation of the regulator", and 30 to 45 degrees to the river's flow is what is normally recommended, so that the flow lines curve into the regulator instead of striking it.16 The near-square offtake therefore belongs to a distributary leaving a canal and not to a headworks on a river. What the two cases share is the siting and the skimming: the intake belongs on the OUTER, concave bank of a bend and toward its downstream end, because a bend sweeps its bottom layers toward the inside, and a raised inlet sill with a skimmer wall at the mouth keeps the bedload and the floating matter out so that what enters is the cleaner upper water.19 These maps draw the moat inlet near square - about 10 degrees upstream of it - which is the canal rule; on the river rule the mouth would lie at 30 to 45 degrees to the current. And a moat wants STAGE - the height of the water surface - rather than throughput: its job is to stay full, not to pass water, so it never needs the momentum-capturing mouth an irrigation canal would want (this rests on general reading; no source is cited).18 Two cautions go with all of this: what the engineering literature appears to hold, on no page that could be read, is that a diversion draws its LEAST sediment at an intermediate angle rather than at the square, and that the often-quoted 90 to 120 degree rule for a headworks is measured from the WEIR's axis rather than from the river's own line. Neither has moved what the map draws.

The decision (the GM, 2026-07-24). The two junction arms met the river as parallel right angles, and the GM asked whether they should not angle with the flow. They were parallel only because each open end of the moat was projected onto the river by dropping a perpendicular - an artifact of how the arc was closed, never a decision anyone made. The answer splits by end, in the way the hydrology above splits: the outlet now sweeps about 22 degrees off square, pointing downstream, and the inlet tilts only about 10 degrees, upstream of square, which is near enough to a right angle to keep its sediment out. Never the reverse: an inlet swept downstream is the one shape the map must not draw. The two angles themselves are a guess - they are the smallest tilts that read as deliberate at this scale, not figures taken from a source - while the directions they point rest on the reading above.

The far side of the ring circulates slowly, and that is accepted as drawn. The two junctions sit only about 800 ft apart along the river, so the head driving water around the ring is tiny and the arc furthest from them barely moves. The alternative - separating the junctions far enough along the bank to drive a real circulation - was not taken, and the reason is that the sluggish version is the historical one: a river moat was kept full by the river's own stage far more than it was ever flushed through, so partial stagnation is period-accurate rather than a defect (this rests on general reading; no source is cited).17 The cost of accepting it is one observable thing: a reader who thinks about the flow will see that the far arc is still water.

The rule the map follows: where a moat's open ends run onto a river, the UPSTREAM end is the inlet and the DOWNSTREAM end is the outlet, and the two meet the river at different angles. The outlet's junction sits clearly downstream of the square, by about 22 degrees of the arm's length; the inlet sits square or a little upstream of square, by about 10 degrees, and never swept downstream. The map's river is drawn from its source toward its mouth, so that which end is upstream is a fact about the drawing and not a guess.

Does a city's canal open its own mouth on the river?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-23; the Suzhou water gate described in the entry above

Not beside the moat's own mouth, it does not. A cargo canal that opens its own river tap some 36 ft from the moat's downstream junction and then rides collinearly inside the moat arm for the whole bank crossing reads as a smeared doubled channel with a sliver fork at the mouth, which is what put the question. The answer that the record supports MERGES the two mouths rather than separating them: in the Suzhou pattern a city's canals communicate with the moat, and the moat's downstream river junction is then the single navigation entrance. Separating them would have been the worse drawing here for a second reason as well: a distinct mouth further downstream would have had to cross the moat's southern arc at grade. So the canal ends ON the corner of the moat's outfall arm, and boats run river, then moat arm, then water gate, then dock basin. What this does NOT forbid is a water gate on the river face itself: real cities had them, and a canal tapping open bank away from the moat's junction is a legitimate second form.

The rule the map follows: a river city has ONE navigation mouth on the river. Either the cargo canal ends on the moat's downstream corner and the moat arm carries the traffic in, or the canal opens its own mouth on open bank well away from the moat's junction. What the map must not draw is a canal tapping the river inside the moat's junction corridor - two mouths side by side, each spoiling the other's reading.

The wharf's working face: piers, quays and stepped landings

Sources: kashi-jawiki (the river-port kashi with its warehouses; gangi as landing stairs)

The GM's question (2026-08-11): is three piers the right number for six granaries and three warehouses, and is there something between a pier and a boardwalk - a dock - that the map should be drawing? ("My guess is no on a boardwalk, because I associate that with tourism rather than an actual wharf. But some marinas have docks and piers both. I don't know how this would have worked.")

The boardwalk instinct is right, and for a better reason than association. A boardwalk is a promenade - a surface for walking along the water for its own sake. Nothing in a pre-modern working river port is built for that. Every surface at a wharf exists to get cargo off a hull and into a store as few times handled as possible.

The finding, China first (Song/Ming river ports), Japan as the tiebreaker. The dominant form is not the pier at all: it is the revetted quay face - the bank itself cut back, faced with stone or timber cribbing, and fitted with stepped landings at intervals. The Chinese matou (碼頭) is characteristically a stone-stepped landing set into a faced bank; the Japanese riverside kashi (河岸) district uses the same arrangement22, with the stepped landing called gangi (雁木)23 and the kura standing directly behind the bank street.12

The reason is hydrology, and it is decisive: a river's level moves through the year by many feet. A fixed-height deck is at the right height for a few weeks and wrong the rest of the time24 - too high to reach in the dry season, awash in the wet. A flight of steps down a faced bank is at the right height at EVERY level, because the boat simply lies against a different tread. That is why the stepped quay is the norm on rivers and the projecting pier is the exception, and it inverts the intuition a modern marina gives you.

So what is the pier FOR? Reach, when the bank shelves too gently for a loaded hull to come alongside - the pier walks the working face out to where the water is deep enough. A port with a steep faced bank needs few or none; a port on a shallow shelving reach needs several.

What this means for the count. The question "is three piers enough for nine store buildings" has the wrong shape: the granaries and warehouses are not each served by a pier. The bank is the unloading face, continuous along the frontage, and its capacity is measured in feet of usable mooring, not in piers. A takasebune river barge is roughly 50-60 ft long, so a few hundred feet of faced bank moors five or six of them abreast of the stores they are filling - which is ample for six granaries and three warehouses, whose turnover is seasonal and dominated by the tax-rice harvest. Three piers on top of that is not too few. It may be slightly too many for a bank this steep, but they are within the band, and a port that also handles timber and stone wants at least one.

Decision for the map. Keep the three piers. The thing that actually does most of the work is the faced quay edge along the granary frontage, with stepped landings at intervals, and mooring posts. That is a real, drawable, map-visible feature - a line of revetment along the bank with two or three step flights notched into it. No boardwalk, no floating dock (a moored floating stage is a modern marina's answer to the same level problem, and the steps are the pre-modern one).

What the map draws. The faced edge is a stone band with a coursing line, stepped landings notched into it (four treads each, descending toward the water), and mooring posts along the top. On Shiro Daika it runs the granary frontage, following the river's own line rather than pinned, so a re-routed river carries the wharf with it. The quay is drawn as a line along the bank (like the towpath), not as a building, and a jetty may spring FROM the faced bank, which is the whole point of the pier - reach, starting at the revetment.
