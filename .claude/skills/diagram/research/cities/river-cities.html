Most provincial cities sit on a river

Grounds: s.river, meta(river_port=True)

Evidence: attested, corroborated

Sources: kaifeng-flood-1642-enwiki (READ: 300,000 of 378,000), kaifeng-pmc7048742 (READ: ~40 floods in 3,000 years), pan-gate-enwiki (READ; the sluice SUMMARY-ONLY). Xiangyang, Pingyao-on-the-Fen, Okayama's Asahi3 and Shanghai's moat not re-read - leftover

  • The history (China first, Japan agreeing). MOST provincial cities sit on a river (Tango, on an off-map stream, is the exception). The trunk river NEVER runs through the walls - the one great city that let a river in, Kaifeng, silted its bed meters above the plain1 and was devastated by floods seven times (the 1642 flood alone killed ~300k of 378k).2 Instead the city stands ON one bank: the RIVER is the water defense on its flank, and the dug MOAT covers the three landward faces, tapping the river upstream and returning downstream so the current flushes it (Xiangyang's Han-river face + dug moat; Pingyao on the Fen; Okayama's Asahi "natural moat"; Shanghai's county-seat moat "connected to the Huangpu"). The ONE way water legitimately enters the walls is a water gate (shuimen) admitting a navigable CANAL, not the river4 - Suzhou's Pan Gate, a paired land-and-water gate5 with a sluiced arch,6 feeding the city's inner canals7 + a dock basin. The riverfront wharf (jetties, warehouses, market) sits OUTSIDE the water-side gate, the river-city version of the gate market (guan-xiang8).

Correction to the record (2026-08-28, feature 143): "devastated by floods seven times" appears in no source read - the Nature/PMC paper counts about forty floods in three millennia9 and Wikipedia's Kaifeng article gives10 "more than 300" floodings with five major ones in 1375-1416. The argument (a river let inside the walls silts and floods) stands on the larger numbers; the "seven" is struck.

The wharf's working face: piers, quays and stepped landings

Evidence: attested (kashi, gangi), researched (the matou form)

Sources: kashi-jawiki (READ: the river-port kashi with its warehouses; gangi as landing stairs); the Chinese matou stepped landing and the river-stage argument not re-read - leftover

The GM's question (2026-08-11): is three piers the right number for six granaries and three warehouses, and is there something between a pier and a boardwalk - a dock - that the map should be drawing? ("My guess is no on a boardwalk, because I associate that with tourism rather than an actual wharf. But some marinas have docks and piers both. I don't know how this would have worked.")

The boardwalk instinct is right, and for a better reason than association. A boardwalk is a promenade - a surface for walking along the water for its own sake. Nothing in a pre-modern working river port is built for that. Every surface at a wharf exists to get cargo off a hull and into a store as few times handled as possible.

The finding, China first (Song/Ming river ports), Japan as the tiebreaker. The dominant form is not the pier at all: it is the revetted quay face - the bank itself cut back, faced with stone or timber cribbing, and fitted with stepped landings at intervals. The Chinese matou (碼頭) is characteristically a stone-stepped landing set into a faced bank; the Japanese riverside kashi (河岸) district uses the same arrangement,13 with the stepped landing called gangi (雁木)11 and the kura standing directly behind the bank street.12

The reason is hydrology, and it is decisive: a river's level moves through the year by many feet. A fixed-height deck is at the right height for a few weeks and wrong the rest of the time14 - too high to reach in the dry season, awash in the wet. A flight of steps down a faced bank is at the right height at EVERY level, because the boat simply lies against a different tread. That is why the stepped quay is the norm on rivers and the projecting pier is the exception, and it inverts the intuition a modern marina gives you.

So what is the pier FOR? Reach, when the bank shelves too gently for a loaded hull to come alongside - the pier walks the working face out to where the water is deep enough. A port with a steep faced bank needs few or none; a port on a shallow shelving reach needs several.

What this means for the count. The question "is three piers enough for nine store buildings" has the wrong shape: the granaries and warehouses are not each served by a pier. The bank is the unloading face, continuous along the frontage, and its capacity is measured in feet of usable mooring, not in piers. A takasebune river barge is roughly 50-60 ft long, so a few hundred feet of faced bank moors five or six of them abreast of the stores they are filling - which is ample for six granaries and three warehouses, whose turnover is seasonal and dominated by the tax-rice harvest. Three piers on top of that is not too few. It may be slightly too many for a bank this steep, but they are within the band, and a port that also handles timber and stone wants at least one.

Decision for the map. Keep the three piers. What is MISSING is the thing that was actually doing most of the work: the faced quay edge along the granary frontage, with stepped landings at intervals, and mooring posts. That is a real, drawable, map-visible feature - a line of revetment along the bank with two or three step flights notched into it - and its absence is why the wharf currently reads as three fingers poking into an otherwise natural riverbank. No boardwalk, no floating dock (a moored floating stage is a modern marina's answer to the same level problem, and the steps are the pre-modern one).

Recorded as a to-draw item, not yet drawn: the quay face and its gangi steps need a glyph, and the granary row's landward path should meet the step flights rather than the pier heads.

Drawn (2026-08-11). settlement.quay(pts, steps=N) renders the faced edge as a stone band with a coursing line, notches steps stepped landings into it (four treads each, descending toward the water), and sets mooring posts along the top. On Shiro Daika it runs the granary frontage, derived from the river's own polyline rather than pinned, so a re-routed river carries the wharf with it. The quay is classified as a LINE feature (like the towpath, not a box), and quays x jetties is a declared allowed pair: a jetty springs FROM the faced bank, which is the whole point of the pier - reach, starting at the revetment.
