Outside the walls: the research behind the estate and in-wall farmland rules

The findings behind a city's farmland, its gate suburbs and its country estates, the decisions they drove, and - where no generator draws the feature yet - the rules a map follows.

Gentry estates are DISPERSED, not clustered at the wall

Sources: cdlib-local-elites (the urbanized gentry living in town as absentee landlords); the fortified Sengoku yakata and the guanxiang (guanxiang-zdic, madian-mosque-zhwiki)

  • Three forces all push AGAINST a wall-cluster. (1) Land: elite holdings were fragmented, scattered parcels and lineages were rooted in specific ancestral villages, so the rural big-house sits among its OWN fields / at the home village, dispersed across the district (the single live-on manor was an earlier Tang-Song form) (unsourced). (2) Security: a row of rich, undefended compounds outside the wall is a bandit magnet that negates the wall - the rural estates that existed were each individually fortified (the Sengoku yakata, walled lineage compounds) precisely because they stood ALONE beyond the city's protection, which spreads them apart (unsourced). (3) Amenity: the seat's exam/office/social life pulled the gentry INWARD through the gate to live in town as absentee landlords1 (in an Edo reading they don't commute - they're resident inside).2 And the belt hugging the gates was the COMMERCIAL commoner suburb (guanxiang43), not a genteel quarter - "close to the gate" describes the wrong land use. So: the bulk of well-off samurai live INSIDE the walls (the samurai ward) (unsourced); the immediate wall-shadow is the commercial suburb (gate markets and flophouses);5 the country estates proper are dispersed ~2-15 miles out (the distance unsourced), each an isolated fortified compound by its own land; at most a few near-city retreats sit within a mile or few, spread apart, never clustered (unsourced).

What the map can honestly show (the GM asked on 2026-07-22 whether the tight belt of estates just outside the wall was accurate). A city view is under a mile across - about 3,800 ft at this tier - so the country estates proper, miles out, are entirely off the sheet. The map draws only the NEAREST one to three, spread apart on the open, capital-facing approaches, each on its own ground with its own drive out to the district road, and leaves the rest implied. That is a deviation, and a deliberate one: the near estate is on the sheet so that a reader learns the type exists, not because the ground within half a mile of a gate held several.

The belt is a slice, and the count is free (the GM's ruling of 2026-07-19). The GM's own domain figures support roughly eight to fifteen estate households in a provincial city's orbit, but a map never claims a census: it draws at least two, at the frame edge, some of them cut by the frame - exactly like the farm fields the frame cuts, where the truncation itself says that this continues beyond the sheet. Any count of two or more is right. The households who live in them are counted in the surrounding village districts, not in the city's own three thousand.

Where the full pattern would show, and why it is deferred. The genuinely dispersed arrangement - estates strewn across the whole countryside, each beside its own fields and its ancestral village, out to perhaps fifteen miles, most of them nowhere near the city - only becomes visible on a map of the district rather than of the city. That is a separate mode nobody has built. At city scale the honest depiction stays the nearest few at the frame edge with the rest off-map.

The rule the map follows: a provincial city draws one to three walled country estates outside its wall, at least two of them falling at least partly inside the cropped window. They stand at least 600 ft apart , each its own compound on its own land and never a tight cluster, and they vary in size, the largest at least half again the smallest. They lie toward the capital, whichever direction that is from this city, on the open approaches the fields leave free; they overlap neither each other nor the wall and moat, and they keep off the highway out of the gate. Their gates do not all face the same way: the formal gate favors the auspicious south, while others face the approach from the city, because each estate fronts its own lane that the map is cropped too tight to draw. The spacing and the size spread are calibrated against the three drawn cities, not derived from a source.

Why is a city ringed by farmland on every side?

Sources: the GM's rulings of 2026-08-11 and 2026-08-12; shizen-teibo-jawiki and kohai-shicchi-jawiki (which ground of a lowland is built on and which is left to paddy); this project's measurement of the three drawn provincial cities

A city is not a thing set down on a plain. It is the market and the garrison of the land that feeds it, and a single token field on one flank draws that relationship backwards. The GM ruled on 2026-08-11, after a first pass at a domain capital drew one field: "The city should be surrounded by farmland. Cities grow up around fertile land, so we should do as we do for Tango and Nagahara and Minami and keep adding rice paddies and farmhouses until there are no more places to put them. Farmland ringing a city is the DEFAULT which should always happen in the future." The three cities already drawn had settled that practice long before it was written down - one carries eleven fields, another seven, the third six - so the ruling fixed the default rather than changing what they show.

Farmsteads stand upslope of the drain. Within an alluvial lowland the badly drained ground is the low ground behind the slight rise a river builds along its own channel, and it is that low ground which is reclaimed as paddy;13 the settlements of such a lowland sit first of all on the rise itself, which within the wet ground around it drains well.6 The record applies that rule to a feature those accounts do not name - the strip immediately below a dug collector rather than a natural back marsh - and takes the wettest ground in the valley to be there. A farmstead's seat is measured against the drain LINE rather than the field's center, since a seat can be above the middle of a field and still below the drain where it bends. The provincial cities never needed that, because their drains march off the view; the capital, whose drain runs on the sheet, did.

The rule the map follows: a city is ringed with farmland until the ground genuinely refuses it, and the floor is at least four fields across at least three flanks. Fewer than that means the ring was not attempted, not that the ground ran out. Each field is a whole water chain rather than a colored polygon: a tap that sits ON the water and is derived from the watercourse's own line rather than picked by eye, a head race that bends rather than ruling straight, a gate at each end, a moat offtake swept downstream with the moat's current, a declared source and a declared drain, the drain carried clear off the sheet rather than stopping in mid-air, and the farmsteads that work it. A farmstead may stand hard against a strip of dry crop but never on it.

The rule the map follows: at the city tier a field's paddy strips run about 78 ft across , its cascade rows step 39 to 57 ft , its supply canals span 180 to 780 ft , its fan falls 270 to 570 ft , and the dry hem quilting the head of the fan is 140 to 265 ft deep . A field counts as large enough to be worth irrigating from the moat past about eleven and a half acres . These are calibrations against the three drawn cities, chosen so that a city-fringe field stays the size of the field it replaces, not figures from a source; the hem's depth in particular is per field, because the pooled value is deeper than some ground is.

The rule the map follows: every field outside the wall is worked by villagers, so every one of them carries farmhouses around it even where it runs off the sheet - and what is counted is the part a reader can see. Along the field's boundary that lies inside the cropped window, the map wants about seven farmhouses for every 3,000 ft of that boundary ; village fields run between four and nineteen by the same measure, averaging about twelve, so seven is a floor rather than a target. Because most of a fringe field lies off the sheet, the ring is drawn densely enough that enough houses land on the visible edge, and the ones that fall off-map are simply not drawn. None of these farmhouses counts toward the city's own population: the farmers who work a city's land live in the surrounding villages and are counted there. On a town sheet the same houses ARE part of the county's farmer cohort and do count, which is the one place the two tiers differ here.

Does the moat feed the fields, or do the fields drain into it?

Sources: the GM's rulings of 2026-08-11; this project's measurement of the drawn provincial cities and the capital

Both, and which one it is depends on where the field sits on the ring. A moat has a direction of flow - the feeder stream enters on one side, and that heading is the way the water goes - so a field on an arc UPSTREAM of the city's own draw cannot be fed from the moat: an offtake there would have to run against the current, and a channel whose field lies upstream of its tap reads as water running out of the field into the moat, backwards. The GM ruled on 2026-08-11 that the relationship simply reverses in that case, and that the other maps already use it: such a field takes its head from high ground off the sheet and DISCHARGES into the moat below, gated at the outfall and swept with the current. The moat is the city's storm drain as well as its reservoir.

The north shelf of the capital stays unfarmed, and the reason is depth. Between the rampart and the sheet edge there is about 360 ft , and a fan cannot be carved in that at any hem, because the fall plus the head band is deeper than the shelf. It is not the current that rules that strip out - a field there could have drained into the moat like any other upstream field - it is the room.

The rule the map follows: a moated city irrigates at least three of its large fields from the moat. Each tap stands about 90 ft outside the moat's rim with a short stub bridging rim to gate, and the channel it feeds ends INSIDE the field rather than near it. Taps sit upstream of the field they serve, so the water runs downstream into it; a field lying upstream of the whole moat is fed from the source stream instead or runs off the sheet with its water implied, and the moat-fed count is made up from a downstream field. A field's collector may empty back INTO the moat, or run off the cropped view. Where a channel meets the moat its mouth is snapped onto the open water's edge so that it covers the rim rather than drawing its bed as a colored line across the water - a map drawing convention, and the same one a channel meeting a pond gets. Channels are drawn with a bend, never ruler-straight, at a hairline stroke whose width on the sheet is a drawing convention rather than a measured ditch, and the ground under each one is kept clear of farmsteads for about 100 ft either side.

The rule the map follows: a field INSIDE the walls records the moat as its sink, but its visible ditch stops short of the patrol road: the runoff drops into an implied underground stone conduit that passes beneath the ring road, the rampart and the moat. The GM's rule is flat - never draw a ditch running through the city wall; the water gate is underground.

What stands outside a city gate?

Sources:chang-morphology-walled-capitals (what grew outside a gate: markets and businesses serving the countryside, inns on the gates that carried the long-distance routes, and the suburb filling before the walled ground did), madian-mosque-zhwiki (the horse market and the forty-eight horse inns outside one Beijing gate), zhangjiakou-fort-zhwiki (a gate suburb established as overflow, and streeted); the GM's ruling of 2026-07-24

A built-up strip outside the gate was normal enough to have its own name, guanxiang: the streets immediately outside a city gate and the ground adjoining them. It is a road-driven suburb - it forms along the approach road at a gate that carries traffic, which is why a city's MAIN gates each earn one while the scale differs from gate to gate. In the drawn cities the wider southern approach carries the bigger market and the northern gate a stall row; a river city's water-side gate carries the whole wharf instead. What GREW in that strip is attested in general terms for the Chinese walled city: because a gate funneled all the traffic of one sector of the countryside, the ground immediately outside it was a favored site for markets and for businesses serving the rural inhabitants, the gates that carried the long-distance routes drew inns and other services for travelers and traders, and at the busiest gates the suburb commonly filled before the walled ground inside it had.7 One described strip fits that pattern item by item: outside Beijing's Deshengmen, on the trunk road north, the tribute horse trains sold their culled animals on the spot, the merchants who dealt in horses, cattle, sheep and camels lodged there, and a market formed of itself.15 One runs the other way, and is worth knowing: the guanxiang of the Zhangjiakou fort was set up in 1480 because the ground inside the fort had filled and the added inhabitants had to be housed somewhere, and it grew streets of its own.14 Its SIZE is a guess: the figure the maps were calibrated against is ten to forty structures at a busy gate, and the nearest counted thing is the same Deshengmen strip, which at its height held forty-eight horse inns - one trade, at the busiest gate of a capital, so a provincial seat's belt sits sensibly below it.8 The GM raised the drawn floor from three structures to six on 2026-07-24 on the strength of that figure, on the reasoning that the belt on the sheet is a slice of a longer suburb.

The rule the map follows: a gate market stands outside EVERY main-road gate of a city, not merely the busiest one: at least six extramural shops within about 1,560 ft of the gate , strung along the approach road with the outermost running off the frame, so that the truncation says the suburb continues. A flophouse belongs in the same belt outside each gate, and a second inside the walls. A purely military sally gate - one that opens onto empty field for sorties, with no road - carries no market, no flophouse and no caravan cluster; no city on the sheet has one today, and a city that declared one would keep its sally gates out of the list the market rule reads.

Does a city farm inside its walls?

Sources: chinese-city-wall-enwiki (the wall drawn wide of the built ground, and what was still inside two of them in the twentieth century), king-forty-centuries (the night soil going out of the city to the gardens), qimin-yaoshu-zhongkui (the hand-worked bed, its reason, and the market garden that backed onto a city wall); the GM's density canon for the provincial-city tier; this project's measurement of the one drawn city that farms inside its wall

Often, and on purpose. The walls of important Chinese cities frequently enclosed an area much larger than the built-up ground, to keep capacity for growth and to secure resources such as timber and farmland in time of war; Quanzhou's wall still contained a quarter of vacant land in 1945, and Suzhou's still enclosed large tracts of farmland into the twentieth century.12 So farmland inside a wall is characteristic rather than odd: where the wall was drawn wide of the built ground, the farms were what the extra ground was for.

What these maps draw, and where that parts from the record. By default a city on these sheets draws none inside its wall, and that default is the drawing's rather than the record's. One of the drawn cities does have an agricultural district - a whole quarter of it - and the reason recorded for it is particular to that city: it has water sources inside the wall, and dimensions that made it save almost nothing to wall the farms out. On the record above that city is the ordinary case rather than the odd one. It is also the reason it can hold an AVERAGE provincial-city population with above-average space per inhabitant inside the wall: the surplus is farmed, not built on. That is the GM's density canon for the tier, and it settles a question that keeps coming back - when contiguous terraces vacate ground a looser scatter had filled, the freed ground is absorbed by more farmland, never by more dwellings, because the caste bands already pin how many households a city holds.

Not all of it is paddy. Some of the in-wall ground goes down as vegetable tracts rather than rice: intensively worked garden ground, fed from wells and from night soil. Both halves of that are attested and the join between them is this record's own. A walled seat did enclose farmland, as the passage above says; and the night soil of a Japanese city went out to the gardens daily by the cartload, in closed containers of forty to sixty pounds and more, the liquid manure diluted with water before it was fed to the plants, which is why such ground wants an abundant and convenient water supply.9 That the surplus ground inside a wall was specifically what was worked this way is the inference. They are exempt from the rules a paddy owes, and for a reason rather than for convenience: a garden tract has no surface channel to show, because it is watered from wells, and no separate farm household to ring it, because it is worked by the households of the quarters around it. East of a city's Imperial road a channel could not reach it in any case, since it would have to cross the road.

A field's fan has a hand, and both hands were real (this rests on general reading; no source is cited).10 The pocket an in-wall field sits in is rarely symmetrical - a ring road inside the wall leaves a crescent that narrows toward one end - and the fan's wide flank falls naturally on one side. Mirroring the whole field, rather than forcing the pocket to accept the default hand, is therefore an honest option and not a cheat, and the map takes it where the crescent demands it. The pond that feeds such a field sits at the pocket's HIGH corner, the way water enters the city from high ground, and the fan fills the ground downhill of it.

A garden bed is smaller than anything the map draws. The sixth-century agricultural treatise Qimin yaoshu sets the hand-worked bed at two paces long by one wide, and gives the reason: any larger and the water cannot be spread evenly over it, and one does not want a person's foot to go into it. The same note says why beds at all - with them the ground is economized and the vegetables are many, one bed supplying one mouth.16 Turning a sixth-century pace into feet needs a metrology this record has not read; on the two common reckonings it comes out at roughly 5 by 10 ft, and that arithmetic is the record's own rather than the treatise's. Either way the parcel the map draws is not a bed - 55 ft across is five to eleven beds wide - so what a reader sees is a garden PARCEL made of many beds, the beds themselves being below the scale the sheet draws at.

The same chapter sizes the commercial garden that served a city, which is this question's own subject: thirty mu of good land backing onto the city wall, worked for the winter crop, with ten wells sunk along its length and a well-sweep or a windlass at each. The wells are set to correspond to one another, because a skew line wastes the ground - one row where the plot is long and narrow, two or three where it is a true square.17 The area, like the bed, is in a measure this record has not converted from a source.

The rule the map follows: a city draws no farmland inside its walls unless it declares an agricultural district, which also holds the in-wall burakumin neighborhood. Where it does, those fields are real farmland and the whole of one is in view, so its whole perimeter must read as worked: it is ringed densely all the way round - about sixteen farmhouses per 3,000 ft of edge, against seven for a field outside , with a tight inner row and one or two denser outer bands - rather than a sparse few on one side that leave long bare edges. The drawn example runs between twenty-four and thirty-seven by that measure. These farmhouses do not count toward the city's population either. Both figures are calibrated against the drawn cities and against village ringing, not derived from a source.

The rule the map follows: a vegetable tract's parcels are the SMALLEST dry parcels on the map - about 55 ft across and no more than 0.15 acre each, against 90 to 100 ft for a strip of the grain hem beside a paddy. A kitchen garden is hand-worked ground, so its parcels are smaller than a grain field's and not larger; the tract's width is tiled into an even number of columns of roughly that size, because a single cut across it leaves one fat column of about 150 ft that ends up the biggest dry parcel on the sheet, which is backwards for a garden. The 55 ft is a calibration against the drawn cities. It is a block of beds and not one bed: the worked bed the record gives is a fifth to a tenth of it, and no readable page gives a width for a garden parcel of the kind the map draws.11
