How big a city's wall is for its population, and how many live outside it

A walled city's population and the ground inside its wall are two sides of one sum: the houses of everyone counted as living in the city, the compounds of its government and its temples, and its streets all have to fit inside the ring. In a Chinese administrative seat the government was a set of named institutions near the center - the yamen, the Confucian temple, the examination hall, the City God's temple, the offices and the officials' residences. In the setting, a city's population counts only those who live inside its walls, while the farmers who work the land around it live in the villages of the county.

Who lived outside a wall depended on whether the wall still mattered. In China's great commercial cities, from the Song dynasty on, the population spread past the gates: perhaps 150,000 to 200,000 outside the wall of Kaifeng in the eleventh century, and Suzhou's busiest shop streets outside its Changmen gate under the Ming and Qing. Japan's castle towns were at first unwalled, and where one was later enclosed, the enclosure took the whole town in.   • A Chinese administrative city's government stood near its center, as a list of named institutions.
    A study of the walled cities of late imperial China places the yamen and its associated buildings and compounds - the Confucian temple, the examination hall, the temple of the City God, public offices and officials' residences - near the center of an administrative city. It gives no share of the walled ground for them.
  • In the setting, a city's population counts only those who live inside its walls.
    A provincial city of about 2,000 to 4,000 inhabitants, or a capital of about 12,000, counts only the population living within its walls; the farmers who work the fertile land the city stands on live in the villages and hamlets of the surrounding county.
  • In the setting, a household is reckoned at five.
    The median household is taken to be five, over two or three generations.
  • How many lived outside the wall of Kaifeng, the Song capital?
    About 150,000 to 200,000, by a modern estimate. In 1021 the ground outside the wall was reorganized into nine districts because so many had settled there; a doctoral study of the city's layout counts fourteen wards in those districts, few because the outskirts held many imperial and private gardens, and estimates the population from them.
  • Suzhou's busiest commercial district lay round its Changmen gate, much of it outside the wall.
    Under the Ming and Qing the district round the Changmen was once the most flourishing commercial quarter in China, its streets radiating outward outside the wall as well as one running inside it, with shops of every trade numbering in the tens of thousands.
  • The Chinese word for the streets outside a city gate is guanxiang.
    A dictionary defines it as the streets outside a city gate and the area nearby, in English a suburb outside the city gate; the entry says nothing about trade.
  • Japan's castle towns were at first unwalled, and later enclosures brought the town inside.
    In the early castle towns only the lord's castle was surrounded by a moat and wall. As the towns grew in economic and political value they needed protecting, and more of them came to be enclosed whole by a moat and earthworks, the sogamae.
  • Did a walled city pack its people tighter than an open one?
    No East Asian page we read says so. The one page found that states it is about medieval Europe, and a Chinese-language discussion holds that Ming and Qing walled cities were often not filled.
