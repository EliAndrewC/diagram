How big a city's wall is for its population, and how many live outside it

A walled city held inside its wall the houses of its inhabitants, the compounds of its government and its temples, and its streets. In a Chinese administrative seat the government's offices and temples stood near the center. In the setting, a city's population counts only those inside its walls - a few thousand in a provincial city, about twelve thousand in a capital - while the farmers who work the land around it live in the villages of the county.

From the late Tang on, as the control of Chinese cities relaxed and they grew, suburbs grew up at the gates of many of them: well over a hundred thousand inhabitants outside the wall of Kaifeng in the eleventh century, and Suzhou's busiest shop streets outside its Changmen gate under the Ming and Qing. Japan's castle towns were at first unwalled, and where one was later enclosed, the enclosure took the whole town in.

  • In the setting, a provincial city counts about 2,000 to 4,000 inhabitants inside its walls, and a capital about 12,000.
    A provincial city of about 2,000 to 4,000 inhabitants, or a capital of about 12,000, counts only the population living within its walls; the farmers who work the fertile land the city stands on live in the villages and hamlets of the surrounding county.
  • In the setting, a household is reckoned at five.
    The median household is taken to be five, over two or three generations.
  • A Chinese administrative city's government offices and temples generally stood near its center.
    A study of the walled cities of late imperial China places the yamen and its associated buildings and compounds - the Confucian temple, the examination hall, the temple of the City God, public offices and officials' residences - near the center of an administrative city. It gives no share of the walled ground for them.
  • Did a walled city pack its inhabitants tighter than an open one?
    No East Asian page we read says so. A study of the walled cities of late imperial China points the other way: in many if not most cities, suburbs grew outside the busiest gates before the ground inside the wall was completely built up.

Who lived outside the wall differed between China's commercial cities, where the wall had stopped mattering, and Japan's castle towns.   • How many lived outside the wall of Kaifeng, the Song dynasty's capital?
    About 150,000 to 200,000, by a modern estimate. In 1021 the ground outside the wall was reorganized into nine districts because so many had settled there; a doctoral study of the city's layout counts fourteen wards in those districts, few because the outskirts held many imperial and private gardens, and from those wards the population is estimated.
  • Suzhou's busiest commercial district lay round its Changmen gate, much of it outside the wall.
    Under the Ming and Qing the district round the Changmen was once the most flourishing commercial quarter in China, its streets radiating outward outside the wall as well as one running inside it, with shops of every trade numbering in the tens of thousands.
  • A dictionary defines guanxiang, the ground outside a city gate, as a place, not a market.
    It gives the streets outside a city gate and the area nearby, in English a suburb outside the city gate; the entry says nothing about trade, so the word alone does not make a gate suburb a market quarter like Suzhou's.
  • Japan's castle towns were at first unwalled, and later enclosures brought the town inside.
    In the early castle towns only the lord's castle was surrounded by a moat and wall. As the towns grew in economic and political value they needed protecting, and more of them came to be enclosed whole by a moat and earthworks, the sogamae.
