Urban fabric: the research behind the frontage and row-packing rules

The findings behind the streets, the frontage, the housing and the fire defense of these cities, the decisions they drove, and - where no generator draws the feature yet - the rules a map follows.

Urban commoners built in continuous street walls

Sources: kyomachiya-jawiki, kyototuu-unagi, nagaya-jawiki

  • What the history says. Urban commoners in both reference cultures did not build detached-with-yard. Machiya street frontage was continuous - party walls or touching eaves (unsourced), because street frontage was taxed and precious;1 back-lot3 nagaya were literally ONE ROOF over a row of family units;2 Chinese county-seat courtyard housing shared rammed-earth or brick party walls in continuous street walls (unsourced). Detached-with-yard was a samurai (and rural) form (unsourced).

The frontage tax is disputed. "street frontage was taxed" is the popular explanation of the narrow lot (kyototuu-unagi carries it), and ja.wikipedia 京町家 disputes it outright - Edo-period Kyoto assessed a lump sum per town, not per frontage.4 The continuous built edge is read either way; the tax is not the reason to cite.

The rule the map follows: a city's commoner housing - the laborer, servant and burakumin dwellings and the small merchant houses - goes down as contiguous terraces rather than as separate buildings standing in their own clearings. At least 55% of those dwellings touch a neighbor, and the median gap from one to its nearest neighbor is no more than 6 ft ; the touching units in a row keep a hairline seam between their outlines because that is what they were, independent houses grown together. Back-to-back rows keep the real eave and drainage gap of about 3 to 6 ft, an idobata court opens every second row, and the block's wells are drawn before the rows so the terraces break into courts around them; the quarter's alleys go down first and are fronted tightly, while a real street keeps a band clear for the shop frontage. Obstacles break a terrace naturally and the occasional gap is honest, but it is not the default texture. The samurai quarter is the exception and keeps detached compounds with yards - the one district that did build detached, and the contrast that makes the terraces read.

The departure the sheet discloses: a true row shared party walls, a gap of zero, and the drawn market frontages do not. On the capital's gate markets the median gap between neighboring shops is 18 ft , which is the entry below measured on the sheet, so that a reader can still count individual buildings and see which way each one faces. Inside a residential terrace the units touch; it is the frontage rows that carry the wider joint. The relative sizes stay honest - only the mortar joint is drawn wider than life.

Machiya row density: a commercial street is a continuous built edge

Sources: kyototuu-unagi (frontage ~2 ken, depth 10-12 ken),5 kyomachiya-jawiki, hiyokechi-jawiki (breaks at block scale after 1657).6 See the frontage-tax note in the entry above

The question (GM, 2026-08-11), looking at Shiro Daika's north gate market: "Is that the correct amount of space between gate market buildings? No objection, they just look more spaced out than I expected."

What the research says. The Japanese urban shophouse (machiya) was built to the street and to its neighbors. Frontage was the taxed and traded dimension, so lots were narrow and deep:10 a Kyoto kyo-machiya typically fronted 2-3 ken12 (about 12-18 ft) on a lot running 60 ft or more back,11 and the fronts abutted, sharing party walls and a continuous eave line down the block (unsourced). The street read as a WALL of shopfronts pierced by gateways and alley mouths, not as a row of detached boxes (unsourced). Post-fire policy in Edo cut firebreaks (hiyokechi) and broad avenues THROUGH the fabric13 and pushed tile roofing (unsourced), but it never spaced individual machiya apart - the breaks were at block scale, and the block itself stayed solid.14

The decision. Shops in a market row stand shoulder to shoulder along the street they front, and a row may front a street that bends.

The deliberate departure from literal reality. True machiya shared party walls - a gap of ZERO. We land at a median of 18 ft on the capital's north and south gate markets (9 ft on the crowded southwest strip, 48 ft on the loose roadside market at the east gate, which is a different thing and should stay loose). An 18 ft gap is enough that a reader can still count individual shops and see which way each one faces, which is the whole job of the drawing. The relative sizes stay honest; only the mortar joint is drawn wider than life.

A county seat's street share, open reserve and civic share

Sources: chang-morphology-walled-capitals (the street grids, the intramural farmland, and Jinan's lake at a fifth of the walled area;7 the further anchors - Quanzhou a quarter vacant in 1945, Suzhou's intramural farmland, Pingyao's "4 big streets, 8 small streets, 72 lanes" - are unsourced)

A Chinese county seat ran a sparse street net - one or two gate-to-gate trunks in a cross or T with the drum tower at the crossing,8 everything else lanes - at roughly 10-20% of the walled ground, and normally enclosed 25-30% (the figure unsourced) deliberately unbuilt ground:16 walls were sized to administrative rank and growth,15 and intramural fields, gardens and ponds were siege insurance9 and flood refuge (unsourced). Civic buildings alone took only a few percent (unsourced); the big open consumer was the drill ground (unsourced) and the under-built remainder. The maps draw streets at ~7% (deep blocks, alley warrens) and admit open ground only as a declared, drawn line (agricultural district, drill ground, gardens), never as ambient slack.

Where does the Imperial road run through a city, and where is it labeled?

Sources: the GM's setting notes for the Imperial roads and for who maintains a city street; chang-morphology-walled-capitals (what most surviving Chinese city plans do with the street between two opposite gates)

An Imperial road is Imperial property and an Imperial responsibility. The same roadway inside a city's walls is a city street, which the city maintains. That is the whole reason the label sits outside the gate: the words name a jurisdiction, and at the gate the jurisdiction changes.

The straight gate-to-gate run is the setting's form rather than the usual historical one, and the entry below on a county seat's street net says why. The regular Chinese models put a cross or a T of trunk streets between the gates, but most surviving plans deliberately deflect the trunk so that no street runs uninterrupted from one gate to the opposite one.38 A road the Empire drove through the country, with the city grown against it, runs straight through: a deviation, taken knowingly.

The rule the map follows: where a city stands on the Imperial road, the road runs straight north to south through it, gate to gate, and off both edges of the sheet; with the implied east-west axis it quarters the interior. The label "Imperial Road" is placed beyond a gate, outside the walls, never on the stretch between them. No other road on a settlement map is labeled at all.

What lines the Imperial road where it runs through the city? A ribbon of shops

Sources: the GM's setting notes for a provincial city's merchant and artisan households; this project's calibration against the three drawn provincial cities

The through-road is a city's prime frontage: the caravans and the travelers pass along it, so both its sides carry shops and traveler lodging rather than the backs of houses or bare ground. A city with no Imperial road keeps its commerce in the market district instead. The stretch that matters is the part running through the built city - bounded by the wall where there is one, by the footprint of the buildings where there is not - and the fault to watch for is a dead stretch between two quarters that both turn their backs on the road.

A provincial city's merchant and artisan households are about a quarter of the total, the highest commercial share of any tier in the setting, which is what makes a ribbon of that density affordable in households as well as in ground.

The rule the map follows: a city on the Imperial road, walled or not, lines it with shops, merchant houses and inns at roughly one commercial building per 390 ft of the stretch running through the city, and lays them as a true two-sided market street rather than a single row. That figure is a floor that catches a bare spine, calibrated against the three drawn cities and not derived from a source. Across the whole city a healthy commercial face is about one storefront per 25 to 30 inhabitants - about 105 shop, merchant and inn frontages for a city of 3,000 - and a road city sits at the denser end of that band, with the difference concentrated on the ribbon. A domain capital, which always has an Imperial road, always carries one.

Does the burakumin quarter stand inside the walls, or outside? At least one stands inside

Sources: the GM's setting notes for the burakumin caste and its trades

The burakumin handle what a city cannot do without and will not touch: the corpses, the hides and the leather, the sandals, the executions. A city shut up under siege needs those hands as much as it needs its granary, so however many burakumin live on the cheaper ground outside the wall, a neighborhood of them always sits within it.

The rule the map follows: a walled provincial city draws at least one burakumin neighborhood inside the walls. Not all of a city's burakumin need be inside - extramural rows are usual - and the intramural neighborhood is the floor, not the whole.

How do a city's streets make a grid?

Sources: this project's calibration against the three drawn provincial cities

A quarter's grid is a few crossing streets - two running north-south against two running east-west, nine blocks, is a good unit - and then a fill dense enough that the buildings themselves line the streets. Density is what makes a grid read: the failure is a sparse fill that leaves a stretch of street with nothing along it, so a quarter is packed to its real per-caste counts rather than to a comfortable spacing. None of the tolerances below is a historical finding. Each is the threshold at which a drawn street stops reading as a street, calibrated against the three drawn cities.

The rule the map follows - lining: no larger street runs a stretch longer than about 420 ft with its nearest building more than about 175 ft off it, because roadside land on a commercial or residential through-street is the prime ground of the city and a wide bare margin reads as an oversight. Narrow gravel alleys threading a block interior are exempt, and so is the government avenue, whose frontage is the spaced ministry compounds rather than shops and houses. Two streets are never drawn on the same line - a doubled corridor pushes the buildings out of fronting range.

The rule the map follows - joining: the streets are one connected network wired to the Imperial road, and two lanes count as joined only where their paved beds actually touch: a lane that stops even a roadbed short of the next one is a separate network, so each quarter's road-facing street is run out until it meets the road. A ward gate does not break a run - the centerline goes on under it. Lanes never come almost together without meeting: a pair closing to within about 6 to 90 ft without crossing, and a lane overshooting a crossing by a sliver of about 9 to 150 ft , both read as mistakes - so lanes either cross and run on, or end cleanly at the junction, and the two outermost streets of a grid end at their shared corner rather than each running past the other. Two lanes of any kind heading straight at each other - within about 37 degrees of aligned, less than about 240 ft apart, with clear ground between - simply connect; a gap that a building, a ward fence or a wall genuinely blocks is no fault, because there the lane is correctly routing around something.

The rule the map follows - drawing: where two lanes cross, the wider is drawn over the narrower - the Imperial road over the city streets it crosses, a street over an alley - and a junction is drawn as a crossroads: every lane's dark curb goes down before any lane's paved bed, so the beds merge into one continuous junction instead of one lane's curb ruling a line across the other's mouth. Walls are drawn over the lanes: a road, street or alley that meets the city rampart or an internal ward fence passes under it, and breaks it only at a gate, where the wall itself carries a real gap so the roadway shows through the opening. These are map drawing conventions - how the sheet reads, not how the ground was built.

Where do the shops stand, and where does the poor housing go?

Sources: the first entry on this page for the street-front shop and the back-lane tenement; the GM's setting notes for the merchant and laborer wealth bands; this project's calibration against the three drawn provincial cities

The pattern is the one the first entry already quotes: the middling and better merchant houses kept an independent shop on the main street, while the rest of the townsfolk and the craftsmen rented in the back lanes. So a street grid cuts a quarter into blocks, the businesses line the paved streets, and the residential majority jams the deep interior, reached by alleys rather than by streets; that those alleys were unpaved gravel or plank rests on general reading and on no page read.39 The more mercantile the quarter, the more streets it wants.

The two commoner quarters are drawn as opposites on purpose. Merchant housing is varied and roomy - the very rich behind compound walls, the rich in large houses, the rest in small ones - while the laborers' is uniform, small and jammed, with a clear minority of wealthier master laborers taking the prime back-street lots. The wealth bands themselves are the setting's own, not a finding of ours: of a provincial city's merchant households about 8% are very rich, 12% rich, and the remaining 80% poor or middling, and about one laborer household in eight is a master laborer.

The rule the map follows - frontage: almost every shop and merchant building stands within about 255 ft of a street, and most laborer and burakumin dwellings do not - they pack the block cores, off the street, which is where the alleys reach them. The exception is the larger master-laborer home, drawn at 6 to 20% of the laborer stock (a generous band around the setting's one-in-eight, because the big homes are limited to the frontage there is room for) and placed on back-street frontage with room around it. Samurai may front their streets; a merchant building counts as a business and as a dwelling at once. Merchant homes are spread: their median gap to the nearest neighbor is at least 1.3 times the laborers', so a merchant quarter is not cut into shallow strips by too many streets - the walled compounds need deep block cores.

The rule the map follows - alleys: an alley is held to a street's rules - it never runs over a building and never crosses the wall or the moat - and it earns its length by uniquely serving dwellings: each dwelling is credited only to the lane nearest it, at about one uniquely served dwelling per 90 ft of the alley's own length. That fails a lane running off into a half-empty corner and a redundant lane laid beside one that already serves the block, both. The rule of thumb is one lane per block: a block interior wants a single spine, not a cross-arm the spine already reaches, and never an alley shadowing a street a few tens of feet away. An alley continuing past its last building out to a ward gate is providing gate access rather than block service, so that terminal stretch is not counted against it. And no large cluster of dwellings - more than about thirty - may sit with no street or alley within about 285 ft : every deep block, including any space-filling band between two quarters, is laced with alleys so that each dwelling can be reached.

Why are there only one or two walled merchant compounds in a city of three thousand?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-23; the GM's setting notes for a provincial city's merchant households; nagayamon-kotobank (the gate whose form followed its holder's rank and office), myoji-taito-kotobank (the surname granted to commoners, and what a domain took for it)

A city of about 3,000 holds a dozen or so very rich merchant families, and most of them could pay for a wall and a gate. One to three of them have one. The GM ruled on 2026-07-23 that a gated compound is not something wealth can buy: it is a right the daimyo explicitly grants to a particular family, of a piece with the other granted merchant privileges - an audience with the daimyo at the New Year's celebration, permission to carry a surname down the generations without being samurai. So the wall marks legal standing rather than money, and a very rich merchant without the grant keeps a large unwalled house like anyone else's, only bigger.

The rule has a real counterpart, and it runs through the GATE. The nagayamon - the gatehouse with a range of rooms to either side of the doorway - was what expressed a warrior residence's formal rank, and its form and structure were laid down by the house rank of the daimyo or bannerman who held it. In the villages it was permitted only to the upper stratum of farmers who served as village officials, and among townsmen to the town elder; on the commoner version there is no guard post, and the flanking rooms are men's rooms, attendants' rooms, sheds and stables, which is what a merchant compound's gate range would in fact hold.17 The companion privilege in the paragraph above is myoji-taito, the surname and the sword. It was a warrior privilege that was at times permitted to commoners too - country samurai, headmen of special pedigree, town elders and purveyor merchants, and besides them filial sons and persons of special merit - and it came in several forms, the surname only, the sword only, some for a single generation and some in perpetuity, and a domain could grant it only to those within its own territory.41 Licensing it was one of the ways the early modern state reached into the localities,42 which is the setting's own reasoning about privilege.

Where the setting departs from it. The load-bearing clause is the one the record contradicts. From the middle of the early modern period the domains, in financial distress, issued the qualification indiscriminately to peasants and townsmen in exchange for monetary contributions, and a large contribution stands beside social merit and administrative service as one of the recognized grounds for the grant.43 It remained the lord's grant and never became simply a purchase, which is the setting's structural point and holds; but "not something wealth can buy" is a deviation this setting takes from the later part of that record rather than a finding about it. What no page read does is attach the money to a WALL or a GATE: the gate sources tie those to rank and to office alone.

The count is rolled per city rather than chosen, one compound 30% of the time, two 40%, three 30%, from the map's own seed. The two cities drawn before the ruling had each hand-placed exactly one, a pattern copied from the first with no reasoning recorded behind it; a rolled count is what keeps two cities honestly different from each other.

The rule the map follows: a provincial city draws one to three walled merchant compounds, the number rolled 30/40/30 from the map's seed, each in the deep core of a block - well inside the rampart, clear of the moat, and off the temple neighborhood - rather than jammed against the wall. A compound's court is treated as a compound for clearance, not just the house inside it, so it overlaps no temple, ministry, house or other court. The rest of the very rich merchants take large houses. A town places its compounds by hand and rolls nothing.

Where may a merchant's compound wall stand? On dry, private ground

Sources: the GM's doctrine of 2026-07-19

The temptation when placing a rich merchant's compound is to run its wall out to the water the merchant's money comes from, or across whatever public ground happens to be free. The GM's doctrine of 2026-07-19 is that it may do neither. A wall footed in water is undermined, and a canal or a dock edge is working waterfront - the boats, the porters, the towpath that makes the merchant rich - so a private wall may come up to the quay but never stand in it.18 The fire watch is municipal: its tower needs its own footing, daylight around the braced frame and a public way for the watchman to reach it, so it can be neither built into a private wall nor shut inside a private court. And a street, an alley or a road is the public way: a compound wall may line one - that is what a walled compound on a block looks like - but the cleared band stays open.

The rule the map follows: the whole perimeter of a merchant compound, the gate gap included, stands clear of canal, dock basin, moat, river and pond; clear of every fire tower, and never closed around one; and out of the cleared band of every street, alley, road and the ring road, though it may run alongside any of them. The compound's gate opens onto open ground and never into the face of the building next door - a wall that abuts a temple turns its gate to the free side. Water and fire towers are therefore drawn before the compounds. Town and city tiers both.

Which way does a city house's door face, and how deep do the rows stack?

Sources: the GM's doctrine of 2026-07-18; townhouse-jawiki (the ridge-split back-lane pair and where its shared well and privy stood), machiya-shoka-jawiki (the one gate between two front shops, and how many rows a frontage bought), roji-jawiki (the width of the lane, and the widening at the shared well)

A farmhouse faces south wherever it stands - its dooryard garden and its threshing ground want the sun, and that orientation is canon, so it is exempt everywhere. A city house has no sun constraint. What it must have instead is an entrance that opens onto ground a person can walk on - a street, a roji, a court - and never into the back wall of another house an eave-gap away - which rests on the GM's doctrine and on general reading; no source is cited.19

The depth rule follows from the door rule rather than standing beside it. Terrace rows come in back-to-back PAIRS with both fronts outward, and the form has a name: the back-lane tenement was mune-wari, ridge-split along the lane, one roof over two ranks that share a rear wall and open to opposite sides, each unit nine shaku by two ken with a four-and-a-half-mat room and a mat and a half of earth floor for the stove and the sink.20 A three-deep stack cannot occur, because the middle row would be walled on both faces and those households would have no way out to a lane; and it did not occur, because the whole block of tenements was entered through one wooden gate set between two front shops and along the lane behind it, and because what a plot's frontage bought was one row along the lane or two facing rows, never a third rank behind them - about three ken of shop frontage gave one side, about five gave both.44 The lane widens where the shared well stands, immediately inside the back of the merchant house or at the lane's middle, and that widening is where a shared well or water supply was put.45

How wide is the lane, and how wide the map draws it. The roji between one back-lane building and the next was three to six shaku, 0.9 to 1.8 m - about 3 to 6 ft, a path so private that almost nobody but the residents of the buildings it served used it.46 That is the historical width, and it is a quarter to a half of the 12 ft this record had for it. These maps draw the roji at 12 ft and continue to: at three feet to the pixel a true roji is one to two pixels wide, and a lane that thin neither reads as a way nor carries a caption, so the drawn width is a map drawing convention - the way is widened so that a reader can see there is one. The real gap between two eaves is 3 to 6 ft.

The rule the map follows: every city dwelling's door opens onto at least 7 real ft of clear ground, at every map scale - the line sits between the eave band, about 6 ft or less, which is for drainage rather than for entering, and the walkable band of about 10 ft. Rows stack at most two deep. In a street-fronting pair the front row faces the street and the row behind it faces the back lane, the ura-dana pattern; a house standing on its own takes whichever cardinal side is open. The farmhouse, facing south, is exempt. Town and city tiers both.

How many cheap lodging houses did a city hold?

Sources: chang-morphology-walled-capitals (the suburb of inns and traffic trades that grew outside a busy gate), song-society-enwiki (when the dusk curfew over the wards ended); the GM's decision of 2026-07-24. Where a claim below carries no note, it rests on this project's general reading and has not been checked against a page a reader can open.

WHERE the cheap lodging stood is answerable: it clustered in the suburb outside a busy gate along with the rest of the traffic trades, which is the guanxiang pattern the extramural rows already draw.21 How MANY such houses a settlement held is not answerable. That the ruling class did not count or record the lodging of the poor, and that the gazetteers which survive foreground the official post-stations instead, rests on general reading; no source is cited.22 Nor is the lodging at the gate because a curfew stranded late travelers outside: the ward gate shut at dusk is a Tang institution, and it was gone well before the later period these cities are modeled on - the curfew was abolished in 1063 and the marketplaces of Kaifeng were then open every hour of the day, where a strict curfew from dusk had held over the two official markets of Tang Chang'an.23 It is at the gate because that is where the traffic is.

So the symmetric count these maps draw - one cheap lodging house inside the walls and one just outside every gate - is a GUESS, and a deliberate stylization on the generous side of a range nobody can bound. The GM decided it on 2026-07-24: a setting more attentive to its peasantry than its historical models are, drawn so that the provision reads at a glance. Two alternatives were priced and declined: concentrating the lodging on the busiest gate alone, and drawing a distinct animal-yard cart-inn compound.

The rest of the lodging program is deliberately invisible. A highway seat of about 3,000 that is not a post-station would run something like eight to fifteen lodging houses all told: the two to four proper inns and two to four cheap houses the map gives their own glyphs, plus five to eight small family hatago that were not distinct buildings at all - ordinary shophouses whose back rooms took lodgers, the household living on the meal trade, a field and porterage with lodging as the add-on, and on a procession night the whole town became the billet.24 So several of the road-market and gate-market shop rows are understood to sleep travelers, and nothing on the map counts them.

The rule the map follows: a provincial city draws one cheap lodging house inside the walls, and one within about 1,560 ft outside every gate. The one inside stands in a humble quarter - the laborer section, or the agrarian sector where a city has one - never within about 330 ft of the temple, merchant or samurai neighborhoods, and never within about 450 ft of the burakumin quarter. Its one exception is the gate caravan cluster below, where it properly stands beside an inn and a stables rather than alone.

What does a city keep for the caravans, the market crowd and the festival?

Sources: the town tier's own entries for a market center's amenities; tokaido-qa-mlit (what a post station was obliged to keep standing), sukego-kotobank (where the animals came from when that was not enough); this project's calibration against the three drawn provincial cities

A provincial city is a bigger version of the market center a town already is, so the town's civic furniture ports up rather than changing in kind: fireproof kura behind the merchant houses, market-day lodging, a theater stage in a temple precinct. Three town rules do not port up. A city has no farmer plurality and no farmers living inside it; it has temples rather than a monastery with a clan patron; and its wall is a regular defensive ring rather than the irregular run a town's arc may take.

What a city adds is scale at the gate. A wagon train arriving at or passing through a city brings many dozens of draft animals - oxen and packhorses - with their guards, porters and drivers, and the order of magnitude is documented from the Japanese post road: each Tokaido station was first made to keep thirty-six horses standing, and from 1638 a hundred post horses and a hundred porters, the requirement rising as the traffic did.25

The contrast the map's gate cluster is drawn from - that a town inn can absorb that into a small stable yard and a city cannot - is this record's own, and the readable evidence cuts against it. Those post stations were themselves towns, a hundred horses standing was routinely not enough, and the answer was never a bigger yard: the neighboring villages supplied men and horses in support, at two men and two horses per hundred koku of assessed yield, and the ring they were drawn from began at two or three ri around the station and was widened past ten.47 So the bottleneck is real and documented; what is unsupported is that a city meets it worse than a town does.

The rule the map follows: a provincial city draws at least five merchant storehouses. Just inside each gate stands a caravan cluster - a cheap lodging house, a prominent inn and a large stables, all within about 1,020 ft of the gate - with open ground around the stables for tying up and penning, which means no more than four dwellings within about 225 ft of it; the fencing itself is not drawn. A city carries a theater stage, adjacent to a temple as a town's is, and its viewing ground is wider than a town's, about 555 ft across . The counts and the distances are calibrated against the three drawn provincial cities rather than derived from a source.

How did a dense wooden city watch for fire?

Sources: fires-in-edo-enwiki (what fire cost the largest wooden city there was), hinomi-yagura-jawiki (what a fire-watch tower was for, and how far it spread), hansho-kotobank (the alarm bell and the meaning of its strokes), machibikeshi-jawiki (who manned, paid for and commanded the townsman fire companies), takayama-jinya-city (what survives on the ground of the one surviving provincial office), dongjing-menghualu-wikisource (the Song capital's fire-watch tower and the posts around it), hinomi-yagura-kotobank (how many machi a tower served, and what the machi between had), chinanews-ancient-firefighting (the night watch and the volunteer companies of a Chinese county town), jinya-kotobank (what an intendant's compound held, and the town that grew round it), jishinban-jawiki (the ladder and bell on the watch house, and the gear inside), hyoshigi-jawiki (the night round and its call), tensuioke-jawiki (the stored water), dozo-jawiki (what makes a plastered storehouse fireproof), gogura-jawiki (the store the tax rice passed through); the GM's question of 2026-07-24. Where a claim below carries no note, it rests on this project's general reading and has not been checked against a page a reader can open.

From a tower, and only where the roofs touch. A premodern Japanese city burned constantly - Edo was called the City of Fires, and the Meireki fire of 1657, the worst in the city's history, claimed up to 107,000 lives26 - and the institution these maps draw is the fire-watch tower, the hinomi-yagura (火の見櫓): a raised lookout platform, kept for spotting a fire early and calling out the brigade, carrying an alarm bell at its top, the hansho (半鐘),27 which the watchman struck in a cadence that told the quarter how near the fire was.28 The terraces the first entry on this page describes are what make the tower necessary: packed wooden rows burn together, so the fire furniture on these maps is not decorative.

Why a walled town and a city carry one, and an open town does not. Two reasons reinforce each other. Fire is catastrophic where the roofs touch, and that is the packed intramural warren; an open road-town strung along a highway, with fields running off every edge, has natural breaks - the road itself, the gaps between holdings, the open ground all round - so a standing tower does not earn its cost.29 And the rampart that keeps raiders out keeps a blaze in, so the watch over the rooftops is wanted exactly where there is a wall.

The unwalled seat is the confirming case, not an exception. The GM asked on 2026-07-24 whether an unwalled tax-administration seat should carry a tower. Its real-world analog is the jin'ya or daikansho town, and the identification is the published one: the official residence in a district administered by a shogunal district governor or intendant went by the name daikan-jin'ya, its grounds held the intendant's own residence, the office for public business, the houses of the subordinate staff, the rice store and the levy store, and a judgment yard and gaol; where a settlement grew up around such a compound and became a town it was called a jin'ya-machi, and often flourished as the central town of the territory administered.40 That town's fire posture was defensive rather than observational, and the neighborhood watch house is where its apparatus stood. Many watch houses were fitted with a ladder on the roof - a small-scale fire tower, in the account's own words - and a hansho bell, and the firefighting gear was kept inside: the company standard, the fire hook, the hand pump, the buckets, ladders and well-buckets, so that when the bell was struck the town officers and the firefighting hands ran to the watch house, took the gear out, assembled and went to the fire together.30 Where a frame was raised on the watch house so that the watchman could look over the whole town, that raised platform IS the hinomi-yagura.48 The night round was walked calling "mind your locks, mind your fire" and striking clappers, though the bodies the readable account names for it are modern ones.49 Water was stored against fire in the tensuioke, the traditional cistern that held rainwater and was used mainly as urban firefighting water in the Edo period.50 The plastered kura is the fire-resistant form: storehouses are named for what they hold, a rice store among them, and are built to protect it from fire, damp and theft, their walls often 300 mm thick or more and their outer doors faced in earth and plaster, so that in the great fires of the Edo period there were many cases in which the fire never reached the inside.51 The tax rice had a store of its own, the gogura, one to a village or to several, a holding place for the rice on its way out and kept under strict guard.52 That losing the tax rice was the career-ending disaster, and that the storehouse was therefore fireproofed rather than the horizon watched, is this record's own reading of those facts. Two pieces of the posture rest on nothing readable: the local temple bell doubling as the alarm, and any of this standing at the daikansho OFFICE rather than at the town's watch post.53 Takayama Jin'ya, the Hida daikansho, is the one such office of more than fifty still standing, and it is standing because no fire ever took it; what its ground keeps is the trace of a single water-supply pond, and a grain storehouse framed in Keicho-era hinoki that is among the foremost in the country.31 The freestanding tower itself is no late arrival: it was built up together with the firefighting system, beginning with Edo in the Edo period, and by the early Showa era towers stood in almost every region of the country.32 What that dates is the SPREAD, and the reading this record takes from it is its own: the institution starts in the shogunal capital, the densest wooden city there was, and reaches the ordinary countryside last, so an unwalled country seat of the period we draw stands at the beginning of that spread rather than at its end.

China agrees at the dense end. Song Kaifeng set a fire-watch tower, the wanghuolou, on high ground and built it of brick, with men keeping lookout on top of it and more than a hundred soldiers quartered below with buckets, swabs, axes and saws, ladders, fire-forks, ropes and grapnels, and a patrol post of five soldiers about every three hundred paces through the wards and lanes besides.33 Below the capital an organized fire WATCH is attested, though not the tower. Ming government at the base level put a headman in charge of fire and theft: the county town of Wanping in Beijing was organized into wards and posts, each post with a head and three to five firemen under him, who kept watch by night in turn, striking clappers and ringing bells to remind the inhabitants to beware of fire and theft, and some places also set up a patrol post where the watch sheltered from rain and snow and where knives and spears, fire-hooks and buckets were kept. In 1743 the governor of Hunan instructed every prefecture and county to organize volunteer fire companies, registered on rolls, reported to the authorities and paid a fixed sum of silver each year.34 What no page read attests below the capital is the brick lookout TOWER itself. A Chinese county seat was walled in any case, so it falls on the near side of the same line.

Whose watch it is - a deviation, taken deliberately. The famous townsman fire brigades, the machi-bikeshi "iroha" companies, are a peace-era institution of the Tokugawa century: the South Town Magistrate Ooka Tadasuke issued the ordinance that organized them in 1718, and by 1720 there were forty-seven iroha companies covering the ground west of the Sumida and sixteen more for Honjo and Fukagawa east of it, one company to about twenty blocks, manned by the blocks and paid for by them but placed under the town magistrate's command.35 So the difference our setting takes is narrower than the phrase "townsman brigade" suggests, because command sat with the magistrate in the historical case too; what changes is who climbs the tower. In a setting pitched earlier and more martial the watch is the magistrate's own people - their yoriki, doshin and ashigaru - rather than townsmen enrolled block by block. On an unwalled seat the fire story is carried instead by the kura on the magistrate's compound, the headman's house and the merchants' houses; a small hansho bell-frame at the magistrate's compound would be the one defensible extra glyph, and drawing nothing at all is equally accurate.

Why more than one tower spreads out. The watch was parceled out by neighborhood, though not one tower to each block-group: in the Kyoho era one fire tower was provided to every ten machi, and a machi without one had a ladder and a bell fitted on the roof of its own watch hut, while the shogunate's standing fire watch kept fire-brigade mansions at several places in the castle town, each carrying a tower with watchmen on it day and night, and the townsmen set a watch house in every machi and a gate watch at the machi boundaries.36 So the watch STATION was per-machi and the tower was one to about ten of them, which is the same figure the capitals page gives for Edo: the two pages say the same thing. Towers spread out because a tower's useful watch is its own visual neighborhood of rooftops. A comfortable distance between two towers is not enough to get that right, which the drawing itself showed: a first pair stood about 1,020 ft apart, a generous gap, and both of them northwest of center, which left the city's largest rooftop mass - the northeast laborer warren - with no tower amid it at all. So the dispersal rule is written in two parts, a floor between towers and a requirement that each stand amid the district it is nearest tower for.

Two things about the drawn tower are map drawing conventions rather than history. A single tower stands in for a quarter's whole fire watch, where a real city kept many. And the glyph is an open braced frame with a red bell, so that it reads apart from the solid guard towers on the rampart: a fire tower is a civilian structure in the interior watching the roofs, not a work of the wall.

The rule the map follows: a provincial city draws at least two fire towers and a walled town one; an open town draws none. Each stands in a commoner quarter, never the samurai ward, and on ground with sightlines - a cleared block or an open seam between building clusters - which is why the towers go down last, onto the seams the finished map already has. Where there is more than one they disperse: no two stand within one watch radius of each other, about 690 ft , and each stands amid the district it watches - every commoner dwelling inside the walls belongs to its nearest tower, and a tower sits within the larger of 0.9 of that district's radius and one watch radius of the district's center of mass, rather than at its edge with an unwatched quadrant behind it. In practice that is one tower amid each major commoner quarter, three for a city with laborer, servant and merchant quarters to cover. A tower keeps at least 15 ft of daylight all round its frame, and the same clear of every wellhead, because an open braced tower's footing and ladder stand off its neighbors' eaves and must never block the court the quarter draws its water from.

Why is there no firebreak on these maps?

Sources: tokyo-archives-hirokoji (what the cleared strips were for and what filled them); this project's two attempts at drawing one and the decision to draw neither; the second entry on this page for what the Edo breaks actually were

The other historical answer to urban fire was the firebreak - the hiyokechi (火除地), and the widened hirokoji (広小路): a cleared strip of ground a blaze could not jump. That the strip, being far too valuable to leave idle, filled up with removable stalls and theater booths and so doubled as the market and amusement ground, until Edo's hirokoji had become its entertainment districts, is what the city archive's own account says: most of the wider streets called hirokoji were laid out about the city as fire-clearance open ground, no permanent building could stand on one, and at busy bridgeheads and before the gates of well-visited temples and shrines they filled with movable stall-shops trading goods, with markets, with street performance and with reed-screened booths staging entertainments, so that many of them grew into pleasure quarters.37 The second entry on this page carries the scale at which those breaks were cut: whole blocks and broad streets, opened after a disaster, never gaps between individual houses.

Two ways of drawing one were tried, and both were dropped. A DRAWN plaza - a shaded clearing placed before the dwellings pack in - makes the packer flow houses around an invented clearing, and the result reads as a designed plaza, which is the opposite of what a firebreak is: the ABSENCE of building. A LABEL-ONLY marking - an annotation dropped onto a gap the finished map happens to leave - only names existing dead space; it adds a word rather than a feature, and getting the gaps and the label orientations to read well costs real effort for no gain. So a firebreak is not a feature of these maps, and the towers carry the fire story by themselves. That is an omission on the sheet, not a claim that these cities had no firebreaks.

The rule the map follows: no map draws a firebreak, and none marks a gap as one. A future map that genuinely needs one - a wide ceremonial fire-plaza that is itself the subject of the drawing - builds it as a real, deliberately placed feature with its own glyph; the marked-gap approach is not revived.
