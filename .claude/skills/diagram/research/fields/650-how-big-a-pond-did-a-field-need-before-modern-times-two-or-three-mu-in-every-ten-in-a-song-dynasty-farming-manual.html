How big a pond did a field need before modern times? Two or three mu in every ten, in a Song dynasty farming manual

Sources: chenfu-nongshu-juanshang (a pond dug for high fields, two or three mu in every ten), kotobank-chenfu-nongshu (the manual's date)

The question. The maps keep a reservoir pond's area in proportion to the paddy it waters, at about 2,000-2,500 cubic meters of storage for each irrigated hectare, a figure no page read gives (at How our maps size a settlement's farmland). Under the GM's ruling of 2026-09-28 that nothing attested only in modern times is drawn, is sizing a pond to its fields, or that figure, only modern?

What the record found. Sizing the pond to its fields is old; the figure was not found in any period.

The Song rule. Chen Fu's farming manual, completed in 1149, sizes the pond of a high field by the land: at the place where the high water gathers, one measures what is needed and digs a pond, giving up roughly two or three mu of every ten mu of field to store the water, its bank high and its middle deep and wide, broad enough to hold the rains that come at the turn of spring and summer. The measure is an area, not a volume: two or three tenths of the land goes to water, so the pond is a quarter to over two-fifths the size of the field it waters.

What was not found. No storage per hectare, and no ratio of pond to field in Japan before modern times, was found.

What it means for the map. Sizing a pond by the field it waters is accurate and stays. The storage figure per hectare is unsourced in any period and goes; the one figure from before modern times is Chen Fu's area. His pond is dug for high fields, broad enough to hold the rains that come at the turn of spring and summer. A pond that a stream keeps topping up, like the reservoir the maps draw above their fields, need hold less of the season's water itself - this record's reasoning, not a finding. Hoshigaoka's pond, about 1.5 ha for 31.8 ha of paddy (at How our maps size a settlement's farmland), is about a twentieth of the land, well under Chen Fu's two or three tenths. A pond that is its fields' only water is drawn to his measure; how much smaller a stream-fed pond may be is not on any page read, so the size the maps give it is a guess, held below his figure.
