Where a farming hamlet grew its coarse grain

A farming hamlet ate the rice of its paddy and, for about a third of its diet, coarse grain such as barley and millet (How much farmland a settlement works, and in what tracts). The grain grew in one of two places: on the paddy itself over the winter, where the paddy drained and could take a crop of barley after the rice, or in dry fields on higher ground (Dry fields and their crops (hatake)).

A hamlet at the toe of an alluvial fan stood where the fan's water comes back up as springs, and settlements and paddy have stood there since long ago. The fan's middle above it was often left wild until late, so such a hamlet had little dry ground to hand.

  • How much dry field did a hamlet need for its coarse grain?
    About two-thirds to three-quarters as much as its paddy, some 0.13-0.18 acre a person: a GUESS resting on assessed rates. The land surveys assessed a middle-grade paddy at 1 koku 3 to to a tan (a to is a tenth of a koku). In the Edo period an upper-grade dry field was assessed at 1 koku 2 to, each grade below it 2 to less, and from about the middle of the period at 1 koku 1 to, again 2 to less a grade. A middle-grade dry field is then 1 koku or 9 to a tan, so grain half as much as the rice needs that share of the paddy's ground: against the 0.20-0.25 acre of paddy that fed one inhabitant, 0.13-0.18 acre.
  • Those rates are assessments, not harvests.
    They are the surveys' statutory yields in rice-equivalent koku, not measured harvests of barley or millet, and the paddy's and the dry field's come from different surveys. Reading both at the middle grade is this record's own choice.
  • Where the paddy drained, a hamlet could grow its coarse grain on it over the winter.
    Double cropping, rice in summer and barley in winter, spread through the Kinai and western Japan in the Kamakura period, but barley will not grow on a wet paddy, so only a hamlet whose paddy drained could grow its grain on the same ground as its rice (The paddy through the rice year). No page we read gives a winter barley crop's yield.
  • Where the paddy lay bare over the winter, did a toe hamlet clear its grain fields up the fan?
    The dry ground beside a toe hamlet is the fan above it, and when a fan's middle was at last cleared, from the end of the Edo period, wheat-and-barley fields were among what it became. No page we read says a toe hamlet cleared the lower edge of its fan's middle for its grain, nor from which end the clearing went.
  • Island fields, dry plots raised inside the paddy, are recorded with cotton, mulberry and figs, not grain.
    Where paddy was opened on fans, terraces and levees, the ground was dug down to where water could reach it and the spoil was piled up in the paddy as dry field, the shimabatake. The mainstream view dates them to about the thirteenth century, and they are said to have been found all over the country. The crops the page gives as examples are cotton, mulberry and figs, changing from era to era, and no grain. That silence does not show that no grain grew there.
  • The dry fields on natural levees belong to the lowland below a fan, not to its toe.
    On an alluvial lowland the old settlements stood first on the natural levees, which were also dry fields; no page we read puts a toe hamlet's grain on one.
