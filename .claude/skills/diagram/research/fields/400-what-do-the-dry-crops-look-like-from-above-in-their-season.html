What do the dry crops - millet, buckwheat, barley and soybean - look like from above in their season?

Sources: omugi-jawiki (barley's year and its pale-brown ripening field), hakubaku-omugi-ichinen (the months barley heads and turns gold, and the rice-barley-soybean rotation), awa-jawiki and kibi-jawiki (the two millets' height and heads), soba-jawiki (buckwheat's height, flowers and short season), maruyanagi-daizu-hatake (a soybean field's height and its turn to yellow-brown), fukui-kenshi-noji (when the Edo farm year sowed and harvested them).

What the research found. The four crops differ less in color than in season and height, and which of them a plot shows depends on the month.

  • Barley is the winter crop. It is sown in autumn, stands through the winter as young plants, heads in spring and is cut in early summer, and from Kanto to Kyushu, the southern island, it was grown on the paddy itself as the crop after the rice. In the Edo calendar it went in between the autumn equinox and early October. Today it heads in April and turns gold in early to mid May, and is harvested around June. The ripening field is pale brown, like a rice field in autumn - hence the season word "barley autumn" for early summer. From above: a green sheet in winter and spring, tawny in May, stubble or bare ground by the time the paddies are planted.
  • Foxtail millet stands about 150 cm tall, its ears ripening yellow and drooping; it is sown in May or June and harvested from late September into October. Proso millet grows 1-2 m tall, its head drooping from summer into autumn like an ear of rice. From above: the tallest and coarsest of the dry crops, green through the summer and yellowing with drooping heads in autumn.
  • Buckwheat stands 60-130 cm, covered in small flowers about 6 mm across, white, pale red or red, on stems that are green, pale red or deep red; it is ready 70-80 days from sowing, and it has a spring-sown summer crop and a summer-sown autumn one. From above in flower: a field of white or pinkish flowers - the one dry crop that is not some shade of green or gold.
  • Soybean: one modern field's top leaves stood near 80 cm, and by September that field was turning from deep green to yellow-brown. Today many places put soybean after the barley that follows rice, three crops in two years; no page read here dates that rotation before modern times. In the Edo farm year soybean and buckwheat were harvested in the gaps between the autumn rice harvest and the threshing that followed it.

Rows. Whether a pre-modern dry field showed rows at all - sown in lines along furrows or ridges, or broadcast - is not on any page read; the modern guides sow in rows on ridges, and that is not carried back. The furrowed texture the maps give a dry plot, and the way neighboring plots turn their furrows (asked on its own), rest on that guess.

What it means for the map. The map is drawn in high summer, the season the paddy stands flooded and green (why the bund shows then). In that season, on this page's reading of the calendars above: barley is already cut, so a barley plot is stubble, bare, or - if today's rotation is carried back, a guess - already under soybean; the millets are tall, green and still heading; soybean is a low green bush; buckwheat is the one crop that can be in flower, white or pinkish, and the autumn sowing may be bare ground just sown. So the dry plots of a high-summer map differ by height and texture more than color, with bare or stubble plots and, rarely, a white buckwheat plot among them. The maps today give each crop its own color at its ripest - barley tan-gold, millet ochre, buckwheat pale with reddish stems, soybean green - which puts May's barley and October's millet beside July's flooded paddy. Whether to keep one season across the sheet or keep each crop at its most recognizable color is a drawing choice recorded as open, not made here.
