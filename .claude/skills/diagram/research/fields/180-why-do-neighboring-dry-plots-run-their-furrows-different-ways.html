Why do neighboring dry plots run their furrows different ways?

Sources: the fragmented-holdings finding in 'Plot sizes' above; the GM's catch of 2026-07; fao-aina-ridging (what contour ridging is for, and the ground it suits best), king-forty-centuries (a family's scattered plots), zuozhuan-chenggong (rows laid out by what suits the land, some south and some east), shijing-xinnanshan (the ode the chronicle quotes), zgkpw-longzuo (ridges shaped to the ground they are on)

Dry crops are ridge-and-furrow row-cultivated, unlike the flooded sheet of a paddy, so a dry plot shows rows and the direction of those rows is a visible choice. The uniform alternative runs EVERY plot's furrows along the contour, on the reasoning that a ridge laid along the contour dams the rain and checks runoff. That much is right - ridge-and-furrow aligned to the contour does serve erosion control and surface drainage together, and for a smallholder with hand tools or an ox it is named a satisfactory method. What is wrong is treating it as a steep-ground measure that a gentle valley margin escapes: the advantages of contour ridging are GREATER the less steep the terrain and the more permeable the soil, and the measures that belong to steep cultivated land are a different family - terraces, contour bunds, contour hedges, straw barriers and grass strips. So contour alignment is one supportable option on the ground these maps draw rather than a rule that stops at the valley margin. What the uniform alternative costs, and what the GM caught, is that every plot's rows come out identical, so adjacent family strips are indistinguishable from one another.

The land set the direction, tract by tract. The one East Asian passage read on which way field rows ran is in a classic, the Zuo Zhuan. In 589 BCE a victorious state made it a term of peace that, within the defeated state's borders, all the field rows be turned to run east. The envoy refused. The early kings, he said, had bounded and ordered the realm by examining what suited the land; as the Book of Songs has it, "south and east run its rows". To order every row east served only the victor's war chariots and paid no regard to what suited the land. The ode's own line reads the same. In the passage's plain sense, a tract's rows ran the way its land called for, and across a countryside some tracts ran one way and some the other, a right angle apart. A modern history of Chinese ridge culture says the same of the ridges themselves: in the rainy, low-lying south they were raised higher, with deeper furrows, to drain, and the practice as a whole was suited to the place.

Scattered holdings do not make every plot its own direction. A family's plots were seldom side by side, and might even be widely scattered. But the passage puts the direction in the land, and neighbors share their land. So plots on one slope, draining the same way, run alike, and the direction changes where the land changes; that is this page's reading of the passage, since no page read describes a later village's dry fields plot by plot. A genuinely steep or terraced village would be the case where every tract's land calls for the contour, so that the rows converge on it and the erosion-control reading is the correct one; no page read says how rows ran on steep or terraced dry fields, so that too is this page's inference, a GUESS.

What the map draws today, and what it should draw. Each dry plot now takes a furrow angle up to about 63 degrees either side of the contour, never straight downslope, dropped into the widest gap between the angles of the neighbors already placed, so that no two adjacent plots run within about six degrees of each other. That reads the strips apart, as the GM asked, but it has the wrong shape: it forbids the neighbors that share a direction, which the record makes the common case, and puts the change between every pair of plots rather than between tracts. That per-plot patchwork rests on the scattered holdings alone and is a GUESS.

The rule the map follows: the dry plots fall into tracts of neighboring plots on the same lie of land; the plots of a tract share one row direction, turned a few degrees from plot to plot, and the direction changes between tracts, by as much as a right angle. Each tract's direction comes from its own ground, along the contour or down to its outfall, never straight down a steep slope. The seams between tracts are what read the family strips apart. How many plots a tract holds, and how far a plot turns within one, are GUESSES.
