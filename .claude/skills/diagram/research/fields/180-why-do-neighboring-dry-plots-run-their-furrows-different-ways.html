Why do neighboring dry plots run their furrows different ways?

Sources: the fragmented-holdings finding in 'Plot sizes' above; the GM's catch of 2026-07; fao-aina-ridging (what contour ridging is for, and the ground it suits best), king-forty-centuries

Dry crops are ridge-and-furrow row-cultivated, unlike the flooded sheet of a paddy, so a dry plot shows rows and the direction of those rows is a visible choice. The uniform alternative runs EVERY plot's furrows along the contour, on the reasoning that a ridge laid along the contour dams the rain and checks runoff. That much is right - ridge-and-furrow aligned to the contour does serve erosion control and surface drainage together, and for a smallholder with hand tools or an ox it is named the satisfactory method. What is wrong is treating it as a steep-ground measure that a gentle valley margin escapes: the advantages of contour ridging are GREATER the less steep the terrain and the more permeable the soil, and the measures that belong to steep cultivated land are a different family - terraces, contour bunds, contour hedges, straw barriers and grass strips. So contour alignment is one supportable option on the ground these maps draw rather than a rule that stops at the valley margin; letting neighboring plots run their rows different ways is a drawing choice made so the strips read apart, not a correction of a misapplied erosion rule. What the uniform alternative costs, and what the GM caught, is that every plot's rows come out identical, so adjacent family strips are indistinguishable from one another.

The accurate look on gentle ground is the patchwork quilt. Holdings were fragmented, each household working several small non-contiguous plots, so neighboring strips were plowed to their own orientations. Each dry plot now takes a furrow angle up to about 63 degrees either side of the contour, never straight downslope, chosen by dropping it into the WIDEST gap between the angles of the neighbors already placed - which guarantees a real separation from each of them rather than hoping for one; no two adjacent dry plots run within about six degrees of each other, an angle being compared as an undirected line. A genuinely steep or terraced village is drawn the other way, by declaring a narrow spread so the rows converge back onto the contour, where the erosion-control reading is the correct one.
