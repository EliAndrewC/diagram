What the record found. The season is not; the depths are.

The season. The Qimin Yaoshu, the Chinese farming manual believed completed in 544, gives it for rice. When the seedlings are seven or eight cun tall the old weeds are cut under the water; as the rice grows it is weeded again; after the weeding the water is let out and the roots sunned to make them firm; the field is watered as the season runs wet or dry; and as the rice nears ripeness the water is taken off again. That is the shape of today's calendar: a drain in the growing season to firm the roots, water as the weather needs it, and a last drain before the harvest. Chen Fu's manual, completed in 1149 on the wet rice of the lower Yangtze, drains the paddies one at a time to weed them: the water is first stored at the topmost plot so that none is lost, then the plots are let dry from the bottom up and each is weeded as it dries. For a seedbed just sown he keeps the water shallow in sunshine so the sun warms it, but not so shallow that the mud's skin dries hard, nor so deep that the seed is soaked through and yellows; he adds a little in a downpour, lets it off in a sudden wind, and says a wide bund is what lets the depth be kept right. Warming the water is the reason today's guidance gives for keeping it shallow (at How deep the water actually stands).

The depths. Neither manual gives a depth, and no depth of paddy water from before modern times was found, in Chinese or in Japanese. The depths the record gives are modern extension guidance and nothing older.

What it means for the map. Nothing drawn depends on the depth. The paddy's season - flooded, drained to firm the roots, flooded again, drained before the harvest - is accurate, on a premodern record that is Chinese; the depths are modern, and are not given as history.
