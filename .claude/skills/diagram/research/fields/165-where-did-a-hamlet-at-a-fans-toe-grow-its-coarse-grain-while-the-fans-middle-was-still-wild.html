Where did a hamlet at a fan's toe grow its coarse grain while the fan's middle was still wild?

Sources: senjochi-jawiki (the springs and the old farmland at the toe), senjochi-kotobank (the middle cleared late, barley fields among what it became), kokumori-jawiki (the assessed rates for paddy and dry field), nimosaku-jawiki (barley on the paddy over the winter, and never on a wet one), shimabatake-jawiki (the island dry fields inside the paddy, and what grew on them), shizen-teibo-jawiki (the dry fields on a lowland's levees)

The paddy on the map is sized for the rice in the diet, and that leaves coarse grain - barley, millet and the like - about a third of what the hamlet eats (Acreage from population). On an alluvial fan whose middle is still wild, the strip of dry fields stands only along the toe's stretch of the fan's edge (Where dry crops go), which is far too little ground for that third. So where did it grow?

How much ground the grain needs. The toe is where the fan's water comes back up as springs, so settlements and farmland, in Asia paddy, have often stood there since long ago. Its paddy grew the rice, two-thirds of the diet. The land surveys assessed a middle-grade paddy at 1 koku 3 to a tan (a to is a tenth of a koku). In the Edo period an upper-grade dry field was assessed at 1 koku 2 to, each grade below it 2 to less, and from about the middle of the period at 1 koku 1 to, again 2 to less a grade. A middle-grade dry field is then 1 koku or 9 to a tan, so the grain third, half as much grain as the rice, needs two-thirds to three-quarters as much dry field as the hamlet has paddy: about 0.13-0.18 acre a person. These are assessed rates in rice-equivalent koku, not harvests of barley or millet, and the paddy and dry-field rates come from different surveys; reading both at the middle grade is this page's own choice, and the map takes the earlier, lower figure. It is a GUESS resting on attested rates.

Where it grew: on the paddy itself, over the winter, wherever the paddy drained. Double cropping, rice in summer and barley in winter, spread through the Kinai and western Japan in the Kamakura period, but barley will not grow on a wet paddy (Did a paddy grow a second crop over the winter?). A hamlet whose paddy drained could grow its coarse grain on the same ground as its rice. No page read gives a winter barley crop's yield, so the map counts a drained paddy's winter crop acre for acre against the dry field it spares, a GUESS.

Or in dry fields up the fan. Where the paddy lay bare over the winter, the grain needed dry fields, and the dry ground beside a toe hamlet is the fan above it. When a fan's middle was at last cleared, from the end of the Edo period, wheat-and-barley fields were among what it became. No page read says a toe hamlet cleared the lower edge of its middle for its grain, nor from which end the clearing went.

Not, on the pages read, in the island fields, on a levee or beside the houses. Island fields (shimabatake) are dry plots inside the paddy: where paddy was opened on fans, terraces and levees, the ground was dug down to where water could reach it and the spoil was piled up in the paddy as dry field. The mainstream view dates them to about the thirteenth century, and they are said to have been found all over the country. But the crops the page gives as its examples on them are cotton, mulberry and figs, changing from era to era, and no grain. Its silence does not show that no grain grew there, but nothing read puts it there, so the map does not. Dry fields on natural levees belong to the lowland below a fan, where the old settlements stood on the levees; nothing read puts a toe hamlet's grain on one. And no page read puts a household's grain in a plot beside its house (Where dry crops go).

What the map does. The winter-crop roll decides it, once per hamlet, and it is rolled only among the forms the hamlet's ground can feed. Where the paddy carries barley over the winter, its drained plots grow the grain and the strip of dry fields stays on the toe. Where it does not, the dry fields climb from the toe into the fan's wild middle, plot by plot, nearest the toe first, out as far from the canal as the ground runs, until they hold the grain the hamlet needs - the middle cleared as far as the grain required and no further. Taking the plots nearest the toe first is a GUESS. Bare winter paddy is offered only where the toe and the middle together can hold the whole need; on a fan whose middle is already cleared, the map draws that cleared ground only as the strip of dry fields along the fan's upper edge, and that strip cannot hold the whole need, so such a hamlet is always drawn growing its barley on the paddy. The island fields and the levees are not drawn for grain.
