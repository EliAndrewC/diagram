What is the Imperial chrysanthemum field, and did a town grow flowers for the market or the shrine?

Sources: the GM's setting notes (the chrysanthemum festival); kikka-monsho-jawiki (the flower as the imperial emblem), kiku-jawiki (the flower's arrival, the court festival, Edo-period breeding, offering flowers), toshima-somei and sugamo-kikumatsuri (the nursery villages on Edo's edge), katsushika-horikiri (the Horikiri iris gardens), mboso-hana (flowers sent from the country into Edo), chinagate-tongxiang (chrysanthemums as a Chinese medicinal crop).

What the map shows. Hirameki, a walled county seat, keeps a field of chrysanthemums inside its west rampart, and no public street is cut through it. The field is the setting's, not history's: the GM's notes make the chrysanthemum the flower of the ninth month's festival, when "Gardens, temples, shrines, and fine homes display fine specimens of the seasonal flowers", and the field is Hirameki's own, standing for the Emperor in a town that changed hands in war.

What the research found - the flower. In Japan the chrysanthemum became the imperial emblem by custom, after the retired emperor Go-Toba took it as his mark in the Kamakura period; it had come from China as a medicine and an ornament, the festival of the ninth day of the ninth month was kept until Meiji and lives on as an imperial garden party, and it has long been an altar and offering flower. Its great breeding began in the Edo period, with distinct lines in Edo, Ise, Kyoto and Kumamoto. Nothing read describes a planted field of chrysanthemums kept for a ruler or a court, so an Imperial field in a provincial town is a deviation - the setting's, and deliberate.

What the research found - flowers for the market. Around the largest city, yes. Somei village, now Komagome in Tokyo, was a home of nurserymen, who were named as a source of primroses, azaleas and chrysanthemums; Sugamo's chrysanthemum growing had three passing booms in the mid-to-late Edo period, and at their height some eighty nurseries. At Horikiri gardens for viewing irises began, it is said, at the end of the period; Horikiri-en, a later garden begun in the Meiji period, was turned into paddy in 1942 to grow food in the war. Flowers also came from farther out: the city page for the Boso peninsula tells of a record, which it does not name, that narcissus from there was sold to Edo's samurai houses and townhouses in the middle of the period, and the page reads the senior councillor Matsudaira Sadanobu's travel diary of 1792 as showing narcissus already grown in quantity around Hota and Motona; lilies for cut flowers and export bulbs followed from 1886, and the page dates the first success at flower growing in the Awa district to the Taisho period. In China the chrysanthemum is today a crop in its own right at Tongxiang in Zhejiang, its dried flower head one of the province's traditional medicinal herbs, sold across the country; tradition has officials of the Southern Song settling there and planting it in gardens.

What the record cannot say. Every market case read is on the edge of a great city - Edo - and none is of the ring of farmland around an ordinary provincial town; nothing read describes flowers grown for a shrine or a temple in a plot of their own; and no page gives the size of any historical flower field or plot. So a town's ring on these maps grows no flower plots, and the one field drawn is the Imperial one.

How big the field is drawn. Hirameki's field is about 220 by 340 ft, some 1.7 acres. That size is a guess: nothing read gives the size of a flower field to set it against.
