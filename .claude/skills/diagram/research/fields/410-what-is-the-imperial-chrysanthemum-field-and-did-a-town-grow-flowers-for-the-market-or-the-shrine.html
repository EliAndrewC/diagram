What is the Imperial chrysanthemum field, and did a town grow flowers for the market or the shrine?

Sources: the GM's l7r.md (the chrysanthemum festival); kikka-monsho-jawiki (the flower as the imperial emblem), kiku-jawiki (the flower's arrival, the court festival, Edo-period breeding, offering flowers), toshima-somei and sugamo-kikumatsuri (the nursery villages on Edo's edge), katsushika-horikiri (a flower garden on a village's low ground), mboso-hana (flowers sent from the country into Edo), chinagate-tongxiang (chrysanthemums as a Chinese field crop).

What the map shows. Hirameki, a walled county seat, keeps a field of chrysanthemums inside its west rampart, and no public street is cut through it. The field is the setting's, not history's: the GM's notes make the chrysanthemum the flower of the ninth month's festival, when "Gardens, temples, shrines, and fine homes display fine specimens of the seasonal flowers", and the field is Hirameki's own, standing for the Emperor in a town that changed hands in war.

What the research found - the flower. In Japan the chrysanthemum became the imperial emblem by custom, after the retired emperor Go-Toba took it as his mark in the Kamakura period; it had come from China as a medicine and an ornament, the court kept the chrysanthemum festival until Meiji, and it has long been an altar and offering flower. Its great breeding began in the Edo period, with distinct lines in Edo, Ise, Kyoto and Kumamoto. Nothing read describes a planted field of chrysanthemums kept for a ruler or a court, so an Imperial field in a provincial town is a deviation - the setting's, and deliberate.

What the research found - flowers for the market. Around the largest city, yes. Somei, a village on Edo's northern edge, was a nursery village whose growers were named as a source of primroses, azaleas and chrysanthemums; neighboring Sugamo's chrysanthemum growing had three booms in the mid-to-late Edo period, with over eighty nurseries. At Horikiri, on the low ground east of Edo, a village's iris garden opened at the end of the period, and a later one there was turned back into paddy in 1942. Flowers also came from farther out: narcissus from the Boso peninsula was sold to Edo's samurai houses and townhouses by the middle of the period, though growing flowers as a farm crop there came later. In China the chrysanthemum was a field crop in its own right, dried as a medicine and a tea, in Tongxiang in Zhejiang, where tradition has it planted since the Southern Song.

What the record cannot say. Every market case read is on the edge of a great city - Edo - and none is of an ordinary provincial town's ring; nothing read describes flowers grown for a shrine or a temple in a plot of their own; and no page gives the size of any historical flower field or plot. So a town's ring on these maps grows no flower plots, and the one field drawn is the Imperial one.

How big the field is drawn. Hirameki's field is about 220 by 340 ft, some 1.7 acres. That size is a guess: nothing read gives the size of a flower field to set it against.
