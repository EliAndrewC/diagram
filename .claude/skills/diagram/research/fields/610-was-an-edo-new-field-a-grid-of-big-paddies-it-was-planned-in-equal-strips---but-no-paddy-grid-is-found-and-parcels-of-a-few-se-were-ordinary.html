Was an Edo new field a grid of big paddies? It was planned, in equal strips - but no paddy grid is found, and parcels of a few se were ordinary

Sources: shinden-shuraku-kotobank (the planned layout and the equal strips), ishizue-musashino (the Musashino strips), ishizue-ariake (the diked stages of a sea polder), ishizue-kojima (an Edo polder consolidated in the 20th century), suido-ishizue-kochi-seiri (the small, irregular early-modern plot and the Meiji block), yashio-kenchi (a 1684 paddy parcel), komonjyo-kenchi (1678 field sizes)

The question. A map whose fields came from a planned reclamation may draw its paddies in even, surveyed rows instead of the grown patchwork, and a valley hamlet sometimes draws its paddies a size larger than the usual. This page calls the tidy rectangular grid a Meiji and Showa consolidation, except on the ancient jori ground. Under the GM's ruling of 2026-09-28 that nothing attested only in modern times is drawn, was an Edo-period new field laid out on a grid, or in big blocks?

What the record found.

  • Planned, in equal strips. An Edo new-field village was laid out on a plan - its roads, its fields and its house plots - so its form came out regular. In the street villages common on the Kanto plain the houses line both sides of the road, and behind each lies its land in a strip of equal area. On the Musashino plateau those strips were dry field and woodland, long and narrow behind a row of houses, allotted nearly equally at the start in many villages, and in the south the rows run the way of the canals dug along the roads. Neither source says whether such strips elsewhere were paddy. The same dictionary gives a reclaimed new field, where land was won from sea or lake, another form: the farmhouses stand in a row on the dike or along the old shore, and each holding lies near its house and fairly compact - no strips are named there.
  • No grid of paddies. No page read says an Edo reclamation's paddies were laid out in a checkerboard. On the Ariake coast each stage of reclamation is a block of paddy inside its own dike, the stages added seaward like fish scales, but nothing says how the paddies within a stage were divided. On Kojima Bay, Kojo village, reclaimed at the very end of the Edo period, had subdivided fields that its leading farmers consolidated into blocks by readjustment, the ground of its prewar mechanized farming, though the page gives the readjustment no date. What consolidation replaced across Japan was the small, irregular plot handed down from the early modern (Edo) period; the regular plots western Japan kept until Meiji were such as the ancient jori ones; and the block of one tan, 30 by 10 ken, every plot touching a canal and a road, was the standard begun in 1902.
  • Parcels of a few se. The survey registers give a parcel's sides and area. In the 1684 register of Kami-Baba village one parcel of middle-grade paddy is 16 by 10.5 ken, 5 se 18 bu - about 555 m2, or 0.14 acre - its sides found by converting it to a rectangle. The 1678 register of Yaho village gives single dry fields from 1 se 12 bu (one side given as 7 and 6 ken) and 14 by 7 ken (3 se 8 bu) to 41 by 10 ken (1 tan 3 se 20 bu). A register parcel is a line of assessment, its area the surveyors' figure, which a slack rope or a shortened rod could push away from the ground's, and neither source says whether a paddy parcel was one bunded sheet or several.

What it means on the map. The larger paddies a valley hamlet sometimes draws are about 2.8 se (0.07 acre), a third larger than the usual plot and half the Kami-Baba parcel: a size the 17th-century registers hold, drawn as irregular as the rest, so they stay. Even rows for a planned reclamation are honest only as what the record found there: equal strips laid off a road or a canal, one after another. A checkerboard of equal paddies is Meiji consolidation, or the ancient jori ground, and is not drawn for an Edo reclamation.

The rule the map follows: a planned reclamation's fields may run in equal strips from the road or canal that founded them; the paddies are never drawn as a checkerboard of equal rectangles unless the ground is jori ground; a paddy is drawn at the size of a few se or less, never at the one-tan block of Meiji or the larger blocks after it.
