How wide is a drain where it starts - as narrow as the ditches that feed it

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25; the taper finding in 'Where does a field's water come from, and how is it shared out?' above, of which this is the mirror; kaogongji-wikisource (the smallest field channels, sized by the spade that cuts them), chi-unit-enwiki (the classical foot in inches), chinese-units-enwiki (each state its own measures until the Qin), hattori-site-yayoiken (an excavated early drain's width), jsslkx-002-2021 (a modern floor on the bottom width, given for the same reason).

A gathering drain is the supply side run backwards. The delivery ditches taper to a thread as they give up their water to the basins, so the collector that receives them has to BEGIN as a thread and earn its full width downstream at the outfall, where it finally carries the runoff of every basin it serves. The GM ruled on 2026-07-25 that the head be drawn as narrow as a delivery tail - about 1.5 ft across - rather than as a ditch already several feet wide.

Two independent arguments land on the same figure, which is why it is not a compromise between them. Hydraulically, the head of a collector drains the toe of ONE basin - a tenth of a hectare, a fraction of a liter a second even under a storm drawdown - so the flow buys it no width at all. Practically, a hand-dug channel is as wide as the tool that cuts it, and a classical Chinese specification of field channels sizes them exactly so: in the Rites of Zhou's book of artisans, a spade is five cun wide, two spades working together cut the finest channel, one chi wide and one chi deep, and the channel at the head of the field is doubled, two chi wide and two deep. The chi of that time was about 23 to 24 cm, so on this record's arithmetic the finest channel is about nine inches across and the one at the head of the field about 1.5 ft - the drawn head's own width. That conversion assumes the book of artisans used the common foot: before the Qin standardized measures each state kept its own, and which state's foot this text used is not settled here, so the inches are this record's reading, not the text's. An excavated drain from the start of the Yayoi period in Japan, at the Hattori site, was 0.5 to 1 m wide, about 1.6 ft at its narrowest. A modern Chinese design standard sets its floor in the same place and for the same reason, no canal bottom narrower than 0.3 m, which is 11.8 inches, so that the thing can be maintained once it is built; it agrees with the older figures, and the width does not rest on it. A channel narrower than the finest cut is not a maintained ditch but the seasonal furrow re-cut at each drawdown. So the floor is a real one, older than any modern rule, and it sits exactly where the supply side already ends. The limits: the classical widths are an ideal, not a survey of dug ditches, and the Hattori drain is one site of tiny plots, far older than the setting; neither says where along a drain its narrowest point lay.

Two channels are exempt on purpose, both of them because they do not gather along a run: a polder's ring canal holds one section the whole way round, because it is the trunk that rings the block and is fed from every side rather than accumulating along a run; and a supply canal's own head is set by the ground it commands.
