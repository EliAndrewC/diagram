How wide is a drain where it starts - as narrow as the ditches that feed it

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25; the taper finding in 'Where does a field's water come from, and how is it shared out?' above, of which this is the mirror; jsslkx-002-2021 (the bottom width below which a channel cannot be maintained, and the side slope it declines to fix).

A gathering drain is the supply side run backwards. The delivery ditches taper to a thread as they give up their water to the basins, so the collector that receives them has to BEGIN as a thread and earn its full width downstream at the outfall, where it finally carries the whole fan's runoff. The GM ruled on 2026-07-25 that the head be drawn as narrow as a delivery tail - about 1.5 ft across - rather than as a ditch already several feet wide, which is what it had been.

Two independent arguments land on the same figure, which is why it is not a compromise between them. Hydraulically, the head of a collector drains the toe of ONE basin - a tenth of a hectare, a fraction of a liter a second even under a storm drawdown - so the flow buys it no width at all. Practically, a ditch that is cleaned out by hand every year needs a bottom a hoe fits into, call it a foot - and a Chinese design standard sets that same floor for that same reason, no canal bottom narrower than 0.3 m, which is 11.8 inches, so that the thing can be operated and maintained once it is built. Side slopes that stand up put about 1.5 ft across the top; that top width is this record's own arithmetic, since the same standard leaves the side-slope coefficient to a stability calculation rather than fixing a number. A channel narrower than the floor is not a maintained ditch but the seasonal furrow re-cut at each drawdown. So the floor is a real one, and it sits exactly where the supply side already ends.

Two channels are exempt on purpose, both of them because they do not gather along a run: a polder's ring canal holds one section the whole way round, because it is the trunk that rings the block and is fed from every side rather than accumulating along a run; and a supply canal's own head is set by the ground it commands.
