What is the farmland around a town or a city made of?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-23; the marketing-community and county-seat material in Tract sizes above; this project's measurements of its own drawn town and city maps; tajima-2007-night-soil (a premodern manual's ladder of fertilizer regimes by distance from a castle town, and the transport cost that produces it), tabayashi-1987

What the research found. A market town or county seat sits in the middle of its best land, and the ring worked hardest is the one closest in. A Tokugawa farming manual sets the gradient out as a ladder around Kanazawa: within one ri, about 4 km, the fields get urine and abundant manure; out to about three ri, manure with rapeseed cake and dried sardine; beyond four, manure, ash and sardine; and in remote places gathered bushes and grasses, which is the cheapest input there is.86 The mechanism is carriage: night soil is mostly water and heavy, so what it costs to move is what limits how far out it goes.103 The gradient is measured in fertilizer regime rather than in the share of ground cultivated, and it is a castle town's, so a county seat's rings are tighter than those distances. The second half of the picture - the labor-limited fallow retreating to the far margins - rests on nothing read: that source says nothing about fallow at all. The tempting alternative - a few paddy clusters in a sea of scrub right up to the gate - has that gradient exactly upside down. What a town map shows is a SAMPLE, though: the far countryside is implied off the sheet, and coverage of the near ring need not be total, since in the GM's words "the presumption is that there's more we don't see".

The decision it drove (GM 2026-07-23). The farmland around a town or a city is drawn EXCLUSIVELY with the same real irrigation works as a village's: a sluice tapped on a real source, a head-race forking into supply canals, flooded basins cascading downhill, a dry hem on the upslope margin, a drain, and farmhouses ringed alongside. The near ring is the settlement embedded in those fans, and the open ground between them reads as pasture and commons and as the head of paddy country carrying on past the frame.

Two fillers are excluded. A quilt of dry and garden tiles, and gridded paddy basins with no waterworks of their own, are both cheaper than comb fans and both are ruled out: each would draw the near ring as something other than comb fans, which is precisely what the ruling forbids.

The rule the map follows: on a TOWN map at least one field runs OFF the map edge. A partial field at the margin is what says "more farmland beyond", and a town that shows only enclosed fans claims to show all of its hinterland, which no town sheet can. A field running off the edge is exempt from the rules that want a field ringed with farmhouses and tied to a water source, because its houses and its water are off the map too.

The rule the map follows: the near ring reads PACKED, not sampled away to bare scrub. Within about 800 ft of the rampart of a walled CITY at least 12% of the ground is cultivated, and on a TOWN at least 28%; the sample is taken in the map's own space rather than in whatever the frame happens to show, so widening or tightening the frame cannot change the answer. Both figures are calibrated against our own drawn maps - a bare city ring measured about 7% and a five-fan town about 31% - rather than derived from a source. The look they aim at is a dense wet-rice near-city plain, roughly 70-75% paddy with the rest bunds, ditches, lanes and farmsteads, at about 1.5-2 farming households per hectare of paddy (this rests on general reading; no source is cited).10287

The rule the map follows: on a CITY map the head of a fan is planted, not left bare - the ground along the supply canals and the triangle between the two arms at the head-race fork. That ground sits at or above its own canals, so gravity cannot command rice onto it, and it is exactly the well-drained high ground that dry crops take: barley, millet and buckwheat beside the head-race.101 No more than a fifth of the ground sampled along the main channels may read as bare parchment. The one-fifth ceiling is calibrated against the drawn maps, which ran about 25% before the heads were quilted and 13-16% after, not derived from a source.
