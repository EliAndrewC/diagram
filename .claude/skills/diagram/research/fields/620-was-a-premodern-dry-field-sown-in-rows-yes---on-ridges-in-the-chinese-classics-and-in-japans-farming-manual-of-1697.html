Was a premodern dry field sown in rows? Yes - on ridges, in the Chinese classics and in Japan's farming manual of 1697

Sources: nogyo-zensho-joun-sera (the ridges of a dry field in the 1697 farming manual), hanshu-daitian-wikisource (seed sown in furrows, ridges hoed down, in the Han), qimin-yaoshu-juan1 (the seed drill, one stand to the chi on good land, and the sower treading the row, under the Wei).

A premodern dry field was worked in ridges and furrows, so it showed rows. The Nogyo Zensho, the Japanese farming manual of 1697, has the weeds pulled from a dry field spread on the high part of its ridges, and the first hoeing of proso and foxtail millet made before the seedlings are as high as the ridge tops. The Chinese classics say the same far earlier. The Han history gives the "alternating field" of Zhao Guo: three furrows to a mu, each a foot wide and a foot deep and running the plot's whole length, the seed sown in the furrows and the ridge soil hoed down onto the roots as the plants grew, furrow and ridge changing places each year. The Qimin yaoshu, written by Jia Sixie under the Wei, knows seed drills still in use in its author's day, leaves one stand (a plant or a clump) to the chi, the Chinese foot, in good land, and has the sower tread the bottom of the row as the ox walks slowly.

What it means for the map. The furrowed texture the maps give a dry plot (what each crop looks like) is accurate; which way each plot's rows run is asked on its own. How far apart the rows stood in a Japanese field is not on any page read, so the spacing the maps draw is a guess.
