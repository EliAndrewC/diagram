How much farmland a settlement works, and in what tracts

A village's paddy had to feed the village and pay its tax. In Edo-period Japan an inhabitant was reckoned to eat about one koku of rice a year, a tan of middling paddy was assessed at 1.3 koku, and the lord took 40 to 50 percent of the crop, while millet, barley and the other coarse grains filled out what the farmers themselves ate. Worked through, that comes to a little under a tan of paddy, about a quarter of an acre, for each inhabitant.

A family's fields were seldom in one piece, and the paddy was not watered farm by farm: the larger works, a river's main and branch canals, were run by villages. The ground one such work waters is a tract. The small pond-fed systems of one Japanese ward water about 10 ha (~25 acres) each.

  • An inhabitant was reckoned to eat about one koku of rice a year.
    One koku is taken to be the rice one adult eats in a year, though a grown man's standard stipend was in practice about 1.8 koku of brown rice a year.
  • A tan of middling paddy was assessed at 1.3 koku.
    The land surveys of early modern Japan assessed a tan of upper paddy at 1.5 koku, middle paddy at 1.3 and lower paddy at 1.1. The figure was a statutory estimate of the yield, not a measured harvest.
  • The lord took 40 percent of the crop early in the Edo period, and is said to have taken half later.
    The rate is given as four parts to the lord and six to the farmer in the early Edo period, and five and five from the Kyoho era (1716-36) onward; one of the two encyclopedias that say so calls the later rate uncertain.
  • Farmers ate much of their grain as millet, barley and other coarse grains.
    Outside Edo, where rice gathered from every part of the country, coarse grain tended to be the staple itself, and it was grown outside the tax levies, as the farmers' own food and against famine.
  • How much of a farm household's everyday rice pot was rice?
    From two parts in five to four in five, in the few accounts found, and less where the land was dry. A farm village's rice was cooked with coarse grain, and around 1830 a village of Ina district in Shinano ate at midday in winter a mixed rice that was 40 percent rice. In one undated account a paddy district in the Katsushika delta cooked about four parts rice to one of barley, while the dry-field districts ate millet and barley with a little rice, and in the Kanto dry-field belt a rice that was 70 to 80 percent barley.
  • How much paddy did it take to feed one inhabitant?
    About 0.8 to 1.0 tan (~0.20-0.25 acre), by this project's reckoning. After a tax of about 45 percent, the midpoint of the two rates and on no page, a tan of middling paddy leaves its farmer about 0.7 koku, so rice alone at a koku a head would take about 1.4 tan. At 0.8 to 1.0 tan, coarse grain makes up 28 to 43 percent of what is eaten, roughly a third.
  • Is a third of the diet in coarse grain about right?
    No source we read gives coarse grain's share of a whole diet, but the everyday rice pot brackets it. A third lies between the Katsushika paddy district's one part in five and the Ina village's three in five that were not rice, and well short of the dry-field districts, as it should be for a village among its paddy. The exact point is this project's own, a GUESS.
  • A family's fields were seldom in one piece.
    An eyewitness account of Japanese farming found that the lands one family worked were seldom contiguous, might be widely scattered and were very often rented, and that the government had passed laws in force since 1900 to consolidate them.
  • How much land did a Japanese farm household work?
    About 2.5 acres, paddy and dry field together. The same eyewitness account puts the average worked by one family nearer 2.5 acres. That is close to 1 cho (~2.45 acres), the holding this project takes for an Edo farm household, a GUESS: no page we read gives an Edo figure.
  • Small irrigation works were run by one farmer or a few.
    A 1987 geographical study of irrigation in Japan finds groundwater irrigation almost always small, because a well serves a small area, and so managed by single farmers or small groups with simple procedures, while river systems with their main and branch canals are run by villages and municipalities.
  • How much land did one small pond system water?
    About 10 ha (~25 acres), in the one case read. In Tsunai ward of Kunisaki, five pond systems water 50 ha (~124 acres) of farmland among 11 farmers; the ten each is this record's arithmetic, since the paper gives no area for a single system.
  • How much land did a village's tank water?
    Tens of hectares, well under 200 ha (~494 acres), a GUESS: Japan's register of irrigation ponds records no area watered, and the Kunisaki case is the only one read.
  • Where mountains left little flat ground, there was little farmland.
    In the Kiso valley steep mountains take most of the land, and cultivated ground is found only on the narrow flats along the Kiso River.
