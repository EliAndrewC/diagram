Why is every rice plot the same green?

Sources: the GM's decision that a color difference on a map must mean something; obata-1977-taue (a survey of customary transplanting: the whole schedule agreed in advance at the hamlet unit, and how long the window runs).

A village transplants TOGETHER: in some places the whole of a year's transplanting schedule was settled in advance by consultation, at the hamlet unit of twenty to thirty households, and where the preceding crop makes the window tight the whole district's transplanting falls inside seven to ten days. At any moment, then, its basins are largely at a single growth stage, with only a minor spread from early and late varieties. What does NOT produce that synchrony is exchanged labor: the same survey puts the agreed schedule and the labor exchange in different zones, and describes yui as working precisely BECAUSE transplanting dates differ - households whose dates coincide cannot lend one another hands. Where the labor was exchanged and water could be drawn at any hour the window ran past twenty days, so the synchronizer is the cropping calendar and the water regime, not the labor. The body of the paddy is therefore ONE green; the bund network and the footpath dots carry the field's structure instead.

What remains is that every color on cultivated ground MEANS something. A blue-green basin is one of the low plots sitting on the drain collector, pooling before the outfall (the entry on the wettest plots below); a gold one is early ripening, where a map uses it at all; and a plot on the upslope margin drawn in its own color with its own furrow direction is a dry crop. So a reader can trust the palette: uniform green is the one rice crop at the one stage, and a different color is a real difference in the ground rather than decoration.
