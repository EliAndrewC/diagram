Why does the drain run across the slope instead of down it?

Sources: this project's reading of how a cascade fan drains; the rules and the declined alternatives below are its own decisions; fao-drainage-systems (where a collector drain sits, what angle the field drains meet it at, and what it discharges into); akusuiro-kotobank (what the Japanese word for the drain means); shonairyo-akusuiro-jawiki (a Saitama farm drain, undated, that carried an irrigated district's spent water to a river); suido-ishizue-minuma (the 1728 reclamation, supply on the edge and the drain in the center), matsuura-2017-minuma (the drain on the paddies' lowest ground).

It looks wrong at a glance and it is right. The drain (akusuiro, in Japanese a channel that carries away unneeded water) crosses the field's low margin roughly PERPENDICULAR to the fall, because it is a collector: it has to gather runoff from every cascade column, and a drain that ran straight downhill would follow one column and collect nothing. That is ordinary drainage practice, stated as such: the drains that carry water off the crop run in the direction of the greatest slope, the collector is built at the LOW end of the field and takes them at right angles - that is, across the fall - and what it discharges into is often a stream, canalized, running through the lowest ground. That source is a modern manual, but the layout is older: when the Minuma reservoir was reclaimed in 1728 the supply canals were set along its edge and a drainage channel in its center, the supply canals a step higher along the edge of the upland and the drain on the paddies' lowest ground, so the drain ran along the low line, across the fall of the paddies between (at Irrigation canals and how they are laid out (yosuiro)). The Japanese farm drain does the same work: one in Saitama, which the article does not date, was built as the drain of the district its canals watered, taking the fields' spent water to a river. The right angle in that manual is the one between the field drains and the collector; the angle at which the collector then meets the stream is this record's own inference from the two facts, and no page read gives it. On a map whose land falls diagonally the effect is stronger still: on a sheet high to the northwest the collector runs from southwest to northeast, across the fall, and its outfall can sit at the northeast end - lower than the end the drain starts from, and also up the page, where an eye expects the high ground to be. The water is not running uphill; the page is.

The rule that was DECLINED. No rule forcing a drain to run within so many degrees of the slant was added, and that is a decision rather than an oversight: such a rule would ban the very collectors that drainage practice builds. What the maps hold to instead are the three things that are actually constrained - the collector runs roughly across the fall rather than along it, more than about fifty degrees off it; the end that discharges is the lower-ground end, never uphill of the head; and the brook that takes the water away heads downhill. Together those say "a collector crosses the slope, and the water only ever moves downhill", which is the true constraint, while the angle rule would have said something false.

Two readability fixes priced and declined, and the one adopted. If the perception still misleads, the answer is to fix the READING and not the hydrology. Slope cues drawn as contour lines were declined as awkward on a map already dense with line work, and flow arrows inside the channels were declined because the channels are drawn too thin to carry one. What was adopted is cheaper: the brook CURVES out of the collector's exit heading over its first few steps, so the junction reads as the collector turning down the valley into the stream instead of meeting it at a hard corner. The turn is held under 65 degrees, each heading averaged over about 40 ft of its own line so that a short jittery segment cannot fool the measure.
