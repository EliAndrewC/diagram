Where does the path to the fields end? On a bund, which carries it on

Sources: kotobank-nodo (the farm road, between the farmhouses and the fields), kotobank-aze-sekai-daihyakka (the bund as boundary and path), aze-jawiki (the bund path, its names, the old large bunds that were both path and dike, bund paths that were public roads), kotobank-keihan (the bund walked for weeding and manuring), kotobank-nawate (the word for the path between paddies, by about 934)

A map draws a path from the hamlet out to its rice. This question asks where that path stops: at the head of a bund, at a gap left in the paddy's outer bund, or wherever the worked ground begins. The way across dry plots, where farmers walk the plot edges, is at What vehicle used a village lane; how wide a walked bund is, at How wide is a bund.

It does not stop: the bund carries it on. A farm road is the way made for farm work between the farmhouses and the cultivated land. Among the paddies the way is the bund itself. A bund holds the water, and serves as a boundary and as a path. A bund that is also the way round the paddies is called an azemichi or a nawate. The word nawate, for the path between paddies, is in a Japanese dictionary of about 934. Farmers walked the bunds to weed and to manure, and rested on them. In the small paddies of the Yayoi and Kofun periods, the large bunds round each block served as both path and dike. And some bund paths were once public village roads. So the way from the houses reaches the paddy's bank, and the walking goes on along the bunds.

No gap for walkers, and no path that stops short. No page read says a gap was left in a bund for people to walk through, or that the way to the fields ended at the field's edge. A path that stops in open ground short of the paddy is therefore not drawn.

What it means for the map. The path to the fields ends on the paddy's outer bund, and the bund carries the way on. Where a hamlet stands so close to its paddy that nothing of the path is left between them, the hamlet's own lane is what reaches the bund.

The rule the map follows: the field path runs from the hamlet to the paddy's outer bund and joins it, however short that leaves the path; it never ends in open ground short of the bund and never passes through a gap in it. Where no path is left to draw, the hamlet's nearest lane runs on to the bund. That the path joins a bund is read; that it joins at the point of the bund nearest the hamlet is a GUESS.
