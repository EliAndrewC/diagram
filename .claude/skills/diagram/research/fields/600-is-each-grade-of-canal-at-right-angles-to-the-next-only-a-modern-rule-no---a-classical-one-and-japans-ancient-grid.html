Is each grade of canal at right angles to the next only a modern rule? No - a classical one, and Japan's ancient grid

Sources: zhouli-zhushu-suiren (the classical commentary laying each channel grade across the next), cssn-jiangnan-weitian (the crossing channels of the Lake Tai polders), suido-ishizue-jori (the jori square on a road and a waterway, and the grid on the slope), gunmaibun-jori (excavated Heian paddies inside a right-angled frame), jsslkx-002-2021 (the same siting as modern design law)

The question. On a plain, the comb draws each grade of canal at right angles to the grade that feeds it: the ditches leave the supply canal square to it, and the drain gathers them square again. A Chinese design guideline of 2021 states the rule. Under the GM's ruling of 2026-09-28 that nothing attested only in modern times is drawn, is the right angle older than that?

What the record found. It is, in China and in Japan alike.

  • The classical rule. The Rites of Zhou lays the countryside out as a ladder of channels, and its standard commentary says how the rungs lie: drawn with the fields running south, the channel between the allotments runs lengthwise and the next one crosswise, and so on up the ladder. The Tang sub-commentary spells it out: a crosswise channel at the head of every ten allotments, a north-south one on the east side for every ten of those, a crosswise one on the south side for every ten of those. The note is Zheng Xuan's, of the Han dynasty, and the sub-commentary Jia Gongyan's, of the Tang.
  • A real plain. On the Lake Tai plain of the lower Yangtze, in the Five Dynasties and the Song dynasty, the polder country took the shape of transverse and longitudinal channels crossing each other, a few li apart each way.
  • Japan's ancient grid. The jori land division of the Nara and Heian periods ruled the plains into squares of one cho, about 109 m, each of them beside a road and a waterway; where the ground tilted, as in the Omi basin, the grid's axes ran with the main slope and at right angles to it. Excavated Heian paddies in Gunma sit inside large bunds and waterways, the large bunds facing east-west and north-south, though the small plots inside them were often out of true.

A modern Chinese guideline for consolidated farmland states the same siting as design law - the main canals on the high ground along the contours, the grade below crossing them, and successive grades at right angles on a flat polder plain. It agrees with the older record; nothing on the map rests on it alone.

The limits are plain. The classical rule is an ideal, written for the grain land of the royal domain rather than paddy, and says nothing of how far real fields followed it; the Lake Tai channels are district waterways a few li apart, not a village's ditches; and the jori grid is a frame of 109 m squares, inside which the smaller plots and ditches went their own way. What they share is the one thing the comb takes from them: on flat ground, a channel leaves the one that feeds it square.
