Did a paddy grow a second crop over the winter? Where it drained and manure could be had

The map shows its paddies in summer, under water. This question asks whether the same ground carried a crop through the winter. On many paddies, yes: rice in summer, barley in winter. Growing two crops a year on one field is nimosaku. On a paddy it meant flooding the field in summer for rice, then letting the water off in winter and working it as a dry field for barley or wheat. Chinese milk vetch (renge), rapeseed, broad beans and peas were among the other crops sown after the rice. It is old. There are records of it from the middle of the Heian period, and by the Kamakura period it was fairly widespread: in 1264 the shogunate ordered the provinces not to tax the winter barley. In the Edo period it spread further, above all in the Kyoto-Osaka region and along the Inland Sea coast west of it, where better seed, bought fertilizer and irrigation works came together.

Not everywhere: a wet paddy, a cold district or a short supply of manure kept the ground to one crop. Before the Second World War the limit of the rice-and-barley year ran from the north of the Kanto plain across to Toyama; north of it, cold, snow and wet paddies stood in the way. Barley does not stand the damp, so a wet paddy could not grow it; and in the medieval period enough fertilizer could be had only near crowded districts, so remote places grew no winter barley. The paddy that takes a winter crop is the dry paddy, the kanden: it drains well, dries to a field when no water is let in, and barley and other winter crops are easy on it.

What it means for the map. Two forms are attested, so this is a knob, rolled per settlement: one whose paddies carry a winter crop, and one whose paddies lie bare over the winter. A summer map draws neither, so nothing on today's maps changes. The seasonal maps the GM deferred are where it would show. There a double-cropped settlement's dry paddies are green with barley, while its wet paddies, which never dry, cannot be. A single-cropped settlement's paddies are left as reaped ground. The drainage and the nearness of a town's manure decide the roll, and how the two weigh against each other is a GUESS.
