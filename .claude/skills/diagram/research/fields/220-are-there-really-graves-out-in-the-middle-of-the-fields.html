Are there really graves out in the middle of the fields?

Sources: henriot-shanghai-graves (graves in the middle of open fields around Shanghai, and that nobody counted them), henan-grave-removal-enwiki (graves scattered across the farmland of the north China plain), ryobosei-jawiki (Japanese burials beside the bunds and on the house plot, and the order that banned the first), yashikibaka-ibaraki-blog (family graves in the corner of a field in Ibaraki)

  • In China, yes - and not rarely. Around Shanghai, in the Yangzi delta, villagers kept small burial grounds, but many buried their dead one by one out in the open fields, wherever a geomancer placed the grave. A column of soldiers marching into Shanghai from Wusong in 1842 found graves in every field, as mounds of earth. The same scatter is reported far to the north, in Henan, where the provincial party secretary saw graves scattered across farmland in 2012 and the province later leveled more than two million graves to make fields. The Shanghai account does not say what those fields grew, so it shows graves standing inside working fields in rice country without saying that any one of them stood in a flooded paddy. Hillside burial is attested in southern China as well (see In-field features), so the mid-field grave is one of two southern forms, not the only one.

  • In Japan, beside the field rather than in it. In the Tenryu river basin and in the Otawa district of Narusawa village in Yamanashi the burial graves are reported to have once stood on private land beside the bunds, and in 1872 the Meiji government forbade burying the dead at the bund edge of one's own fields, having heard that people were doing so; the order does not say how common the practice was. Elsewhere the family grave stood on the house plot, and in Ibaraki such graves are still seen in the garden or in the corner of a field. No Japanese page read puts a grave out in the middle of a paddy plot.

  • How often. No source gives a rate. The Shanghai account says outright that nobody can now tell how many such graves there were or where they stood; what it does give - "many" villagers, graves "in every field" on one march - argues that where the custom held they were common, not a rare flourish.

  • What the maps do with it. A grave island standing inside a plot is the Chinese form, and is historically accurate as that form. A grave at the bund edge or in the corner of a plot is the Japanese form, and is equally attested. They are two placements of one thing, so which a hamlet uses is a choice rolled per settlement rather than a single rule. How often a map draws an in-field grave at all is not a figure from any source: it is a degree chosen for the maps, and the evidence argues for it being common where the custom holds.
