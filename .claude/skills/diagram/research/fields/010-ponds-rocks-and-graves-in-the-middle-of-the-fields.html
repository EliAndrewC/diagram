Ponds, rocks and graves in the middle of the fields

A paddy field was rice, plot after plot, but not only rice. Small ponds stood among the paddies to store water for them: before its modern canal, the pond country of Kagawa drew most of its farm water from ponds. Japan came to have over a hundred thousand, more of them built in the Edo period than in any other, together with the irrigation channels of newly opened fields. One form, seen mostly on the plains, was the "dish pond" (sara-ike), made where low ground held water easily.

Graves stood in some fields too: many villagers of the Yangzi delta buried their dead one by one out in the open fields. Of rocks left standing in a paddy, no page we read speaks at all.

  • The dish pond, a form seen mostly on the plains, was low ground ringed with an embankment and dug out.
    The dish pond, a form seen mostly on the plains, was made by ringing with an embankment a place that would hold water easily - a hollow in the ground or low wet land - and then digging down its bottom. How a reservoir pond's shore and embankment were kept is at Reservoir ponds (tameike).
  • In the hills, the usual pond was a dammed valley, and three or four were often strung in steps along one.
    In the hills and mountains the usual pond was made by damming a valley, and three or four such ponds are often strung in steps along one valley.
  • How large were the ponds?
    The largest covered several hectares (a hectare is about 2.5 acres); the smallest, about 10 m (~33 ft) on a side, were owned and kept by one person.
  • How many ponds does Japan have?
    Japan is estimated to have from over a hundred thousand to about 200,000 ponds.
  • More of Japan's ponds were built in the Edo period than in any other.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on reservoir ponds puts the most of them in the Edo period, built together with irrigation channels as the domains opened new fields.
  • About half of all Japan's ponds lie around the rain-poor Inland Sea.
    They are thickest in the rain-poor Kinki, San'yo and Shikoku regions around the Seto Inland Sea, which together hold about half the country's total. Kagawa's Sanuki plain, in Shikoku, lies on its shore.
  • Kagawa, on the dry Sanuki plain, has the densest ponds in Japan.
    Its 12,187 ponds rank third in number among the prefectures, after Hyogo and Hiroshima, but first for the prefecture's area.
  • How many of Kagawa's ponds are small?
    About 6,600 of them, about half the whole, hold under 1,000 tonnes of water (~35,000 cubic feet).
  • Before its modern canal opened, Kagawa drew 70% of its farm water from ponds.
    The prefecture's own pond data gives the share for the years before the Kagawa canal opened.
  • On the Sanuki plain, once the good sites ran out, flat farmland was dug out for ponds.
    Once no place suited to a new pond remained, dish ponds were made even by converting cultivated land on the flat.
  • How often did a field have a pond among its paddies?
    It varied by degree, from a valley whose stream watered every plot to the dense pond country of a dry plain like Sanuki - our reading of the pond counts in this section. The counts we read are per prefecture and per country; none gives a rate per field or per village.

Where the dead were buried was a matter of custom, and the customs differed from place to place. The whole of it is at Where a village buries its dead: its own ground, the temple yard, the fields or the home plot; the fields' share of it is below.

  • In south China, feng shui often put graves on the highest hills.
    There it often placed villages on high hills safe from flood and erosion, and graves on the highest hills, far from water and on land otherwise of no value for farming.
  • Many villagers of the Yangzi delta buried their dead in single graves out in the open fields.
    In the countryside around Shanghai, villagers usually had a small burial ground, often a lineage's, but many buried their dead in single graves in the middle of the open fields, at a spot a Taoist priest or a geomancer chose.
  • In parts of Japan, graves once stood beside the bunds.
    In the basin of the Tenryu river in Shizuoka, and in the Otawa district of Narusawa village in Yamanashi, the burial graves anciently stood on private land such as the ground beside the bunds.
