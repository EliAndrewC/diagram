Why ruled rows waited for Meiji

Sources: seijoue-kotobank, seijoue-seika, kubota-transplanting (seijoue rare before Meiji, promoted nationally in the 1890s-1900s with the tauchiguruma (田打車) weeder, ropes and rulers), cultivator-enwiki, tauchiguruma-jawiki, obata-1977-taue

  • WHY rows waited for Meiji when row planting is ancient (GM 2026-07-23 - the idea was never the bottleneck, the economics were). Dry-crop rows are FREE: the seed goes into a plowed furrow, and the furrow IS the row. Wet rice is TRANSPLANTED into a puddled flooded sheet with no furrows and no guide lines, so rows must be PURCHASED - marked ropes or a rolled gridding frame, plus every planter aligning to them - and the bill lands in the year's tightest labor window (the whole village transplants in days, on a shared water schedule). And for centuries the purchase bought nothing: rows pay when a tool travels BETWEEN them (the ancient dry-field hoe/cultivator), but nothing could travel between rows in a flooded paddy - weeding was by hand (bare hands or a hand claw, crouching) either way. What changed in Meiji was the arrival of the between-rows tool for mud: the hand-pushed ROTARY PADDY WEEDER, which only works on plants ruled in both directions - so seijoue + marking frame + rotary weeder spread as ONE package, pushed by state extension hard enough that police sometimes stood over farmers to enforce straight lines ("saber farming"), itself evidence the private payoff was marginal before the full package. Traditional transplanting was 乱雑植え, "disorderly planting", not evenly spaced, and only became evenly spaced once row planting arrived - which is exactly what the sparse unruled shoot-mottle renders.

How dense that mottle is drawn - and a decision deliberately left unmade. Traditional transplanting set hills, clumps of two to six seedlings, at roughly 20-30 cm centers, which is about one hill per square foot (this rests on general reading; no source is cited). So even on the hamlet sheet, drawn at one foot to the pixel, one dot per plant would be one dot per pixel: a solid fill, not a texture. Any mottle that can be drawn at all is therefore a SAMPLE of the planting, and how coarse a sample is a drawing choice rather than a finding. The parcels of rice left between a polder's fish ponds, where the crop has to read distinctly against the water, took a denser one at the GM's choice: centers about 4.5 ft apart with alternate rows half-offset, about 1.5 ft of jitter on each and roughly a tenth of them dropped, so the shoots land irregularly with no ruled row or column anywhere. The comb maps keep the sparse random scatter. Whether to bring the denser sample to the ordinary comb paddies is a decision deliberately LEFT UNMADE: nothing in the record decides between them, both are honest samples of the same irregular planting, and the change would move the surface of every comb map at once.
