Where does a field's water come from, and how is it shared out?

Sources: tabayashi-1987, kagawa-tameike, jsidre-minumadai, beitang-studies, jsslkx-002-2021 (the canal-layout doctrine as a design standard states it), bungotakada-tagoshi (paddy-to-paddy irrigation as the old form), kochi-seiri-jawiki (the Meiji date of a ditch beside every paddy)

  • Pond: a valley-head tameike behind an earthen dike, sitting ABOVE its fields ("located at a valley head and constructed by dividing off the valley mouth with an earthen dike"; "located at elevations higher than the surface of the paddy fields they serve" - Tabayashi 1987, Geographical Review of Japan 60(1)). The outlet: an inclined intake (shahi) feeding a bottom conduit (sokohi) through the dam, with a separate works that passes heavy-rain inflow safely downstream (Kagawa pref. tameike docs); that the intake is the pond's ONE outlet, and that the flood works plays no part in sharing the water out, is this page's reading of them. Parent-child pond irrigation systems (oyaike/koike - the Japanese names are this page's, not the source's) are attested, with Manno-ike in northeast Shikoku, Japan's largest irrigation pond, named under them: the large parent pond distributes water to the smaller child ponds, some 60 of them off Manno-ike. "Melon-on-the-vine" (长藤结瓜, literally "a long vine bearing melons") in China is reported as the name of larger systems of this kind.
  • Distribution: from the intake sluice down the head-race to division points (bunsuiguchi, this page's Japanese term) - a branching TREE in this page's own words, whose taper Tabayashi gives: "Main canals gradually decrease in size as they are tapped by branch canals" (Tabayashi) - hence the drawn taper. The smallest ditches "are often considered parts of the paddy fields they serve" - hence the smallest ditch drawn as the boundary of the plot it serves. The old form passes water from paddy to paddy (tagoshi kangai) rather than down a ditch to each; that the network is therefore sparse, a village digging the minimum, is this page's own reading; a ditch beside every paddy (yohaisui bunri, this page's term for separate supply and drain channels) is, on this page's reading of that contrast, a Meiji land-readjustment anachronism, from the Ishikawa method of 1887 (parcels squared, each on a road and a channel) and the 1899 land-reorganization law on. How the water was shared out at a division point between villages - by rule and rotation, and by the few works that stood at a split - is How was a ditch's water split between villages, and what shows at the split?
  • Layout modes (terrain-driven):
    • COMB (the default): supply canals along the HIGH margins, delivery ditches perpendicular down-slope, one drain along the low line. It follows the Edo Kishu-school layout (the Minuma-dai canal on the Kanto plain, built in 1728 - the LAYOUT is sourced, the NAME less so: the Kishu school (紀州流) is attested as Izawa Yasobei's river-channelization method rather than as a field layout; the layout is supply on the elevated margins, drainage channel on the lowest line, water reused downstream) AND codified Chinese canal doctrine (mains along contours/ridges on high ground, lateral canals crossing the contours, each grade at right angles to the next in the plains). Chinese beitang pond systems - in this page's reading the direct tameike analogue - were a leading village-scale mode in rice China (8.3M systems serving ~39% of irrigated area in the 1950s, ~71% in hilly regions); the GM chose the Chinese default deliberately (Rokugan demographics anchor to the China of the Song dynasty and the Ming).
