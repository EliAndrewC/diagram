Where does a field's water come from, and how is it shared out?

Sources: tabayashi-1987, kagawa-tameike, jsidre-minumadai, japanese-wiki-corpus, beitang-studies, jsslkx-002-2021 (the canal-layout doctrine as a design standard states it), jori-jawiki, bungotakada-tagoshi (paddy-to-paddy irrigation as the old form)

  • Pond: a valley-head tameike behind an earthen dike, sitting ABOVE its fields ("located at a valley head and constructed by dividing off the valley mouth with an earthen dike"; "located at elevations higher than the surface of the paddy fields they serve" - Tabayashi 1987, Geographical Review of Japan 60(1)). The outlet: an inclined intake (shahi) feeding a bottom conduit (sokohi) through the dam, with a separate works that passes heavy-rain inflow safely downstream (Kagawa pref. tameike docs); that the intake is the pond's ONE outlet, and that the flood works plays no part in sharing the water out, is this page's reading of them. Parent-child pond irrigation systems (oyaike/koike) are attested, Manno-ike in northeast Shikoku the largest; how a parent pond and its child ponds are linked is not in the passage read. "Melon-on-the-vine" (长藤结瓜, literally "a long vine bearing melons") in China is reported flavor for larger systems.
  • Distribution: from the intake sluice down the head-race to division points (bunsuiguchi) - a branching TREE in this page's own words, whose taper Tabayashi gives: "Main canals gradually decrease in size as they are tapped by branch canals" (Tabayashi) - hence the drawn taper. The smallest ditches "are often considered parts of the paddy fields they serve" - hence ditch-as-plot-boundary. The old form passes water from paddy to paddy over the bund (tagoshi) rather than down a ditch to each; that the network is therefore sparse, a village digging the minimum, is this page's own reading; a ditch beside every paddy (yohaisui bunri) is a Meiji land-readjustment anachronism, from the Ishikawa method of 1887 and the 1899 law on.
  • Layout modes (terrain-driven):
    • COMB (the default): supply canals along the HIGH margins, delivery ditches perpendicular down-slope, one drain along the low line. It follows the Edo Kishu-school layout (Minuma-dai, dated 1728 by this page, a date the page read does not give - the LAYOUT is sourced, the NAME less so: 紀州流 is attested as Izawa Yasobei's river-channelization method rather than as a field layout; the layout is supply on the elevated margins, drainage channel on the lowest line, water reused downstream) AND codified Chinese canal doctrine (mains along contours/ridges on high ground, lateral canals crossing the contours, each grade at right angles to the next in the plains). Chinese beitang pond systems - in this page's reading the direct tameike analogue - were a leading village-scale mode in rice China (8.3M systems serving ~39% of irrigated area in the 1950s, ~71% in hilly regions); the GM chose the Chinese default deliberately (Rokugan demographics anchor to Song/Ming China).
    • FAN: gently-descending canals radiating from a valley-mouth apex, the geometry this page draws for an alluvial fan such as the Tedori fan, whose paddy irrigation system is attested; the radiating layout, and the likeness to China's Dujiangyan, are this page's own reading. Correct where the land fans out below the pond.
    • JORI GRID: from the 8th century (jori land notation first appears in 743 CE, the mid Nara period) much of Japan's long-settled PLAINS carried a north-south / east-west 109 m grid, tilted in many regions (jori-sei: 1-cho squares in 6x6-ri blocks, cut into ~11 x 109 m tan strips). Regular jori parcels were widespread in western Japan, so a long-settled plains village there shows semi-regular GRIDDED paddies, not organic patchwork - Rokugan analog: ancient heartland provinces (e.g. Crane/Phoenix cores). The organic patchwork is correct for terrain-following villages like Kikuta.
