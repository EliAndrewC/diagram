Why are the dry plots beside a canal square to the canal?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07; ishizue-musashino (Edo new-field strips of dry field running the way of the canals), zhouli-suiren-wikisource (a channel between every farmer's allotment in the classical scheme), komonjyo-kenchi (the sizes of single dry fields in survey registers of 1591 and 1678); Buck's surveys of the 1920s and 1930s for the size of a Chinese dry parcel; this project's measurement of its own maps; jsslkx-002-2021 (today's orthogonal field grid, and the watering direction that follows the fall on irrigated dry land as on paddy), mu-land-enwiki (the mu the Qing defined)

A dry field bordering a supply canal is surveyed TO the canal: one edge runs along it and the other stands perpendicular to it, running upslope, so the plots come out as clean rectangles. Today's design guideline says as much: on flat ground the canals of successive grades run perpendicular to one another, a field canal's length and command area are taken from the length of the cultivated block beside it, and the direction a field canal waters in follows the field surface's own fall - which the rule states for field canals generally, in a clause that goes on to set the grade for irrigated dry land as well as for paddy. What no page read says is which grid is the parent, and that is what this rule turns on; that the parcel takes its frame from the canal rather than from the rice around it is this record's own. Built in the paddy's own frame instead - columns down the fall, rows on the contour - the plots would come out sheared into parallelograms, since the head-races on these maps commonly run diagonally to the fall.

Is it only modern? No. That guideline, T/JSSLKX 002-2021, is today's design guideline, but dry strips squared to the canal they front are Edo work. The row villages of the Musashino plateau west of Edo were founded by the period's new-field development: houses in a row along a road, and behind each a long, narrow strip of dry field and woodland, where barley, the millets and vegetables were grown. In the plateau's south, watered by the Tamagawa aqueduct, the Nobidome canal and their branches, the rows run the way of the canals dug along the highways; in the north, where the water came from wells, they run in a comparatively free direction, for settling the upland needed water, drinking water above all. Each strip there is, on this record's reading of a strip behind every house, a whole holding rather than a parcel beside rice, so they attest the frame - dry strips square to a canal - and not this map's setting of it at a paddy's edge, which stays this record's own. The classical Chinese scheme for the grain countryside draws the same frame, a channel with a path along it between every farmer's allotment, though it is an ideal of the state, not a survey. So the maps keep drawing the form, labeled accurate for the frame (the paddy topic has the strips' planning).

A softened shear, REJECTED. Keeping the paddy's frame and merely softening the parallelograms' lean - STILL orienting the plots to the paddy - was priced and turned down by the GM: the plots take their orientation from the CANAL, not from the rice. The plots are laid end to end along the canal's own curving line and each is built as a rectangle standing square to the canal at that point, so neighbors abut continuously along the canal, nothing is sheared or stepped, and only the outer upslope edge is ragged. That the strips therefore run near-horizontal along one map's head-race and near-vertical along another's is the point, not an inconsistency. They stay on the dry side throughout, both edges upslope of the canal behind a thin berm, and where a map carries more than one head-race's paddy a hem plot that would lap onto a neighboring paddy's rice is dropped: dry crops on flooded ground contradict the whole reason the hem is where it is.

The rule the map follows: a dry parcel is a smallholder's strip, and the map-wide MEAN dry parcel stays under 0.25 acre. The figure it is calibrated to is the survey registers'. The seven single dry fields quoted from a Musashi village's register of 1678 run from 1 se 12 bu to 1 tan 3 se 20 bu, about 0.03 to 0.33 acre, and average about 0.12 acre; the five dry fields quoted from a register of 1591 run from 96 bu to 1 tan 25 bu and average about 0.16 acre at the same bu. What a register's areas can and cannot say is on the new-field question. China gives the same order: a mean dry parcel near one mu, about 0.15 acre at the mu of 614.4 m2 that the statute of 1915 took straight from the Qing definitions; but that mean is Buck's, from surveys of the 1920s and 1930s, and here it only agrees with the registers. The drawn village strips land at 0.16-0.17 acre, inside the registers' range and near the 1591 average, with the hamlet's garden-scale strips at about 0.04, near the smallest 1678 field. The tiling is in real feet at every scale - roughly 92 ft across a parcel and 72 ft down a row on the village map - so a dry cell is never drawn dwarfing the rice basins beside it, which is what happens when a pixel figure is carried unscaled onto a coarser sheet.
