Was a paddy drained in midsummer before modern times? In some places, where water was plentiful

Sources: horikawa-2011-nakaboshi (the drain's history: Edo farm books, the prewar spread, the postwar rise, where it is not done), doyoboshi-jawiki (the doyoboshi, the drain at the summer doyo)

The season as modern guidance gives it, with the mid-season drain about a month after transplanting, is at How deep the water actually stands. This question asks whether a paddy before modern times was drained at all.

Yes, in some places, by the Edo period at the latest. When the drain came to Japan is not known. But a 1964 study points to several Edo-period farm books that describe it, so it was done in some places by the Edo period at the latest. It is also called doyoboshi, drying at the summer doyo. The water is let out of the paddy to make the rice stand up to wind and fill its ears, for about a week.

Only where water was plentiful. A farming manual of 1933 records it, as doyoboshi, in 22 prefectures. Before the war it had spread only to some regions with plentiful water. After the war it spread fast. By 1966, 59% of Japan's cultivated paddy was drained in midsummer, and where it was not, the commonest reason given was a poor water supply. Today terraced paddies whose water is unreliable are reported not to drain at all. The same review cites one agronomist who calls the drain a modern technique, of the age when water could be used freely and chemical fertilizer came in. The review dates that confinement to the prewar decades; of the Edo period it says only that the drain was done in part. Carrying it back to places with water to spare before modern times is this page's inference, on either reading.

What it means for the map. The map shows one moment and draws its paddies under water, so nothing drawn changes. If a map is ever set in midsummer, the drain becomes a knob. A settlement whose water is plentiful could have its paddies drained and cracking. One short of water keeps its paddies flooded.
