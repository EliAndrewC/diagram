Is any paddy left to rest - and where does a resting plot lie?

Sources: nishitani-2023-chusei-nogyo (how much paddy lay out of crop, the yearly re-choosing, and how the unstable land was overcome), kotobank-kataarashi-yamakawa (the resting land mixed in among the cropped, and grazed in common), kotobank-kataarashi-nipponica (rest in alternate years, for poor soil or short water), koden-jawiki (ground lost to floods or failed water works, and the dispute over alternate years), mizkan-2005-sato-kyuko (the end of resting with the land surveys)

In the early medieval period, a great deal of paddy lay out of crop in any one year. In the late Heian period, on average only 60.9% of paddy was under crop. In 1102, on the estate of Oyama in Tanba province, about 44 of some 89 cho of paddy were worked. About 26 cho were resting that year (nenko), and about 18 had been out of use for years (joko). Short water and thin soil left much of this ground unstable. Each year the plots to be worked were chosen anew from among the cropped and the resting ground, to let the soil recover and to fit the water to hand.

The resting plot: kataarashi. One encyclopedia defines it as Heian-period paddy or dry field rested in alternate years, because the soil was poor or the water short. Not every source agrees that the rest came every other year. One historian's review defines the resting land as ground not worked in that year only. One encyclopedia article holds, without a source, that no alternate-year farming existed anywhere and that the word meant a single-crop paddy resting half of each year. This page asserts only what they share: a plot of paddy could lie out of crop for a year while those around it were worked.

Where it lay: in among the worked plots, not in a block of its own. The resting land was usually not one solid tract but mixed in among the land under crop. While it rested it was not marked off, and it served as common ground for grazing cattle and horses. Nothing read says which plots of a village's paddy were the ones left to rest. That the ones short of water lay at the far end of the water's run is this page's reading of "short water", and a GUESS.

Resting ground is not lost ground. Paddy could also go out of use for good, from poor soil, from flood damage (kawanari, ground taken by a river) or from the difficulty of keeping up its water works. That is abandoned ground, not a plot resting between crops; this question is only about the resting plot.

How it ended. In the advanced farming regions, from the Kamakura period on, water supply and fields were improved and the unstable land was overcome. Nucleated villages formed around the late thirteenth and fourteenth centuries in the Kinai, and a century or so later in the east and Kyushu. In such villages the extensive farming of the old unstable land, which spread its labor thin over ground chosen anew each year, gave way to intensive farming, which worked the same stable ground every year. One rice historian, giving his own view, dates the last of the resting to the early modern period. Once the lords' land surveys taxed paddy by its area, resting a plot cost its holder, and the same paddy was cropped every year.

What the setting says. The GM's notes on total rice production hold that a wet-rice paddy, once established, can be cropped continuously for centuries without depleting the soil. What limits Rokugan's rice is labor: only about a third of its rice-suitable land is under wet paddy in any year. So the setting's unworked rice land is ground the village does not have the hands to work. It is not a plot resting inside the paddy the village does work. The notes do not rule out a plot left dry for want of water.

The decision: two forms, one rolled per settlement. The record attests two forms of a village's paddy, and a settlement rolls one of them from the map's seed.

  • Settled paddy, cropped every year: no plot rests. This is the form of the nucleated village on stable ground, and of the early modern village after the land surveys. The setting's canon, which needs no rest for the soil, sides with it.
  • Unsettled paddy: this year one or a few plots rest. Each is scattered among the cropped plots, never grouped into a block, and is grazed rather than planted. This is the form of the early medieval estate, where water or soil could not carry every plot every year.

The rule the map follows: most settlements roll the settled form and draw no resting plot. That the settled form is the commoner is this record's reading of the setting's nucleated villages and its canon; the weight between the two forms is a GUESS. A settlement that rolls the unsettled form rests a few whole plots, each a complete basin inside its own bunds, never a patch within a basin. They are scattered among the cropped plots, not side by side, and drawn as grass, since they were grazed. They favor the plots at the far end of the water's run, a GUESS. How many rest is a liberty the map takes, kept within the record: a few plots of a hamlet's paddy. The estate of 1102, with nearly a third of its paddy resting, is the upper bound of the record, not a hamlet's norm.
