Dry fields and their crops (hatake)

A dry field, hatake, is farmland that is never flooded. On the Edo-period new-field villages of the Musashino plateau its crops were barley and wheat, millets such as foxtail and barnyard millet, and vegetables; an Edo-period farm year in Echizen also reaped soybean and buckwheat. Dry fields took the ground the paddy water could not reach: on a river plain the natural levees, the low ridges a river builds along its banks, which the old settlements shared with them, while the wet ground behind the levee was made paddy. Single fields were small: in two survey registers they averaged a tenth to a sixth of an acre.

A dry field was worked in ridges and furrows, and its crop grew in rows: Chinese manuals describe it from the Han dynasty on, and a Japanese farming manual of 1697, in the Edo period, still weeds along the ridges. Which way a field's rows ran was set, in a Chinese classic, by what suited its land. The rice paddy beside it was the opposite: its seedlings were transplanted into flooded mud in no ruled order, and evenly spaced rows came to it, but for a few districts, only in the Meiji period (Rice planted in rows).

  • Dry fields and houses shared the raised ground of a river plain.
    A natural levee is sandy, and higher and drier than the ground around it, so settlements, dry fields and roads have stood on it since long ago. The lower, marshy ground behind it, the backswamp, was cleared and has traditionally been used as paddy.
  • River terraces were taken early for settlements and dry fields.
    Being flat, high and dry, they were used early for settlements, dry fields and roads. Where water was short they were sometimes left uncleared, and in many places irrigation works later turned them to paddy.
  • Was the dry middle of an alluvial fan farmed?
    Often not until late. Where the river sinks underground the middle of a fan is short of water, hard to use as paddy, and was often left as coppice. In Japan it was long left wild and was cleared for dry fields and orchards only from the end of the Edo period into Meiji, while the spring-fed toe of the fan was settled early, with paddy beside it. That is a tendency, not a rule: in old heartlands such as Kinki and the Kofu basin, fans were cleared from early times.
  • Where did the dry fields lie in the hills?
    On the slopes around the village, in the one example found. The satoyama, the border zone between the mountain foothills and the flat land, holds rice paddies, "dry rice fields", woods, grassland and ponds together in one mosaic. In the Yoshino mountains the satoyama is the dry fields and coppice woods on the slopes around the settlement.
  • The broadleaf woods of the satoyama were cut back every 10 to 20 years for firewood and charcoal.
    By the early modern period, though, most of Japan's satoyama had become red pine, grass or bare hill.
  • Did the dry fields always lie above the paddy?
    Mostly, on the landforms above: levee, terrace and fan put the dry fields on the higher, well-drained ground the water could not command, and the paddy on the wet ground below. That this order holds across a countryside is this record's reading, pieced together from those entries; the page on satoyama speaks only of a mosaic. The Tonami plain shows the other arrangement: there the settlers built their houses on ground slightly higher than its surroundings and made the ground around them paddy.
  • What did a household grow in the plot by its house?
    Greens for its own table, on this record's reading, not grain. Its name, yashikibatake, "homestead field", is the ground a household cultivates for its own consumption. This record reads it as the kitchen bed (Kitchen gardens beside farmhouses (yashikibatake)). No page we read puts a household's grain or beans in a plot at its house.
  • How big was a single dry field?
    On average a tenth to a sixth of an acre in the two survey registers read, though single fields ran from under a twentieth to a third of an acre. The seven dry fields quoted from a register of 1678, in the Edo period, of a village in Musashi, run from 1 se 12 bu to 1 tan 3 se 20 bu, about 0.03 to 0.33 acre, and average about 0.12 acre; the five quoted from a register of 1591, of another Musashi village, run from 96 bu to 1 tan 25 bu and average about 0.16 acre.

What a dry field looked like depended on the month more than on its crop. The heights and colors below are the plants' own, from today's botany; the seasons are those of the Edo farm year where a source gives them.

  • Barley was the winter crop, sown in autumn and cut in early summer.
    It stands through the winter as young plants, and heads and flowers in spring. An Edo-period calendar from Echizen sows it between the autumn equinox and early October.
  • Ripening barley turns a field pale brown in May.
    Today it heads in April, its ears turn gold in early to mid May, and it is harvested around June. The ripening field looks like a rice field in autumn, hence the name "barley autumn" for early summer. By high summer, on this record's reading, a barley field was stubble or bare ground.
  • How tall did the millets stand?
    Foxtail millet stands around 150 cm (~5 ft), its ears ripening yellow and drooping. It is sown in May or June and harvested from late September into October. Proso millet grows 1 to 2 m (~3-7 ft) tall, its head drooping from summer into autumn like an ear of rice.
  • Buckwheat flowers white, pale red or red, and is ready 70 to 80 days after sowing.
    It stands 60 to 130 cm (~2-4 ft), covered in small flowers about 6 mm (~0.2 in) across, on green, pale red or deep red stems. It has a spring-sown summer crop and a summer-sown autumn one.
  • How tall did soybean stand?
    In the one field found, a modern one in Hokkaido, its top leaves stood near 80 cm (~3 ft) in late August, and by September the field was turning from deep green to yellow-brown.
  • In the Edo farm year of Echizen, soybean and buckwheat were harvested in the gaps of the rice harvest.
    The rice was reaped from the eighth month into the tenth, then threshed; threading through the gaps between these tasks, farmers sowed barley, wheat and rapeseed and harvested soybean, buckwheat, cotton and daikon.
  • From Kanto to Kyushu, barley was also grown on the paddy itself, after the rice.
    Autumn-sown barley spread across those regions as the crop that follows the rice.
  • Is the rotation of rice, barley and soybean old?
    Many places today grow the three in two years, soybean after the barley that follows the rice. No page we read dates that rotation before modern times.

The one Japanese manual we read agrees with Chinese manuals far older.

  • A Japanese farming manual of 1697 weeds its dry fields along the ridges.
    The Nogyo Zensho has the weeds pulled from a dry field spread on the high part of its ridges, and the first hoeing of proso and foxtail millet made before the seedlings are as high as the ridge tops.
  • A Han-dynasty official's "alternating field" sowed grain in furrows and hoed the ridges down onto the roots.
    The Book of Han, the dynasty's official history, gives the "alternating field" of Zhao Guo: three furrows to a mu, each a chi wide and a chi deep and running the plot's whole length, the seed sown in the furrows and the ridge soil pushed down onto the roots as the plants grew, furrow and ridge changing places each year.
  • A Northern Wei manual sows with a seed drill and leaves, in good land, one plant or clump to the Chinese foot.
    The Qimin Yaoshu, by Jia Sixie under the Northern Wei, knows seed drills still in use in its author's day, leaves one stand, a plant or a clump, to the chi in good land, and has the sower tread the bottom of the row as the ox walks slowly.
  • A modern history credits the same manual with making ridges the core of dry farming in north China.
    It credits the manual with ridge rules for soybeans and millet.
  • How far apart were a Japanese dry field's rows?
    No page we read gives it: the one translated passage of the 1697 manual gives ridges but no spacing. The only spacings found are in modern crop guides.

Which way a dry field's rows ran was a choice, and the Chinese record says the land made it.

  • In a Chinese classic, the land set which way a field's rows ran.
    In 589 BCE the victorious state of Jin demanded, as a term of peace, that every field row in Qi be turned to run east. Qi's envoy refused: the early kings had ordered the realm by what suited the land, and as the Book of Songs has it, "south and east run its rows"; to turn every row east served only Jin's war chariots. The ode's own line reads the same. In the passage's plain sense a tract's rows ran the way its land called for, some one way and some the other, a right angle apart.
  • Chinese ridges were shaped to their ground.
    In the rainy, low-lying south the ridges were raised higher, with deeper furrows, to drain, and ridge culture as a whole was suited to the place.
  • Ridges along the contour check the runoff, and do it best on gentle ground.
    Ridge-and-furrow laid along the contour serves erosion control and surface drainage together, and its advantages are greater the less steep the ground; for a smallholder with hand tools or an ox it is a satisfactory method. Steep cultivated land calls instead for contour hedges, straw barriers or grass strips, and on large mechanized farms for terraces and contour bunds.
  • In the early 1900s, an East Asian family's plots were seldom side by side.
    An American soil scientist touring the farms of China, Korea and Japan found them seldom contiguous, sometimes widely scattered, and very often rented.
  • Did neighboring plots run their rows the same way?
    Probably, where they shared their ground. The classical passage puts the direction in the land, and neighbors share their land, so plots on one slope draining the same way would run alike, and the direction would change where the land changes. That is this record's reading: no page we read describes a village's dry fields plot by plot.
  • Did rows follow the contour on steep or terraced ground?
    Probably, a GUESS: no page we read says how rows ran on steep or terraced dry fields, but the classical passage puts the direction in the land, and on such ground every plot's land calls for the contour.

Where a canal watered the dry fields, their strips took their line from it.

  • On the Musashino plateau, Edo new-field villages laid long strips of dry field and woodland behind their row of houses.
    The period's new-field development founded these row villages: houses in a row along a road, and behind them long, narrow strips of dry field and woodland.
  • Did the strips stand square to the canals that watered them?
    In the plateau's south, on this record's reading, yes. Settling the upland needed water, drinking water above all, and it advanced as the canals were built. There, watered by the Tamagawa aqueduct, the Nobidome canal and their branches, the villages formed along the canals dug beside the highways; in the north, where the water came from wells, in a comparatively free direction. The page gives the line of the villages; that the strips behind their houses therefore stood square to the canal is this record's reading.
  • The classical Chinese scheme put a channel between every farmer's allotment.
    The Rites of Zhou lays out the countryside with a channel and a path between the allotments, and larger channels for every ten, hundred and thousand, a scheme on paper, on this record's reading, not a survey of real fields.
