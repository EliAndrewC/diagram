Paddies left to rest (kataarashi)

In the late Heian period much of Japan's paddy lay out of crop in any one year: on average only about three plots in five were worked. Short water and thin soil left the land unstable, and each year the plots to be worked were chosen anew from the cropped and the resting ground, to let the soil recover and to fit the water to hand. A plot left to rest for a year was a kataarashi. It lay in among the worked plots, not in a block of its own, and was grazed in common while it rested.

From the Kamakura period on, in the advanced farming regions, water works and fields were improved and the unstable land that had to be rested was overcome.

  • One historian puts the paddy under crop in the late Heian period at only 60.9% on average.
    The figure is Takashima Masanori's, reported in a 2023 review of medieval farming. Dry fields stood at 68.2%.
  • In 1102, late in the Heian period, about half the paddy of the Oyama estate in Tanba province was worked.
    Of some 89 cho of paddy, 44 cho were worked. About 26 were resting that year (nenko), and about 18 had gone unworked for many years (joko).
  • Resting ground is not abandoned ground.
    Paddy could also go out of use for good: from poor soil, from floods (kawanari, ground taken by a river) or from the difficulty of keeping up its water works. That was ground abandoned, not a plot resting between crops.
  • In the early medieval period, where water or soil was short, the plots to work were chosen anew each year.
    In the early medieval period, the age of great land clearance, much farmland was unstable for want of irrigation water or soil fertility and had to be rested. Each year the land fit to work was chosen again from the worked and the resting ground, to let the soil recover and to fit the water to hand.
  • Did a resting plot rest every other year?
    Accounts differ. One encyclopedia defines kataarashi as Heian-period paddy or dry field rested in alternate years, because the soil was poor or the water short. The 2023 review defines the resting land as ground not worked in that year only. The Japanese Wikipedia article on ruined paddy holds, without a source of its own, that no alternate-year farming existed anywhere and that the word meant a single-crop paddy resting half of each year. What they share is a plot of paddy lying out of crop for a year while those around it were worked.
  • The resting plots usually lay mixed in among the worked plots, not in a block of their own.
    The resting land was usually not one solid tract but mixed in with the land under crop.
  • While a plot rested, it was common grazing for cattle and horses.
    No marker closed it off while it rested, and it served as common ground for grazing.
  • Which plots of a village's paddy were left to rest?
    No page we read says. That the plots short of water lay at the far end of the water's run, and so rested oftenest, is our reading of "short water", a GUESS.
  • From the Kamakura period on, the advanced farming regions improved their water and fields until the land no longer needed resting.
    There water supply and fields were improved, and the land entered a stage at which it could be used intensively.
  • As villages gathered into nucleated settlements, the same ground was worked every year.
    Villages nucleated around the late thirteenth and fourteenth centuries in the Kinai, and a century or so later in the east and on the southwestern island of Kyushu. In those villages the extensive farming of the old unstable land, which spread its labor over ground chosen anew each year, gave way to intensive farming of stable ground.
  • When did the last paddy stop resting?
    One rice historian, giving their own view in an interview, dates it to the early modern period: once the lords' land surveys taxed paddy by its area, resting a plot cost its holder, and the same paddy was cropped every year. Historians of medieval farming place the change centuries earlier, from the Kamakura period on in the advanced farming regions.
