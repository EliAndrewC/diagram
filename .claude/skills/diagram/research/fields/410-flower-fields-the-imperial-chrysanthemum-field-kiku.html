Flower fields: the Imperial chrysanthemum field (kiku)

The chrysanthemum came to Japan from China as a medicine and an ornament, and became the emblem of the imperial house. The court kept its festival on the ninth day of the ninth month, and in the Edo period, before 1868, growers bred it into many distinct lines. In China the festival had long been a city's show of the flower.

Flowers were also grown to sell, on the edge of Edo, the largest city: nursery villages there raised chrysanthemums, and one of them had some eighty nurseries at its height. No page we read describes a field of the flower kept for a ruler or a court, or flowers grown for a shrine in a plot of their own.

  • The chrysanthemum became Japan's imperial emblem by custom.
    In the Kamakura period the retired emperor Go-Toba took the flower as his own mark; later emperors carried it on, and by custom it became fixed as the imperial crest.
  • It came from China as a medicine and an ornament, and has long been an offering flower.
    The flower reached Japan as a medicinal herb and an ornamental plant, and in Japan it has long been used as an altar flower and an offering flower.
  • The court kept the chrysanthemum festival into the Meiji period.
    The Choyo festival, on the ninth day of the ninth month of the old calendar, was held until the Meiji period, and lives on as the imperial garden party for viewing the flower.
  • Its great breeding began in the Edo period.
    Breeding took off from the Edo period, above all from the end of the seventeenth century, with distinct groups of varieties in Edo, Ise, Kyoto and Kumamoto.
  • In twelfth-century China the festival filled a capital with the flower.
    A memoir of Kaifeng, the Northern Song capital, says that at the Double Ninth the city viewed chrysanthemums of several named kinds, that there was nowhere without them, and that the wine shops bound them into doorways.
  • Was a field of chrysanthemums ever kept for a ruler or a court?
    No page we read describes one.

Around Edo, flowers were a market crop, and the record of it reaches back into the Edo period itself.

  • Somei's nurserymen grew chrysanthemums near Edo by the early nineteenth century.
    Somei village, now Komagome in Tokyo, was a home of nurserymen, whom a catalog of Edo's plants and products published in 1824 named as a source of primroses, azaleas and chrysanthemums.
  • Sugamo grew chrysanthemums in some eighty nurseries at its height.
    A guide to Edo's flowers of 1827 put chrysanthemums at Sugamo, "where there are nurseries here and there". Sugamo's growing began with the flower beds of the mid-Edo period and had three great booms in the mid-to-late period, at their height some eighty nurseries.
  • Horikiri's gardens for viewing irises began, it is said, at the end of the Edo period.
    The Katsushika city history says so. Horikiri-en, a later iris garden begun in the Meiji period, was turned into paddy in 1942 to grow food in the war.
  • How early did the Boso peninsula send flowers to Edo?
    By the middle of the Edo period, on a record the city page for the peninsula does not name: it tells of narcissus from there sold to Edo's samurai houses and townhouses, and reads the senior councillor (roju) Matsudaira Sadanobu's travel diary of 1792 as showing narcissus already grown in quantity around Hota and Motona. Lilies for cut flowers and export bulbs followed from 1886, and the page dates the first success at flower growing in the Awa district to the Taisho period.

In China the chrysanthemum is grown today as a medicinal crop, and how far back that growing goes is not settled.

  • Tongxiang in Zhejiang grows the chrysanthemum as a crop today.
    Its dried flower head is one of the province's traditional medicinal herbs, sold across the country.
  • How old is Tongxiang's chrysanthemum growing?
    Tradition has officials of the Southern Song settling there and planting it in gardens, but no page we could read dates Tongxiang's fields of the flower before modern times. So the Chinese case is today's, and the market growing this record rests on is Edo's.
  • Did an ordinary town grow flowers for the market, or for its shrine?
    Not on any page we read. Every market case found is on the edge of a great city, Edo, and none is of the ring of farmland around an ordinary provincial town; nothing read describes flowers grown for a shrine or a temple in a plot of their own.
  • How large was a flower field?
    No page we read gives the size of any historical flower field or plot; the only areas found are of today's parks.
