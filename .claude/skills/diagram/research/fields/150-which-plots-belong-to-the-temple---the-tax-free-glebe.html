Which plots belong to the temple - the tax-free glebe

Sources: the GM's setting notes on village headmen and the allocation of the common fields; warichi-kotobank and warichi-seido-kotobank (a village re-allotting its land, and why the lots lay scattered), jochi-kotobank (temple fields exempted from tax); the drawing choice below is this project's, made on a rendered comparison.

A village's common fields are doled out by the headman in non-contiguous strips held in usufruct, so a household's holding is scattered rather than blocked - which is the same fragmentation the attested plot sizes above show from the other direction. The religious figure's allocation is tax-free and law-bounded to about two households' worth. This rests on the GM's setting notes rather than on the historical record.

How this compares with Japan. Allotment by the village has a real counterpart, though a regional one: some Edo-period villages divided their farmland among the cultivators for a term and then divided it again, to even out the tax and the quality of the soil, chiefly where the harvest was unsteady, as on land the rivers damaged. Such a lot lay in pieces by design: the land was sorted into groups by paddy or dry field, soil, terrain and flood risk, each group was split into as many parts as there were shares, and each share took one part from every group. Tax-free temple land is real as well: the precincts, fields and house land of temples and shrines were exempted from tax and levies, either left out of the village's assessment or entered and then exempted. Where in the village a temple's exempt fields lay, and how large they were, the sources read do not say, so the scatter drawn below follows the setting's allotment, not an attested temple holding.

The drawing choice, and the one declined. Where a map marks the tax-free plots they are tinted vermillion and outlined: high contrast against the greens, and the shrine's own sacred color. A thin gold outline was tried first and declined - it blended into the tan bunds and was hard to pick out at map view.

The rule the map follows: marking the tax-free plots is OPTIONAL. A village map that marks none is correct and complete. A map that marks them marks two or three, scattered non-contiguously across the common fields rather than blocked together, in vermillion - and if it marks any, it marks that many, because the allocation is bounded by law and a larger one would say something about the temple that the setting does not.
