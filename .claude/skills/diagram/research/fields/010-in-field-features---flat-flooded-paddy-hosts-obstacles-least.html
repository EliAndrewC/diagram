In-field features - flat flooded paddy hosts obstacles least

Sources: ryobosei-jawiki, henriot-shanghai-graves, fengshui-enwiki, tameike-jawiki

  • What the research found. In the rice south, feng shui put graves on the highest ground, away from water and on land otherwise worth little for farming, so the dead go on the slope, not the wet center; and ponds were made in hollows and low wet ground, so a small pond in a low pocket or a header tameike belongs in the wet middle.

    What the maps assume (a guess). The rest of the table is this page's reading, not a sourced claim: that flat, flooded valley-bottom paddy, as the most valuable and most intensively worked ground, hosts non-rice obstacles least of any field type; that feng shui wants a backing hill and a view of water downslope; that rock outcrops belong to terraces (bedrock the risers wrap around) and are absent on alluvial valley, polder and delta dike-pond ground; and that a half-moon pond before a lineage hall is a third kind of in-field pond. No publicly readable source stating these was found in the 2026-07 pass, and none was searched for again in 2026-09. The maps follow a per-archetype table of which features each field type may host.

  • The in-field grave island (GM 2026-07-20: both placements acceptable). The hillside grave is not the only southern form: villagers in the Yangzi delta also buried their dead one by one out in the open fields, while in parts of Japan (the Tenryu basin in Shizuoka, a district of Narusawa in Yamanashi) burial graves are reported to have once stood on private land such as the ground beside the bunds. The evidence, and what the maps make of it, is under Are there really graves out in the middle of the fields? How often a map draws the island is a choice made for the maps, not a figure from any source.
