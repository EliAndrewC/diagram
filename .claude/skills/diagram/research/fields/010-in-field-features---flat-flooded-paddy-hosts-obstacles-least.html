In-field features - flat flooded paddy hosts obstacles least

Sources: ryobosei-jawiki, fengshui-enwiki, tameike-jawiki (the plains pond dug into farmland, and how many ponds Japan has), inamino-saraike (the plains pond against the valley pond, and the smallest ponds), kagawa-tameike-data (the densest pond country, as the upper bound)

  • What the research found. In southern China feng shui put the graves on the highest hills, far from water and on land not worth farming, so the dead go on the slope, not the wet center of the paddy; and in the plains a pond was made by embanking a hollow or low wet ground, so small OPEN-WATER ponds (a low pocket, or a tameike serving as header reservoir) are, on this page's reading, the one thing that genuinely belongs among flat, flooded fields. That flat, flooded valley-bottom paddy hosts non-rice obstacles LEAST, being the most valuable and most intensively worked ground, is this page's reasoning, which no source states. That rock outcrops are a TERRACE feature (bedrock the risers wrap around), absent on alluvial valley or polder fields and delta dike-pond, is a guess, which nothing read confirms. The half-moon pond before a hall is the crescent pond the Homesteads page describes, and its record found no readable source for it. The maps follow a per-archetype table of which features each field type may host.

  • A deliberate deviation (GM 2026-07-20). The in-field grave ISLAND (the "graves among the paddy" look) is held to be a north-China-dry-plain signature, with only a partial Japanese parallel, NOT the rice-south default (which is margin/slope). The GM accepted both placements, so it is drawn rarely (about 30% of valley-bottom, terraced, and narrow valley-floor fields) as a deliberate departure, not an oversight. That rate is a choice made for the maps, not a figure from any source. The evidence for graves standing out among the paddy is thin: the Japanese passage places burials beside the bunds, at a field's edge rather than out in a plot, and no page read gives the north-China dry-plain half.

  • How often a field keeps a pond or a rock - the rates are guesses. What the record can say is where a pond among the paddy comes from and how common ponds were overall. In the plains a pond was made by embanking a hollow or low wet ground and digging its bottom down, the "dish pond"; in the hills a pond was a dammed valley instead, often three or four in steps up one valley. Ponds ranged from several hectares down to one about 10 m square owned and kept by an individual, and when the good sites were used up, farmland on the flat was itself dug out to make dish ponds in Sanuki. Japan has well over a hundred thousand ponds, the greatest number of them built in the Edo period alongside the new fields. Kagawa, on the dry Sanuki plain, has the most ponds for its area of any prefecture - over twelve thousand, about half of them holding under 1,000 tonnes - and before its modern canal drew 70% of its farm water from them. So how often a field keeps a pond is a degree along a continuum, from a valley whose stream waters every plot to the dense pond country of a dry plain like Sanuki; it is not a choice between forms (this page's reading of the figures above).

    No page read gives a rate per field or per village, and none speaks of rocks left standing in a paddy at all. The maps' rates are therefore guesses, disclosed as such: a field on low ground in the pond-hosting field types keeps a pond a little over half the time; a terraced field always keeps one to three rock outcrops, and a narrow strip of paddy along a valley floor does so about half the time.
