Temple fields (jochi)

Before 1868, in the Edo period, the land of Japan's temples and shrines, their precincts, their fields and their house land, could be free of tax and levies: the lord exempted it. Such exempt land was called jochi.

In the setting our maps are drawn for, each village district keeps a single country monk on one plot of tax-free land; this rests on the setting's own notes, not on the historical record. The village's common fields are given out by the headman in strips held in usufruct, so a household's holding lies scattered rather than in one block.

  • Exempt land was either never assessed or assessed and then excused.
    Some temple and shrine land was never measured at the land survey, so it never entered the village's assessment. Other land had its yield entered in the survey register and was then exempted.
  • Where in a village did a temple's fields lie, and how large were they?
    No page we read says. The sources give the exemption and a table of the temple and shrine lands of the late Edo period, but neither the size of a village temple's fields nor where they lay in the village; the scatter of a re-allotted share is described, not that of temple land. The setting's notes give the monk's plot no size either.

Whether a temple's fields lay in one block or in pieces is not recorded, but how a village's ordinary farmland could lie in pieces is, and it is the real counterpart of the setting's allotment by the headman.

  • Some Edo-period villages re-allotted their farmland among the cultivators for a term.
    The land was divided by the number of cultivators, farmed for a fixed period and divided again when the term ended, to even out the tax and the quality of the soil. It was practiced where the harvest was unsteady, as on land the rivers damaged.
  • Where a village re-divided its farmland by lot, each share lay in pieces by design.
    The village sorted its land into groups by paddy or dry field, soil, terrain and how often it flooded; each group was split into as many parts as there were shares, and each share took one part from every group, the shares then drawn by lot.
