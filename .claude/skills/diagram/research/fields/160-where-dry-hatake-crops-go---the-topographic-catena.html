Where dry (hatake) crops go - the topographic catena

Sources: satoyama-enwiki (the mosaic and the foothill-to-flat border zone), satoyama-jawiki (the woodland's slopes, its cutting cycle and what it had become), shizen-teibo-jawiki and shizen-teibo-kotobank (houses and dry fields on the natural levee, and why), kohai-shicchi-jawiki (paddy in the backswamp), dankyu-kotobank (dry fields on the terraces), senjochi-jawiki and senjochi-kotobank (the fan's dry middle and its spring-fed toe)

WHERE dry crops go: the satoyama, the border zone between the mountain foothills and the flat land, holds paddy, dry fields, woods, grassland and ponds together in one mosaic. Which ground each kind of field takes follows the lie of the land, a topographic CATENA: DRY fields (hatake) on the higher, well-drained ground the water cannot command, and paddy on the low wet ground between. On a river plain the old settlements stand first on the natural levees, the low ridges a river builds along its banks, and the levees are farmed as dry fields, because a levee is sandy and higher and drier than the ground around it; the wet backswamp behind it was cleared as paddy. River terraces, flat, high and dry, were taken early for settlements and dry fields, left unworked where water was short, and in many places turned to paddy only once irrigation works reached them. On an alluvial fan the middle, where the river sinks into the gravel, is too short of water for paddy and was often left as coppice, in Japan long left as wild ground and brought into dry fields and orchards only from the end of the Edo period, while the spring-fed toe was settled early with paddy beside it. The lower slopes of the hills are the last of these positions, in the Yoshino example below. In the Yoshino mountains the satoyama is the dry fields and woods on the slopes around the village; its broadleaf woods were coppice cut back every ten to twenty years for firewood and charcoal, though by the early modern period most satoyama had become red pine, grass or bare hill. So dry fields are NOT one neat strip: they sit in SEVERAL positions, and on levee and terrace alike they share the raised ground with the houses. That the dry plots lie above all AROUND each house (each family has some paddy and some hatake - the household's dry plots near its home) is this page's reading, and one source read puts paddy round houses built on slightly higher ground instead.
