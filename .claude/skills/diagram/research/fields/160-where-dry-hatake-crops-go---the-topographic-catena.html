Where dry (hatake) crops go - the topographic catena

Sources: satoyama-enwiki (the mosaic and the foothill-to-flat border zone), satoyama-jawiki (the woodland's slopes, its cutting cycle and what it had become), shizen-teibo-jawiki (the settlement and its dry fields on the levee), kohai-shicchi-jawiki (the paddy in the wet ground behind it), sanson-jawiki (houses on the higher ground with paddy round them), kateisaien-jawiki (the household's own plot by its house). The catena itself - which ground each kind of field takes - is this page's reading; no source read places them.

WHERE dry crops go: the satoyama, the border zone between the mountain foothills and the flat land, holds paddy, dry fields, woods, grassland and ponds together in one mosaic. This page reads that mosaic as a topographic CATENA - irrigated paddy on the flat valley bottom / plain, DRY fields (hatake) on the HIGHER, well-drained ground the water cannot command (river terraces, natural levees / micro-highs threading the plain, the edges of alluvial fans, lower slopes, AND the slightly raised ground the homesteads also sit on, though not the plot beside a house) - an order no source read gives. In the Yoshino mountains the satoyama is the dry fields and woods on the slopes around the village; its broadleaf woods were coppice cut back every ten to twenty years for firewood and charcoal, though by the early modern period most satoyama had become red pine, grass or bare hill. So dry fields are NOT one neat strip: they sit in SEVERAL positions, and on an alluvial lowland the old settlements and the dry fields share the natural levee's slightly raised ground, while the wet ground behind it is the paddy - though one source read puts paddy round houses built on slightly higher ground instead.

The plot a household works beside its own house is the yashikibatake, the ground a household cultivates for its own consumption, which this record reads as the kitchen bed, a few tsubo of daily greens (How big was a dooryard garden?). No page read puts a household's grain or beans in a plot at its house; the places the pages give dry fields are the ones above. So the map draws the dry crops where the paddy water stops: the hem of dry plots along the supply canal at the field's high edge. It lays no grain plot beside a house.
