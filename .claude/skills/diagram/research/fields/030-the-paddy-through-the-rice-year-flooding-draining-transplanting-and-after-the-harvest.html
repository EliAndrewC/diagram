The paddy through the rice year: flooding, draining, transplanting and after the harvest

A rice paddy is not under water all season. Today's guidance keeps it flooded about an inch deep, drains it about a month after transplanting until the mud cracks, floods it again by turns, and lets it off for good a week before the harvest. The shape of that year is old - a Chinese farm manual of 544 already lets the water out to firm the roots and again before the rice ripens - and Japanese farm books of the Edo period describe the midsummer drain, done in some places. Every depth, though, is modern: no older figure was found.

After the harvest the threshed straw was stacked in ricks on the reaped paddy or its bunds. A paddy that drained well, and could be manured, was then worked as a dry field for a winter crop of barley or wheat; a wet one lay to its single crop of rice.

The year opened with transplanting. A survey of transplanting customs made in 1975-76, reported for Wakayama, found it done by a whole hamlet or district within a set span, not plot by plot at each household's own pace.

  • In some places a hamlet settled its whole year's transplanting schedule in advance.
    A survey of customary transplanting made in 1975-76, reported for Wakayama, found places where the entire schedule of that year's transplanting was settled beforehand by consultation, and some that settled even the menu of the meals for the period.
  • On the plains, where the crop before the rice left a short window, transplanting took seven to ten days.
    There the season fell in the last third of June, around the summer solstice, on account of the preceding crop; because the window was short the transplanting was short too, and it was done mainly with hired labor.
  • Where valley water could be drawn at any hour, transplanting often ran past twenty days.
    In the mountain districts of central and southern Wakayama, hamlets of 20 to 30 households did much of their work together, but the area was wide and the valley water could be drawn at any time, so the transplanting period was long and not seldom exceeded twenty days.
  • Households lent one another hands, yui, by using the difference in their transplanting dates.
    The survey describes yui as help given by making use of the difference in transplanting dates, so, on this page's reading, households whose dates coincided could not lend one another hands.
  • Were a district's paddies all at one stage of growth at once?
    Largely, where the window was short - this page's reconstruction. The survey puts the short window down to the calendar of the crop before the rice, and the long one to water that could be drawn at any hour; the labor exchange, which works through differing dates, spread the planting rather than gathering it. At any moment, then, a district's paddies stood largely at one growth stage, with a minor spread from early and late varieties; how wide that spread was, no page we read gives.

Through the growing season the water rose and fell. Japan's present-day extension guidance gives the stages and their depths.

  • From transplanting until the seedlings root, the water is kept a little deep, 3-4 cm (~1-2 in).
    The slightly deep water guards the young plants against cold and wind.
  • Once they root, it is kept shallow, 2-3 cm (~1 in), so that it warms.
    The shallow water raises the water temperature and brings the tillers on early.
  • About a month after transplanting the paddy is drained until it cracks: the mid-season drain, nakaboshi.
    The field is dried until small cracks show on its surface and a light footprint marks it; a drain too light leaves too many stems, thin culms, and a crop that falls flat before harvest. Cracking the soil also lets the water pass through it better.
  • After the drain the paddy is flooded and let dry by turns, a few days each.
    The alternation keeps the roots sound and the ground firm enough to be walked and worked.
  • From heading, when the ear emerges, the paddy stands under 2-3 cm (~1 in) of water for twenty days.
    It is not let run dry in those twenty days.
  • Through ripening the water comes and goes again.
    The watering is intermittent through the ripening, to keep the soil moist and the grain from cracking.
  • When is the water let off for good?
    The two guides differ. One's season chart ends with the final drain, rakusui, 5 to 7 days before the reaping; the other drains the field about 25 days after heading, as the husks turn yellow.
  • Deeper water, 10 cm (~4 in) and 20 cm (~8 in), is a guard against cold, not the usual depth.
    The same guidance gives 10 cm (~4 in) at panicle formation and 20 cm (~8 in) at booting only when the temperature threatens to fall, to protect the young panicle.
  • Why is a bund a foot high over an inch of water?
    Today's standard bund stands 30 cm (~1 ft) high, and the old ones a few inches (at Bunds between the paddies (aze)). The ridge has to hold the deep water of a cold spell, keep something in hand in heavy rain, and be walked on - a GUESS, reasoned rather than found.

Rice farmers in China were writing the same season down many centuries earlier, though without its depths.

  • A sixth-century Chinese farm manual drains the paddy to firm the roots, and again before the rice ripens.
    The Qimin Yaoshu, believed completed in 544, cuts the old weeds under the water when the seedlings are seven or eight cun tall, weeds again as they grow, then lets the water out and suns the roots to make them firm; it waters as the season runs wet or dry, and takes the water off again as the rice nears ripeness. That is the shape of today's calendar: a drain in the growing season, water as the weather needs it, and a last drain before the harvest.
  • A twelfth-century manual of the lower Yangtze drains the paddies one at a time, from the bottom up, to weed them.
    Chen Fu's manual, completed in 1149 and a source for the wet rice of Jiangnan, first stores the water at the topmost plot so that none is lost, then lets the plots dry from the bottom up and weeds each as it dries.
  • The same manual keeps a new seedbed's water shallow in sunshine, so the sun warms it.
    Not so shallow that the skin of the mud dries hard, nor so deep that the seed is soaked through and yellows; he adds a little water in a downpour, lets it off in a sudden wind, and says a wide bund is what lets the depth be kept right. Warming the water is also the reason today's guidance gives for keeping it shallow.
  • Was any depth of paddy water recorded before modern times?
    None was found. Neither Chinese manual gives one, and searches in Japanese and Chinese found none. So every depth above is modern extension guidance. That the plant's need for shallow, warm water was the same in older centuries is reasoning, not evidence, and is not asserted here.

In Japan the midsummer drain has a history of its own, told in a 2011 review of the practice.

  • Several farm books of the Edo period describe the midsummer drain, so it was done in some places by then.
    When the drain came to Japan is not known, but a 1964 study points to several Edo-period farm books that describe it.
  • It is also called doyoboshi, drying at the summer doyo.
    The water is let out of the paddy to make the rice stand up to the wind and fill its ears.
  • Today the drain lasts about a week.
    About a week is the present-day guide, varying by region, not an Edo figure.
  • Before the Second World War the drain had spread only to some regions with plentiful water.
    A farming manual of 1933 records it, as doyoboshi, in 22 of Japan's modern administrative regions, but before the war it had reached only some regions where water was plentiful.
  • After the Second World War it spread fast, and by 1966 more than half of Japan's paddy was drained in midsummer.
    The share was 59%, and where it was not done, the commonest reason given was a poor water supply.
  • Today terraced paddies whose water is unreliable are reported not to drain at all.
    Three studies of 2001 to 2010 report it.
  • One agronomist calls the drain a modern technique.
    The review cites him holding that it belongs to the age when water could be used freely and chemical fertilizer came in.
  • Was the drain also confined to well-watered places before modern times?
    Probably. The review dates the confinement to the decades before the war; of the Edo period it says only that the drain was done in some places. Carrying the limit back before 1868 is this page's inference, a GUESS.

Once the rice was reaped and threshed, the paddy had its straw to dispose of and, in some places, a second crop to carry.

  • The threshed straw was stacked into ricks, warazuka, on the reaped paddy or on its bunds.
    The bundles were piled into a cylinder; the rick is a season word of autumn. Its form differed from region to region; one was a cylinder built round a pole at its center.
  • A rick's straw went to compost, straw goods, fodder and fuel.
    It was chopped for compost, or made into straw goods such as bales and rope. In Hirogawa, in Wakayama, the threshed straw stacked in the paddy went to fodder for cattle and horses, to manure, and sometimes to fuel.
  • How long did a rick stand?
    Into the next year, in the one account found - of farm life in the Meiji, Taisho and early Showa periods; that an older village kept its straw the same way is this page's inference, a GUESS. How far into the next year a rick lasted, no page we read says. That a rick on the paddy floor was gone before the paddy was flooded and planted is this page's reasoning; whether one on a bund still stood at midsummer, no page we read says.
  • Where did a rick stand on a paddy sown to winter barley?
    The dictionary gives both places, the reaped paddy and its bunds; where the paddy carried barley, a rick on its floor would stand in the barley's way, so the bund is the likelier place. That is this page's reasoning, a GUESS.
  • On many paddies, rice in summer was followed by barley or wheat in winter: nimosaku, two crops a year.
    The paddy was flooded in summer for rice, then the water was let off in winter and it was worked as a dry field for barley or wheat.
  • Chinese milk vetch, rapeseed, broad beans and peas were among the other crops sown after the rice.
    The same entry lists them among the paddy's back crops. The vetch, renge, grown to feed the paddy's soil, is at Rice paddies and their plots (suiden).
  • The rice-and-barley year is old: recorded from the middle of the Heian period, and fairly widespread by the Kamakura period.
    In 1264 the Kamakura shogunate ordered the provinces not to tax the winter barley.
  • In the Edo period it spread further, above all around Kyoto and Osaka and along the Inland Sea coast west of them.
    There better seed, bought fertilizer and irrigation works came together.
  • Before the Second World War the rice-and-barley year stopped at a line across central Japan, from the north of the Kanto plain to Toyama on the Sea of Japan coast.
    North of it, cold, snow and wet paddies stood in the way.
  • Barley could not grow in a wet paddy.
    Barley is weak to damp and cannot be grown in a wet paddy.
  • In the medieval period, only places near crowded districts could manure a second crop.
    Two crops a year need twice the fertilizer of one, and enough could be had only near densely settled districts, so remote places grew no winter barley.
  • The paddy that takes a winter crop is the dry paddy, the kanden.
    It drains well, dries to a field when no water is let in, and barley and other winter crops are easy on it. Its opposite, the wet paddy that never dries, is at The wettest plots are their own kind of ground.

For rape, one of those crops sown after the rice, the calendars found are modern: from Japan's vegetable growers, and from Taiwan and southern China.

  • Rape grown for its flower shoots is sown in autumn and cut in early spring.
    Rape grown for its flower shoots, nanohana, is sown in autumn, carried through the winter, and its flower stalks cut from early March to early April.
  • In Taiwan some rice farmers sow rape on the paddy after the rice, as green manure.
    They scatter its seed after the second rice crop is cut, and plow it in before the next year's rice is transplanted.
  • In Jiangxi, in southern China, the rape today comes off in April or early May, just as the rice goes in.
    The same page gives no month for the rice harvest.
  • Did rape ever stand beside growing rice?
    Not in the calendars found: in each, rape is sown after the rice is cut and is off before the next rice goes in, so rice and rape are the two halves of one rotation on the same plot. The calendars are modern; that the rotation held before modern times is this page's reconstruction. That the spring plot showed yellow rape against bare stubble is a GUESS: no page we read describes it.
