The settlement tiers: what each is, and what its map states

The findings behind the settlement tiers on these maps, the decisions they drove, and - where no generator draws the feature yet - the rules a map follows.

What are the five kinds of settlement, and how big is each?

Sources: l7r-median-domain (the domain's settlement ladder, the household of five, and the Place Names convention); the setting's own budget notes (the caste tables each tier is sized from). This entry rests on the setting's canon rather than on historical work, and is stated as canon

The maps draw five sizes of place, and the differences are not only of scale - each tier keeps institutions the one below it does not.

  • A hamlet is 10 to 20 households, 50 to 100 inhabitants, usually working one to three small fields. Below ten the place is an outlying farmstead or two; above twenty it is a small village and starts growing the things a hamlet must not have. A hamlet has no headman of its own - it falls under the headman of the village district it belongs to - no shrine, and no tax-free plot.
  • A village is 40 to 100 households, 200 to 500 inhabitants, around 350 in the ordinary case, and it does have a headman, a shrine and its two or three tax-free plots.
  • A town is a county seat of about 1,200 inhabitants in roughly 238 households: about 156 of them farm, and the other 82 are about 24 merchant, 29 laborer, 13 servant, 12 burakumin and 4 samurai households. A town is the first tier that is not peasant-only, and the state is present in it as the county magistrate in a walled manor rather than as a peasant headman.
  • A provincial city is a provincial governor's seat of about 3,000 inside the walls in about 600 households - and, structurally, no farmers at all (below).
  • A domain capital is the daimyo's castle-town seat, about 12,360 inhabitants, which inherits the whole provincial-city tier and overrides it.

The household itself is about five, over two or three generations under one roof, which is where every population figure on these maps comes from.

Villages and hamlets are peasant-only: no samurai lives in one. The samurai of a county live in its town, and the country estates of a city's senior houses stand outside that city's walls.

The rule the map follows: a hamlet draws 10 to 20 households and no headman's house, no shrine and no tax-free plot; a village draws 40 to 100 households with a headman's house (the largest on the sheet), a shrine and two or three tax-free plots; a town draws its 82 non-farm households at their documented counts - 24 merchant, 29 laborer, 13 servant, 12 burakumin, 4 samurai - a magistrate's walled manor, and as much of its ~156-household farming cohort as the sheet holds; a city draws about 600 urban households and no farmers; a capital draws about 12,360 inhabitants' worth. No samurai house stands in a hamlet or a village.

How many inhabitants does a map's house count stand for?

Sources: l7r-median-domain (five to a household); the setting's own budget notes (which castes a city's figure counts)

A settlement's population is its DWELLINGS times about five, never its buildings. A town or a city draws a great deal that houses nobody: shops, the ministries and the governor's yamen, flophouses, storehouses, the furniture inside a gate. Count those and the place looks half again as populous as it is. So ten kinds of building on these maps are homes and everything else is not: a laborer's dwelling and the larger house of a master laborer, a servant household, a burakumin household, a samurai house and a senior samurai's larger one, a merchant's small house, the merchant's house-over-shop and a rich merchant's large house, and a monk's household by a temple precinct. The monks' households are real resident families and count as housing, but they are deliberately outside the caste bands: clergy are not a lay caste.

What counts as a home differs above the village. A town counts its drawn farmhouses among its dwellings, because those farmhouses are part of its own farming cohort. A city counts no farmhouse at all. A provincial city's figure is its urban castes, and the farmers who feed it - whether in the surrounding village districts or in the unusual agricultural district some cities keep inside the wall - belong to those districts and counties and are counted there. So a city of about 3,000 places about 600 dwellings, and its total building count is higher than that, because the shops and the civic ground are extra.

The rule the map follows: a map's stated population is its dwellings times five. Shops, civic and government buildings, storehouses, barns and gate furniture are not dwellings and are never counted. At town scale the farmhouses on the sheet count; at city scale no farmhouse counts, whether it stands outside the wall or inside it.

Is every household in a hamlet actually drawn?

Sources: the GM's own wording of the village floor, "~70 households ... at least 50 houses"; this project's measurement of the seventeen shipped settlement maps

Essentially yes, at hamlet and village scale. These are to-scale maps, so a declared household is expected to have a roof on the sheet: the placer is held to between 0.85 and 1.05 occupied farmhouses for every household declared, and a hamlet that declares fifteen and seats twelve is a failure to be fixed rather than a map to be shipped. Measured across the shipped maps, sixteen of seventeen sit at exactly 1.00 - one house per household - and the seventeenth at 0.94.

The GM's figure that used to be read as a ratio is a floor: about seventy households want at least fifty houses drawn, which is a minimum on what a sheet may show, not a statement that three households in ten share a roof with another. Extended families genuinely do share a roof - that is why a household averages five over two or three generations - but the sharing is inside the household, not between two of them. Abandoned houses do not count toward the households: they are former homes.

Where the two numbers do differ, the map's own card says so rather than leaving a reader to divide: one sheet drawing 66 farmhouses for 70 households names both, because "66 farmhouses, population ~350" otherwise invites the reader to divide and get 5.3 to a house.

The rule the map follows: a hamlet or village map seats between 0.85 and 1.05 occupied farmhouses per declared household, and aims at one apiece. When the placer runs short it widens the ground it draws candidate seats from rather than re-rolling the map, so the houses already seated stay where they are. Abandoned houses are not counted against the household total, and a map whose drawn houses and declared households differ states both.

What does the page beside a map say about its size?

Sources: the GM's rulings of 2026-08-29; l7r-median-domain (towns, villages and hamlets as separate lines of the domain); the setting's own budget notes (the town's household breakdown)

A hamlet and a village state their farmhouse count, exactly, with no tilde. The GM, 2026-08-29: "You should not use a ~ for the number of farmhouses, because that actually IS an exact map feature - the number of farmhouses listed should be whatever is actually displayed on the map itself for hamlets and villages." Every household is drawn, so the count is a statement about the sheet in front of the reader, who could sit and count them. The population that follows it is an inference from that count - five to a household - and carries the tilde.

A town and a city state their non-farm dwelling count instead, also exact, and no farmhouse count at all. The GM's reason is that both "are surrounded by significant farm fields and farmer populations, which are deliberately not all rendered on the map. Therefore, that number should not be included." Their populations are then explained rather than left bare, because the two figures mean different things and a reader would otherwise take them for the same. A city's excludes farmers entirely: its figure is urban dwellings times five, and the farms on its sheet belong to village districts and counties that are counted separately. A town's is the town's own roughly 238 households, some 156 of which farm the fields around it - so the figure is about 1,200 while the sheet draws every one of the 82 non-farm dwellings and only a sample of the farmhouses.

The 1,200 is the town's own, not its county's. The breakdown adds up on its own - 156 farming plus 24 merchant, 29 laborer, 13 servant, 12 burakumin and 4 samurai households - one town map draws exactly those 82 non-farm dwellings, and the domain's own tables list towns, villages and hamlets as separate lines summing to the domain. Reading a town's figure as taking in the farmers of its county's village districts would therefore double-count about 172,000 inhabitants across the domain. The GM, 2026-08-29: "The default population of a town is already 1,200 So you can make that the population for all of the towns which we have already generated and document that. Though, again, that is the total population including farmers, which means that the sum total of that population is not visible on the map, which is intentional."

It is therefore the tier's figure rather than the sheet's, and that is a disclosed departure from what the rest of a map's card does: everywhere else the card describes the drawing, and here it describes the place the drawing is a sample of.

The rule the map follows: a hamlet's or a village's card states the exact number of farmhouses drawn, untilded, and a population of about five per household. A town's or a city's card states the exact number of non-farm dwellings drawn and no farmhouse count; a town states the tier's own ~1,200 and says that it includes farmers who are not all drawn, and a city states a figure that excludes farmers altogether. A card whose drawn houses and declared households differ names both.

Which district and county does this settlement belong to?

Sources: l7r-median-domain (the Place Names convention: a village and its district carry one name)

A village IS its district, and a map never says otherwise. The GM, 2026-08-29: "the name of the village is the name of the village district ... So a village should never be listed as belonging to a village district with a different name. In fact, there is no reason to ever say what village district a village belongs to." A village and the district it heads share one name, always. What a village's card does say is its county, which every village belongs to.

A hamlet is the other case: it genuinely belongs to a district named for a different place - the village at the district's head - and it says so, which is also how a reader learns which village the hamlet's lanes lead to.

The shared name is a deliberate departure from how such places were actually named, and the maps do not flag it: it is set out in the GM's own core materials and has been reviewed with the players, so a reader of these maps already knows.

The rule the map follows: a village map names its county and never names a village district; a hamlet map names the district it belongs to, and the direction the district's village lies in.

How far is it across this map? The scale, tier by tier

Sources: the GM's decision of 2026-07 and this project's 2026-07 audit of the drawn maps' real areas; the anchors that fix the compound scale are on the Mode A scale entry

Each tier is drawn at a fixed number of real feet to the pixel, and the numbers are round on purpose, so that a reader can measure a distance off the sheet in their head: one foot per pixel for a hamlet and a town, two for a village, three for a provincial city and a capital, and a third of a foot for a compound plan, which is the finest of the ladder. The reason for that particular ladder, and the real building complexes that bracket the compound figure, are on the Mode A scale entry linked above.

What the ladder bought was consistency in real feet. The audit that drove it found the dwellings already mutually consistent across every map, but town fields running four to eight times under their real area, and fixed-pixel constants - roads, streets, an inn, a stable yard, a storehouse - silently changing their real size from one tier to the next, simply because they were written in pixels. Every such constant is declared in real feet now and converted per map.

The one thing that is not to scale is the width of a thin line, and that is a map drawing convention. A five-foot alley at three feet to the pixel would be 1.7 pixels wide, which is invisible, so thin linework is floored at four pixels on the sheet. The floor only ever raises a line to legibility; nothing is inflated past it, and anything genuinely wider than the floor is drawn at its true width.

The rule the map follows: a hamlet and a town sheet are drawn at 1 ft per pixel, a village at 2, a provincial city and a domain capital at 3, and a compound plan at 1/3. Every size in the engine is declared in real feet and converted at the map's own scale, so a feature that is thirty feet across is thirty feet across on every tier. Thin linework is floored at 4 pixels of sheet width and is otherwise drawn true.

What must a map be told that its own ground cannot settle?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-08-11; l7r-median-domain (the clans and their patron Fortunes)

Three facts about a settlement cannot be worked out from its own geography, and each of them fixes a whole family of placements, so all three are settled before any line is drawn.

  1. Which way does the water flow? Everything downstream of that answer follows it: which end of the river takes the wharf, which way the drainage ditches discharge, which way a feeder is tapped so that the current turns into it, and where the nuisance trades stand relative to the drinking water. This is the first question at every tier, not only in a city.
  2. Which clan holds it? The clan's two patron Fortunes take the sovereign temples, and one of the two is drawn larger than every other temple on the map.
  3. Which direction is the Imperial capital? The samurai country estates stand on the side of the settlement that faces the capital - that is where a clan's senior houses want their land, on the road their lord and their peers travel - and nothing in the map's own ground says which side that is.

The third of these is the one the record was written for: a capital's country seats were drawn on the wrong flank because the bearing had never been asked for, and the GM ruled on 2026-08-11 that it is a fact of the same class as the other two - a single answer, given once at the start, that a dozen placements then obey.

The rule the map follows: the drainage bearing, the holding clan and the bearing of the Imperial capital are declared with the settlement itself, not left in a note, so that every later placement can be held to them. The estates face the capital; the larger of the clan's two patron temples outranks every other temple on the sheet; and the water runs the way it was declared to run.

When is a settlement allowed to break a rule?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-27 on the walled town's history; this project's own experience of building the first city and town maps

Some settlements are not ordinary, and a rule that is right for every planned seat can be wrong for one of them. A map may be excused a rule in writing, with the reason stated at length - never by quietly relaxing the rule for everybody.

The worked case is a walled town that was never planned as one (the GM, 2026-07-27). It stood unwalled, changed hands during a war between two Great Clans, and was walled in haste afterwards: the rampart was thrown around the town that was already there, so unlike a planned seat its enclosure was never sized to a population budget at all. Two consequences follow, and both cost it dwellings a planned town of the same footprint would have housed - a chrysanthemum field stands inside the walls for symbolic reasons, and there is no burakumin quarter, excluded for logistical ones. So its housing cannot come out to its declared population, and it is excused that arithmetic on exactly that ground and no other.

Reach for a waiver only when the settlement's premise conflicts with the rule - not when a map is merely hard to tune. The distinction is worth being strict about, because from the outside the two look identical. Another city also could not meet its declared population at first, but its oddity - eight temple precincts scattered through it by trade - turned out to be something the space budget could price honestly, so the answer there was a bigger wall, not a waiver. Fix the model first; waive only when the map is telling you something true about the place.

And the process lesson, which is the more valuable half: lock the rules in against ordinary settlements first. The first city and the first walled town were both drawn early and both are atypical, so for a long time the defaults were being bent to fit the exceptions. Build the normal cases until the rules are settled, then let the unusual ones earn their waivers.

The rule the map follows: a settlement that cannot meet a rule because of its own history is excused that one rule, with a written reason that names the history and what it cost; the excuse lapses when it stops excusing anything. A settlement that merely comes out awkward is re-planned instead - most often by growing its wall. New rules are calibrated against ordinary settlements, never against the unusual ones.

Who lives in a provincial city?

Sources: the setting's own budget notes (the city caste tables, which list no farmers at all); l7r-median-domain (the ranks of the provincial government)

A provincial city holds between 2,000 and 4,000 inside its walls, about 3,000 and 600 households in the ordinary case, and the mix is roughly 40% laborer, 20% servant, 25% merchant, 10% samurai and 5% burakumin households - about 240 laborer, 120 servant, 150 merchant, 60 samurai and 30 burakumin. There are structurally no farmer households in the count: the farmers who work the city's land live in the surrounding villages and hamlets and are counted in those totals.

The samurai tenth is the provincial government and its households: the governor, the six provincial ministers and their deputies, the clerks under them, and the bushi of the garrison - together with a small foreign Imperial sub-station of yoriki who audit the tariffs taken at the gates and answer to the Imperial ministry rather than to the governor.

The mix matters as much as the total, because a city that meets its population with the wrong castes reads as the wrong kind of place. The failure this guards against is a real one and mechanical: when the households are packed one caste after another, the last caste in the list gets whatever ground is left, which can be nothing - a city that asks for 445 laborers and then 50 servants places the laborers and has nowhere left for the servants. The servants live among the households they serve, so they are placed among them rather than after them.

The rule the map follows: a provincial city places about 600 households at roughly 40% laborer, 20% servant, 25% merchant, 10% samurai and 5% burakumin, each caste within about 30% of its share, and no farmer households at all. Servants are placed among the laborers and merchants they serve. A walled samurai estate is a household like any other, so the estates outside the wall and the merchant estates inside it count toward their castes and the ordinary houses need only make up the remainder.
