Martial training grounds and dojo

In Edo-period Japan, formal training in the sword and the spear belonged to the towns. Nearly every domain came to keep a school, the hanko, for its retainers' sons, where the martial arts were taught after letters, and many domains attached a practice hall to it; the two schools read on stood inside their castles. Private halls, kept by masters at their own houses, flourished late in the period. Before the middle of the period, practice was mostly held outdoors or on an earthen floor.

A domain's hall was a long single-story building: the one that survives whole, at Hagi, is about 124 by 35 ft, half board-floored for the sword and half earth-floored for the spear, and at the head of such a hall hung scrolls, not a shrine. China put the state's martial schooling in its seats of government too, and a county seat drilled its soldiers on a walled ground of several acres.

  • The domain school taught the martial arts after letters.
    Pupils entered at seven or eight, learned letters first and studied the martial arts afterward, and in the late Edo period swordsmanship was among the arts added. The sons of the domain's samurai were all enrolled, and some schools were later opened to the domain's other subjects.
  • Nearly every domain had a school from the middle of the eighteenth century.
    Domain schools were set up across the country from the Horeki era (1751-1764) on, 255 of them at their height, of the 261 domains still standing when the domains were abolished in 1871. They were built in the domain's own territory at its expense, with the partial exception of schools attached to a domain's residence in Edo.
  • Did a domain school stand in its castle town?
    The two read on did. Hagi's Meirinkan was opened inside the castle moats in 1719, and Mito's Kodokan was built inside the castle's third bailey. That every domain's school and its martial hall stood so is a GUESS: no page we read says where the schools stood in general.
  • Many domains attached a practice hall to their school.
    In populous Edo, pupils became so many that the small private practice rooms of before turned into large halls, and in the domains, too, many attached a practice hall to the domain school.
  • Practice halls took board and tatami floors from the middle of the Edo period.
    Before that, practice was often held outdoors or on an earthen floor, and at the end of the Warring States period masters traveled to their pupils and practiced in a private house, with no hall built for it. From the middle of the Edo period, as bamboo-sword practice took over, board-floored and tatami halls were built.
  • The one domain hall that survives whole, at Hagi, is about 124 by 35 ft.
    The Yubikan is the old school's fencing and spear halls, moved and enlarged: a single-story wooden building with a hip-and-gable roof of tile, 37.8 m (~124 ft) by 10.8 m (~35 ft), long north to south. Its north half is a board-floored fencing hall of 39 mats, its south half an earth-floored spear hall of 54, each with a place on its west side from which the lord watched, and a waiting room between them for his visits. The domain's men also fought bouts there with trainees from other domains.
  • How much practice floor did the Hagi halls hold?
    Roughly 650-765 sq ft of fencing floor and 900-1,060 sq ft of spear floor. The page gives the halls in mats only, and taking a mat as about 1.55 to 1.82 m² (~17-20 sq ft) is a GUESS.
  • The largest domain school split its martial arts among three halls.
    The Kodokan at Mito, on the largest grounds of any domain school in the country, about 105,000 m² (~26 acres), kept a fencing hall on the north, a spear hall between, and a hall for iai, jujutsu, the halberd and the rest on the south, with an open bout ground for martial training in the south garden.
  • The head of a hall held scrolls to the gods of the sword, not a shrine.
    A practice hall of the Edo period had a floor from which the master watched the practice, and on it hung scrolls naming the gods of Kashima and Katori, held to be the gods of the sword. The shrine shelf, the kamidana, was not in the traditional Edo-period hall: a Ministry of Education meeting of 1936 made it required in school halls, under State Shinto.

Beside the domains' halls stood private ones, the machi-dojo, where a master taught pupils who enrolled with him.

  • Private halls flourished late in the Edo period, across the country.
    Masters came to keep practice halls at their own houses in the Edo period, and the halls flourished above all in its last years, in every part of the country, townsmen and farmers training beside samurai.
  • Commoners could learn swordsmanship from the middle of the Edo period.
    Until then they could not, partly by prohibition; with protective gear and the bamboo sword it spread fast, and from the Tenpo era (1830-1844) on several thousand styles came into being, led by the three great halls of Edo.
  • The three great private halls of late Edo stood in Edo itself.
    They were founded in 1773, 1822 and 1826, all in Edo, a city of about a million by 1721. One of them is Chiba Shusaku's Genbukan, founded at Nihonbashi in 1822 and later moved to Kanda Otamagaike; the page gives no count of its pupils.
  • Did an Edo-period pupil pay a monthly fee?
    A modern dictionary defines a private hall as one whose enrolled pupils pay a monthly fee. Whether a pupil in the Edo period paid one, and when the boom in private halls ended, no page we read says.
  • How big was a private dojo?
    No page we read gives one's size.

In the countryside, the record finds practice on open ground rather than in halls.

  • A small domain's seat laid out a drill ground, not a hall.
    At the jin'ya of a small domain, the residence, offices, retainers' houses and storehouses were laid out with a drill ground inside an earth rampart and moat. A small domain's seat is not a county intendant's office, though the word jin'ya covers both.
  • Did a county intendant's office keep a martial hall?
    The buildings listed for an intendant's office, the daikansho, include none, but no page says it had none, so its absence is a GUESS from the silence.
  • Where did rural samurai train?
    At home in an earthen yard, in a hall cleared for practice, or on shrine grounds, once the city halls were built: this is a GUESS, drawn from where practice was held before the halls.

China put its martial schooling in the towns as well, and there the state's training ground was a drill ground rather than a hall.

  • An order of 1637, late in the Ming dynasty, required schools down to the county to enroll military students.
    The order was that the prefectural, departmental and county schools across the realm should all set up military students.
  • The military examination ran at the provincial, metropolitan and palace levels.
    The last was held in the Hall of Supreme Harmony, the emperor examining in person.
  • A Chinese county seat drilled on a walled ground.
    Every seat of county rank and above generally kept a drill ground, the jiaochang. Chongming's, founded in 1586, lay outside the east gate inside an earth wall, about 37.5 mu (~6 acres), with a drill hall of three bays at the middle of its north end, a rear hall behind and a tea hall beside, a general's platform in front and a gate in the south wall; a small drill ground lay inside the town too. The same page also calls the ground about one square li, which would be some ten times the figure in mu.
  • Chongming's drill hall was enlarged and repaired through the Qing.
    A regional commander enlarged the ground in 1658; in 1662 the commander Zhang Dazhi raised the hall by 3 chi and named it, and it was repaired again in 1799 and 1846.
  • How big was a county's drill hall?
    The hall that stood at Chongming until it was pulled down about 2000 was nine bays, 29 m (~95 ft), wide and 11.5 m (~38 ft) deep, but the page gives no date for that building. The drill hall of the emperor's own Jianrui camp at Xiangshan, built in 1748, is five bays wide, and no other page we read measures a county's drill hall from before 1912.
  • Did a Chinese county keep a training hall in its yamen, or private academies in its town?
    The pages that list a county yamen's buildings name no training hall, so that it had none is a GUESS from the silence. That a county seat had no dense network of private martial academies is a GUESS too.

What stood on an open practice ground is recorded for one Japanese tradition, and the racks that held weapons for others.

  • The swordsmanship of Satsuma trained by striking a standing timber.
    Jigen-ryu drills by striking a standing timber, the tategi, hard from left and right with a shout, over and over. An encyclopedia names that practice as the school's basic technique, a local form kept in Satsuma, and its founder as Togo Shigetaka (1561-1643), a retainer of the Shimazu. Nodachi Jigen-ryu, which its own page sets apart from Jigen-ryu, trains the same way, on a practice ground open to the sky, barefoot.
  • How tall was a striking post?
    A little over 1.3 m (~4 ft) above the ground. A martial-arts writer, without naming the schools, gives the main line's post as a log of a little over 2 m (~7 ft) set upright about 70 cm (~28 in) into the ground, struck with diagonal cuts from one's own shoulder down to the pit of the stomach.
  • Was a bundle of branches struck instead?
    Only in the modern period, as far as any page shows. The same writer describes a branch line that strikes a bundle of a dozen or more long thin branches laid at knee height, and holds that it was once a children's exercise of one to a few springy branches, but gives no date. The earliest bundle that can be dated is at Iwama, some time after 1946, where a student of the founder of aikido laid bundled branches on a stand of two posts for his teacher to strike; another student's remark that the Satsuma samurai of old struck bundles of brushwood is undated hearsay. No page places the bundle before the Meiji Restoration of 1868.
  • How many posts did a practice ground keep?
    No page we read says, nor whether schools outside Satsuma kept any.
  • A sword rack held swords lying level on forked supports.
    The katanakake is a stand or shelf with forked supports - deer antlers, for one - set at the same height left and right, usually in two tiers for the long and the short sword; a story of 1914 has a man take off his sword and hang it on the rack of a guardroom.
  • Long arrest weapons were kept at guard posts, and shown standing in the open.
    The "three tools" of arrest - the tsukubo, the sasumata and the sodegarami, all on long shafts - were kept at checkpoints and guard posts. A screen painting of the early Edo period shows them stood in a row along a bamboo fence beside a theater's entrance, from which the encyclopedia reckons they were probably used as a set from early on, as symbols of order and authority.
  • Was there a rack for practice weapons at a practice ground's edge?
    No page we read describes one, or its size.
