Stables (umaya)

A warrior's stable (umaya) was a building of its own. A small one was three bays long, with its horses in stalls one bay wide on board floors, a matted room for the attendants and an earth-floored room for fodder. A lord's could be far larger, and a farmhouse's stable was narrow or wide by region.

  • A warrior's stable was a substantial freestanding building.
    The stables of the warrior class were built in the shoin style of their residences, as buildings of their own.
  • It held the stalls, a matted room called the tozamurai, and an earth-floored room for fodder, the kusa-no-ma.
    So a Japanese encyclopedia describes the stable of a shoin-style residence.
  • A gable-entered stable three ken long was held the most formal of its time.
    The Rakuchu rakugai screens and the master carpenters' manual Shomei both show one.
  • The classic warrior stables of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries were three, five or seven bays long, and none survives.
    The Shomei records them, and so does the excavation of the Asakura mansion at Ichijodani.
  • A warrior's stalls were one bay wide, on raised board floors.
    Each horse had a separate stall of one bay's width, and its floor was boards, not earth.
  • Hikone castle's stable is L-shaped, with 21 bays and a grooms' rest room at the end.
    A castle lord's stable could be that much larger than a household's.
  • A farm stable in the Kinki region is narrow, about 3 m (~10 ft) square.
    Where winters are long, as in Tohoku, farm stables are wide.
