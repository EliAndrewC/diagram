Stables (umaya)

A warrior's stable (umaya) was a building of its own, built in the same shoin style as the residence it served. A small one was three bays long, with its horses in stalls one bay wide on board floors, a matted room called the tozamurai, and an earth-floored passage before the stalls, the kusa-no-ma. A lord's could be far larger, and a farmhouse's stable was narrow in some regions and wide where winters are long.

  • A warrior's stable was a substantial freestanding building.
    A dictionary of Japanese architecture calls the stables of the warrior class substantial freestanding buildings, in the shoin style of their residences.
  • A warrior's stable held the stalls, a matted room and an earth-floored passage.
    So a Japanese encyclopedia describes the stable of a shoin-style residence, naming the matted room the tozamurai and the earth-floored one the kusa-no-ma. A dictionary of Japanese architecture puts the kusa-no-ma in front of the stalls, running the length of the building, and the tozamurai across it from them.
  • The grooms and retainers had quarters in a warrior's stable.
    Illustrated handscrolls show such quarters as early as the Kamakura period, and senior members of the household used the rooms when they came to view their horses.
  • The master carpenters' manual Shomei shows a gable-entered stable three bays long as the most formal of its day.
    The Shomei, handed down in the Heinouchi family, the shogunate's master carpenters, records the warrior stables of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. It and the Rakuchu rakugai screens both show this stable, and a Japanese encyclopedia calls it the most formal of its time.
  • The classic warrior stables of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries were three, five or seven bays long.
    The Shomei records them, and so does the excavated site of the Asakura mansion at Ichijodani, in Fukui.
  • None of those classic stables survives.
    A dictionary of Japanese architecture says so in its entry on the stable.
  • A warrior's stalls were one bay wide, on raised board floors.
    A dictionary of Japanese architecture gives each horse a separate stall one bay wide on a raised timber floor, and a Japanese encyclopedia says the stall's floor was boards, not earth.
  • How deep was a warrior's stall?
    No page we read gives its depth, only its width.
  • A castle lord's stable could be far larger: Hikone castle's is L-shaped, with 21 bays and a grooms' rest room at the end.
    A Japanese encyclopedia describes it as a large stable of 21 small bays.
  • A farm stable is narrow in the Kinki region and wide where winters are long.
    A Japanese encyclopedia, writing of the farmhouse's stable, gives the Kinki one as about 3 m (~10 ft) square, and names Tohoku as a region of wide ones.
  • How many horses did a county magistrate's post keep?
    No page we read says, and the setting's campaign notes count horses only at waystations and in the legions.
