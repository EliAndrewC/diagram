Did a guest stay in the house, or in a guest house of their own?

Sources: shirobito-1717-takayama (the guest room of the Takayama residence), boso-no-mura-takei (the guest space of a middle-rank samurai house), honjin-jawiki (where travelling lords and officials lodged), yakage-honjin (a surviving honjin's guest rooms and main house)

The guest rooms of a samurai house were part of the house. The residence at the Takayama Jin'ya had, among its rooms, a room for receiving guests. The Takei house, a middle-rank samurai house of the late Edo period reproduced at the Boso-no-mura museum, divides into living space (the living room, the dining room, the kitchen and the doma) and guest space (the shikidai genkan, the anteroom where a visitor was announced, and the zashiki, the guest room), both within its one main house.

A travelling daimyo, a hatamoto or a shogunal official lodged at a honjin, a house in a post town that was allowed what an ordinary inn was not: a front gate, a genkan with a shikidai and a jodan-no-ma, the raised room of honor. At the Yakage honjin the guest facilities, the o-zashiki with its jodan-no-ma and a gate for the lord's arrival (the onari-mon), are listed apart from the main house where the family lived and ran its sake brewery, among more than ten buildings on the site; the page does not say whether the o-zashiki is a building of its own. A guest house standing apart from the residence at a samurai house or a posting was searched for and not found.

What this means for a plan: a posting's guest quarters are the zashiki and the rooms beside it, inside the residence. A guest house drawn as a building of its own rests on nothing found at a samurai house and is a GUESS; the nearest attested form is the honjin's suite of honor, which belongs to a post town's lodging house, not to an office.
