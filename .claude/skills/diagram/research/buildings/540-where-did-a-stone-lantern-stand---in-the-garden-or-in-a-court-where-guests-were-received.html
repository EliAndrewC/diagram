Where did a stone lantern stand - in the garden, or in a court where guests were received?

Sources: kotobank-ishidoro (the stone lantern, its parts and its history), kotobank-teien (the lantern in the tea garden and the garden)

A stone lantern (ishidōrō) is built of stacked stone parts: from the top, a jewel, a cap, the fire-box that holds the light, a middle platform, a post and a base. There are many kinds, named by their use and shape - the Kasuga, the snow-viewing (yukimi), the Enshū and the Oribe among them. It came from China and Korea with Buddhism as a votive light for shrines and temples, where a single lantern stood at the center of the front; from the Momoyama period, the late sixteenth century, it was set in tea gardens and ordinary gardens as well.

The garden history says how it got there. Lanterns were first set in the tea garden to light gatherings held in the evening, and when the tea garden, once nearly flat, took on made hills, streams and ponds, the stone lantern became one of its chief sights. By the time the reference lists a garden's parts, the stone lantern is one of its accessories, with the stepping stones, the paving and the fences.

A lantern standing in a court where guests of rank were received, rather than in a garden, is not described on any page read. The nearest attested use is the older one: a single lantern at the center of the approach to a shrine or temple.

What this means for a plan: a stone lantern in a residence garden is ACCURATE, one of the garden's ordinary furnishings; the many kinds are drawn as one small glyph, a map drawing convention. A lantern in a receiving court is a GUESS, borrowed from the shrine and temple use, and if one is drawn it stands singly on the line of approach, as that use set it. How many a garden holds, and where, are a GUESS.
