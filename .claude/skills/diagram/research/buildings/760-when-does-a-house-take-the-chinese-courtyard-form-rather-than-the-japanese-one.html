When does a house take the Chinese courtyard form rather than the Japanese one, and how big is a courtyard house?

Sources: siheyuan-zhwiki (the courtyard house, its courts by household and ground, its smallest and largest forms), qixian-cnr (a Chinese county seat's shops on courtyards), kotobank-machiya (the Japanese townhouse's row of rooms along an earth-floored passage), machiya-shoka-jawiki (the rich Edo townsman's house behind the shop, joined by a garden)

The two traditions build different houses, not the same house in two styles. The Chinese courtyard house (siheyuan) closes a yard with ranges - buildings each one row of rooms - on all four sides; technically it is the three-sided house with a gate range added to close it. Its smallest form has one court: through the street gate you are straight in the yard, and single-story ranges - the main range on the north, wings east and west, and the south range facing north - stand around it; the complete house has three courts in a row. The Japanese townhouse (machiya) is instead one building to the street, its rooms - the shop, the middle room, the formal room - in one row or two along a long, narrow earth-floored passage, much the same plan across the country. Even the rich townsman of the Edo period who built a dwelling apart, behind the shop in the middle of the block, joined it to the shop by a veranda and an inner garden rather than closing a court with ranges. So the choice is not made house by house by tier or class: a settlement follows one tradition or the other.

In the Chinese tradition the size follows the household. A courtyard house was usually lived in by a large family, and the owner built it to the ground held and the number in the household - from a single court to three or four, or two houses wide with side courts. In Beijing the deepest possible house ran from one lane to the next, about 77 m (about 250 ft). And the courtyard house served the Chinese town merchant too: in the old county seat of Qixian the shops on the old streets mostly follow the northern plan of one court or several behind the street, the street building of two stories with the shop below and a storeroom above. No page read says which households of a Chinese town lived in a one-court house and which in more, nor gives the one-court house's own frontage and depth.

What it means for a map. Inside an administrative compound the interiors stay Japanese-first, as Administrative culture is JAPAN-first for compound interiors records. Outside it, the house form is a knob.

The rule the map follows: each settlement rolls, from its seed, whether its houses follow the Japanese forms or the Chinese; the same roll governs the town's shops (the frontage knob in How wide is a merchant's shophouse, and how does wealth change it?). On the Japanese roll a townsman's house is one building on the street with its rooms in a row along a passage (the plan in What does a merchant's house hold, from the shop front to the back of the lot?). On the Chinese roll the house is a court closed by ranges, one court for an ordinary household and two or three in a row for a large or rich one, never deeper than about 250 ft; the shop, where there is one, is the street range. Which households get more than one court is a guess until a source is found.
