How big was a samurai's country manor, and what did it hold?

Sources: l7r-median-domain (the setting's country estate), fukui-kenshi-bushi-yakata (the usual size of a warrior's moated residence, the fields at its gate, one deed's house site), toyama-bushi-yakata (two excavated residences measured), ranhaku-bushi-yakata (what one large residence held, part by part), kotobank-shimoyashiki (the lord's suburban estate and what it grew), edo-hantei-jawiki (estates bought on farmland, still paying its tax)

In the setting a samurai family may keep a country estate close to farmland, but the estate is never part of the village. History offers two forms of such a house.

The moated residence among its own fields. A medieval warrior lived in a residence of about one cho square - about 109 m, some 360 ft a side, near 3 acres - enclosed by a moat and an earthen bank, and his bondservants worked the paddy and fields in front of its gate. The measured ones read here run smaller: a fourteenth-century residence in Toyama sat inside a square moat about 62 by 80 m (about 200 by 260 ft, about 1.2 acres) and 2.5 m deep, with an earthen bank inside built of the moat's spoil, and a castle site whose dig found a moat, wells and household goods is put at about 90 m square (about 2 acres). One deed of 1310 hands over five cho of paddy together with a dwelling on a house site of five tan - roughly half a hectare, a little over an acre, on the later measure of the tan - and the site held more than one building, among them a separate kitchen house. Scholars' reconstruction of a large Kanto warrior's residence, paraphrased by one town's history, fills in the parts: a near-square site, a moat dry or wet, a bank one or two meters high and a fence running round; a gate with a lookout over it; before the gate, thatched row houses of the servants and the paddy they worked; inside the gate more servants' houses and a stable; the master's house at the center, roofed in thatch or board; a riding ground behind the house or outside the moat; and a storehouse for the rice, barley and beans paid in by the farmers around. That residence was a large one, 150 to 200 m a side (about 5 to 10 acres), and the town history suggests its own district's residences, if it had any, may have been smaller versions of it.

The garden estate. The Edo-period form was the lord's suburban residence: at Edo it held gardens for the lord's leisure and vegetable gardens and bamboo groves that supplied the main residence, and lords and upper retainers are found keeping such houses in other castle towns too. Some were bought on farmland and went on paying that land's tax. No page read gives the size of one kept by a single samurai family rather than a lord.

What stands inside. The house, its stable, its vegetable garden and its bath are the buildings page's questions on a samurai's house by rank, a samurai's stable, a residence's vegetable garden and a residence's own bath; what they found holds for the manor's house as for any samurai's.

What it means for a map. The drawn country manor of about 1 acre is smaller than every moated residence measured here, which run from about 1.2 acres to about 3, and far more for a great house.

The rule the map follows: a country manor stands apart from any village, beside its own fields, and its form is a knob rolled per manor. The moated form is a near-square enclosure of about 1.2 to 3 acres (about 230 to 360 ft a side), rolled along that range, inside a moat and an earthen bank, with one gate; inside, the master's house at the center, a stable, a storehouse and a row of servants' houses; before the gate, the manor's own paddy. The garden form has no moat: a walled house with a garden, vegetable plots and a bamboo grove, its size kept at the drawn 1 acre as a guess until a source is found.
