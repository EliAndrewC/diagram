Samurai country manors (bushi yakata)

In this setting a samurai family may keep a country estate close to farmland, but the estate is never part of the village. History offers two forms of such a house. The older is the medieval warrior's moated residence, a square enclosure of about 1 to 3 acres inside a moat and an earthen bank, with its own paddy before the gate. The later is the Edo-period lord's suburban residence, a garden estate with vegetable plots and bamboo groves.

The medieval warrior lived among the fields his bondservants worked.

  • How big was a warrior's moated residence?
    Commonly about one cho square - about 109 m (~360 ft) a side, near 3 acres - enclosed by a moat and an earthen bank, with the bondservants working the paddy and fields in front of its gate. The measured ones read here run smaller: a fourteenth-century residence in Toyama sat inside a square moat about 62 m (~203 ft) by 80 m (~262 ft), about 1.2 acres, and 2.5 m (~8 ft) deep, with an earthen bank inside built of the moat's spoil, and a castle site whose dig found a moat, wells and household goods is put at about 90 m (~295 ft) square, about 2 acres.
  • A medieval deed of 1310 hands over a warrior's paddy together with a house site holding several buildings.
    It transfers five cho of paddy together with a dwelling on a house site of five tan - roughly half a hectare, a little over an acre, on the later measure of the tan - and the site held more than one building, among them a separate kitchen house.
  • What did a large warrior's residence hold?
    Scholars' reconstruction of a large Kanto warrior's residence, paraphrased by one town's history, fills in the parts. It had a near-square site, a moat dry or wet, a bank one or two meters (~3-7 ft) high and a fence running round, and a gate with a lookout over it. Before the gate stood the thatched row houses of the servants, with the gate paddy before them, and inside the gate more servants' houses and a stable. The master's house stood at the center, roofed in thatch or board, with a riding ground behind it or outside the residence, and a storehouse for the rice, barley and beans paid in as rent.
  • That reconstructed residence was a great one, 150 to 200 m (~492-656 ft) a side.
    That is about 5 to 10 acres. The town history suggests its own district's residences, if it had any, may have been smaller versions of it.

The Edo-period form was a lord's, and a garden rather than a fortified farm.

  • A lord's suburban residence was a garden estate that supplied the main house.
    At Edo it held gardens for the lord's leisure, and vegetable gardens and bamboo groves that supplied the main residence; lords and upper retainers are found keeping such houses in other castle towns too.
  • Some were bought on farmland and went on paying that land's tax.
    A residence a lord built on land bought from commoners, mostly in Edo's outskirts, still bore the rent and levies laid on that land.
  • How big was a garden estate kept by a single samurai family?
    No page we read gives the size of one kept by a samurai family rather than a lord.

The buildings inside a manor - the house, its stable, its vegetable garden and its bath - are those of any samurai's house, at Samurai residences and their rooms (buke yashiki), Stables (umaya), Vegetable gardens at samurai houses and government offices (saien) and Baths (furo).
