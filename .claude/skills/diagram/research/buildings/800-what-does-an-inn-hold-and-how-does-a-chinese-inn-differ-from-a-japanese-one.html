The Japanese inn was a townhouse with rooms to let. The post town's inn (hatago) was built on the same plan as a townhouse: past the big street door the earth floor ran to the back, beside it the board-floored shop and kitchen, and several guest rooms upstairs or at the back; on the late-Edo Tokaido the inn's upper story on the road had a projecting lattice. At Akasaka on the Tokaido an inn was made of an earth floor, a board-floored room, rooms, formal rooms, a kitchen, a bath and a privy, and was usually two stories where it faced the road. One of its large inns, the Ohashiya, had about 9 ken of frontage and 23 of depth (about 54 by 138 ft). The common Chinese inn was a carters' yard. The Ming had inns of the "drink in front, lodgers behind" kind, eating house and lodging in one; but most common were the plain inns called "mule-and-horse great inns", mostly out in the open country, whose guests were mostly carters who cooked for themselves. The one account read of such an inn's parts is late and from the Northeast, in the late Qing and the Republic of China (1912-1949): a thatched, mud-walled yard on high ground near water beside the main road, fenced with tall stakes, holding mud-brick lodging houses and always a large stable, with bundles of hay in the corners; its rooms held long heated sleeping platforms running north and south, each sleeping twenty and more. That account is self-published and unsourced, and is used only for the parts, never for a size; no page read gives a Chinese inn's size.
