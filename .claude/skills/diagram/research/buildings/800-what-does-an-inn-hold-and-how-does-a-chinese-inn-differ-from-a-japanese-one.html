The Japanese inn was a townhouse with rooms to let. The post town's inn (hatago) was built on the same plan as a townhouse: past the big street door the earth floor ran to the back, beside it the board-floored shop and kitchen, and several guest rooms upstairs or at the back; on the late-Edo Tokaido the inn's upper story on the road had a projecting lattice. At Akasaka on the Tokaido an inn was made of an earth floor, a board-floored room, rooms, formal rooms, a kitchen, a bath and a privy, and was usually two stories where it faced the road. One of its large inns, the Ohashiya, had about 9 ken of frontage and 23 of depth (about 54 by 138 ft). The common Chinese inn was a carters' yard. The Ming had inns of the "drink in front, lodgers behind" kind, eating house and lodging in one; but most common were the plain inns called "mule-and-horse great inns", mostly out in the open country, whose guests were mostly carters who cooked for themselves. A British traveler of 1863 found the same yard on the road northwest of Beijing: at the inn at Nankou the courtyard was already full of travelers' gear, with horses, mules and donkeys loose all over it, and the travelers slept in a common dormitory; in a Beijing inn the travelers' horses and mules stood tied up in the courtyard. The Northeast's cart inn is dated from the end of the Qing, and its rooms had heated platforms sleeping about twenty to a platform. The one account read of such an inn's parts covers the late Qing and the Republic of China (1912-1949) together, without telling them apart: a thatched, mud-walled yard on high ground near water beside the main road, fenced with tall stakes, holding mud-brick lodging houses and always a large stable, with bundles of hay in the corners; its rooms held long heated sleeping platforms running north and south, each sleeping twenty and more. That account is self-published and unsourced, and is used only for the parts, never for a size; no page read gives a Chinese inn's size. So the yard inn, its lodging round the yard and its animals are attested before 1912; the large stable as a building and the hay in the corners rest on the late-Qing-and-Republic account alone, which the map takes as it stands. The rule the map follows: the inn is a knob rolled with the settlement's house forms. On the Japanese roll an inn is a townhouse on the street, two stories on the road front, 18 to 54 ft wide and up to about 140 ft deep, with an earth-floored passage to the back, its guest rooms upstairs and behind, a bath and a privy at the back, and no yard or stable of its own - where the bath and privy stand, and the want of a yard or stable, are a guess. On the Chinese roll the carters' inn is a fenced yard on the road with lodging houses along its sides and a large stable, hay stacked in its corners; its size is a guess.
