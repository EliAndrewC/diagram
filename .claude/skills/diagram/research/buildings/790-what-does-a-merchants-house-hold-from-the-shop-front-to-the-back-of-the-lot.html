From the street back, in a Japanese town. Most Kyoto townhouses have one plan: a long, narrow earth-floored passage from the street to the back, with three rooms in a row beside it - the shop room on the street, the kitchen, and at the back the most formal room - and a small garden of about one tsubo, about 36 sq ft, set into the house for light and air. Beyond the house, at the back of the lot, stood detached rooms, outbuildings and the storehouse; in Kyoto the storehouse was as a rule never built on the street but at the back, where the storehouses of the block's lots together made its boundary and a firebreak. The provincial towns - castle towns, post towns, ports, temple towns and country market towns - built three plans: Kyoto's through passage, Edo's earth floor across the front made a little wider, and a fusion of the two; the through passage was common near Kyoto, the front earth floor or the fusion farther away. A form common in Kyoto split the house in two: the shop on the street, the dwelling behind as a building of its own, an inner garden between them and a narrow entrance wing joining them.
