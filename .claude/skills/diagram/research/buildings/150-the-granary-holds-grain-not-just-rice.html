The granary holds grain, not just rice

Sources: hatakata-men-jawiki (dry-field tax commonly in coin, 関東畑永法, the Kanto hata-ei rule), kuramai-jawiki (the tax rice as brown rice in bales), kakoimai-jawiki (the emergency reserve of rice and other grain, also called kakoimomi), kureki-jawiki (the upland Ina villages' tax in timber, later in cash), nta-nengu-nonyu (the share of the tax paid in money), nagaokakyo-nengu-jono (a tenth of the tax reckoned in soybeans near Kyoto). Soybean bales delivered in kind, horse fodder as the reason, and the Chinese mixed-grain regulation have no readable source

What the research found: Edo paddy tax was rice, received into the lords' kura and paid out as brown rice reckoned in bales - so, in this page's reading, rice dominated a wet-rice county's kura - but the DRY-FIELD share of the tax often arrived as CASH (Kanto dry-field tax paid in money; ~30-40% of the whole tax came as coin), with SOYBEANS as the recurring exception (in the shogunal lands a tenth of the land tax was, in principle, paid in soybeans (silver); that several regions collected the dry-field tax in soybean bales, and that the state wanted them for horse fodder, is this page's reading, confirmed in no page read); an emergency reserve of rice and other grain, also called kakoimomi (reserved unhulled rice), was kept in community and charity granaries - this page keeps it instead as a corner of the county granary itself (a deliberate simplification: one granary on the map, not two), since unhulled rice stores longer than hulled, in this page's reading; and in the upland Ina villages the tax arrived as split timber and later as cash, the granary shrinking toward a strongroom. Chinese county granaries were, in this page's reading - confirmed in no page read - mixed-grain by regulation (rice south, millet north, plus wheat and soybeans). The Rokugan-specific decision (GM canon 2026-07): Rokugan is wealth-rich but coin-poor, so cash commutation is LESS common than in Edo Japan - the dry-field share of a Rokugani county's tax arrives IN KIND (soybean and barley bales beside the rice) more often than its Japanese model, and an upland county's tax arrives as goods rather than coin, making its granary part warehouse-of-goods, part strongbox. So: a wet-rice county's granary is rice-dominant with a visible minority of other grain; the label is "granary".
