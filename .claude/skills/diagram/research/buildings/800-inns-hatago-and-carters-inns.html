Inns (hatago and carters' inns)

A Japanese inn on the highway (hatago) was a townhouse with rooms to let: an earth floor running back from the street door, the shop and kitchen beside it, and guest rooms upstairs or at the back. A large one at the post town of Akasaka was about 54 ft wide and 138 ft deep. The commonest Chinese inn was something else: a yard in the open country where carters stopped with their animals and often cooked for themselves.

  • A post town's inn was built on the same plan as a townhouse.
    Past the big street door the earth floor ran to the back, beside it the board-floored shop and kitchen, and several guest rooms were fitted out upstairs or at the back.
  • On the late Edo-period Tokaido, an inn's upper story on the road had a projecting lattice.
    The lattice marked the building as an inn.
  • At Akasaka on the Tokaido an inn held an earth floor, rooms, a kitchen, a bath and a privy.
    It was made of an earth floor, a board-floored room, rooms, formal rooms, the kitchen quarters, a bath and a privy, and was usually two stories where it faced the road.
  • One of Akasaka's large inns, the Ohashiya, was about 9 ken wide and 23 deep.
    That is about 54 by 138 ft.

Chinese inns of the road were of two kinds under the Ming dynasty, and the commoner was built for carts and animals rather than on a townhouse's plan.

  • The Ming had inns that were an eating house in front and lodgings behind.
    A survey of China's old inns calls them "drink in front, lodgers behind", eating and lodging in one.
  • The commonest Ming inn was the plain "mule-and-horse great inn" of the carters.
    Such inns stood mostly in the open country, and their guests were mostly laborers driving carts, who often cooked for themselves, so the service was simple and the price cheap.
  • A traveler of 1863 found north China's inn yards full of animals.
    At the inn at Nankow (Nankou) the courtyard was already full of travelers' gear, with horses, mules and donkeys loose all over it, and amid the yells of fellow travelers waking from nightmares the party retreated to its dormitory; at another inn the travelers' horses and mules stood tied up in the courtyard.
  • The Northeast's cart inn arose at the end of the Qing dynasty.
    The Chinese Wikipedia article on the cart inn dates it there, though it says the word's first use cannot be fixed more closely than the late Qing and early Republic.
  • A cart inn's rooms held heated platforms sleeping about twenty each.
    Against the cold its rooms had heated platforms (kang) along the north and south walls, each a common bed for about twenty. The one account of such an inn's parts gives more than twenty.
  • What did a cart inn's yard hold?
    A thatched, mud-walled yard fenced with tall stakes, on high ground near water beside the main road, holding mud-brick lodging houses and always a large stable, with bundles of hay in the corners. That rests on one account, self-published and unsourced, which covers the late Qing and the Republic of China (1912-1949) together without telling them apart.
  • The yard inn, its lodging round the yard and its animals are attested before the Qing dynasty fell in 1912.
    The carters' inn is Ming by name, the yard full of animals is seen in 1863, and the cart inn is dated from the end of the Qing. The large stable as a building and the hay in the corners rest on the later account alone.
