The size of a compound and the rank of its buildings

An intendant's office (jin'ya) was a compound from which an official of the shogunate governed part of the shogunate's own territory - at Takayama, the whole of Hida province. It held a hall for the office's business, the intendant's residence, storehouses and service buildings. At the one such office measured building by building, the rice storehouse was the largest, at about 11,200 sq ft, then the office hall at about 8,100 sq ft, the intendant's residence and the staff's rowhouse, with the small storehouses and the gatehouse far below.

How the other buildings of a compound ranked - its kitchen, its stable, its shrine - no page we read records, and the order given for them below is this project's reading of the few sizes that are known.

  • The Takayama Jin'ya is the one intendant's office that survives.
    It is preserved as the only jin'ya still standing.
  • At the Takayama Jin'ya the office hall out-measured the residence, and the residence the staff's rowhouse.
    The office is about 8,100 sq ft and the intendant's residence about 6,400 sq ft, so the working office is about a quarter again the size of the household's. The storehouse-keepers' rowhouse is about 3,700 sq ft. The kitchen storehouse (about 740 sq ft), the book storehouse (about 450 sq ft) and the gatehouse (about 400 sq ft) are far below. The residence and the rowhouse are restorations of the 1990s, not the old buildings themselves.
  • At the Takayama Jin'ya the office's rooms were one building.
    The entrance, the examination room, the office and the great hall were rebuilt together in 1816 as parts of the one intendant's office.
  • The largest building at the Takayama Jin'ya was a storehouse.
    Its rice store was built as a rice storehouse in the third bailey of Takayama castle and moved to the jin'ya in 1695, in the Edo period. At about 11,200 sq ft it is larger than the office and about three-quarters again the size of the residence. This project reads it as one long building divided into the numbered storehouses among its surviving buildings. It held the rice of a whole province (The granary and the tax rice (gokura)), so it is the top of the range, not a county magistrate's granary - but it shows that a storehouse could out-size every hall.
  • A small branch office had just one central building.
    The Joge branch office, set up away from its own intendant's office, in the shogunate's territory, and staffed by three officials by the end of the shogunate in 1867, had one central building of about 80 tsubo (about 2,850 sq ft).
  • How big was a kitchen against the house it served?
    A fraction of it, not its equal. A retainer's whole house ran about 49 to 67 tsubo on the two measured: a main house of about 67 tsubo (about 2,380 sq ft) at Matsue was lived in, in turn, by retainers of about 500 to 1,000 koku, and a middle-rank retainer of 150 koku at Matsushiro had one of about 49 tsubo (about 1,740 sq ft). An elite kitchen (daidokoro) was large within such a house, but that it stayed a fraction of it is a GUESS, since no page we read gives a kitchen's area against its house's. More on the size of a house against its owner's rank is at how big a samurai's house was.
  • How big was a county magistrate's stable beside its barracks?
    A fraction of it, on this project's figures: a stable of two to four horses, at about 55-70 sq ft a stall, beside a barracks for ten to twenty men, this project's figure for a small office's guard. The horse count, the stall area and the barracks' strength are a GUESS: no page we read gives the horses of a posting or a stall's depth, and none says where a stable ranked among an office's buildings. What a warrior's stable and its stalls were like is at Stables (umaya).
  • How large were a shrine's halls?
    Small, even when grand. A provincial shrine's worship hall (haiden) is taken here as 3 to 5 ken across, a GUESS: no page we read gives it a figure. The national outlier, the main sanctuary (honden) at Izumo, stands about 24 m (~79 ft) tall on a near-square plan divided into four rooms, which this project reads as about 2 by 2 ken, a GUESS.

Between the buildings lay open courts. The one layout of courts we read closely is Chinese, a county yamen's.

  • A Chinese county office was built around a run of courts.
    The county yamen at Neixiang, in China, covers more than 47,000 m² (~11.6 acres) with 18 successive courtyards, and a visitor going north from its main gate crosses two rectangular courts in turn.
  • How much of a jin'ya's site was open forecourt?
    At Takayama the plaza in front of the jin'ya is about an eighth of the designated site, some 15,200 sq ft of about 120,800. The forecourt and the sanded court of the hearing (oshirasu) together are taken here as about 12-18% of a site, a GUESS: no page we read measures them.
  • How much of the Takayama Jin'ya's ground did its buildings cover?
    About three-tenths at most. The prefecture gives its site as about 9,807 m² (~105,566 sq ft) and the total floor area of its buildings as about 3,018 m² (~32,486 sq ft), about 31% of the site. That total counts the floor of every story, not the ground a building stands on, so wherever a building has an upper floor the ground covered is less. It also counts a modern concrete pump house of about 71 m² (~762 sq ft), without which it is about 30%. The buildings counted are those standing after the restorations that, the prefecture says, brought back nearly all of the jin'ya as it was in the Edo period. It is the one office whose site and buildings we found measured.
