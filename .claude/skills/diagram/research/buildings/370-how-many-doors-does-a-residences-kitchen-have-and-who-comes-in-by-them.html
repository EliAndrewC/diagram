How many doors does a residence's kitchen have, and who comes in by them?

Sources: kotobank-katteguchi (the kitchen door as the way in from outside), kotobank-uchigenkan (the household's own entrance beside the formal one), matsue-bukeyashiki (the inner entrance and the formal entrance of one house), homes-jin-bukeyashiki (who came in by the doma, on a plan read for a hatamoto's house)

The sources read name three ways into a samurai house: the formal entrance, the inner entrance and the kitchen door. The formal entrance with its shikidai step was for guests (see where the formal entrance is). Besides it the household had an inner entrance, the uchigenkan, made in addition to the front entrance for the family and those of the house to come and go by; the Matsue house names both, its inner entrance and its formal shikidai entrance. The third was the kitchen door, the katteguchi, the doorway leading from outside into the kitchen. On a floor plan inferred for a hatamoto's house of the late Edo period, those of lower status than the master came in and out through the doma and not through the entrance. A kitchen with two outside doors, one for deliveries and one for serving, was searched for and not found.

What this means for a plan: the kitchen has one door to the outside, on its doma, the service entrance; the family's everyday door is the separate inner entrance, not the kitchen's.
