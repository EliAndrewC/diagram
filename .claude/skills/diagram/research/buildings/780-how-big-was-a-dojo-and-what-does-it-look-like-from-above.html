The state hall. One domain school's fencing and spear hall survives whole: the Yubikan at Hagi, the old school's two halls moved and enlarged. It is a single-story wooden building with a hip-and-gable roof of tile, 37.8 m by 10.8 m (about 124 by 35 ft), long north to south. Its north half is a board-floored fencing hall of 39 mats, its south half an earth-floored spear hall of 54, each with a place on its west side from which the lord watched, and a waiting room between them for his visits; it was also where the domain's men fought bouts with trainees from other domains. At about 1.6 m² a mat that is some 680 and 940 sq ft of practice floor. The largest domain school, the Kodokan at Mito, gave each art its own building: a fencing hall on the north, a spear hall between, and a hall for iai, jujutsu, the halberd and the rest on the south, three buildings in all, with an open bout ground for martial training in the south garden.

The head of the hall held scrolls, not a shrine. From the mid-Edo period, as bamboo-sword practice took over, practice halls came to be floored in board or tatami; in Edo the famous dojos outgrew small private practice rooms into large halls, and many domains attached a practice hall to their school. Such a hall had a raised floor from which the master watched the practice, and on it hung scrolls naming the gods of Kashima and Katori, the gods of the sword. The shrine shelf (kamidana) came later: it was not in the traditional Edo-period hall, and a Ministry of Education meeting of 1936 made it required in school halls, under State Shinto.

The Chinese counterpart was a drill ground, not a hall. A Chinese county seat and every seat above it generally kept a drill ground. Chongming's, founded in 1586, lay outside the east gate inside an earth wall, about 37.5 mu (about 6 acres); at its north stood the drill hall of three bays facing south, with a rear hall behind and a tea hall beside, a general's platform in front and a gate in the south wall; there was a small drill ground inside the town too. The last drill hall, before it was pulled down about 2000, was nine bays (29 m) wide and 11.5 m deep, about 95 by 38 ft.

The rule the map follows: from above a state martial hall is a long single-story building with a tiled hip-and-gable roof, about 124 by 35 ft, split across its length into a board-floored fencing hall and an earth-floored spear hall, each with a viewing place along one long side; a large city's state institution may instead draw three halls side by side with an open bout ground before them. The head of any hall is the master's raised seat, hung with scrolls, and draws no shrine. On a settlement that rolls the Chinese model, the state institution is a walled drill ground of about 6 acres outside a gate, with a drill hall of about 95 by 38 ft at its north facing south, a platform before it and a gate in the south wall.
