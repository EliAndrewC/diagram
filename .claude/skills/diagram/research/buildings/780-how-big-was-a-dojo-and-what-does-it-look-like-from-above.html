How big was a dojo, and what does it look like from above?

Sources: hagi-meirinkan-guide (a surviving domain fencing and spear hall, measured), kodokan-mito-jawiki (a large domain school's three martial halls and bout ground), dojo-jawiki (the boarded hall, the master's seat and its scrolls, and when the shrine shelf came in), chongming-yanwuchang (a Chinese county's drill ground and its hall), tuancheng-yanwuting-zhwiki (an imperial camp's drill hall of 1748)

The state hall. One domain school's fencing and spear hall survives whole: the Yubikan at Hagi, the old school's two halls moved and enlarged. It is a single-story wooden building with a hip-and-gable roof of tile, 37.8 m by 10.8 m (about 124 by 35 ft), long north to south. Its north half is a board-floored fencing hall of 39 mats, its south half an earth-floored spear hall of 54, each with a place on its west side from which the lord watched, and a waiting room between them for his visits; it was also where the domain's men fought bouts with trainees from other domains. Taking a mat as somewhere between about 1.55 and 1.82 m², a guess, the floors were roughly 650-765 and 900-1,060 sq ft of practice floor respectively. The domain school with the largest grounds in the country, the Kodokan at Mito, split its martial arts among separate buildings: a fencing hall on the north, a spear hall between, and a hall for iai, jujutsu, the halberd and the rest on the south, three buildings in all, with an open bout ground for martial training in the south garden.

The head of the hall held scrolls, not a shrine. From the mid-Edo period, as bamboo-sword practice took over, practice halls came to be floored in board or tatami; in Edo the famous dojos outgrew small private practice rooms into large halls, and many domains attached a practice hall to their school. Such a hall had a floor from which the master watched the practice, and on it hung scrolls naming the gods of Kashima and Katori, the gods of the sword. The shrine shelf (kamidana) came later: it was not in the traditional Edo-period hall, and a Ministry of Education meeting of 1936 made it required in school halls, under State Shinto.

The Chinese counterpart was a drill ground, not a hall. A Chinese county seat and every seat above it generally kept a drill ground. Chongming's, founded in 1586, lay outside the east gate inside an earth wall, about 37.5 mu (about 6 acres; the same page also calls it about one square li, which would be some ten times that, and the record takes the figure in mu); at its north, in the middle, stood the drill hall of three bays, with a rear hall behind and a tea hall beside, a general's platform in front and a gate in the south wall; there was a small drill ground inside the town too. A regional commander enlarged the ground in 1658; in 1662 the commander Zhang Dazhi raised the hall by 3 chi and named it, and it was repaired again in 1799 and 1846. The hall that stood on the site until it was pulled down about 2000 was nine bays (29 m) wide and 11.5 m deep, about 95 by 38 ft, but the page gives no date for that building, and no other page read dates it or measures a county's drill hall from before 1912; the drill hall of the emperor's own Jianrui camp at Xiangshan, built in 1748, is five bays wide. So the map does not take the nine-bay hall's size: it draws the three-bay hall the county's own gazetteer records.

A private dojo's size was found on no page read; the drawn private hall, 44 by 24 ft (some 54-63 mats), is about the size of the Yubikan's spear hall and stays a guess. The rule the map follows: from above a state martial hall is a long single-story building with a tiled hip-and-gable roof, about 124 by 35 ft, split across its length into a board-floored fencing hall and an earth-floored spear hall, each with a viewing place along one long side; a large city's state institution may instead draw three halls side by side with an open bout ground before them. The head of any hall is the floor from which the master watched, hung with scrolls, and draws no shrine. On a settlement that rolls the Chinese model, the state institution is a walled drill ground of about 6 acres outside a gate, with a drill hall of three bays at its north facing south, a platform before it and a gate in the south wall; the hall's width, about 32 ft on the last hall's bay of about 3.2 m, and its depth are a guess. A private dojo's size is a guess.
