How thick was a compound's earth wall?

Sources: kunishitei-toyonaga-neribei (a surviving neribei measured at its base), kojodan-dobei (the neribei and the rammed-earth tsuijibei), hei-jokaku-jawiki (a castle wall's thickness), mlit-kanazawa-dobei (which samurai walled their land, and with what)

Two forms, a lighter wall and a heavy one. The lighter is a wall of earth or clay laid up without a frame, or plastered over a frame of posts: a castle's frameless earth wall, faced with plaster, was a shaku, about a foot, or more, a frameless neribei perhaps 30 cm, and a dye shop's surviving late-Edo neribei of head-sized stones laid in red earth measures 0.6 m across its base and 1.6 m high, capped with tiles - about 1 to 2 ft in all. The heavy form is the rammed-earth tsuijibei, built up in forms to a meter (about 3.3 ft) thick and three meters high.

Which houses walled their land at all was a matter of rank. In the samurai quarter of Kanazawa the earthen walls were rammed; foot soldiers were not allowed to build walls and planted hedges, while samurai of middle rank (heishi) and above enclosed their land with earthen walls. No page read measures the wall of a magistrate's post itself.

What this means for a plan: a compound wall is a choice between the two attested forms - the lighter wall at about 1.5 to 2 ft, which is how the town, city and capital tiers draw it (How large are the gates, walls and funerary features drawn?), or the rammed-earth tsuijibei at about 3 ft, which is how a building plan draws it, by the GM's ruling, as a drawing convention for legibility (A compound wall is a building, not a boundary line). Both are real walls of the period; neither is attested for a magistrate's post in particular.
