A dojo is a city institution; county training is courtyard keiko

Sources: hanko-jawiki, edo-three-dojos-jawiki (the privately opened fencing dojos of late Edo); jinya-jawiki (a small domain's drill ground - the scope note below), dojo-jawiki

The city tier (GM 2026-07-25): every provincial city draws exactly one state martial hall plus a count of private dojos that follows its samurai population. The county finding below is the floor of that same ladder. What the research found: the standalone dojo with a resident sensei and enrolled students is an URBAN institution in Edo Japan. There the domain (han) school taught the martial arts, the domain schools and their martial halls standing - this page guesses - in castle towns and cities, and the famous privately opened fencing dojos of late Edo stood in the city of Edo itself; the buildings listed for a rural daikansho/jin'ya (the county magistracy's direct model) include no bugeijo, and before the mid-Edo period practice was often held outdoors or on an earthen floor, and at the end of the Warring States period in private houses, with no purpose-built hall. That rural samurai went on training that way - at home in an earthen yard, a hall cleared for practice, or on shrine grounds - once the city halls were built is this page's guess. The Chinese county yamen is starker still: no training hall at all - military drill happened at separate garrison grounds. The setting's own numbers force the same conclusion from inside the setting: a county town holds ~15 samurai total - no student body, no living for a sensei - while the provincial city (~225 samurai) and capital (~800) are where the setting notes' own dojo references all sit (the "many dojos" of Toshi Ranbo, the Empire's third-largest city; the train-at-a-dojo-in-every-clan pilgrimage vow). A county samurai's FORMAL schooling happened in their youth at a clan school in a city; the county posting only maintains that edge with daily keiko, and ambition rides to the provincial city. The decision: no magistracy draws a dojo by default. Every magistracy still TRAINS - the working platoon and the duty watch keep daily practice - but the venue is multi-use open ground in the compound, not a dedicated building. A magistracy WITH a true dojo is a particular of that place, characterizing its resident (a noted swordsman, a retired sensei) and should read as a local distinction, the kind that draws young samurai from neighboring counties. The MARKING convention (GM 2026-07-24): every magistracy draws a practice ground - the swept patch, weapon rack and striking-post markers this map draws - beside the watch's lodging, sized at ~90-135 sqft per samurai actually drilling (a solo kata footprint runs ~8-10 ft square; paired work roughly doubles it). Marking the GROUND and its GEAR rather than labeling a building is the honest form of the finding - rural practice left equipment, not architecture.

The scope of what is read: ja.wikipedia 陣屋 (jin'ya) lists a 調練場 (chorenjo) drill ground in a small DOMAIN's jin'ya program. That is a domain seat, not the bakufu daikansho this entry models, and it is ground rather than a hall - so it CONFIRMS the practice-ground convention drawn here, but it does NOT confirm the negative claim about a daikansho specifically, which carries its own note above; the word "jin'ya" covers both institutions.
