How wide was the main gate of a magistrate's post?

Sources: nagayamon-jawiki (the nagaya-mon as the gate of jin'ya and samurai houses, its form set by rank), tamba-kashiwara-jinya (a surviving jin'ya gate range), bunka-saito-nagayamon (a nagaya-mon's two-ken opening), bunka-adachi-yakuimon, bunka-omi-yakuimon and bunka-fujioka-yakuimon (one-bay gates and their frontages), fuchu-joge-pamphlet (the gate of a three-man branch office), sohu-yamen-gate (the county yamen's gate building), bjd-qing-yamen (a surviving three-bay yamen gate)

The Japanese post. Its gate took one of two forms. The first is the nagaya-mon, a gate opened through a range of rooms: it arose as the gate of castles, jin'ya and samurai residences, and its design and structure were fixed in detail by the house's standing and stipend. A domain's jin'ya at Kaibara keeps its gate range of about 1714, thirteen and a half ken long and two deep, with rooms either side of a gate with a side door, but no page read gives the width of its doorway. A registered nagaya-mon of the same form, seven and a half ken long, opens in its central two ken, with double-leaf board doors hung between its main posts; two ken is about 12 ft. The second form is a gate of one bay, the yakuimon: registered examples measure 2.6 m, 2.5 m and 1.8 m across their frontage, about 6 to 8.5 ft. None of these three pages, nor the nagaya-mon's, says whose house the gate served.

The smallest post read, the shogunate's branch office at Jōge, drew on its plan a main gate with a small gate and a gatekeepers' room beside it, at the head of stone steps in its east wall; the pamphlet gives the gate no width.

The Chinese yamen. A county office's main gate was not a simple opening in the wall but a building with a roof; by law and ritual, however large the county, it could be only three bays wide, with two black-lacquered leaves in each bay, six in all. The Zhili governor-general's surviving office has such a three-bay black gate raised on a stone platform a meter high, with an open front court beyond it. No page read gives a yamen gate's width in meters.

What this means for a plan: a Japanese post's main gate is a choice between two attested forms, rolled per plan - a one-bay gate of about 6 to 8.5 ft, or a nagaya-mon whose two-ken opening is about 12 ft. The 9 to 12 ft the town and city tiers draw a samurai residence gate at (How large are the gates, walls and funerary features drawn?) falls within that span. A yamen's gate drawn as a roofed building of three bays is accurate; the 18 to 24 ft it is drawn at is a GUESS, since no width could be read.
