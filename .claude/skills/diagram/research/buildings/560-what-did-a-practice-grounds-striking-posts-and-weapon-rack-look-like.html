What did a practice ground's striking posts and weapon rack look like?

Sources: jigen-ryu-jawiki (the Satsuma school that strikes a standing timber), kotobank-tategi-uchi (the same practice in an encyclopedia), nodachi-jigen-ryu (an open-air practice ground), bujutsukarate-tategi (the post's size, and the knee-high bundle), aikidoshibuya-yokogi (the earliest datable bundle of branches, at Iwama after 1946), kotobank-katanakake (the sword rack), kotobank-mitsu-dogu (the long weapons stood at a guard post)

The striking posts

The swordsmanship of Satsuma is the one tradition found that trains against a post. Jigen-ryū drills by striking a standing timber (tategi) hard from left and right with a shout, over and over. An encyclopedia names that practice against a standing timber as the school's basic technique, a local form kept in Satsuma, and its founder as Tōgō Shigetaka (1561-1643), a retainer of the Shimazu. Another school, Nodachi Jigen-ryū, which its own page sets apart from Jigen-ryū, trains the same way, on a practice ground that is open to the sky, barefoot.

A martial-arts writer gives the post's size, without naming the schools: in the main line it is a log of a little over 2 m set upright about 70 cm into the ground, so that about 1.3 m, some 4.5 ft, stands above it, and it is struck with diagonal cuts at the height of one's own shoulder down to the pit of the stomach. A branch line strikes a crosspiece instead: a bundle of a dozen or more long thin branches laid at knee height. That is the bundle as it is struck today; the same writer holds that it was once a children's exercise of one to a few springy branches, but gives no date for it. The earliest bundle that can be dated is not in Satsuma at all: at Iwama, some time after he joined in 1946, a student of the founder of aikido laid bundled branches on a stand of two posts for his teacher to strike, and another student's remark that the Satsuma samurai of old struck bundles of brushwood is hearsay he gives no date for. No page found places the bundle before the Meiji Restoration. How many posts one practice ground kept, and whether schools outside Satsuma kept any, no page read says.

The weapon rack

A sword rack (katanakake) holds swords lying level on a stand or shelf, each resting on forked supports - deer antlers, for one - set at the same height left and right, usually in two tiers for the long and the short sword; a story of 1914 has a man take off his sword and hang it on the rack of a guardroom. Long weapons were kept at guard posts, and a painting shows them standing in the open. The "three tools" of arrest - the tsukubō, the sasumata and the sodegarami, all on long shafts - were kept at checkpoints and guard posts, so that they were called "the guard post's three tools"; a screen painting of the early Edo period shows them stood in a row along a bamboo fence beside the entrance of a theater, from which the encyclopedia reckons they were probably used as a set from early on, as symbols of order and authority. A rack for practice weapons standing at the edge of an open practice ground, and its size, are not described on any page read.

What this means for a plan: the upright post standing about 4.5 ft is ACCURATE as Satsuma practice, its school's founder a retainer of the early Edo period. The knee-high bundle of branches is attested only in the modern period - as practice today and at Iwama after 1946 - and what is said of it before that is undated hearsay, so the maps do not draw it: the striking posts are upright posts only. Carrying the post to a practice ground outside Satsuma is a GUESS, and so is the count. A post drawn as a small location marker rather than at its own size is a map drawing convention. A rack for swords laid level on forked supports is ACCURATE for a guardroom; long arrest weapons kept at a guard post are ACCURATE, and standing them upright in the open at the post's front is a GUESS drawn from a painting of them standing at a theater's entrance; a rack for practice weapons at a practice ground's edge is a GUESS joined from the two, and its size of about 8 by 2 ft is a GUESS.
