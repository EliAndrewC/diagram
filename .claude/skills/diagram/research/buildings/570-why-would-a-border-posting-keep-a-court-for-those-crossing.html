Why would a border posting keep a court for those crossing?

Sources: kotobank-bansho (the domains' border guard posts), bunka-nuruyu-bansho (the Nuruyu border post and its layout), bansho-jawiki (Nuruyu as a rare survivor), hakone-seki-jawiki (what a great checkpoint held)

In the Edo period each domain set guard posts, the kuchidome bansho, where its roads crossed into a neighboring domain or the shogun's land, to watch the people and goods going in and out - chiefly to stop the domain's produce leaving - and sometimes to tax the goods at a fixed rate. Such posts rarely survive; the Nuruyu post of Sendai domain is one that does.

Nuruyu shows how a border post was laid out around what crossed. It commanded a mountain pass where the road left Sendai for Akita domain. Its front gate stood across the road itself; facing the gate was the inspection hall (kendansho), and the officer's residence stood to the right, behind it. The road entered by the gate and ran on past the front of the inspection hall, so the ground between gate and hall was the road itself, and whoever crossed passed before the hall. The residence, 23.19 m by 11.86 m, about 76 by 39 ft, is a farmhouse in form, with an earth-floored work side, but it has a formal reception room in the shoin style. Hakone, the shogunate's own checkpoint on the Tōkaidō, held within its fence a front post with an upper guardroom, guards' room, rest room and bath, an opposite post with a lockup, stables, a street watch and a notice board.

A court at a border post kept for receiving officials or delegations from the neighboring domain is not described on any page read. What is attested is ground for those crossing: the road inside the gate, before the inspection hall.

What this means for a plan: a border posting's court is ACCURATE as the ground those crossing passed over, between the gate on the road and the inspection hall facing it, and a formal reception room in the officer's house is ACCURATE even at a plain post.
