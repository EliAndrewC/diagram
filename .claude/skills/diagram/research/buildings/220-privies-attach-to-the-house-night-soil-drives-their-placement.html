Privies attach to the house; night-soil drives their placement

Sources: tajima-2007-night-soil (the annual contract paid in rice or cash; farmers paying the estates in kind; how far the trade reached), guernica-night-soil (night soil as a good with a price; the carter at the tenement); kotobank-benjo, sinyoken-madori (the privy built into the house; the upper and lower privies; the retainers' row houses; the farm case), nagano-shishi-yokota (the two privies of a magistrate's house of 1794). The sand box for inspection under the master's privy has no readable source.

What the research found: the samurai-residence kawaya/setchin was built INTO the house (in town and farm houses that had a guest parlor, a privy stood at the rear of the parlor; the pull-out sand box under the master's for health inspection is unsourced), not a free-standing block in the yard. The house of a district magistrate at Matsushiro, built in 1794, has two privies, though the town history that counts them does not say whether both were there from the start, and it names other rooms added before about 1868. A writer on the history of the toilet divides the privies of samurai houses into an upper one for the head of the house and the men and a lower one for the women, without saying from when; the site that quotes it traces the roots of the idea of two privies in one house back to the Kamakura period. Where the second one stood is less certain. The guest privy at the rear of the parlor is the town and farm house's form given above. A family privy jutting out near the living rooms is described for urban houses after Meiji; before 1868 the nearest form read is in the row houses of a daimyo's retainers, whose privy stood at the end of a board floor projecting past a corridor. A family privy placed near the living rooms of a samurai house before 1868 was searched for and not found. Night-soil was a commodity with a positive economic value - well-off farmers supplied the castle and the daimyo estates with firewood, charcoal and seedlings for the right to empty their privies - so collection was by outside carters emptying cesspits, which means pits sit toward a service edge/wall/gate for cart access - and, our rule rather than a finding, never so deep that a collector crosses the inner court. The trade stayed close: night soil moved within about 3 to 5 km of most rural towns and up to 20 km around Edo, by fertilizer boat where a canal allowed it. The decision: privy count scales with occupancy (~1 per functional zone, ~3-4 at a county manor), the residence privy attaches to the house with its cesspit to the rear/service wall, and servants'/outer privies line service walls near a gate. A residence has two privies, a guest privy at the rear of the parlor and a family privy; setting the family privy near the living rooms is a GUESS, drawn from the urban house after Meiji and the retainers' row houses, since nothing read places it in a samurai house before 1868.

A town house's privy and a tenement's shared one, and who carried the night soil off in town, are under where is a town house's privy.
