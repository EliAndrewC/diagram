Did a residence keep the storm shutters shut on rooms it was not using?

Sources: amado-kotobank (what the storm shutter was, when it appeared, and when it stood shut)

The storm shutter, the amado, is a board door set outside the openings of a Japanese building, running on a one-groove sill and usually stored in a box at the end of its run, the tobukuro, when not in use; it was devised in the Momoyama period, the late sixteenth century, and Toyotomi Hideyoshi's Jurakudai palace is an early example. The form with a tobukuro and a one-groove sill dates from the seventeenth century, and the amado's purposes are protection against wind and rain, watchfulness at night and warmth; it is stored in the tobukuro by day. That a household kept the amado shut by day on rooms it was not using was searched for and not found.

What this means for a plan: a residence of the period has amado along its veranda faces, closed at night and stowed by day. A wing drawn with its shutters closed in daylight because its rooms stand unused is a GUESS: the shutters are real, but nothing read says an unused wing was kept shut up.
