The hearing court (shirasu)

A hearing court (shirasu) was the floor below a magistrate's hall where suits were heard and the accused examined and judged. The magistrate and the officials sat above, in the hall; the parties knelt below, on straw mats spread on the floor. The court was roofed, or was an earth floor inside the building: the open court of white gravel is the image of period dramas, not the history.

Intendants' offices kept hearing courts too. The one intendant's office that survives, the Takayama Jin'ya, keeps its court paved with river cobbles and roofed, beside an examination room that was one part of the office's main block. The fullest account of who sat where comes from the town magistracy of Edo, the shogun's capital. How big the office hall itself was is at The office hall and its clerks (goyakusho).

  • A magistracy's hearing court was roofed, not open to the sky.
    It either had a roof built over it or was an earth floor inside the building, spread with gravel; the open-air court of white gravel is the scene of period dramas.
  • The earliest courts were bare earth, and the white gravel came later.
    In the oldest times the earth floor was used as it was; the floor of white gravel came later.
  • Intendants' offices and barrier checkpoints had hearing courts too.
    Hearing courts were set up at intendants' offices and at the barrier checkpoints (sekisho) on the roads, as well as at the magistracies.
  • At Takayama, the one intendant's office that survives, the court is paved with river cobbles and roofed.
    Its examination room (ginmisho) and court are both paved with cobbles and roofed. The Takayama Jin'ya is the only one of the more than fifty such offices across the country that survives.
  • Takayama used cobbles because its province had no white sand.
    Hida province has no sea, and not enough white sand could be had to spread on the court, so cobbles from the riverbed were spread in its place.
  • Takayama's court was built inside because of the snow.
    Hida is snow country, and a court with no roof would be buried in winter and unusable, so the court was made inside the building; so the jin'ya's guide explains it.
  • Takayama has two hearing courts.
    One received the villages' suits and petitions, as an office's service window would now; at the other, those who had committed crimes were examined and judged, as in a court of law.
  • At Takayama, the examination room was one part of the office's main block.
    The office was rebuilt in 1816, in the Edo period, in such parts as its entrance hall, examination room, working office and great hall, which this project reads as one block. So the court lay against a room of the office hall, not a stage of its own.
  • Takayama's office block was built to display the office's rank.
    The great hall of the office is of a design that sets great weight on formality, and its entrance room keeps, as rebuilt in 1816, a great alcove marking a rank of 100,000 koku and a wave pattern that even daimyo refrained from using.
  • At Takayama, most criminal cases went to the shogunate for its ruling.
    The office heard them, but for the majority the shogunate's ruling was sought.

At Edo, the shogun's capital, the town magistracy's court was a hall in tiers, and its plan records who sat in each.

  • At Edo the court was a hall in tiers: a tatami room at the top, a gravel floor at the bottom.
    By a plan of the South Town Magistracy made in 1810, the top tier was a tatami room, the kujiba, where the magistrate and the other officials sat, and the bottom tier a gravel floor on which straw mats were spread for the plaintiffs and defendants.
  • The magistrate's tatami room was divided into three rooms, with a board veranda at its front.
    The veranda was 3 shaku (about 3 ft) wide, with a stair of three steps, one ken wide, at its middle.
  • The magistrate sat in the innermost room, and the clerk with the examining officer in the room before it.
    The examining yoriki and the clerk, a doshin, sat in the middle room; the witnessing doshin in the room nearest the court; and doshin crouched at the court's left and right.
  • Did the examiners and scribes have a tier of their own?
    At some magistracies. One dictionary puts the examiners and the scribes on an upper tier below the magistrate's room; another says only some magistracies set that tier apart.
  • How large was the clerks' place in the hall?
    No page we read gives it.
  • Samurai, priests and monks did not kneel on the gravel, but sat on the hall's verandas.
    Samurai (not masterless ones), Shinto priests, monks and townsmen who were official purveyors sat on the verandas of the hall, which ran in two steps: samurai, priests and monks on the upper, the rest on the lower.
  • Peasants, townsmen and lesser ronin knelt in the open, on straw mats spread on the gravel.
    Two dictionary entries say so.
  • Did an accused samurai sit on the upper veranda or the board veranda?
    Accounts differ. The imidas encyclopedia also puts commoners on mats, but seats a samurai or monk accused of a crime on the board veranda, where the Wikipedia account puts samurai on the upper of the two verandas.
  • The accused sat roped at the center of the court, with the plaintiff and the town officials behind.
    A servant held the end of the accused's rope. Behind the accused sat the town officials, headmen and landlords on straw matting on the left, and the plaintiff on the right.
