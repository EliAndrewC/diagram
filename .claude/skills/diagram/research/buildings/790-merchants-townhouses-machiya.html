Merchants' townhouses (machiya)

A merchant's townhouse (machiya) in a Japanese town was one building on the street: the shop at the front, where it opened onto the road, and the family's rooms behind it in a row along a long, narrow earth-floored passage - a plan much alike across the country. In Kyoto it held three rooms in a row, with a garden of about one tsubo, about 36 sq ft, set into the house for light and air, and the storehouse stood at the back of the lot. Many had a low upper story used for storage.

  • Most Kyoto townhouses had one plan: a through passage with three rooms in a row beside it.
    A long, narrow earth floor ran from the street to the back, and beside it stood the shop room on the street, the kitchen, and at the back the most formal room.
  • A small garden inside the house gave it light and air.
    It was about one tsubo, about 36 sq ft, set at the back or the middle of the building.
  • The back of the lot held detached rooms, outbuildings and the storehouse.
    In Kyoto the storehouse was as a rule never built on the street but at the back, and the storehouses at the backs of a block's lots together made its boundary and a kind of firebreak.
  • The provincial towns built three plans.
    Castle towns, post towns, ports, temple towns and country market towns built Kyoto's through passage, Edo's earth floor across the front made a little wider, or a fusion of the two.
  • The nearer a town stood to Kyoto, the likelier the through passage.
    The through passage was common near Kyoto, the front earth floor or the fusion farther away.
  • A form common in Kyoto split the house into two buildings.
    The shop stood on the street and the dwelling behind it as a building of its own, an inner garden between them and a narrow entrance wing joining them.
  • The upper townsmen of the Edo period built their dwelling apart, behind the shop.
    From the second quarter of the seventeenth century such a house set its dwelling in the middle of the block, joined to the shop on the street by a veranda and an inner garden, its entrance off an alley.
  • Many townhouses had a low upper story used for storage.
    A Japanese encyclopedia entry on townhouses says many took this form.
  • In Kyoto a full second story belonged to the large merchant houses, and came late.
    Through the Edo period the upper floor of a Kyoto townhouse stayed a low half-story, used as a storeroom and for live-in servants, except in the large merchant houses. At first it was built only at the front; the full second story, used for guest rooms, came later.
