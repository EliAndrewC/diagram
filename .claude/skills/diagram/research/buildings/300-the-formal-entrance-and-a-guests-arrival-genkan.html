The formal entrance and a guest's arrival (genkan)

A samurai house of standing had a formal entrance, the genkan, kept for the head of the household and honored guests; the rest of the household came and went by an inner entrance. In the Edo period the word meant an entrance with a shikidai, the low board step where guests were bowed in and seen off. A guest of rank came by palanquin, and the palanquin was brought right alongside the step, so that its rider went into the building without setting foot on the ground.

So a guest's arrival was staged: the gate, then open ground a palanquin could cross, then the step. Not every house had such an entrance. The houses of senior retainers did; a middle-rank house could do without one, and bring its guest in through a garden instead.

  • In the Edo period, "genkan" meant an entrance with a shikidai.
    In the shoin style of building, the genkan is the room or building set at a residence's formal entrance. In a warrior's house the projecting board floor where a palanquin was set down had been called the genkan, and the room of reception the shikidai; in the Edo period the whole came to be called the genkan, and the shikidai the lower board step where guests were bowed in and out.
  • The shikidai entrance was for receiving formal guests.
    It was a special entrance room for them, and it had a step alongside which the palanquin was brought, so its rider stepped up from the palanquin straight into the building.
  • The main genkan was for the head of the household and honored guests.
    The rest of the household and its senior staff used an inner entrance, the uchigenkan.
  • The houses of senior retainers had a shikidai.
    They had earthen walls, a gatehouse with rooms in it (a nagaya-mon) and a shikidai.
  • Where an office and a residence shared one compound, the genkan could belong to the office.
    At the Takayama jin'ya, the only jin'ya in the country whose principal parts survive, a visitor enters by the genkan, and the route goes round the rooms of the office and continues on to the residence where the intendants lived. The page describes a visitor's route, not how the office and the residence were laid out against each other.
  • A middle-rank house could have no genkan with a shikidai at all.
    The old Koseki house at Shiroishi, the house of a middle-rank retainer, has none. The gate from the street leads along the earthen wall to the kitchen entrance, which the page calls the front entrance. A guest instead passes through a middle gate (nakamon) in the wall and along the roji, the walled garden path, straight to the veranda of the principal zashiki; the earthen wall encloses the roji in an L.
  • Open ground lay between the gate and the entrance.
    On a drawing made in 1869, just after the Edo period, of the shogunate's small branch office at Joge, the entrance with its step faces the open ground inside the main gate.

A Chinese governor's office was entered the same way, through its gate into open ground.

  • Through the main gate of a Chinese governor-general's office is an open front court.
    At the Zhili governor-general's office, a yamen, the open front court inside the main gate is planted with dozens of stout junipers.
  • Was the ground before the entrance ever a garden?
    No page we read says so: in every account we read, the ground between the gate and a genkan is open ground or a court. The garden approach found is the middle-rank house's, where there was no genkan.
  • Did a posting fence its forecourt off from its garden, or set a pond on the way in?
    No page we read describes a forecourt before a posting's genkan fenced off from its garden, or a pond set on the line from the gate to the entrance.
