The granary and the tax rice (gokura)

In the Edo period, before 1868, most of a village's land tax was paid in rice. The rice was received into the storehouses of the shogunate and the domains and paid out as brown rice, reckoned in straw bales. It went first to the village's store, the gokura, one to a village or to several, a temporary store for carrying the tax rice out and in some places a reserve against a bad harvest as well. It stayed there only a short time before it was carried on, to the lord's rice storehouses in the castle town or to a river landing to be shipped. Not all of the tax was rice: three to four tenths of it was paid in money.

Storehouses stood at each step of that journey. An intendant's office kept a rice storehouse of its own; the one that survives, at Takayama, covers about 11,000 sq ft.   • The village store held the tax rice only a short time.
    A historian of the village storehouses of two districts of Gunma found that the store only received the baled tax rice and held it for a short time: soon after, the rice was carried to the rice storehouses in the castle town that the lord named, or to a river landing to be shipped. A dictionary of Japanese history says the same of the country at large: the rice went on, in the shogun's lands to the shogunate's stores at Edo and Osaka, in a domain's lands to the domain's stores or to market.
  • The village store stood within the village, mostly on dry-field ground.
    A 1794 manual of village administration, quoted in the great dictionary of Japanese, puts it inside the settlement, mostly on dry-field land, and on a house lot where one stood empty.
  • Did the tax rice pass through the magistrate's compound on its way?
    This project reads it so: the village store is the chain's first link, and an office's storehouse holds rice in transit beside a local reserve. That is a reconstruction; the pages we read say only that the village store was a temporary store for the rice's shipment.
  • The shogunate's tax rice went by sea on hired ships under an official pennant.
    Kawamura Zuiken's reform of the shipping of rice to Edo, until then handled by merchant contract, made the cargo ships direct hires of the shogunate. The ships flew a red-circle pennant showing they were on government service, and he had the intendants set up stations at the ports of call to protect them and inspect their loads strictly. The office that sent the rice owned no ships of its own.
  • Did a remote province keep its rice at the office instead of shipping it on?
    Perhaps. The Takayama office (jin'ya), from which the shogunate ran the mountain province of Hida as its own land, keeps among its surviving buildings a row of numbered storehouses. That a territory far from cheap transport kept large storehouse rows at its office, as Takayama's may have held the rice of all Hida, is this project's GUESS: no page we read says the province's rice was gathered there before shipment.
  • An intendant's office kept its own rice storehouse.
    The encyclopedias list, among what stood in an intendant's office, a rice storehouse and a levy storehouse, beside the residence, the office, the staff's houses, the white-sand court and the jail.
  • Takayama's rice storehouse is one of the largest grain storehouses in the country.
    It was built as a rice storehouse in the third bailey of Takayama castle and moved to the intendancy in 1695. Its frame is of about 1600, of good hinoki, and in age and scale it is one of the foremost earth-walled grain storehouses in the country. Its floor area is 1,042.56 m² (~11,222 sq ft); that it is the building divided into the numbered storehouses is this project's reading.
  • A store shared by a few villages is measured.
    In Echigo, groups of villages - from two or three to several tens - kept the tax rice of the shogun's estates through the winter at a shared storehouse site. A drawing of 1769 of one such site, serving three villages, gives each storehouse's size: one for each village, of about 37 by 20 ft, 34 by 22 ft and 21 by 21 ft, and a measuring storehouse the three shared, about 38 by 19 ft - four storehouses of some 440 to 750 sq ft each. The villages were then land of a domain close to the shogunate, the shogun's own from 1786, and the Nagaoka domain's from 1789.
  • How big was a county office's grain storehouse?
    No page we read gives the size of a county-scale office's grain storehouse, or how many it kept. The measured stores bracket it: from a three-village storehouse's 440 to 750 sq ft to the 11,222 sq ft of a store that served a whole province. Nor does any page we read describe storehouses standing at a county's river landing, where its rice waited to be loaded.

A provincial city's store was the larger end of the same chain.

  • A castle kept a rice storehouse in its third bailey.
    Takayama castle's rice storehouse stood in its third bailey, the outer ward of the castle.
  • A great domain gathered its tax rice at a river port.
    The Kumamoto domain gathered its tax rice at Kawashiri, a river port that was also its naval port. The domain kept a magistrate's office, a lodge and storehouses there; normally 200,000 bales of tax rice came in from five of its districts and were shipped on to its warehouse estate (kurayashiki) at Osaka. The old drawings show nine storehouse buildings under three names, and a landing about 150 m (~492 ft) long survives on the river.
  • The shogun's rice store at Edo was a ground of up to 67 storehouses.
    It stood on the Sumida river, bounded by the river on one side and by moats on the other three - the capital's scale, not a provincial city's.
  • How large was any one storehouse at a castle or a port?
    No page we read gives it.

The storehouses themselves took one of two forms.

  • The storehouse on posts, the takakura, kept its floor high against rats and damp.
    Its high floor kept rats from the grain, with rat-guards, and let the air through against damp; in Japan such storehouses were still built on Amami Oshima, on Hachijojima and among the Ainu of Hokkaido into modern times. Neither reason is a river's: on this project's reading they hold wherever grain is kept, by the water or away from it.
  • An intendancy's grain store was an earth-walled kura set with ventilation gaps.
    The rice storehouse at Takayama, moved from the castle's third bailey in 1695, is an earth-walled grain storehouse (dozo), its walls sloping inward and set with gaps for ventilation. A kura was sorted by what it held, the rice store and the unhulled-grain store among its kinds, and it guarded them against fire, damp and theft.
  • How was an earth-walled grain storehouse's floor raised?
    No page we read says how far it sat above the ground, or whether on posts or on a stone base.

Not everything a granary held was rice.

  • The dry-field share of the tax was often paid in coin.
    The tax on dry fields was often paid in cash, as under the Kanto dry-field rule, and three to four tenths of the whole land tax was paid in money, delivered by the village to the office its lord named. So, on this project's reading, rice dominated a wet-rice district's storehouses.
  • Near Kyoto, a tenth of the shogunal land tax was reckoned in soybeans.
    In the shogun's villages near Kyoto a tenth of the tax was, in principle, paid in soybeans (in silver), three tenths in silver and six tenths in rice, though the instructions varied from year to year.
  • Did any region deliver its dry-field tax in soybean bales?
    No page we read says so, nor that the state wanted soybeans as horse fodder; that several regions took soybean bales in kind, for fodder, is this project's GUESS.
  • An emergency reserve of rice and other grain was kept in community and charity granaries.
    The shogunate, the domains, towns and villages stored rice and other grain there against an emergency; the reserve was also called kakoimomi ("reserved unhulled rice").
  • In the upland Ina villages the tax was paid in split timber, and later in cash.
    The villages paid split timber in lieu of the rice tax, and later money.

China's county granaries are recorded less closely.

  • Were Chinese county granaries stocked with mixed grain by regulation?
    Perhaps - rice in the south and millet in the north, with wheat and soybeans - but that is this project's GUESS. The pages we read say only that the Qing ever-normal granaries stood mostly in county seats under the magistrate and stored grain, naming no grain by region or by rule.
