Storehouses for the tax rice

In the Edo period, before 1868, a village's land tax was received into the storehouses of the shogunate and the domains as rice, and paid out from them as brown rice, reckoned in straw bales. It went first to the village's store, the gokura, one to a village or to several, a temporary store for carrying the tax rice out and in some places a reserve against a bad harvest as well. Where this has been studied it stayed there a short time in Gunma and through the winter in Echigo, before it was carried on, to the lord's rice storehouses in the castle town or to a river landing to be shipped. Part of the tax was paid in money instead.

Storehouses stood at each step of that journey. An intendant's office kept a rice storehouse of its own; one that survives, at Takayama, covers about 11,000 sq ft. A great domain's collecting store at a river port ran to nine buildings. The storehouses took one of two forms: a timber storehouse raised on posts, or an earth-walled kura like Takayama's, built to guard what it held against fire, damp and theft.

  • Where it has been studied, in Gunma, the village store held the tax rice only a short time.
    A historian of the village storehouses of two districts of Gunma found that the store only received the baled tax rice and held it for a short time: soon after, the rice was carried to the rice storehouses in the castle town that the lord named, or to a river landing to be shipped. A dictionary of Japanese history says the same of the country at large: the rice went on, in the shogun's lands to the shogunate's stores at Edo and Osaka, in a domain's lands to the domain's stores or to market.
  • In Echigo, groups of villages kept their tax rice through the winter at a shared store.
    Groups of villages, from two or three to several tens, kept the tax rice of the shogun's estates through the winter at a shared storehouse site.
  • One such store, shared by three villages, had four storehouses of some 440 to 740 sq ft each.
    A drawing of 1769 of the site gives each storehouse's size: a tax-rice storehouse for each village, of about 37 by 20 ft, 34 by 22 ft and 21 by 21 ft, and a "measuring storehouse" the three villages used together, about 38 by 19 ft; that their rice was measured there is this project's reading of its name. The villages were then land of a domain close to the shogunate, the shogun's own from 1786, and the Nagaoka domain's from 1789.
  • A 1794 village-administration manual puts the village store inside the village, mostly on dry-field ground.
    The manual, quoted in the great dictionary of Japanese, puts it inside the settlement, mostly on dry-field land, and on a house lot where one stood empty.
  • An intendant's office kept its own rice storehouse.
    The encyclopedias list, among what stood in an intendant's office, a rice storehouse and a levy storehouse, beside the residence, the office, the staff's houses, the white-sand court and the jail.
  • Did the tax rice pass through an intendant's office on its way?
    This project reads it so: the village store was the chain's first link, and an office's storehouse held rice in transit beside a local reserve. That is a reconstruction; the pages we read say only that the village store was a temporary store for the rice's shipment.
  • The rice storehouse of the Takayama intendancy is one of the largest grain storehouses in the country.
    It was built as a rice storehouse in the third bailey of Takayama castle and moved to the intendancy in 1695. Its frame is of about 1600, of good hinoki, and in age and scale it is one of the foremost earth-walled grain storehouses in the country. Its floor area is 1,042.56 m² (~11,222 sq ft); that it is the building divided into the numbered storehouses is this project's reading.
  • Did a remote province keep its tax rice at its intendant's office instead of shipping it on?
    Perhaps. The Takayama office (jin'ya), from which the shogunate ran the mountain province of Hida as its own land, keeps among its surviving buildings a row of numbered storehouses. That a territory far from cheap transport kept large storehouse rows at its office, as Takayama's may have held the rice of all Hida, is this project's GUESS: no page we read says the province's rice was gathered there before shipment.
  • How big was the grain storehouse of an office that governed a district, not a whole province?
    No page we read gives the size of such an office's grain storehouse, or how many it kept. The measured stores bracket it: from the 440 to 740 sq ft of a storehouse at a three-village site to the 11,222 sq ft of the store at the office that ran a whole province.

At the chain's larger end, the lord's rice was gathered in the castle town or at a river port.

  • Takayama castle kept its rice storehouse in its outer ward.
    The storehouse now at the Takayama intendancy (above) was built in the castle's third bailey, its outer ward.
  • The Kumamoto domain gathered its tax rice at a river port, Kawashiri, and shipped it on to Osaka.
    Kawashiri was also the domain's naval port. The domain kept a magistrate's office, a lodge and storehouses there; normally 200,000 bales of tax rice came in from five of its districts and were shipped on to its warehouse estate (kurayashiki) at Osaka.
  • Kawashiri's store ran to nine storehouse buildings beside a landing on the river.
    The old drawings show nine storehouse buildings under three names, and a landing about 150 m (~492 ft) long survives on the river.
  • Did a district's rice wait in storehouses at its river landing?
    The rice was carried to a river landing to be shipped; that it waited there in storehouses of its own, as a great domain's rice did at Kawashiri, is this project's reading.
  • On the sea route to Edo, the shogunate's rice went on hired ships flying an official pennant.
    Kawamura Zuiken's reform of the shipping of rice to Edo, until then handled by merchant contract, made the cargo ships direct hires of the shogunate. The ships flew a red-circle pennant showing they were on government service, and he had the intendants set up stations at the ports of call to protect them and inspect their loads strictly.
  • The shogun's rice store at Edo was a ground of 67 storehouses.
    It stood on the Sumida river, bounded by the river on one side and by moats on the other three - the capital's scale, not a provincial city's.
  • How large was any one storehouse at a castle or a port?
    No page we read gives it.

The storehouses themselves took one of two forms.

  • The storehouse on posts, the takakura, kept its floor high against rats and damp.
    Its high floor kept rats from the grain, with rat-guards, and let the air through against damp. Neither reason depends on flooding: on this project's reading, a raised floor served grain wherever it was kept.
  • Storehouses on posts were still built into modern times on outlying islands and among the Ainu.
    In Japan they were built on Amami Oshima, on Hachijojima and among the Ainu of Hokkaido into modern times.
  • The Takayama intendancy's rice storehouse is an earth-walled kura set with ventilation gaps.
    It is an earth-walled grain storehouse (dozo), its walls sloping inward and set with gaps for ventilation.
  • A kura was built to guard what it held against fire, damp and theft.
    A kura was sorted by what it held, the rice store and the unhulled-grain store among its kinds, and it guarded them against fire, damp and theft.
  • How was an earth-walled grain storehouse's floor raised?
    No page we read says how far it sat above the ground, or whether on posts or on a stone base.

Not all of the tax came in as rice, and not all that was stored was rice.

  • The dry-field share of the tax was often paid in coin.
    The tax on dry fields was often paid in cash, as under the Kanto dry-field rule.
  • Three to four tenths of the whole land tax was paid in money, delivered by the village.
    The village was responsible for delivering it to the office its lord named.
  • Did rice fill most of a wet-rice district's storehouses?
    On this project's reading, yes: the tax came into the lord's storehouses as rice, and much of what came in money was the dry-field share.
  • Near Kyoto, a tenth of the shogunal land tax was reckoned in soybeans.
    In the shogun's villages near Kyoto a tenth of the tax was, in principle, reckoned in soybeans but paid in silver, three tenths paid in silver and six tenths in rice, though the instructions varied from year to year.
  • That some regions paid their dry-field tax in soybean bales, as horse fodder, is this project's guess alone.
    No page we read says any region did, nor that the state wanted soybeans as horse fodder.
  • An emergency reserve of rice and other grain was kept in community and charity granaries.
    The shogunate, the domains, towns and villages stored rice and other grain there against an emergency; the reserve was also called kakoimomi ("reserved unhulled rice").
  • Split timber could stand in for the rice tax, and villages of Ina county, in Shinano, paid in it.
    Split timber paid in lieu of the rice tax was later paid in money instead; eleven of the shogunate's villages in Ina county paid in split timber.

China's county granaries are recorded less closely.

  • That Chinese county granaries held mixed grain by regulation (rice in the south, millet in the north, with wheat and soybeans) is this project's guess alone.
    The pages we read say only that the Qing ever-normal granaries stood mostly in county seats under the magistrate and stored grain, naming no grain by region or by rule.
