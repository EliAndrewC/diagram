Who sat where at a hearing, and on what?

Sources: oshirasu-jawiki (the tiered hall of the Edo South Town Magistracy, who sat in each tier, and where the parties knelt), shirasu-kotobank (the examiners' and scribes' tier, and the commoners on straw mats in the open), shirasu-imidas (commoners on mats, an accused samurai or monk on the board veranda)

At the Edo town magistracy the court was a hall in tiers. By a plan of the South Town Magistracy made in 1810, the top tier was a tatami room, the kujiba, where the magistrate and the other officials sat, and the bottom tier a gravel floor on which straw mats were spread for the plaintiffs and defendants. The kujiba was divided into three rooms, the front one with a board veranda 3 shaku (about 3 ft) wide. The magistrate sat in the innermost room; the examining yoriki and the clerk, the kakiyaku doshin, in the next; the witnessing doshin in the room nearest the court; and doshin crouched at the court's left and right. One dictionary puts the examiners and the scribes on an upper tier below the magistrate's room, and another says only some magistracies set that tier apart.

Where the parties sat was set by status. Samurai (not masterless ones), Shinto priests, monks and purveyors to the magistracy did not sit on the gravel but on the verandas of the hall, which ran in two steps: samurai, priests and monks on the upper, the rest on the lower. Peasants, townsmen and lesser ronin sat in the open, on straw mats spread on the gravel. A third account also puts commoners on mats, but seats a samurai or monk accused of a crime on the board veranda, where the first two put samurai on the upper, tatami one. The accused sat at the center of the court, roped, with a servant holding the rope's end; behind the accused sat the town officials, headmen and landlords on komo matting on the left, and the plaintiff on the right.

What this means for a plan: the kneeling parties sit on straw mats laid on the court's gravel, and that is ACCURATE; a marked place with no mat has no attested counterpart. The accused kneels at the center, the plaintiff behind to one side and the village's officials behind to the other. The clerks sit in the tier between the magistrate's room and the court - a room of the hall, never the court itself - and that placing is ACCURATE. How large that place was is not recorded, so the size drawn for the clerks' seats is a GUESS.
