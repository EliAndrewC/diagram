The main gate and its gatekeepers (nagaya-mon)

A walled compound of rank, a magistrate's post among them, was entered through one main gate in its front wall. In Japan that gate took one of two forms. The nagaya-mon was a gate opened through the middle of a long range of rooms, its form fixed by the standing of the house it served, with an opening of about 12 ft. The yakuimon was a gate of one bay, about 6 to 8.5 ft across. A Chinese county office, the yamen, built its main gate instead as a roofed building three bays wide.

The gatekeepers who kept the gate sat in one of two places: in a room of the gate range itself, or in a gatehouse, a small building of its own.

  • The nagaya-mon arose as the gate of castles, jin'ya and samurai residences, and the house's rank set its form.
    It began as the gate of the castles, jin'ya and samurai residences of the daimyo, and its design and structure were fixed in detail by the house's standing and stipend.
  • A registered nagaya-mon opens in its central two ken, about 12 ft.
    The gate range is seven and a half ken long and two deep, with rooms on both sides of the opening and double-leaf board doors hung between its main posts; two ken is about 12 ft. The page does not say whose house the gate served.
  • The gate range of the jin'ya at Kaibara survives, about 81 by 12 ft.
    The Kashiwara domain's seat at Kaibara keeps the front gate of its jin'ya from the year it was built, 1714. The range is 13.5 ken long and 2 ken deep, with three eight-mat rooms on one side of a gate with a side door, and on the other an eight-mat room and an earth floor. No page we read gives the width of its doorway.
  • A one-bay gate (yakuimon) measures about 6 to 8.5 ft across.
    Three registered examples measure 2.6 m (~9 ft), 2.5 m (~8 ft) and 1.8 m (~6 ft) across their frontage. None of these pages says whose house the gate served.
  • Even the smallest post we read of had a main gate and a gatekeepers' room.
    A drawing of 1869 of the shogunate's branch office at Joge labels a main gate, a small gate, a gatekeepers' room and stone steps together at its east wall. The pamphlet that reproduces it gives the gate no width.

Where the gatekeepers sat, the record shows both forms.

  • In the first form the guardroom is a room of the gate range.
    At Kaibara the guardroom and an earth floor lie on one side of the gate, and a horse-viewing room and a gun store on the other.
  • At Matsue the gate range was also where the gatekeepers and servants lived.
    The nagaya-mon of a samurai residence there served as the dwelling of its gatekeepers and of the household's servants.
  • In the second form the gatekeepers' house is a building of its own.
    At the Takayama jin'ya the gate and the gatekeepers' house were both built in 1832 and are listed separately, each a gabled building of one story; both survive.
  • Edo castle's gates had guardrooms, and the Akamon has one on each side.
    Guardrooms were set at the gates of Edo castle, and the Akamon, the red gate now at the University of Tokyo, has one on each side of it.
  • How large was a gatehouse of its own?
    About 18 by 12 ft, in the one example measured: the guardroom of the Kita-in temple at Kawagoe, a small gabled building 3 by 2 ken, of the mid to late Edo period. It is a temple gate's guardroom, not a samurai compound's. It now stands against the side of the temple's gate, but a plan of 1841 draws it inside, behind the gate, and the temple believes it was moved later, without saying when. No page we read gives the size of the Takayama gatehouse, or of any gatehouse of its own about 40 ft long.
  • Both forms are about 2 ken (~12 ft) deep.
    The Kaibara gate range is 2 ken deep, and so is the Kita-in guardroom.

A Chinese county office's gate was a building in its own right, not an opening in a wall.

  • A yamen's main gate was a roofed building, by law three bays wide.
    It was not a simple opening in the wall but a building with a roof, and by law and ritual, however large the county, it could be only three bays wide, with two black-lacquered leaves in each bay, six in all.
  • The Zhili governor-general's surviving office has such a gate, raised on a stone platform.
    Its three-bay black gate stands on a stone platform a meter (~3 ft) high, with an open front court beyond it.
  • No page we read gives a yamen gate's width.
    The pages found count its bays, not its meters.
