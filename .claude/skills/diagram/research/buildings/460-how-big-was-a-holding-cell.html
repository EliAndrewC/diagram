How big was a holding cell?

Sources: agariya-jawiki (the sizes of the Tenmacho and Osaka agariya cells), roya-kotobank (the 30-mat common cell, and the temporary cell inside the magistracy), edo-ashigaru-bugyosho (the temporary cell in the Edo town magistracy's court quarter), jinya-kotobank (the jail inside an intendant's compound), takayama-jinya-official (Takayama's jail in the town), jishinban-jawiki (the watch house's size and its tethering ring)

The cells that are measured are the great jails'. At Edo's Tenmacho the two ranges of agariya cells were three rooms each, of 15 mats and of 18 mats (about 18 by 15 ft and 18 by 18 ft), each with a privy about half a ken across, and Osaka's jail added a men's cell of 6 mats (about 12 by 9 ft) in 1739. The commoners' great cell was of 30 mats (about 30 by 18 ft), and over a hundred could be packed into it.

A magistracy that judged cases kept a temporary cell inside its own compound for the prisoners called before its court, and the Edo town magistracy's stood in the quarter that held its court. An intendant's compound generally held a jail of its own, though Takayama's jail stood in the town. A town's watch house was meant to be 9 shaku by 2 ken (about 9 by 12 ft) and was in practice 2 to 3 ken, with an iron ring on a post of its back room to tie a captive's rope to. The size of a magistracy's or an intendant's temporary cell was searched for and not found.

What this means for a plan: a holding cell between about 6 mats (12 by 9 ft) and 18 mats (18 by 18 ft) spans the single cells read, and a county office's cell, which holds a few people until their hearing (Cells are remand, not punishment), belongs at the small end of it. The size is a GUESS within that span: a remand cell of about 10 by 12 ft sits by Osaka's 6-mat cell and one of 18 by 15 ft is Tenmacho's 15-mat room; a cell drawn at 23 by 17 ft, about 22 mats, is larger than any single cell read short of the great cell for a city's commoners.
