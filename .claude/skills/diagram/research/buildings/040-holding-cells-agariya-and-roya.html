Holding cells (agariya and rōya)

In the Edo period a jail (rōya) held the accused until their case was judged, and those found guilty until their sentence was carried out: it was closer to a present-day detention house than to a prison. Confinement as a sentence existed, but only by exception; among the punishments carried out at Edo's great jail, at Tenmachō, were flogging, tattooing and beheading.

The cells that were measured are the great jails'. Their cells ran from rooms of 6 tatami mats to a great cell of 30, each shared by several prisoners and the largest by over a hundred. A magistracy that judged cases kept a temporary cell inside its own compound for the prisoners called before its court. A city's jail, inside the governor's compound or a compound of its own, is at Where a city keeps its jail.

  • The largest of the shogunate's jails was Edo's Tenmachō.
    Others stood at the magistrates' and intendants' offices of Kyoto, Osaka, Nagasaki and elsewhere, and Edo's own courts kept temporary jails.
  • An Edo-period jail held the accused until judgment, and the condemned until their sentence.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on the Tenmachō jail names these among its functions, and calls it a place of custody and of execution rather than a prison.
  • Prison as a sentence existed, but only as an exception.
    Two sentences of confinement, eiro and katairo, were served in the jail, but they were imposed only exceptionally.
  • Flogging, tattooing and beheading were carried out at the Tenmachō jail.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on the Tenmachō jail names them among the punishments done there.
  • This project takes exile and fines to have been sentences too.
    It is a GUESS: no page we read names them among the jail's punishments.
  • This project takes light offenders to have been sent home to their villages rather than held.
    It is a GUESS: no page we read says so.
  • How large was the Tenmachō jail?
    About 8,600 to 8,850 square meters (~93,000-95,000 sq ft, over 2 acres): the city of Chuo gives 2,600 tsubo and more, the Japanese Wikipedia article 2,677 tsubo.
  • The Tenmachō jail was a block of its own, walled and moated.
    Moat and embankment enclosed it on three sides, all but the south; an earth wall 7 shaku 8 sun (~8 ft) high ran round it, with a moat outside.
  • At Tenmachō the agariya cells were rooms of 15 and 18 mats.
    Two ranges of three rooms each, of 15 mats (~18 by 15 ft) and of 18 mats (~18 by 18 ft), each with a privy about half a ken (~3 ft) across. The page gives mats; the feet are this project's, at about 18 square feet a mat.
  • Osaka's jail had a men's agariya cell of 6 mats (~12 by 9 ft).
    It was added in 1739, in the Edo period, the Japanese Wikipedia article on agariya cells records.
  • Edo's great jail held its commoners in a great cell of 30 mats (~30 by 18 ft), and over a hundred could be packed into it.
    An encyclopedia entry on the jail calls the great cell the jail proper, the one that held commoners, and tells of over a hundred packed into its 30 mats.

Prisoners were also held outside Edo's great jail: at the offices of magistrates and intendants, and in a town's watch house.

  • A magistracy that judged cases kept a temporary cell inside its compound.
    It held the prisoners called before the court, and the Edo town magistracy's stood in the quarter that held its court rooms.
  • An intendant's compound generally held a jail of its own.
    An encyclopedia lists the jail among the buildings within an intendant's grounds.
  • The intendant's office at Takayama had its jail in the town, not in its compound.
    The jin'ya's own page places it by the town's present street names.
  • How big was a magistracy's or an intendant's temporary cell?
    No page we read gives its size. The single cell rooms that are measured run from Osaka's 6 mats to Tenmachō's 18, each shared by several prisoners.
  • A town's watch house was meant to be 9 shaku by 2 ken (~9 by 12 ft).
    In practice it was 2 to 3 ken (~12-18 ft), with an earth floor and a room at the back.
  • A watch house held a captive by an iron ring on a post.
    The ring was fixed to a post of its back room, so the end of a captive's rope could be tied to it.

In China, a county office's jail had a traditional place.

  • Where did a Chinese county office put its jail?
    An old rule set it to the south; the two county offices we read put it on the west side. The Neixiang county yamen, in Henan, follows the old rule that sets the prison to the south, and sets it on the west side of its first courtyard; it stands in the southwest of the yamen. At Pingyao the prison is on the west side of the office.
  • No page we read gives a meaning for the jail's place on the west, such as west being a direction of death.
    Nor does any speak of a western "ghost gate" the condemned left by; the pages give only the placement itself.

In Edo-period Japan, questioning under torture belonged to the jail, not to the court.

  • Edo justice tortured a suspect who kept refusing to confess, on a ruling by the senior councillors (roju).
    The suspect was first urged once more to confess in the jail's inquiry room.
  • No torture was done in the hearing court (shirasu).
    Instruments of torture were set out there, but they are thought to have been meant to overawe the plaintiffs and defendants.
  • At the intendant's office at Takayama, torture is reported to have been done in the jail in the town, not in the compound.
    The compound had an examination room (ginmisho), roofed like its court, and its instruments of torture are thought to have been there to overawe.
