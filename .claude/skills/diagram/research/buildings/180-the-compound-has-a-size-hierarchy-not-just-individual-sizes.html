The compound has a size HIERARCHY, not just individual sizes

Sources: matsue-bukeyashiki-about (the Matsue main house's tsubo and the rank of its households), matsushiro-bukeyashiki (a middle-rank main house's tsubo at Matsushiro), jaanus-umaya (a warrior stall's width); no readable page gives the other anchors - the Izumo sanctuary plan and the county stable's horses and stall depth - and each carries its note in the text

What the research found: in a lord's walled compound the domestic footprints rank in a fixed order - the residence is the dominant DOMESTIC building (the administrative office hall may exceed it, being the institution's working core, but no service/utility/storage/sacred building should). Anchors: an elite daidokoro is large but a fraction of the living quarters, not their equal (a whole house is about 67 tsubo for retainers of 500 to 1,000 koku at Matsue and about 49 tsubo for a 150-koku middle-rank retainer at Matsushiro, and a kitchen that size is a tell; see how big a samurai's house was); a county stable held ~2-4 horses (~55-70 sq ft/stall, this page's figure) so it is a fraction of a ~10-20-man barracks; a warrior's stall was one bay wide (see the samurai's stable); a Shinto worship hall is small even when grand (Izumo's main sanctuary, the national outlier, is ~2×2 ken on this page's reading, its pages giving only the 24 m height; a provincial haiden is 3-5 ken), so a shrine hall out-footing the residence is impossible at county tier. So the ranking matters as much as the sizes: a compound where every building is individually plausible can still show an impossible order (a kitchen at or above the living block, a stable over the barracks, a shrine over the residence). The deliberate departure: the office hall is allowed to be the largest building - it is not domestic, and a magistracy's public working block genuinely dominated (Takayama).
