What was a samurai's stable like, and how big was a stall?

Sources: kotobank-umaya (the parts of a warrior's stable, the formal three-bay stable, Hikone's, and the farm stall's size), jaanus-umaya (the warrior stable as a building of its own, its length in bays and its stalls one bay wide)

A warrior's stable was a building of its own, and a small one was three bays long. A stable of a shoin-style residence had the stalls where the horses were tied, a matted room for the attendants called the tozamurai, and an earth-floored room for fodder, the kusa-no-ma; the Rakuchu rakugai screens and the master carpenter's manual Shomei show a gable-entered stable three ken long as the most formal of its time, and its stall floors were boards, not earth. The warrior stables of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries were substantial freestanding buildings three, five or seven bays long, none of which survives, with separate stalls one bay wide on raised timber floors. A lord's stable could be far larger: Hikone castle's is L-shaped, with 21 bays and a grooms' rest room at the end. A farm stable in the Kinki region is narrow, about 3 m (about 10 ft) square. How deep a warrior's stall ran, and how many horses a county post kept, were searched for and not found.

What this means for a plan: a posting's stable is a small building of its own, a few bays long, with its stalls about one ken (about 6 ft) wide on board floors, and room in it for the grooms and the fodder. A stall's depth, and so its area, is a GUESS; so is the number of horses.
