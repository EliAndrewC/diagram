Did the magistrate post notices at the office's own gate, or on the town's notice board?

Sources: kosatsu-jawiki (where the boards stood, and the notices at court gates after the boards ended)

On the town's board, as far as anything read says. The boards of standing law were hung high where traffic was heavy - at crossroads in a town's center, at its entrances, at bridge ends and at barriers (the checkpoints on the roads) - and the shogunate set their places at busy points, barriers, ports, the ends of great bridges and the entrances and centers of towns and villages. Laws came to be posted at the gates of courts and prefectural offices after the boards were abolished, in the Meiji period. No page read puts a board at the gate of an intendant's office or a magistracy, and the Chinese county office's own posting walls at its gate could not be read.

What this means for a plan: the town's notice board, the kosatsuba, stands where the traffic is and is ACCURATE (The notice board (kosatsuba): siting is a traffic decision). A board of the office's own at its gate, apart from the town's, is a GUESS: its likely counterpart is the Chinese county office's gate, which no page here could be read for.
