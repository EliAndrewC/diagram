Did the magistrate post notices at the office's own gate, or on the town's notice board?

Sources: kosatsu-jawiki (where the boards stood, and the notices at court gates after the boards ended), adachi-kosatsu (boards before village officials' gates), gmw-tianxia-yamen (what a Chinese county office posted on the walls at its gate)

Both, before the modern era, though in Japan the office's own board is found only at the village level. The boards of standing law were hung high where traffic was heavy - at crossroads in a town's center, at its entrances, at bridge ends and at barriers (the checkpoints on the roads) - and the shogunate set their places at busy points, barriers, ports, the ends of great bridges and the entrances and centers of towns and villages. In the Edo period the boards also hung before the gates of village officials' houses, as well as at bridge ends. In Ming and Qing China the county office posted at its own gate: the splayed walls flanking it carried the emperor's edicts, which the people could read there every month (one set is quoted from 1519), and beside them the county magistrate's own notices and bans, among them a notice to arrest fleeing murderers and escaped criminals, with the lists of tax arrears, rulings on complaints, verdicts and examination results. Laws came to be posted at the gates of Japan's courts and prefectural offices only after the boards were abolished, in the Meiji period. No page read puts a board at the gate of a Japanese intendant's office or a magistracy.

What this means for a plan: the town's notice board, the kosatsuba, stands where the traffic is and is ACCURATE (The notice board (kosatsuba): siting is a traffic decision). Notices posted at the office's own gate are ACCURATE for a Chinese county office before the modern era, wanted notices among them, so a "bounty board" at the gate has a premodern counterpart. Drawing them on a freestanding board rather than on the gate's walls is a GUESS joined from the Chinese walls and the Japanese boards at village officials' gates.
