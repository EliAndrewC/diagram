Did the servants' rowhouse have one door, or a door for each household?

Sources: mitamura-nagaya-tcpip (an entrance to each house of a rowhouse), hoppou-shibata-ashigaru (a servants' rowhouse, each household with its own entrance), kochi-hantei-jstage (staff and servants' dwellings on plans of the 1790s), kotobank-chugen (the chugen living together in large rooms), nagayamon-jawiki (the chugen room in a gate range), shinke-nagayamon-kanazawa (a chugen room and its entrance), daozuofang (servants in a Beijing house's south range), qianggen-siheyuan-layout (a three-bay range entered by one door)

A rowhouse divided into dwellings gave every dwelling its own door, and servants' rowhouses were no exception. The historian Mitamura Engyo (mitamura-nagaya-tcpip) writes that the front rowhouse of a lord's residence ran on without a break but was partitioned house by house, each house with an entrance of its own; and that in the middle rowhouse, many dwellings in a row, each dwelling's entrance bay was an earth floor for leaving shoes, closed by a lattice door of two leaves. The Shibata domain's rowhouse of 1842 holds eight households under one thatched roof, each household's rooms opening from its own small entrance earth floor; its households were not foot soldiers but retainers of very low status: servants of the gate-guard, flag-bearer and other companies, and rope men. The Kochi domain's plans of its Osaka and Kyoto residences, drawn in the 1790s, as Uematsu, Nakajima and Tani read them (kochi-hantei-jstage), carry the same division down to the lowest staff. At Osaka the lower staff, from clerks to gatekeepers, had fourteen dwellings spread through the east and west residences, all but one of a single room; in the rowhouse where the scribes, clerks and small-boat captains lived, every dwelling had an earth floor at the front and another at the back. At Kyoto the permanent servants had dwellings of two rooms and the gatekeepers of one; all but the gatekeepers' had an earth floor, and one servants' dwelling held a kitchen that the authors take to have been for cooking in common. The plans draw no difference between a wall and a door, and the authors worked the dwellings out from their entrances, earth floors and privies, so a dwelling's own way in is their reading; where each door stood cannot be read from the plans.

The chugen lived together in common rooms; the one such room described has only one entrance named. The chugen, the attendants ranked below the foot soldiers, were organized in groups under a head and lived together in large rooms such as the chugen room of their lord's residence. In a samurai's own residence the gate range held rooms such as the gatekeeper's room and the chugen room, in its parts on either side of the gateway. In the gate range of the Araie residence in Kanazawa the chugen room is two rooms of six tatami mats, entered by an entrance fitted with a sliding door of fine lattice, with an earth floor and a sink behind them; the page names that one entrance to the room and no other way into it, though it does not say there was none. How big a lord's common rooms were, and how many men shared one, is on no page read.

In China the servants lived in the south range; how its rooms were entered was not found. In a Beijing courtyard house the rooms of the south range between the family school at its east end and the privy at its west end were generally where the servants lived. The family's own north range of three bays had a door only in its middle bay, the hall, the side bays opening only onto the hall: a suite of rooms behind one door. Whether the servants' bays of the south range were entered the same way, or each by a door of its own, is on no page read.

So both forms are historical, and they follow how the servants were housed. Where servants were housed in dwellings, a household or a few men to each, every dwelling had its own door; where they lived communally, as the chugen of a lord's residence did, they shared a room with the one entrance a room has. No page read shows a row of dwellings walled off from one another behind a single shared door, and which form a country magistrate's post gave its servants is on no page read either. How the rooms of the staff's own barracks were divided is in How were the rooms of a compound's barracks divided?, and how big a staff rowhouse was in How big was the rowhouse a magistrate's staff lived in?

The rule the map follows: a compound's servants' quarters takes one of the two historical forms, chosen per compound: a common room, its bays open to one another or divided (a guess) by sliding partitions and entered by one door; or a row of dwellings of one or two bays each, every dwelling with a door of its own. Both forms are accurate; the share of compounds that take each is a guess, and so is how many bays a common room runs to (the one described is two rooms of six mats, about 9 by 12 ft each). A row of bays walled off from one another behind one shared door is never drawn. Ubame's servants' quarters, four bays with one door, is the common-room form and is accurate as drawn, provided its bay divisions read as sliding partitions within one room and not as walls between dwellings; drawn in the other form, it takes a door to each dwelling.
