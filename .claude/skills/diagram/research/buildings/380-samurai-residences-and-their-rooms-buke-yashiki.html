Samurai residences and their rooms (buke yashiki)

A samurai residence, a buke yashiki, was a house in enclosed grounds of its own, its rooms grouped by use: a formal side where guests were received, and a private side where the household lived. At a middle rank it was modest. The house of a 150-koku district magistrate at Matsushiro, built in 1794, in the Edo period, is about 49 tsubo (~1,740 sq ft), on grounds of about a thousand tsubo (~0.8 acre). Some surviving houses were altered, or restored to a later plan, after Japan began to modernize in 1868, so a house's size speaks for the Edo period only where it is dated from before then. Such a house was one block under one roof: corridors joining separate halls belonged to the mansions of shoguns and daimyo, and even a guest was received inside the one house. The formal garden lay to the south, before the formal rooms, and a storehouse could stand behind the house.

  • How big was a middle-rank samurai's house before 1868?
    About 49 tsubo (about 1,740 sq ft), at the one house of that rank firmly dated. The Yokota house at Matsushiro, the representative residence of a middle-rank samurai of that domain, a 150-koku man who served as district magistrate among other posts, has a main house of 1794 of about 49 tsubo on grounds of 1,012 tsubo (~0.8 acre). The town history dates it by an ink inscription on a pillar and by its style, and gives it as 17.1 m (~56 ft) by 9.6 m (~31 ft).
  • The Yokota house at Matsushiro, built in 1794, had been added to by the time Japan began to modernize in 1868.
    By then its plan had been considerably altered, with rooms and a bath room added and the kitchen changed, though little from the entrance to the parlor.
  • How big was the house of a samurai of about a thousand koku?
    Between about 41 and 74 tsubo (~1,470-2,630 sq ft), by two official measures of one house, the Kuchiba house at Hagi, that do not agree. The national register calls it the house of a Hagi domain retainer of about 1,000 koku, and gives the main house as 12.9 m (~42 ft) by 10.0 m (~33 ft) with an east wing of 2.0 m (~7 ft) by 3.9 m (~13 ft), about 41 tsubo in all. The prefecture gives it as 14.9 m (~49 ft) by 11.9 m (~39 ft), with a west wing of 3.0 m (~10 ft) by 8.9 m (~29 ft) and an east wing, the kitchen, of 5.9 m (~19 ft) by 6.9 m (~23 ft), about 74 tsubo in all.
  • When was the Kuchiba house, a thousand-koku samurai house at Hagi, built?
    Before 1868, by both accounts, though the prefecture says no document dates it and the register cites none. The register dates it only to the late Edo period, in the 19th century. The prefecture, which reports that the household is said to have held 1,018 koku at the end of the period, dates it to the late 18th or early 19th century.
  • The Kuchiba house was little altered, the prefecture says, apart from later additions.
    Apart from later extension there is little alteration, the entrance now being in an addition on the north.
  • The Matsue house of about 67 tsubo was restored to a plan from after 1868.
    The Matsue samurai residence's main house, about 67 tsubo (about 2,380 sq ft), was lived in by turns by retainers of about 500 to 1,000 koku. It was rebuilt after the fire of 1733, extended and altered several times, and restored from drawings of the Meiji period, which began in 1868, so the figure measures the house as it stood after 1868, not as it was built.
  • How much of a house was its kitchen?
    About a sixth, at the one house whose kitchen is measured. By the prefecture's measure the Kuchiba house's east wing, a kitchen of board floor and earth floor, is about 12 tsubo (~440 sq ft); the register's east wing is far smaller, and neither says whether the wing is original. No other house we read gives its kitchen's share.

Inside, the rooms ran in an order, and the best-recorded order is a lord's palace's.

  • A lord's palace ran from front to back: the omote, the lord's own rooms, the oku.
    The shogun's own palace in the main bailey at Edo was divided by use into three parts: the omote, the naka-oku and the Ooku, the great oku.
  • The omote, at the front, was where the lord received retainers and envoys.
    It held the entrance, the great halls and the shoin, and audiences, events and ceremonies were held there.
  • The lord's own rooms were a room for daytime work and a bedroom.
    They lay behind the omote and are now called the naka-oku, though the word is not strictly a historical one, and Edo-period drawings count the shogun's own office and bedroom as part of the omote.
  • The oku held the family's rooms, where coming and going was strictly limited.
    It held the lord's private rooms and the quarters of the lord's wife and consorts, and because women of high status lived there, who came and went was strictly limited.
  • At Edo the great oku was walled off from the rest of the palace and joined to it by one corridor.
    The Ooku stood beyond the naka-oku, on its north side, walled off from it and joined to it only by the Osuzu corridor.
  • A daimyo mansion was divided the same way, into a formal omote and a private oku.
    Its goza-no-ma stood with the great hall and the audience hall on the omote side, and the lady's palace on the oku side.

A smaller house could run the other way, its formal room deepest in.

  • Did a smaller house set its formal room at the far end?
    One did, as this project reads its plan. The Kuchiba house at Hagi, of an upper samurai of about a thousand koku, is described from the entrance room through an anteroom, the living room and a second room, to the zashiki and the inner zashiki. Those rooms stand in two rows, front and back, and the zashiki as the deepest of them is this project's reading of the order the page gives.
  • At a house of 500- to 1,000-koku retainers at Matsue, the master's living room was finished playfully, unlike the formal zashiki.
    The master's living room is finished playfully, in contrast to the front of the house, while the zashiki where the master received guests is built for formality; the house keeps a family room and the wife's living room besides.

The great houses were built as many halls joined by corridors, and at Katsura the halls went up one at a time, each stepped back from the last.

  • Corridors between buildings belonged to the mansions of shoguns and daimyo.
    Corridors developed greatly in the Momoyama and Edo-period mansions of shoguns and daimyo, where watari-roka, corridors built between buildings, joined the buildings to one another, while farmhouses, townhouses and the houses of middle and lower-ranking samurai had almost none.
  • At the Katsura villa four halls stand in echelon, stepped back one behind the next like geese in flight.
    Katsura Rikyu's four shoin halls stand in echelon from northeast to southwest on the flat ground west of the pond.
  • Katsura's halls went up one after another over about half a century.
    They were not built as one: they were built in three phases, from about 1615 to about 1662.
  • The Kuchiba house, of a thousand-koku samurai at Hagi, is one main house.
    Its six rooms are all in that one house, with its kitchen projecting from one side.

A house's guests stayed in the house; the traveler of rank had a lodging house of their own.

  • A samurai house kept its guest rooms inside the house.
    The residence at the Takayama Jin'ya had, among its rooms, a room for receiving guests. The Takei house, a middle-rank samurai house of the late Edo period reproduced at the Boso-no-mura museum, divides into living space (the living room, the dining room, the kitchen and the earth-floored doma) and guest space (the shikidai entrance, the anteroom where a visitor was announced, and the zashiki, the guest room), both within its one main house.
  • Was a guest ever lodged in a building of its own at a samurai house?
    No page we read shows one, at a samurai house or at the official residence of the Takayama Jin'ya.
  • A traveling lord or official lodged at a honjin, a post town's house for guests of rank.
    A traveling daimyo, a hatamoto or a shogunal official lodged at a honjin, a house in a post town that was allowed what an ordinary inn was not: a front gate, a genkan with a shikidai, and a jodan-no-ma, the raised room of honor.
  • At the Yakage honjin the guest rooms are listed apart from the family's house.
    Its guest facilities, the o-zashiki with its jodan-no-ma and a gate for the lord's arrival, are listed apart from the main house where the family lived and ran its sake brewery, among more than ten buildings on the site; the page does not say whether the o-zashiki is a building of its own.

Outside, a house's grounds were divided by the sun: the south was the garden's, and the house's working ground lay behind it. Where a house kept its vegetable garden is at Vegetable gardens at a samurai residence (saien)

  • The formal garden lay to the south of the formal rooms.
    In the shoin style of formal rooms the garden spread out on their south side, beyond the veranda-corridor, and the shoin window of the raised seat faced south for its light.
  • At the Higuchi house at Matsushiro the garden lay south and the house and storehouse north.
    A watercourse runs through the grounds, with the wood, the bamboo and the garden to its south, and to its north the main house, the storehouse, the rowhouse, the household shrine and the front gate.
  • The Higuchi storehouse stood behind the house, beyond open ground.
    Behind the main house lies a niwa, the word a yard as much as a garden, and the storehouse stands beyond it.
  • The Yokota house kept two storehouses.
    Along with the house it has a vegetable garden, a pond, a retirement house and two storehouses.
  • How big was a household storehouse?
    Small: about 12 by 18 ft is the common size a builder gives. The Yokota house's two measure 2.7 m (~9 ft) by 3.6 m (~12 ft) and, of two stories, 5.5 m (~18 ft) by 4.5 m (~15 ft). A plasterer who builds them puts the common size at about 2 by 3 ken, 3.64 m (~12 ft) by 5.46 m (~18 ft).
  • At Matsue the gatekeepers and servants lived in the gate range.
    The nagayamon, the gate built into a range of rooms, was also the dwelling of the gatekeepers and the household's servants.
  • Where in the grounds did a house keep its well, its family privy and its servants' rooms?
    For the well and the family privy, no page we read says; the servants at Matsue lived in the gate range. The Takayama residence had rooms for its servants, a kitchen, a well and a bath, but no page we read places a well or the family privy anywhere in a samurai house's grounds, or a servants' range behind the house. The article on the shoin style says nothing of the north side; reasoning from the garden's place in the sunny south, this project guesses that the well, the family privy and any servants' range stood in the shady rear, with the storehouses: a GUESS.
