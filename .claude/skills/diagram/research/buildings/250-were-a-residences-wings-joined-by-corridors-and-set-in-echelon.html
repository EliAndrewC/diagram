Were a residence's wings joined by corridors and set in echelon, like geese in flight?

Sources: watariroka-kotobank (what a watari-roka is), irikawa-kotobank (corridors between buildings in the great mansions, and almost none in lesser houses), katsura-rikyu-jawiki (four shoin halls in echelon, built one after another), touken-world-buke-madori (an upper samurai's house under one roof)

A watari-roka is a corridor between two buildings that joins them. Corridors of this kind belong to the great houses: interior corridors developed greatly in the Momoyama and Edo mansions of shoguns and daimyo, where corridors joined the buildings to one another, while farmhouses, townhouses and the houses of middle and lower-ranking samurai had almost none.

The offset massing - halls stepped back one behind the next, like geese in flight - is best seen at Katsura, whose four shoin halls stand in echelon from northeast to southwest on the flat ground west of the pond. They were not planned as one: the halls went up one after another over about half a century, from about 1615 to about 1662. An upper samurai's house of about a thousand koku, the Kuchiba house at Hagi, is by contrast one main house of six rooms with its kitchen projecting from one side.

What this means for a plan: the residence's massing is a knob with two attested forms. The ordinary form is one block, its rooms under one roof. The echelon of separate halls joined by corridors is the form of the great house, and at Katsura it is the form a house takes when it grows hall by hall across generations - so where a residence is drawn in echelon, the plan should read as one that was added to, and a corridor joins each hall to the last.
