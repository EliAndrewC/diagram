Kitchens (daidokoro)

The kitchen, the daidokoro, was where a samurai household cooked, on a kamado, a clay range with fire-mouths. A samurai house was built in the shoin style with nearly every room matted, but its kitchen was board-floored, and its doma, the earth-floored part, was smaller than a farmhouse's. The sunken hearth, the irori, was the fire of the common house.

A kitchen had its own door from outside, the katteguchi, a word already in use in 1666. The family came and went by an inner entrance of its own, and guests by the formal one (The formal entrance and a guest's arrival (genkan)).

  • A samurai house's kitchen was board-floored, though its other rooms were matted.
    A house-builder's history of the Japanese kitchen says so of the shoin-style samurai house, and that its doma was smaller than a farmhouse's.
  • As a rule, the kamado stood on the doma.
    The same history says that basically the kamado was placed on the doma, but that in a narrow row house it sometimes had to go on the board floor between the matted room and the doma.
  • Did a samurai kitchen set its kamado on the board floor?
    Modern accounts say it sometimes did. The history says that because the doma of a samurai house was smaller, the kamado was sometimes set in the board-floored kitchen, and it captions a photograph of a middle-rank samurai's house with a narrow doma and a kamado on the board floor. No page from before 1868 that we could read, or dating the form before 1868, puts it there.
  • A museum's reproduction of a retainer's house has a movable kamado on its kitchen's boards.
    The Boso-no-mura house reproduces the late Edo house of a middle-rank retainer of the Sakura domain. Its kitchen is board-floored, with a movable kamado and a sink, under a ceiling whose boards are set with gaps to let the smoke out.
  • In an Edo tenement, a kamado could stand on legs on the board floor.
    In a tenement kitchen of the Edo period as the Fukagawa Edo Museum reproduces it, the kamado was built straight onto the doma or set on the board floor; set on the boards, its base was boxed in boards on four legs of about 10 cm (~4 in), and it could be moved.
  • A Hagi retainer's kitchen, of about 1800, was part board floor and part doma.
    The Kuchiba house at Hagi, of a retainer of 1,018 koku, was probably built in the late eighteenth or early nineteenth century, and its east wing is a kitchen of board floor and doma. The page does not say where its kamado stood.
  • A Matsushiro magistrate's kitchen of 1794 was all board floor, with its sink set on the floor.
    The Yokota house, of a 150-koku man who served as district magistrate, dates from 1794, and its kitchen is among the parts the town history says were altered by about 1868. The page does not say where its kamado stood.
  • The Matsue samurai house names a kamado among its features.
    Retainers of about 500 to 1,000 koku lived in it by turns, and its page lists the kamado among the house's features without describing it.
  • The sunken hearth belonged to the common house.
    In the traditional common house, the minka, it is cut into the floor of a room near the doma or the kitchen, most often at the center of the floored part, and sometimes set in the doma itself.
  • Did a samurai kitchen have a sunken hearth?
    No page we read puts one there.

The kitchen also had its own way in from outside.

  • The kitchen door was the doorway leading from outside into the kitchen.
    That is how the Japanese dictionary defines the katteguchi.
  • The word is old: its first recorded use for a kitchen's doorway is in a poetry collection of 1666.
    The dictionary's earliest example is a verse in a haikai collection of that year.
  • The family had an inner entrance of its own, apart from the formal one.
    The uchigenkan was made in addition to the main entrance, for the family and those of the house to come and go by. The Matsue house names both, its inner entrance and its formal entrance with a step for guests.
  • Who came in by the kitchen door?
    Only modern pages say. A column of 2018, on a floor plan drawn to a manga author's instructions for the house of a fictional high-ranking family in a manga set at the end of the Edo period, has those of lower status than the master come in and out through the doma, not the entrance. A Japanese encyclopedia has tradesmen taking orders, neighbors and children use the kitchen door, of houses today. No page from before 1868 we could read says who came in by a samurai house's kitchen door.
  • Did a kitchen have a second outside door, or a way of its own to carry food to the rooms?
    No page we read shows either.
