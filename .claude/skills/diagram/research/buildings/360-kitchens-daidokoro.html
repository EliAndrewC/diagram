Kitchens (daidokoro)

The kitchen, the daidokoro, was where a samurai household cooked, on a kamado, a clay range with fire-mouths. A samurai house was built in the shoin style with nearly every room matted, but its kitchen was board-floored, and its doma, the earth-floored part, was smaller than a farmhouse's. A common house, the minka, also had a sunken hearth, the irori, cut into its floor.

A kitchen had its own door from outside, the katteguchi, a word already in use in 1666. The family came and went by an inner entrance of its own, and guests by the formal one (The formal entrance and a guest's arrival (genkan)).

  • A samurai house's kitchen was board-floored, though its other rooms were matted.
    A house-builder's history of the Japanese kitchen says so of the shoin-style samurai house, and that its doma was smaller than a farmhouse's.
  • A house-builder's history says that in the Edo period the kamado, as a rule, stood on the doma.
    That history of the Japanese kitchen says that basically the kamado was placed on the doma, but that in a narrow row house it sometimes had to go on the board floor between the matted room and the doma.
  • The preserved samurai house at Matsue lists a kamado among its features.
    Retainers of about 500 to 1,000 koku lived in it by turns, and its page lists the kamado among the house's features without describing it.
  • Did a samurai kitchen set its kamado on the board floor?
    Two modern accounts say it sometimes did. The house-builder's history of the Japanese kitchen says that because the doma of a samurai house was smaller, the kamado was sometimes set in the board-floored kitchen, and it captions a photograph of a middle-rank samurai's house with a narrow doma and a kamado on the board floor. The Boso-no-mura museum's reproduction of the late Edo house of a middle-rank retainer of the Sakura domain has a board-floored kitchen with a movable kamado and a sink, under a ceiling whose boards are set with gaps to let the smoke out; the museum says its house is modeled on the retainer's, not that its kitchen is the one he had. No page from before 1868 that we could read, or dating the form before 1868, puts it there.

Outside those modern accounts, a kamado on the board floor is shown in a commoner's tenement of the Edo period, not in a samurai house.

  • In a commoner's tenement of the Edo period, a kamado could stand on legs on the board floor.
    In a tenement kitchen of the Edo period as the Fukagawa Edo Museum reproduces it, the kamado was built straight onto the doma or set on the board floor; set on the boards, its base was boxed in boards on four legs of about 10 cm (~4 in), and it could be moved.
  • A Hagi retainer's kitchen, probably built around 1800 in the Edo period, was part board floor and part doma.
    The Kuchiba house at Hagi, of a retainer of 1,018 koku, was probably built in the late eighteenth or early nineteenth century, and its east wing is a kitchen of board floor and doma. The page does not say where its kamado stood.
  • A Matsushiro magistrate's house, built in 1794 in the Edo period, has a kitchen all of board floor, with its sink set on the floor.
    The Yokota house, of a 150-koku man who served as district magistrate, dates from 1794. The town history says its kitchen is all board floor with the sink set on the floor, and counts the kitchen among the parts altered by about 1868. The page does not say where its kamado stood.
  • A common house cut its sunken hearth into the floor near the doma or the kitchen.
    In the traditional common house, the minka, it is cut into the floor of a room near the doma or the kitchen, most often at the center of the floored part, and sometimes set in the doma itself.
  • Did a samurai kitchen have a sunken hearth?
    No page we read puts one there.

The dictionaries say more of the kitchen's own door than the houses we read do.

  • The kitchen door was the doorway leading from outside into the kitchen.
    That is how the Japanese dictionary defines the katteguchi.
  • The word is old: its first recorded use for a kitchen's doorway is in a poetry collection of 1666, early in the Edo period.
    The dictionary's earliest example is a verse in a haikai collection of that year.
  • The family had an inner entrance of its own, apart from the formal entrance (genkan).
    The uchigenkan was made in addition to the main entrance, for the family and those of the house to come and go by. The Matsue house names both, its inner entrance and its formal entrance with a step for guests.
  • Who came in by the kitchen door?
    Only modern pages say. A column of 2018, on a floor plan drawn to a manga author's instructions for the house of a fictional high-ranking family in a manga set at the end of the Edo period, has those of lower status than the master come in and out through an earth-floored doma, not the entrance. A Japanese encyclopedia has tradesmen taking orders, neighbors and children using the kitchen door, of houses today. No page from before 1868 we could read says who came in by a samurai house's kitchen door.
  • Did a kitchen have a second outside door, or a way of its own to carry food to the rooms?
    No page we read shows either.
