Staff rowhouses and barracks (nagaya)

The junior officials of a shogunate intendant's office, and a lord's foot soldiers and lower retainers, lived in rowhouses (nagaya), which served as their barracks: long, narrow, single-story buildings divided along their length into small dwellings, each an earth-floored entry and a raised plank floor. The rowhouses that were measured run about 80 to 145 ft long and 12 to 24 ft deep. The historian Mitamura Engyo (mitamura-nagaya-tcpip) traced the form to the soldiers' lodging huts at the foot of the Warring States mountain castles.

Where officials and a lord's retainers lived varied. A rural intendant's office kept its junior officials' huts and rowhouses on its own grounds, while the great town magistracy of Edo, the shogun's capital, housed its constables in a district of their own.

  • An intendant's office or a small lord's jin'ya kept its staff's quarters on its grounds.
    A Japanese encyclopedia counts, among the parts of a shogunate intendant's office, the office itself, the intendant's residence, the storehouses, the jail, and the huts and rowhouses that were the official quarters of its junior officials. A small domain's jin'ya likewise laid out its retainers' official residences among its hall, offices and storehouses.
  • At Takayama, an intendant's office, the storehouse keepers' rowhouse stood on the grounds and survives, restored.
    The Takayama jin'ya, an intendant's office, was restored to nearly its Edo-period condition from a plan of 1830, and the storehouse keepers' rowhouse was one of the parts restored.
  • Did all the staff of a rural intendant's office live on its grounds?
    That they did is a reconstruction. The sources name the junior officials' quarters and the storehouse keepers' rowhouse, but none gives a count of the staff or says who else lived inside.
  • Edo's town magistracy housed its constables in a district of their own, Hatchōbori.
    After the temples of Hatchōbori were moved to Asakusa in 1635, the group residences of the town magistrate's constables (yoriki and doshin) were set up on their sites.
  • Edo's town magistrate lived inside the magistracy, apart from the constables.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on the office says so of the magistrate's residence alone, and not who else lived inside.
  • The lower warriors who came to Edo with their lord lived in rowhouses inside his residence.
    Many retainers followed a lord to Edo on his alternate attendance, and the lower warriors among them lived in rowhouses set up inside the domain's residence there.

Five rowhouses of warriors and officials survive or are measured: Takayama's storehouse keepers' rowhouse, two rows of foot soldiers' dwellings, and the gate ranges of an intendant's office and a lord's seat, whose gateway ran through the rowhouse itself (The main gate and its gatekeepers (nagaya-mon)).

  • The gate rowhouses of the intendant's office at Omori run 33 m (~108 ft) at a width of about 4 m (~13 ft).
    The office governed the Iwami silver mine and some 150 villages around it, and its front gate survives with the gate rowhouses attached on its left and right.
  • The rowhouse gate of the Kaibara jin'ya is 13.5 ken long by 2 ken deep (~81 by 12 ft).
    Kaibara was the seat of a lord of 20,000 koku. The prefecture's designation of the gate gives these measurements. The city's own description gives it as about 13 ken long.
  • The Kaibara rowhouse gate was divided into rooms on either side of its gateway.
    The prefectural designation gives it three rooms of eight tatami mats (about 12 ft square) on one side, and one such room and an earth-floored room on the other. The city's own description names them: a guardroom on one side, and a horse-viewing room and a gun store on the other.
  • The Shibata domain's foot soldiers' row is 43.6 m (~143 ft) long and 7.3 m (~24 ft) deep.
    The national record of cultural properties dates it to 1842, late in the Edo period, and says the domain's lower-ranking warriors lived in it, eight households joined in one building.
  • Who lived in the Shibata domain's foot soldiers' row?
    The national record names the building a foot soldiers' rowhouse and its residents lower-ranking warriors. A local history page says they were not foot soldiers but retainers of lower status still: by post, servants of the gate-guard, flag-bearer and other companies, and rope men.
  • In the Obata domain's foot soldiers' row, each dwelling is 2 ken wide and 3.5 ken deep (~12 by 21 ft).
    Each is one living room, an entrance and a kitchen, the smallest unit of dwelling. A row held ten of them, and two such rows stood, east and west.
  • Takayama's restored storehouse keepers' rowhouse has a floor area of 342.83 m² (~3,690 sq ft).
    A Gifu prefecture document lists its floor area with the date of its restoration, March 1992.
  • How big was a staff rowhouse?
    On these measurements, 12 to 24 ft deep and 80 to 145 ft long, built as a row of bays about 12 to 18 ft wide: the Obata dwelling's 2 ken, and the Shibata row's length shared among its eight households, on this project's arithmetic.

Inside, a warriors' rowhouse was divided into dwellings, though the servants below the foot soldiers shared large rooms.

  • The historian Mitamura Engyo gives a warrior's rowhouse a standard dwelling of 9 shaku by 2 ken (~9 by 12 ft).
    A page quoting his collected works has him call it the middle rowhouse, where a lord's men below full samurai rank lived, down to the foot soldiers: one ken at the entrance an earth floor for taking off shoes, the other a raised plank floor.
  • Mitamura traced the rowhouse dwelling to the soldiers' lodging huts at the foot of the Warring States mountain castles.
    That origin is his own reading. He cites a war tale, the Dobora Gunki, on the servants' room at Okuwa castle: a rowhouse dwelling so short that a spear hung inside it had its butt sticking outside.
  • Three retainers on duty in Edo late in the Edo period shared one dwelling of their domain's rowhouse.
    In 1860 Sakai Hanshiro, a retainer of the Wakayama domain, lived in the duty rowhouse of his domain's Edo residence at Akasaka with his uncle and a colleague, the two juniors cooking by turns.
  • The chugen, servants below the foot soldiers, lived together in large rooms.
    The attendants below the foot soldiers were organized in groups under a head, and in their lord's residence lived communally in large rooms such as the chugen room.
  • A samurai's gate range held a room for the chugen beside its gateway.
    In a samurai's own residence the gate range held rooms such as the gatekeeper's room and the chugen room, in its parts on either side of the gateway, and they lodged retainers and servants.
  • How big were a lord's common rooms, and how many men shared one?
    No page we read says.
  • How many men shared one dwelling of a staff rowhouse?
    No page we read says. The pages count a row's dwellings as households, and the Obata domain's plan of its grounds writes twenty names in all on the rowhouse's site, where two rows of ten dwellings stood - one name to a dwelling, on this project's arithmetic, which fits a single man to a dwelling as well as a family. The Edo duty rowhouse of 1860 held three men to one.
  • Did a rowhouse before modern times have beds or bunks?
    No page we read puts a bed, a bunk or a sleeping shelf in a premodern rowhouse or servants' room.
  • When did beds come into a Japanese barracks?
    The earliest barracks we found with beds is a modern army's: an infantry barracks built at Nagoya in 1873, whose squad rooms are shown today furnished with wooden beds. It stood at Nagoya until it was taken down in 1963, and its page does not say when the beds were put in, so 1873 dates the building, not the beds. When beds first came into a Japanese barracks we did not find.

How a rowhouse's dwellings were entered followed how their occupants were housed.

  • The historian Mitamura Engyo gives every dwelling of a warriors' rowhouse an entrance of its own.
    He writes that the front rowhouse of a lord's residence ran on without a break but was partitioned house by house, each house with an entrance of its own, and that in the middle rowhouse, many dwellings in a row, each dwelling's entrance bay was an earth floor for leaving shoes, closed by a lattice door of two leaves.
  • In the Shibata row, each of its eight households of low-ranking warriors or servants entered by a small earth floor of its own.
    A local history page describes the row of 1842 as eight households under one thatched roof, each household's rooms opening from its own small entrance earth floor.
  • The Kochi domain's plans of its Osaka residence give even its lower staff dwellings of their own.
    The plans were drawn in the Kansei era (1789-1801), and Uematsu, Nakajima and Tani read them (kochi-hantei-jstage). At Osaka the lower staff, from clerks to gatekeepers, had fourteen dwellings spread through the east and west residences, all but one of a single room. In the rowhouse where the scribes, clerks and small-boat captains lived, every dwelling had an earth floor at the front and another at the back.
  • At its Kyoto residence the Kochi domain gave its permanent servants dwellings of two rooms, and its gatekeepers one.
    All but the gatekeepers' had an earth floor, and one servants' dwelling held a kitchen that the authors take to have been for cooking in common.
  • Did each Kochi dwelling have a door of its own?
    That is the authors' reading. The plans draw no difference between a wall and a door, and the authors worked the dwellings out from their entrances, earth floors and privies, so where each door stood cannot be read from the plans.
  • How was a chugen room entered?
    The one described, in the gate range of the Araie residence in Kanazawa, is two rooms of six tatami mats, each about 9 by 12 ft, entered by a sliding door of fine lattice, with an earth floor and a sink behind them. The page names that one entrance to the room and no other way into it, though it does not say there was none.
  • Did servants' dwellings each have a door, or share one?
    Both forms are historical, and they follow how the servants were housed. Where servants lived in dwellings, a household or a few men to each, every dwelling had its own door; where they lived in common, as the chugen of a lord's residence did, they shared a room with the one entrance a room has.
  • Were dwellings ever walled off from one another behind one shared door?
    No page we read shows such a row, or says which form a rural intendant's office gave its servants.

China shows the other form, several rooms behind one door: a Beijing courtyard house lodged its servants not in a rowhouse but in one of the ranges of rooms around its courtyard.

  • In a Beijing courtyard house the servants lived in the south range.
    The rooms of the south range between the family school at its east end and the privy at its west end were generally where the servants lived.
  • In a Beijing courtyard house, the family's own north range had one outside door for its three bays.
    Only its middle bay, the hall, opened outward; the two side bays opened only onto the hall, a suite of rooms behind one door.
  • How were the servants' rooms of the south range entered?
    No page we read says whether they were entered as the north range was, or each by a door of its own.
