Did a residence garden have a pond, and did a county post keep one?

Sources: kotobank-teien (the kinds of Japanese garden), oniwa-takayama-jinya (the intendancy office's pond garden), okutono-jinya-garden (a small domain's jin'ya garden, restored from its drawing), chiran-bukeyashiki-gardens (seven samurai-house gardens, one with a pond)

A Japanese garden was one of three kinds: the pond garden, built around real water; the dry landscape (karesansui), which shows water without using any; and the tea garden, the roji. Of the things a garden was made from, the reference lists the pond and its water first, then the stone arrangement, the made hills and the planting.

Both of the first two kinds stood beside samurai rooms. The two offices whose gardens can be read today kept water. At the Takayama Jin'ya, an office of the shogunate's intendants, a pond garden lies further in, beyond the stepping stones that lead out from the great hall and from the Arashiyama-no-ma, one of its rooms. At the Okutono Jin'ya, the seat of a small domain in Mikawa, the garden laid out again after an Edo-period drawing of it is a pond garden in two tiers, the lower one seen from the shoin, the formal reception room; the jin'ya itself was pulled down once the domains were abolished, so what stands there now is a restoration. Among the samurai houses of Chiran, one of the samurai settlements the Satsuma domain formed around the residence of a district's steward or lord once it had divided its land into 102 districts, the balance runs the other way: of the seven gardens protected there, one is a pond garden and the other six are dry landscape.

How large a pond was in a residence garden, no page read says. The one garden size found is not a residence's: the dry garden beside the shoin of the Daisen-in, a temple, is a level plot of about 100 square meters, about 1,100 square feet, made to be looked at from the room and its veranda.

What this means for a plan: a residence garden is a KNOB between two attested forms, rolled from the map's seed - a pond garden with open water, or a dry garden of stones and white gravel standing for water - and both are ACCURATE. A county post keeping a pond is ACCURATE as a form, but both offices read that had one governed far more than a county (a domain and a province), so it is the grander of the two forms for a small posting; the weight between the forms is a GUESS. The pond's form and size are a GUESS, drawn small enough to sit within sight of the rooms that face the garden.
