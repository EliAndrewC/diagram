Hunting dogs and kennels (inugoya)

Warrior households hunted with dogs. The great warlords loved hawking, and it spread widely among the daimyo; in it the dogs drove the game out of the thickets for the hawk, and boar was hunted with packs of dogs in the mountains. Keeping and training the dogs was a trade of its own.

Where a household kept its dogs is not recorded. The one compound known to have housed dogs, an official's at Kitami, kept about forty of them in 1693, in the Edo period, as dogs in care rather than a hunting pack, and no page we read describes a household's hunting kennel.

  • The great warlords, and then the daimyo widely, hunted with hawks.
    Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi and Tokugawa Ieyasu all loved hawking, and it spread widely among the other daimyo.
  • In hawking, dogs drove the game out of the thickets.
    Many birds of prey will not go into a thicket, so dogs flushed the game from it for them.
  • Dog-handlers under the chief falconer trained and kept the hawking dogs.
    The hawking dogs were trained and managed by dog-handlers under the command of the chief falconer. The shogun's own falconers had, besides the falconers themselves, gatekeepers for the hawk house and these handlers of the hawking dogs.
  • Raising hunting dogs was a trade of its own, the dog-keeper's.
    A national dictionary of Japanese defines the dog-keeper as one who raises the hunting dogs used in hawking.
  • A domain lord is said to have nursed his own sick hunting dog.
    A place in Oita takes its name, the story goes, from a hunt at which the lord of Taketa domain nursed a sick hunting dog himself.
  • Boar was hunted with packs of dogs, three or four to a hunter.
    The Kishu breed was fixed from native dogs used to hunt boar in the Kii mountains. In the traditional boar hunt, seven or eight hunters went into the forest, each with three or four dogs.

Dogs are recorded inside an official's compound once, and those were dogs in the shogunate's care, not a household's hunting dogs.

  • The shogunate's first dog houses stood inside an official's compound.
    They were set up within the grounds of the jin'ya of Kitami Shigemasa, the shogun's chamberlain, at Kitami village, in what is now Setagaya in Tokyo.
  • In 1693 about forty dogs were kept at Kitami, in buildings for sick dogs and pups.
    After the Kitami house was abolished in 1689 the site passed to the shogunate. An account book of 1693 finds about forty dogs kept there, in a care house, a nursing house and sleeping quarters for sick dogs and pups, beside the office, a gatehouse, a kitchen, a hulling shed and a chicken house.
  • The shelters built next were on a pound's scale.
    The shogunate went on to build dog houses at Okubo, Yotsuya and Nakano, chiefly for ownerless dogs. By 1696 Nakano had 290 buildings of dog rooms and dog-feeding rooms, each of 25 tsubo (~900 sq ft).
  • No page we read describes a household's hunting kennel.
    Its form, its size, its place in a samurai compound and any tie to the stables are all unrecorded in what we could read.
