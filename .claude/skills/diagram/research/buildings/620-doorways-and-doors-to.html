Doorways and doors (to)

The sources give the widths of Japanese doorways in ken and shaku: a ken is about 6 ft, half a ken about 3 ft, and a shaku about a foot. A farmhouse's main way in was a big sliding door, commonly about 6 ft wide, that opened onto its earth floor, and the household came and went through a low wicket set into it. An ordinary door was narrower, about 3 ft by this project's reading. A storehouse was closed by a pair of heavy plastered leaves.

  • A farmhouse's main entrance was a big board door onto the earth floor.
    It filled a large opening at the front of the house that led to the earth-floored side, and slid to one side or parted to both.
  • At a farm or fishing-village house, the everyday entrance was usually one ken wide, about 6 ft.
    The entrance to the earth floor was a sliding door one ken wide with a wicket set in it, and the wicket was what the household normally used. Such houses had a second entrance at the back, for cooking.
  • A wicket is the door of a low, narrow doorway that one ducks through.
    One cannot pass it without lowering one's head.
  • How wide was an ordinary door?
    Up to half a ken, about 3 ft. The big door is described as wider than an ordinary door, and the word for it originally meant a door wider than half a ken; read the other way, that puts an ordinary door at up to half a ken. That is this project's reading of the definition, not a width the page states.
  • A storehouse's doorway closed by two plastered leaves is old.
    One survives as it was built at the end of the Edo period, its leaves turning on pivots rather than hinges, and a storehouse built between 1830 and 1867 has massive paired doors. Neither page gives a width.
  • How wide was each of a storehouse's leaves?
    The one width found is a present-day plasterer's standard size for each hinged leaf, 3.5 shaku wide and 6 high, about 3.5 by 6 ft. No width for a leaf from before the Meiji Restoration was found.
  • How wide were a kitchen door, a guest entrance, or a storehouse's opening?
    No page we read gives any of them.
