Poverty texture is historically genuine

Sources: kotobank-kuchimai (what the tax surcharge paid for, and the 1725 change); kuchimai-jawiki (the offices where it fell short); bushijyoshi-daikan-jinya (the allowance still short after 1725, the intendants' debts, and a gundai's letter home)

The shogunate's intendants first paid for their offices out of kuchimai, a small rice surcharge collected with the tax, and spent it at their own discretion on their staff's wages and on brush, ink and paper. It did not fall evenly. One encyclopedia says the costs came in below the surcharge, while at offices where it yielded little, with no prospect of more, it became one cause of spending the main tax on the office, and of bribery. From 1725 the surcharge went to the shogunate, which paid the offices' costs instead. A popular account written from two books on the intendants says that the wages and travel costs then paid to the intendant were still not enough, and that nine intendants in ten carried a debt, called hikioi, from spending tax they had collected. The same account gives a gundai at Takayama writing home that drafts came through the double shoji and that on a snowy day the least wind piled snow on the veranda - a cold house in a snowy province, laughed off with his family, and not in itself a sign of want.

So a poor magistracy should LOOK underfunded: patched walls, weathered timber, a hall too small for its ceremonies. That picture is a guess: what was read shows that the money ran short, not what the shortfall looked like. The shabby end of the range is qualitative and rests on no figure, the nine in ten included.
