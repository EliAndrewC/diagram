Which faces of a residence carry the veranda, and how wide is it?

Sources: engawa-kotobank (the veranda inside the building line and the one outside it; the wide veranda between the main room and the garden; when farmhouses took it up), shoinzukuri-kotobank (the great mansion's rooms ringed on four sides), irikawa-kotobank (the irikawa one ken wide; the veranda on two or three sides late in the period), katsura-rikyu-jawiki (the widths hall by hall in one surviving house)

The veranda of a shoin-style house came in two layers: the irikawa inside the building line, and the en or nure-en on the outer edge, usually a step below the floor within. Its first place was the face toward the garden: in the shoin-style house a wide veranda stood between the main room and the garden and served as a secondary space of that room.

How many faces it wrapped went with the size of the house. In the great daimyo mansions of the early seventeenth century the rooms were ringed on all four sides by hiroen and irikawa. Farmhouses, townhouses and the houses of middle and lower-ranking samurai had almost no corridors; late in the Edo period houses appear with a continuous veranda along two or three sides, used as the way to the privy and the bath. Farmhouses, varying by region, took the veranda first outside the zashiki, from about the middle of the seventeenth century, and then along the front of their main rooms from the middle of the eighteenth.

The dictionary width of the irikawa is one ken, about six feet. A surviving house shows the width changing from face to face: at Katsura the Ko-shoin has a veranda one ken wide on the east of its main rooms that turns the corner and narrows to half a ken on the south, and the Naka-shoin carries a half-ken tatami irikawa round its east and south faces. No readable source gives a general width for the outer nure-en.

What this means for a plan: which faces carry the veranda is a knob with two attested forms at the scale of a provincial residence - the garden face alone, or a veranda wrapping two or three faces - and a ring on all four faces belongs to the great mansion. The width is a degree along a continuum, half a ken to one ken, and may change from one face to the next.

The rule the map follows: a residence's veranda runs along its garden face, and at some postings, rolled from the map's seed, continues round one or two further faces; it is drawn 3 to 6 feet wide, the garden face at the wider end. The garden-face-wider choice is this project's reading of the Katsura widths, not a stated rule.
