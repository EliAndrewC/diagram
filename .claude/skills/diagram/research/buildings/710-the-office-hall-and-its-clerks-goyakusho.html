The office hall and its clerks (goyakusho)

The office hall was the working building of an intendant's office (jin'ya). At the Takayama Jin'ya, which governed Hida province for the shogunate, one block held the entrance hall, the examination room where hearings were held, the working office and the great hall. Its floor came to about 8,100 sq ft, while a small branch office's whole central building was about 2,850 sq ft.

The paperwork of such an office was done by clerks hired from the district, the tedai, while the officials over them were sent by the shogunate, and a Chinese county office kept its paperwork in six clerical chambers of a few men each. The clerks worked in rooms of the office hall, not in a building of their own.

  • A province's intendancy office had a floor of about 8,100 sq ft.
    The office of the Takayama Jin'ya measures 754.75 m² (~8,124 sq ft). It is the block rebuilt in 1816, in the Edo period, that held the entrance hall, the examination room, the working office and the great hall together.   • A small branch office's central building was about 80 tsubo, some 2,850 sq ft.
    The Joge branch office was an outlying office set up away from the intendant's own, to which three officials came at the end of the shogunate; a plan of 1869 shows its buildings.
  • How big was a county magistrate's office hall?
    Somewhere between a small branch office and a province's intendancy office, on this project's reading: a county magistracy, with a platoon of staff, is larger than a three-man branch office and far smaller than a province's intendancy. Neither source gives a length or a depth, only a floor area, and no page we read gives an office hall's length and depth.
  • Japan's intendants hired their clerks, the tedai, from the district.
    The tedai were chosen from the peasants and townsmen versed in rural administration, with the shogunate's finance office's formal permission. Most were the children of village and town officials, hired first as scribes and promoted to tedai and then to chief tedai.
  • A Chinese county's six clerical chambers were set at two or three men each.
    The chambers, one for each of the court's six ministries, stood on the east and west of the county office's second courtyard; in Ming and Qing times they often ran over their establishment, to more than a hundred men.
  • In China, a few families held whole offices of clerks, and grew rich.
    In the capital the clerks of the silver treasury were mostly of one surname and the tax clerks of the Chongwen Gate of a few, entrenched there, and while the court's officials were often too poor to support themselves, the clerks amassed vast fortunes. That, as permanent locals, the clerks were an office's memory under one magistrate after another, and its natural pressure point for tax-farming, is this project's reading.
  • How many clerks would a county office in this setting keep?
    Three or four, paid about 4-5 koku a year each. That is this project's scaling of the Chinese county office to a county of this setting, of about 6,800 inhabitants against about 100,000-200,000 for a Chinese county - a figure of this project's own, which no page we read gives.
  • In this setting, clerks are heimen scribes who live in town.
    Scribes are heimen artisans by caste, hired locally from the county town; they live in town and come in to the manor each day.
  • At Takayama the clerks worked in rooms of the office hall.
    Beside the room where the officials sent from the shogunate worked, and partitioned from it, was the goyoba, the workroom of the officials hired mostly from the district. A room used only for writing, the kakiyaku-beya, was where the documents sent to the shogunate were drawn up, and they were bound in the room beside it.
  • At the Edo town magistracy, two of its sections' duty rooms stood in its court's quarter.
    The duty rooms of two of its sections, the shacho-sen'yokata and the reikurikata, stood in the same quarter of the compound as its court, among the rooms given to suits and examinations.
  • What an office's staff had apart was a house.
    The tetsuki and tedai of an intendant's office lived in huts and row houses inside the compound.
