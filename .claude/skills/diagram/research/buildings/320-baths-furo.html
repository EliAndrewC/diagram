Baths (furo)

A bath of one's own was a mark of rank, because hot water at home meant a well, fuel and a risk of fire. Through most of the Edo period only a few upper-rank samurai houses had a bath room, the yudono; most households washed from a tub or went to the public bath, and home baths spread only late in the period. Where a page we read places a residence's bath, it was inside the house or an addition to it, and no page we read shows a samurai compound's bath as a building of its own.

  • Around 1600, as the Edo period opened, a warrior family's bath was a one-person tub in a corner of the doma.
    In the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries nobles and warrior houses kept a small bath for the family, but it was only a tub one person could get into, with a hearth built on, in a corner of the doma, the earth-floored part of the house. It was seldom used, for fear of fire and for the cost of fuel.
  • In the Edo period a bath room was built in only a few upper-rank samurai houses.
    A yudono was built in a limited number of shoin-style residences of upper-rank samurai and the like, while middle and lower samurai and ordinary people made do with washing from a tub, or went to the public bath.
  • In an Edo-period city a home bath was a great luxury.
    It meant the cost of digging a well and of fuel.
  • Home baths spread gradually in the latter half of the Edo period.
    They spread partly because coopered tubs that could hold hot bathwater came to be mass-produced.
  • The intendant's residence at the Takayama Jin'ya had its own bath.
    The residence at the Takayama Jin'ya had, besides the rooms of the intendant, the family and the servants, an earth floor and kitchen, a well and a bath.
  • A hatamoto's house plan from late in the Edo period names its bath room.
    The 1843 plan of the Okubo family's house in Edo writes the yudono and the privy among the rooms it draws, though the page does not say whether the yudono stood in the house or apart from it.
  • The houses the Sayama domain supplied to its middle-rank retainers were built for a bath to be added on.
    An account of the Sayama jin'ya says the bath and the privy of those houses were planned from the start as additions to them.
  • Was the bath in the Yokota house at Matsushiro, a district magistrate's home, added on later?
    The Yokota family, of about 150 koku, served as district magistrates. The town history counts one bath and two privies in their house, built in 1794, and lists a bath room among the additions made to the house's first plan by about 1868. That suggests the one bath it counts was added on, though the page does not say so.
  • No page we read shows a samurai compound's bath as a building of its own.
    Where a page places the bath, it is in a corner of the doma or an addition to the house, and the hatamoto's plan does not say whether its yudono stood in the house or apart. No page we read sets it apart.
  • No page we read gives a residence's bath its size.
    The pages of the samurai houses and museums we read name the bath room, when they name it at all, without its size.
