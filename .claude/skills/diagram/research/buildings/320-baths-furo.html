Baths (furo)

A bath of one's own was a mark of rank. In the Edo period a bath room, the yudono, was built only in the few shoin-style residences of upper-rank samurai; middle and lower samurai and ordinary people washed from a tub or went to the public bath. Where a residence did have one, it was a room of the house or a small addition to it, and no page we read shows a samurai compound's bath as a building of its own.

  • In the Edo period only upper-rank samurai houses had a bath room.
    A yudono was built in a limited number of shoin-style residences of upper-rank samurai and the like, while middle and lower samurai and ordinary people made do with washing from a tub, or went to the public bath.
  • A home bath in a city was a great luxury.
    It meant the cost of digging a well and of fuel.
  • Home baths spread gradually in the latter half of the Edo period.
    Coopered tubs that could hold hot bathwater came to be mass-produced.
  • Around 1600, as the Edo period opened, a warrior family's bath was a one-person tub in a corner of the doma.
    In the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries nobles and warrior houses kept a small bath for the family, but it was only a tub one person could get into, with a hearth built on, in a corner of the doma, the earth-floored part of the house. It was seldom used, for fear of fire and for the cost of fuel.
  • At a posting the bath went with the residence.
    The residence at the Takayama Jin'ya had, besides the rooms of the intendant, the family and the servants, an earth floor and kitchen, a well and a bath.
  • A hatamoto's house plan from late in the Edo period names its bath room.
    The 1843 plan of the Okubo family's house in Edo writes the yudono and the privy among the rooms it draws, though the page does not say whether the yudono stood in the house or apart from it.
  • At the Sayama domain's middle-rank houses the bath was meant to be added on.
    The bath and the privy were both to be built as additions to the house.
  • Was the bath of an Edo-period district magistrate's house an addition?
    The town history counts one bath and two privies in the Yokota house at Matsushiro, built in 1794, and lists a bath room among the additions made to the house's first plan by about 1868. That suggests the one bath it counts was added on, though the page does not say so.
  • No page we read gives a residence's bath its size, or sets it apart as a building of its own.
    The pages of the samurai houses and museums we read name the bath room, when they name it at all, without its size.
