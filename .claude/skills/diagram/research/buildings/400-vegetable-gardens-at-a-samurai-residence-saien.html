Vegetable gardens at a samurai residence (saien)

Samurai households grew vegetables for their own table, on anything from a kitchen plot of about 100 m² (~1,076 sq ft) to half of their grounds. Offices kept vegetable ground too, inside their walls or on a parcel of their own.

Where the plot lay varied from house to house. The formal garden of a samurai house commonly held the sunny south, but a working plot is found west of one house and south of another's pond, beside that garden. How many hours of direct sun a bed of vegetables needs is at Sunlight and shade on the farm, and how a house's grounds were laid out at Samurai residences and their rooms (buke yashiki).

  • At the Takei house, a soup-greens plot of about 100 m² (~1,076 sq ft) lay west of the house.
    The house at the Boso-no-mura museum is a reproduction of the house of the Takei, a middle-rank samurai family of Sakura. Its vegetable garden is on the west side of the house, about 1 se, or 100 m² (~1,076 sq ft). Farmers from nearby came to work it, and it was called the soup-greens field, planted mainly with leafy greens.
  • At the Yokota house in Matsushiro, about half the grounds were vegetable field.
    The Yokota were middle-rank retainers of the Matsushiro domain, drawing 150 koku, who served as district magistrate among other posts. Their grounds have a frontage of 40 m (~131 ft) and cover 3,340 m² (~35,951 sq ft), or 1,012 tsubo, and about half of it is vegetable field, a sign of the household's effort to feed itself. The house keeps, with the vegetable garden, a pond, a retirement house and two storehouses.
  • At the Higuchi house, the field lay south of the pond, where the formal garden is now.
    A spring-fed watercourse runs through the middle of the grounds, and the wood, the bamboo and the garden south of its pond were a field in the Edo period, a vegetable garden that fed the household.
  • A Matsushiro house set its formal garden south of the house, and kept farm ground as a working space.
    The town's samurai houses have their garden on the south side of the building, generally with a pond, and their farm ground was a space for farm work, housework and other tasks.

Government offices, Japanese and Chinese, kept a vegetable plot as well.

  • One jin'ya had about two tan of vegetable garden inside its grounds.
    The compound was enclosed by a bamboo fence, and its officials are said to have worked the garden themselves. Two tan is about half an acre.
  • One daikan's office had a vegetable field of 30 by 20 ken on a parcel of its own.
    By the land survey register of 1736, in the Edo period, the office had a residence lot of 70 by 60 ken and, as a separate entry, a vegetable field of 30 by 20 ken, 2 tan. At about 6 ft to the ken, the field was some 180 by 120 ft.
  • A Chinese county office listed its vegetable plot behind the residence gate, near the end of its buildings.
    The Xie'e county office's list runs from the front gate to the rear. After the residence gate come the main building, its wings and the west hall, then a study, a kitchen, a stable, an archery ground and one plot of vegetable garden, and after it only the granaries.
