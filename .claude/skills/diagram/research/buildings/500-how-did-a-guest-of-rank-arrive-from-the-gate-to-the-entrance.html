How did a guest of rank arrive, from the gate to the entrance?

Sources: kotobank-shikidai (the genkan and the shikidai in a warrior's house), kominkai-genkan (the palanquin brought alongside the step), jaanus-uchigenkan (who used the main entrance and who the inner one), fuchu-joge-pamphlet (a branch office's gate and entrance), bjd-qing-yamen (the court inside a yamen's gate)

By palanquin, across open ground, to a step. In a warrior's house the projecting board floor where a palanquin was set down was called the genkan and the room of reception beyond it the shikidai; in the Edo period the whole came to be called the genkan, and the shikidai the lower board step where guests were bowed in and out. The palanquin was brought right alongside that step, so that its rider went from the palanquin into the building without setting foot on the ground, and the entrance was one for receiving formal guests. The main genkan was kept for the head of the household and honored guests; the rest of the household and its senior staff used an inner entrance.

So between the gate and the entrance lies ground a palanquin can cross and turn in. On the 1869 drawing of the shogunate's small branch office at Jōge, the entrance with its step faces the open ground inside the main gate; and through the main gate of a Chinese governor-general's office is an open front court. That this ground was ever a garden, rather than a court, no page read says.

What this means for a plan: the arrival is staged - the gate, then an open court, then the genkan's step - and a guest's door opens onto that court, never straight from the street into a room or against a building's side. This is ACCURATE, and it is the rule Guest doors feed courts, not flanks draws. A guest garden in place of the court is a GUESS.
