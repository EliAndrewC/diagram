What fire did a residence's kitchen cook on - a kamado range, or a sunken hearth?

Sources: boso-no-mura-takei (the board-floored kitchen of a middle-rank house, with its movable kamado and sink), liq-takayasu-daidokoro (the samurai kitchen on a board floor, and where the kamado stood), matsue-bukeyashiki (the kamado among the rooms of the Matsue house), irori-jawiki (where a common house cut its sunken hearth)

A samurai's kitchen cooked on a kamado, a clay range with fire-mouths, and it stood on a board floor more often than a farmhouse's did. A samurai house was built in the shoin style with nearly every room matted, but its kitchen was board-floored, and because its doma, the earth-floored part, was smaller than a farmhouse's, the kamado was sometimes set on the kitchen's board floor rather than on the earth. Of Edo-period houses in general, the rule was that the kamado stood on the doma. The Boso-no-mura house, a reproduction of the late Edo house of a middle-rank retainer of the Sakura domain, shows the second form: its kitchen is board-floored, with a movable kamado and a sink, under a ceiling whose boards are set with gaps to let the smoke out. The Matsue house, lived in by turns by retainers of about 500 to 1,000 koku, names its kamado among its rooms.

The sunken hearth, the irori, is the common house's fire. In the traditional common house, the minka, it is cut into the floor of a room near the doma or the kitchen, most often at the center of the floored part, and sometimes set in the doma itself. An irori in a samurai residence's kitchen was searched for and not found.

What this means for a plan: a residence's kitchen hearth is a kamado range. Where it stands is a knob between the two attested forms, on a small doma at the kitchen's edge or on the kitchen's board floor, rolled per settlement. A sunken irori in a samurai kitchen is a GUESS: it is the farmhouse's hearth, and nothing read puts one in a samurai house.
