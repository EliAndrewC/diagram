Fire, and the fireproof storehouses (dozō)

The halls of a Japanese administrative office were wooden buildings, and wooden halls burned: the Sado magistracy burned and was rebuilt five times. The answer to fire was the earth-walled storehouse (dozō), whose thick plastered walls were built to keep it out. In 1720, in the Edo period, the shogun's government came to permit and then encourage earthen-storehouse building for the townhouses of Edo that had been forbidden it, and in a fire, valuables that could not be carried out were put into the storehouses.

An office kept its tax grain and its papers in storehouses of this kind. The one office whose records storehouse is measured, Takayama's, kept its documents in a small one of about 450 sq ft. Against fire in the wooden buildings themselves, the townspeople of Edo kept tubs of standing water at the front entrance and on the roof.

  • An office's halls were timber, its storehouses earth-walled.
    The Takayama Jin'ya's facilities table groups its buildings by construction: the office, rebuilt in 1816, among the timber buildings, and the kitchen storehouse, the great rice storehouse and the book storehouse among those of earthen-storehouse construction.
  • Wooden halls often burned.
    The Sado magistracy, built in 1603, burned and was rebuilt five times. Takayama's office, rebuilt several times, by good fortune never suffered a fire.
  • Edo came to encourage earth-walled building against fire.
    A town ordinance on fireproof building was issued in 1720, permitting and then encouraging tile roofs, earthen-storehouse construction and earth-plastered walls on the townhouses that had been forbidden them. Buildings came to be ordered to adopt such fire-resistant construction, and the plastered storehouse was where valuables went in a fire.

  • How big was an office's records storehouse?
    Small, on the one measure found. The Takayama Jin'ya kept its documents in a book storehouse built in 1841, earth-walled, with a floor of 41.46 m² (~446 sq ft); its kitchen storehouse, built the year before, has a floor of 68.82 m² (~741 sq ft). The book storehouse survives. It was the records store of the office that governed the whole province of Hida.   • Edo's townspeople kept standing water at the entrance and on the roof.
    Rainwater tubs (tensuioke) and other fire water were kept ready, as a matter of daily habit, at the front entrance and on the roof, with soaked fire-beaters to put out falling sparks.
  • An order required tubs of water kept ready, and Edo formed companies of town firemen.
    In 1720 the town fire brigades were reorganized into companies of about twenty blocks each, the forty-seven companies of the iroha-gumi west of the Sumida River; an order of the period required tubs filled with water to be kept ready against fire.

The Qing emperors' palace in Beijing, the Forbidden City, kept its fire water in great vats instead, and the record of where they stood is fuller.

  • The Forbidden City kept 308 great water vats.
    Vats of gilt bronze, bronze and iron stood before its great halls and in its courtyards, called "taiping vats" or "auspicious vats", 308 of them by the Qing statutes, of which 231 remain. Their popular name, it is said, was men-hai, "the sea before the gate".
  • Their places were set by regulation and graded by the building.
    Qing regulations said which kind of vat stood in which courtyard and how many: gilt bronze beside the great halls and gates, plain bronze or iron in the courtyards of the rear palace.
  • The vats were placed where water was far.
    Because the palace's halls were mostly timber and caught fire easily, vats stood everywhere far from a water source, so a burning hall could be put out in time.
