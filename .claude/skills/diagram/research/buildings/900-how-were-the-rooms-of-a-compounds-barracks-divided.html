How were the rooms of a compound's barracks divided?

Sources: edo-hantei-jawiki (lower retainers lodged in rowhouses inside a lord's residence), mitamura-nagaya-tcpip (the lower retainers' rowhouse unit and its floors, and a castle's servants' room), sakai-hanshiro-bakumatsu (three retainers sharing one rowhouse unit in 1860), kotobank-chugen (servants living together in large rooms), meijimura-hohei6-heisha (an army barracks built in 1873, shown with beds)

A retainers' rowhouse was divided into dwellings, not bunk rooms. The lower warriors who came to Edo with their lord lived in rowhouses set up inside his residence. The historian Mitamura Engyo (mitamura-nagaya-tcpip) gives the middle rowhouse, where a lord's men below full samurai rank lived down to the foot soldiers, a standard unit of nine shaku by two ken (about 9 by 12 ft): one ken at the entrance an earth floor for leaving shoes, the other a raised plank floor. He traces it, on his own reading, to the lodging huts for soldiers at the foot of the Warring States mountain castles, and cites a war tale, the Dobora Gunki, on the servants' room at Okuwa castle, a rowhouse unit so short that a spear hung inside it had its butt sticking outside. How long such a range ran, and how wide its bays were in a magistrate's compound, are answered in How big was the rowhouse a magistrate's staff lived in?

Men on duty in Edo shared a unit, and the lowest servants a common room. In 1860 a retainer of the Wakayama domain on duty in Edo, Sakai Hanshiro, lived in the duty rowhouse of his domain's residence at Akasaka with his uncle and a colleague, the three of them together, the two juniors cooking by turns. The attendants below the foot soldiers, the chugen, were organized in groups under a head and lived together in large rooms such as the chugen room in their lord's residence.

Beds came with the modern army. No page read puts a bed, a bunk or a sleeping shelf in a premodern rowhouse or servants' room. The earliest barracks found with beds is a modern army's: an infantry barracks built at Nagoya in 1873, the sixth year of Meiji, whose squad rooms are shown today furnished with wooden beds. The building stood at Nagoya until it was taken down in 1963, and the page does not say when its beds were put in, so 1873 dates the building, not the beds; when beds first came into a Japanese barracks was not found.

The rule the map follows: a compound's barracks is a rowhouse divided into dwellings, each an earth-floored entry and a raised floor, with a few men on duty to a dwelling; the servants of the lowest rank may instead share one large room. It is never divided into bunk rooms, and nothing in it is labeled or drawn as a bunk. Its size is that of the staff rowhouse.
