Where is the formal entrance, and how does a guest reach it?

Sources: genkan-jawiki (what the word genkan named in the Edo period), bukeyashiki-wiki (the shikidai as a mark of the senior retainer's house), shirobito-1717-takayama (the genkan of the Takayama intendancy office and the route from it to the residence), shiroishi-koseki (a middle-rank house with no genkan with a shikidai, reached through a middle gate and a walled garden path)

In the Edo period an entrance with a shikidai was what the word genkan meant; in the shoin style of building it is the room or building set at a residence's formal entrance. The houses of senior retainers had earthen walls, a gatehouse with rooms in it (a nagaya-mon) and a shikidai.

Where a posting's office and residence share one compound, the genkan can belong to the office. At the Takayama Jin'ya, the only jin'ya in the country whose principal parts survive, a visitor enters by the genkan, and the route goes round the rooms of the office and continues on to the residence where the intendants lived. The page describes a visitor's route, not how the office and residence were laid out against each other.

A house of lower rank could lack a genkan with a shikidai. The old Koseki house at Shiroishi, the house of a middle-rank retainer, has no genkan with a shikidai: the gate from the street leads along the earthen wall to the kitchen entrance, which the page calls the front entrance, and from a middle gate (nakamon) in the wall a guest passes through the roji, the walled garden path, straight to the veranda of the principal zashiki; the earthen wall encloses the roji in an L. A forecourt before a posting's genkan fenced off from its garden, and a pond set on the line from the middle gate to the entrance, were searched for and not found.

What this means for a plan: two forms are attested, a knob rolled from the map's seed where the rank allows either. In the first, the guest comes to a genkan with a shikidai: on the office block where office and residence share the compound, as at Takayama, with the way to the residence running on through the office; on the residence where it stands alone. In the second, there is no genkan with a shikidai; the guest's way runs through a middle gate in a wall and along a walled garden path to the zashiki's veranda, and the household's everyday door is the kitchen entrance. The first is the senior house's form and the second is attested at a middle-rank house only. A pond on the gate-to-entrance line, and a fenced forecourt, rest on nothing found and are a GUESS wherever a plan draws them.
