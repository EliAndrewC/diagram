Shrines kept inside government offices and houses (yashikigami)

Both of the offices we read on, a very small one and a large one, kept shrines inside their walls: a Japanese branch office of three officials had two, and a full Chinese county yamen, Neixiang's, kept shrines to three gods. At both, a shrine was part of the office's equipment; what differed was how many it kept and which gods they served.

In Japan the god of a household's own ground, the yashikigami, was kept in a corner of the lot, in a small shrine or at an old tree or a stone. It began, it is thought, as worship in the woods near the house, which drew in over time until the shrine stood inside the lot.   • Even a Japanese government office of three officials had shrines inside its walls.
    The shogunate's branch office at Jōge had three officials at the end of the shogunate, in 1867. A drawing of it made in 1869 labels an Inari shrine and a Tenjin shrine inside its walls. What else the office held is at Magistrates' compounds (jin'ya and yamen).
  • Did the excavation of the office at Jōge find its shrines?
    No. The excavation of the site turned up the office's walls, steps, ditches, foundation stones and a well, and no shrine. The shrines are known from the drawing of 1869.
  • The Chinese county yamen at Neixiang kept shrines to three gods.
    At Neixiang a temple to the god of the office and a shrine to the god of the land stood on the east side, in a courtyard behind the guesthouse, and inside the jail was a temple to the jail god, with a statue of him.

A Japanese house could keep a shrine of its own, to the god of the household's ground, and at a samurai house it could be an Inari, one of the two gods the Jōge office kept.

  • A Japanese household's god was kept in a corner of its lot.
    A Japanese encyclopedia defines the yashikigami as a god kept in a corner of the residence lot, or on a small plot joined to it, either in a small shrine or at an old tree or a stone that served as the seat the god came down to.
  • On which side of the house did a household's shrine stand?
    Often the northwest or the northeast: the Japanese Wikipedia article on the household god says it was often kept in a corner of the lot, and then often at the northwest or the northeast of the house.
  • Where did the household shrine come from?
    From the woods near the house, it is thought. The place of worship there was at first no shrine at all, only an old tree or a natural stone used at the time of the rite, and it drew closer to the house over time until the shrine stood inside the lot, the form seen widely today.
  • A rebuilt samurai house at Sakura keeps an Inari shrine among the fixtures of its grounds.
    The Takei house, a middle-rank samurai's house in Sakura rebuilt at an open-air museum, has besides its main house an Inari shrine, a gate and a vegetable garden, the whole enclosed by an earth bank and hedges.

    No page we read describes one, at a samurai house or anywhere else.
