kotobank-gisou

"義倉" ("gisou", the famine-relief granary, title translated), entries from the Digital Daijisen, Nihon Kokugo Daijiten, the Heibonsha World Encyclopedia, Nipponica, Yamakawa and others, on Kotobank (in Japanese; https://kotobank.jp/word/%E7%BE%A9%E5%80%89-50697)

What it is: A long page of dictionary and encyclopedia entries on the gisou, a granary system for famine relief, tracing it through Sui, Tang, Song, Ming and Qing China, Korea and Japan from the Ritsuryo period to the Edo period.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives figures for the system as written in the codes and in surviving ledgers: Tang 2 sho of millet per mu, Japan's nine household grades with only 10 to 20 percent of households paying in the Echizen and Awa records, and about 80 percent of Awa households in 750 below the lowest grade. It is mostly Japan's Nara-period state scheme and China's tax and charity granaries. On the village store it has only a few lines. Sui granaries were kept by each village she of 25 households under a village officer and filled by a levy graded by wealth. The Heibonsha and Obunsha entries say the Ming and Qing put the charity granary in the cities and the shecang in the villages, though Nipponica says the Ming kept only the shecang. In the first years of the Kansei era (1789-1801) the shogunate had storehouses (gokura) built in its villages. The Edo revival is only sketched: mostly small stores, filled by gifts from the rich and levies on farmers, and managed by the lords.
