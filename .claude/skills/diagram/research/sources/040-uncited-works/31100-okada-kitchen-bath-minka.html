okada-kitchen-bath-minka

Okada Kenji (housing journalist), "今と昔の台所と浴室考" ("imatomukashi no daidokoro yokushitsu kō", thoughts on kitchens and baths, then and now, title translated), in "にっぽん民家紀行" (Nippon minka kikō), chapter 2 of a Kitchen and Bath Industry Association publication, revised from its bulletin Nos. 38-45 (January 1995 to October 1996) (in Japanese; https://kitchen-bath.jp/wp-content/uploads/2015/12/24imatomukasinodaidokoroyokusitukou.pdf)

What it is: A magazine-style essay by a housing journalist, reprinted as a PDF chapter, touring the kitchens, hearths, water jars, sinks and baths of old farmhouses, merchant houses and inns, many moved to open-air museums (the Kawasaki Japan Open-Air Folk House Museum, the Hokkaido Historical Village), others still standing where they were built, such as the Ito estate in Niigata, the Monzai house in Yamanashi (still lived in) and the Muroya merchant house in Yanai. It gives the stated sizes of several of them.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives dated, measured examples of Japanese house kitchens: a detached kitchen wing joined to the main house in a T (the Mochizuki house, built mid-18th century, main house 4.5 by 5 ken and kitchen wing 5 by 3 ken), farmhouse hearths 30 to 80 cm high (the 30 cm one a saltpeter cauldron, the 80 cm one a post-station inn's hearth for its horses) with the fire bed at floor level so the cook squatted (the page says samurai kitchens in Edo-period pictures and the 1861 Wada-juku honjin set the fire bed higher, to work standing), and the Ito landlord's 70 tsubo kitchen serving about 60 residents and cooking a bale of rice a day. The houses are surviving buildings, many relocated, from the 17th to 19th centuries with some Meiji and Taisho examples (Hokkaido settlers' houses, the gas-fired brick range of the Ito house), the Ito mansion and its 70 tsubo kitchen were built about Meiji 20 (about 1887) and the landlord reached 1,000 cho only in the Meiji 30s, so that kitchen and its 60 people are a Meiji landlord's household, and the author gives no survey method or count of how common any form was.
