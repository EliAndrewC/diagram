kotobank-machiya-chokacho

"町家" ("machiya", the townhouse, title translated), entries from the Heibonsha World Encyclopedia (Suzuki Mitsuru), Britannica's concise encyclopedia and other dictionaries, on Kotobank (in Japanese; https://kotobank.jp/word/%E7%94%BA%E5%AE%B6-171219)

What it is: A Kotobank page of dictionary entries on the machiya, the house of a merchant or craftsman built facing a town street. The Heibonsha entry gives the layout (shop, a long dirt passage to the back, a low second floor) and the contrast between western Japan's tile roofs and plastered walls and eastern Japan's board roofs and exposed posts, with the Kyoto udatsu and the Edo fire-resistant storehouse style.

Why it applies, and its limits: It supports the long, narrow townhouse of a premodern town, with a shop at the front, a dirt passage along one side and a low upper floor, and with a plastered storehouse style in Edo after fires. The Britannica entry says the form arose in the 10th to 12th centuries and that earlier yards allowed vegetable gardens; the Heibonsha entry's example of a surviving storehouse-style townhouse, in Kawagoe, was built after the fire of 1877, in the Meiji era, so it shows the late form rather than an Edo-period building. It states the east-west contrast in general terms and gives no frontage or depth for an Edo-period townhouse; its only size is the older Heibonsha edition's reading of Kamakura-period scroll paintings, a townhouse about 2 to 3 ken wide and 2 ken deep.
