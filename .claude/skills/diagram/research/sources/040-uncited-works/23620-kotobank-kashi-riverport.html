kotobank-kashi-riverport

"河岸" ("kashi", the riverbank landing, title translated), entries from the Heibonsha World Encyclopedia (Hotate Michihisa and Kawana Noboru), Nihon Kokugo Daijiten and the Yamakawa Japanese History Dictionary, on Kotobank (in Japanese; https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%B2%B3%E5%B2%B8)

What it is: A Kotobank page of dictionary entries on the kashi, the river landings of Edo-period Japan where boat cargo was loaded and unloaded. They cover the word's origin in the mooring stake, how the Tokugawa government and daimyo founded landings for tribute rice, the wholesalers, boat owners, boatmen and porters who lived there, and the landings' decline after the Meiji Restoration.

Why it applies, and its limits: It describes who lived at a premodern river landing and how it differed from an ordinary village: a wholesaler who often doubled as the village headman, boat owners, boatmen, porters, inns and teahouses, and a market for goods in transit. It counts landings only for the Kanto, 124 across ten provinces in the shogunate's 1689 survey with 88 on the Kanto river systems, and names the Tone and Yodo rivers, so it speaks for those trade rivers, not for a small stream's landing. The encyclopedia entry itself says that rivers with narrow basins and steep valleys carried no large boats, so a minor river had little of this.
