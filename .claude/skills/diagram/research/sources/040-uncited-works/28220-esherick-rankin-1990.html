esherick-rankin-1990

Joseph W. Esherick and Mary Backus Rankin (eds.), Chinese Local Elites and Patterns of Dominance (University of California Press, 1990; papers of the Banff conference of 1987), full text in the UC Press E-Books Collection Archive (https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/view?docId=ft0q2n99mz&chunk.id=d0e4744&toc.id=d0e4705&brand=ucpress)

What it is: The full text of an edited academic volume on the gentry, merchants, militarists and lineages that led Chinese counties and below, from Ming to 1949: an introduction, eleven case chapters (Ningbo, Hanyang, the Fu-Rong salt yard, Wuxi, Tianyue, Xingyi, Rugao, Beijing, Ha Tsuen, North China villages, Xunwu), concluding remarks, then the endnotes, glossary, bibliography and index. It studies the elite's strategies and resources, not settlement form, fields or buildings, and most of its detail is of 1850-1940.

Why it applies, and its limits: Settlement evidence is in a few chapters only. Rowe on Hanyang (Hubei) has 14 lineages with estates, ancestral temples, ritual land and market-town seats; Bell on Wuxi has lineage estates of nearly 4,000 mu and market-town cocoon firms; Schoppa on Tianyue gives village-to-embankment distances and the rice market of Linpu; Watson on Ha Tsuen gives 82 ancestral estates, 377.64 acres of corporate land, a landless rate of about 55 percent, and a lineage hall that ran the market and pier; Duara gives household and land counts for four Hebei villages (110, 70 and 57 households, and others by mu); Averill on Xunwu gives lineage halls, fortified dwellings and the market town of Jitan; Brook on Ningbo gives where gentry lineages lived against the city wall. Most of these figures are late Qing and Republican (1850-1940, with Ha Tsuen's land records of 1905 and fieldwork of 1977-78) and so fall after China's cut-off of about 1895, so they show how lineage estates and markets worked, not their premodern size; only parts of Brook's and Rowe's Ming-Qing background reach earlier. The Introduction, the chapters on Xingyi, Rugao and Beijing, and parts 14 to 17 (endnotes, bibliography, index) hold little or no settlement evidence, and the Xingyi, Rugao, Beijing, salt-yard and Wuxi chapters describe an upland frontier county, a commercial Lower Yangzi county, a capital city, an industrial town and a treaty-port silk district, not ordinary farm villages.
