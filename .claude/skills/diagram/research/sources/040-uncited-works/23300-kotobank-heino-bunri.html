kotobank-heino-bunri

"兵農分離" ("heino bunri", the separation of warrior and farmer, title translated), entries from the Digital Daijisen, the Nihon Kokugo Daijiten, the Heibonsha World Encyclopedia (by Miki Seiichiro), the Nipponica, the Heibonsha Mypedia, the Obunsha Japanese History Dictionary, the Britannica and the Yamakawa Japanese History Dictionary, on Kotobank (in Japanese; https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%85%B5%E8%BE%B2%E5%88%86%E9%9B%A2-129004)

What it is: A Kotobank page gathering several Japanese dictionary and encyclopedia entries on the policy and process that separated the warrior class from the farming class between the late Sengoku period and the early Edo period. The longest, the Heibonsha entry, follows the process through Takeda's 1563 land survey, Hideyoshi's land survey, the 1588 sword hunt and the 1591 status edict.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the premodern reason a samurai household lives in a castle town and not in a village: warriors were gathered into castle towns and cultivators were fixed on registered land, so the two classes held different holdings by rule. It describes late-sixteenth-century Japan only, and the entries disagree on emphasis, some tying the separation to Hideyoshi's measures and others to the daimyo's earlier surveys; none of them describes a settlement's layout.
