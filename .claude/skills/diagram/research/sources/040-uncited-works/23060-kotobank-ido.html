kotobank-ido

"井戸" ("ido", a well, title translated), entries in the Digital Daijisen, Nihon Kokugo Daijiten, Nipponica (by Kobayashi Mikio, Miyamoto Mizuo and Tamura Katsumi), the revised Heibonsha World Encyclopedia (by Kayane Isamu, Machida Akira, Miura Keiichi and Miyata Noboru) and others, on Kotobank (in Japanese; https://kotobank.jp/word/%E4%BA%95%E6%88%B8-31656)

What it is: A long set of encyclopedia entries on the well, covering shallow and deep wells and how they are dug, the history of Japanese and ancient Chinese wells, well folklore, and the standing of wells in cities and abroad. It includes the spiral-stair mai-maizu wells of the Kanto uplands, well curbs and frames of wood and stone, and the bucket types (pole sweep, pulley, rope).

Why it applies, and its limits: It supports how a well in a village or town was dug and framed, that shallow wells reached groundwater a few to ten-odd meters down while uplands and fans needed far deeper ones, and that many Japanese cities drew daily water from channels or bought river water until Meiji waterworks. It is a survey over thousands of years and several continents. It dates the well linings: plank frames at the Nara palace, stone curbs from the late Heian period, rings of bottomless pots in small medieval wells, and round cobble-lined wells by the medieval and early modern periods. Its technical sections also cover modern methods: concrete casings, the hand pump (from 1897), the electric pump (after 1945) and machine-bored deep wells (from the Taisho period). None of these is the setting's. Its statement that a typical Chinese village has a well at its center is given without a period or region.
