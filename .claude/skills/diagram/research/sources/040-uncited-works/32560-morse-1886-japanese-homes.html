morse-1886-japanese-homes

Edward S. Morse, Japanese Homes and Their Surroundings (Harper & Brothers, 1886; the 1889 printing), full text in the Project Gutenberg edition (https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/52868/pg52868.txt)

What it is: The full text of an American zoologist's illustrated account of Japanese houses, drawn from his own sketches on three residences in Japan before 1885: house frames, roofs, mat-sized rooms, kura storehouses, kitchens, wells, entrances, gates, fences and gardens (chapters I to VII), then an ancient-house chapter drawn from the Kojiki and Satow's translations, and a chapter comparing Ainu, Hachijo, Ryukyu, Korean and Chinese houses. It has 307 figures, a glossary and footnotes, including the framing dimensions of an ordinary two-storied house.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives measured and dated particulars of houses, and of the walls, gates, wells and storehouses around them, as a Westerner saw them in the late 1870s and early 1880s: a six-mat room about 9 by 12 feet, a tile-and-plaster wall 2 to 3 feet wide at its base and 8 feet or more high, a thatched roof lasting 15 to 20 years, kura walls 18 inches to 2 feet thick, and a farmhouse compound at Kabutoyama in Musashi (a walled court with long store and servants' buildings, a guest house and a covered passage) said to be nearly three hundred years old. Its evidence is Meiji, so the prices, the tax and the lot value are Meiji, and it describes Tokyo, Kyoto and merchant, samurai and daimyo houses far more than peasant ones. It gives no farm layout, field, grove, barn or threshing yard, no kura floor area and no village plan beyond a road-side line of houses, and the Sakai garden plates are from a Japanese book of about two hundred years before and not his own sketches.
