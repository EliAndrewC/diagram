施耐庵 水滸傳 (attributed to Shi Nai'an, Water Margin), 第十回 林教頭風雪山神廟 陸虞候火燒草料場 ("Chapter 10: Arms Instructor Lin at the Mountain Spirit Temple in the snow; Captain Lu burns the fodder depot", title translated), on Guoxue Daohang (http://www.guoxue123.com/xiaosuo/jd/shqs/10.htm)

What it is: The tenth chapter of the Ming-dynasty novel Water Margin, in a modern simplified-character text on a Chinese classics site, in which the exiled Lin Chong is set to keep an army fodder depot outside a garrison town and its keeper hands it over, counting the stacks.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the fullest premodern description readable here of how a fodder store looked on the ground: a yard inside an earth wall with a two-leaved gate, thatched buildings used as storehouses, the horse fodder in stacks all round, and two thatched halls in the middle. It is fiction, written in the Ming about a Northern Song setting, so it shows what a Ming writer and reader took a fodder depot to be rather than any one depot; it is a state army store, not a town's or a farmer's; it gives no sizes; it calls the storehouses 仓廒, the ordinary word for granaries, and says they are under official seal, but does not say what they held; and its stacks are 马草, "horse fodder", filled by what was "paid in" each month, without saying whether that was cut grass or crop straw, so it shows fodder kept in stacks, not that the stacks were hay.

Used for: horse fodder kept in stacks in a walled yard with thatched storehouses (Towns)
