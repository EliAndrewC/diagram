"Getting close to the history of irrigation in ancient China", The Paper (government account), 2024 (in Chinese; https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_27710049)

What it is: A popular survey of Chinese irrigation history from the Neolithic to the Qing, reposted without references on The Paper by an unnamed government-affairs account (the platform disclaims its views), describing among others the Mulan weir in Putian, Fujian, begun in 1064.

Why it applies, and its limits: It describes the one means of shutting a water opening read in this pass: boards slotted into cut grooves, added or removed to set the water held. The Mulan weir is a large river work, not a village intake, and the article is a modern popular summary. It describes the weir as it stands today and does not date its gate grooves or boards; the weir has been repaired and altered over nine centuries (its 32 openings are now 28), so it shows the method at this weir, not that these grooves are Song work. The same article's Tang-era Tashan weir, whose body its editor calls a stop-log chamber, is an older instance of the form.

Used for: that a flow was regulated by gate boards slotted into grooves and added or removed (water)
