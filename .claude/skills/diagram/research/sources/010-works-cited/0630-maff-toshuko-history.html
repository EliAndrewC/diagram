maff-toshuko-history

MAFF (Japan, Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries), land-improvement design standard, technical appendix on head works (土地改良事業計画設計基準 設計 頭首工 付録技術書), chapter 1, the historical background of head works (https://www.maff.go.jp/j/nousin/attach/pdf/tousyukou-3.pdf)

What it is: The first chapter, on the historical background of head works, of the technical appendix to the Japanese Ministry of Agriculture's design standard for head works. It describes how intakes were made before modern materials - natural intake with no weir at all, temporary weirs for the intake season, half-river closures, oblique weirs of timber frames packed with stone, gabions and brushwood - with named examples such as the Tatai weir of 1183 on the Takahashi River and the Ishi-ibi works of 1615, and it quotes a 1902 treatise on where to place the works.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the Japanese state's own engineering history of the intake, written from the record of named works, and it is the source for the two premodern forms our maps roll between - the bare bank intake and the built weir - and for what a weir was made of. Its limits are that its examples are rivers of some size rather than a hamlet's brook, so the forms are borrowed downward in scale; that it is a modern engineering document whose interest in the past is as background, and its premodern account a compressed secondary narrative rather than a reading of period documents; and that the dimensions it gives are river weirs' - the Yamada weir of the 1640s at 324 m long, 10.8 m wide and 1.5 m high, the Tahara weir of 1700 at 490 m by about 30 m - with no cross-section for a small stream's weir, so the thickness we draw at a brook is our own guess.

Used for: natural intake with no weir in old times, temporary seasonal weirs, half-river closures; weir materials (timber frames packed with stone, gabions, brushwood); the oblique weir; the Tatai weir of 1183 rebuilt each irrigation season; the Ishi-ibi of 1615 returning its surplus to the main river; the intake on the concave bank of a bend; the 1902 rule to place the head works where the river leaves the mountains
