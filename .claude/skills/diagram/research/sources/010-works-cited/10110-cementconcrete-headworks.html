cementconcrete-headworks

Cement Concrete (cementconcrete.org), "Diversion head works in irrigation- Layout and functions of component" (https://cementconcrete.org/water-resources/diversion-head-works-in-irrigation-layout-and-functions-of-component/1778/)

What it is: An unsigned tutorial page on a general civil-engineering website, posted under the site's own name and dated on the page only as seven years before it was read in 2026, describing the layout of a river diversion headworks - the weir, the undersluices, the divide wall, the canal head regulator - and what each part is for.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the page read for the rule that a canal head regulator is aligned at 90 to 120 degrees to the axis of the WEIR, which shows that the figure is taken across the headworks rather than from the river's own line. It is a modern teaching summary of twentieth-century South Asian irrigation practice - masonry, rock-fill and concrete weirs and gated barrages, the concrete weir designed by Khosla's theory of flow under the floor, beside a linked case study of a project in Nepal - unsigned, with no references and some typing errors, and the figure it points to is not described in its text; a second page read, aboutcivil.org, gives only 90 degrees to the weir, so the 120 rests on this page alone, and nothing on it concerns premodern works: the angle is a modern design rule for keeping silt out of a gated canal, used here only as a bound on where a premodern intake sat off its weir, not as evidence that Chinese or Japanese builders set one so. The rule also assumes a weir built across the whole river, with the regulator next to gated undersluices, so it bounds only an intake that sat off such a weir, not one taken off a dividing bund with no weir across the river, as at Dujiangyan.

Used for: the headworks regulator's alignment measured from the weir's axis (cities/river-cities)
