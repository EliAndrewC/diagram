kotobank-sakaya

kotobank 酒屋 ("sake house", title translated from the Japanese by this project) (https://kotobank.jp/word/%E9%85%92%E5%B1%8B)

What it is: The Kotobank page reprinting the encyclopedia entries on the sake house, brewer and retailer, among them the Kaitei Shinpan Sekai Daihyakka Jiten entry by Wakita Haruko (medieval) and Yunoki Manabu (early modern), the Nipponica entry by Endo Motoo and the Mypedia entry.

Why it applies, and its limits: It says the shogunate first confined brewing to castle towns, post towns and ports, licensed every brewer, and only in the eighteenth century let landlords brew in the villages, and that brewers commonly lent money and took pledges - which is why a county town keeps a brewer and a village does not. Signed reference entries on Japan, tertiary but written by the historians (Wakita, Yunoki) whose own books they cite. The confinement to towns was a Tokugawa rice-price policy of the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, not a general premodern rule. The same entries say medieval brewers gathered in towns because brewing needed capital, not because the law required it; that from the 1730s landlord brewing spread widely through the villages; and that by the eighteenth century villages had their own sake retailers and drinking shops. Nothing here speaks to China. The only counts are for the capital, Kyoto (342 sake houses in the 1425-26 register, a brewer holding 20 to 120 jars of 2-3 koku), which are a capital's figures, not a county town's; there are no building sizes.

Used for: brewing confined by law to towns and licensed, brewers as moneylenders and pawnbrokers (urban-features)
