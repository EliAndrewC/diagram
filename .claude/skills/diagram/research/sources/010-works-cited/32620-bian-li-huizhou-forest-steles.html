Bian Li (Center for Huizhou Studies, Anhui University), 「A study of the forest-protection prohibition steles of Ming and Qing Huizhou」 (translated; original: 「明清时期徽州森林保护禁碑研究」), first printed in Zheng Zhenman, ed., Minjian lishi wenxian luncong 2: Beiming yanjiu zhuanji (Beijing: Social Sciences Academic Press, 2014), posted without its notes in 2015 by the Center for Research on Local History and Documentation, Xiamen University (in Chinese; https://crlhd.xmu.edu.cn/info/1037/5175.htm)

What it is: A historian's study of the about thirty steles, one Ming and the rest Qing, that Huizhou's villages, lineages and provincial, prefectural and county offices carved to protect forests - the groves at water mouths, on dragon hills and over tombs among them - transcribing their rules and setting them beside lineage covenants and family codes.

Why it applies, and its limits: It quotes the rules themselves - who held a grove, how the rules were agreed, the fines and what might be taken - so the record reads the keepers' own words, in the author's transcription. Huizhou is a hilly prefecture where Anhui, Zhejiang and Jiangxi meet, with strong lineages and a timber economy, not the Pearl River Delta where the record's groves are measured; the dated steles it quotes are mostly of the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, several of them for tomb groves rather than a village's back grove, and the page's text is a web transcription of the article, without its notes.

Used for: who held a Huizhou village's groves, how the rules were made and enforced, the fines and what a villager could take, and Wangkou's reason for keeping its screen hill planted (The countryside)
