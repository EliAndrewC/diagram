rwsshp-hand-dug-well

RWSSHP Resource Manual 3a, Hand Dug Well Construction Manual, Ethiopia Rural Water Supply, Sanitation and Hygiene Programme, 2007 (https://www.fhdesigns.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Manual-3a-HDW-Construction.pdf)

What it is: A field manual for building a hand-dug, hand-lined, bucket-drawn village well, written for the Ethiopian government's rural water supply and sanitation program. It works through siting, digging, lining and finishing, and tabulates a traditional well against an improved one row by row.

Why it applies, and its limits: It writes down the siting rule that no premodern source bothers to write down, because a contractor has to be told it: such a well is not located in swampy or flood-prone ground, and the whole detailing of its head - a raised headwall, the top three metres of the shaft sealed, an apron, a drain, a diversion ditch to turn surface water away - exists to keep surface water out of the shaft. A well standing in a reed bed fails both at once, which is our rule reached from the other end. Its limits: the rule it writes down is a modern SANITARY norm, adopted here as a standard the map follows rather than as a finding about where premodern wells stood - this record's own harie-kabata entry describes a village whose water rises inside the houses. It is a modern rural water-supply document from Ethiopia, not an East Asian historical source, so what carries is the physical relation between a shaft, a water table and the ground around it rather than any detail of construction; its improved well is a concrete-ringed one; and the reason it gives is sanitary, keeping pathogens out of the water, where our record's reason is practical - that the rim and the windlass need somewhere dry to stand is implied by the raised headwall and the apron rather than stated anywhere, and our record says so.

Used for: a hand-dug well not sited in swampy or flood-prone ground; the raised headwall, sealed upper shaft, apron, drain and diversion ditch that keep surface water out of it
