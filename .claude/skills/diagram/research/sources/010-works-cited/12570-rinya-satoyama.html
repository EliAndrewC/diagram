rinya-satoyama

Forestry Agency of Japan, 里山林の薪炭や、きのこ生産資材としての利用 ("Using satoyama woods for firewood and charcoal and as material for mushroom growing", title translated from the Japanese by this project) (in Japanese; https://www.rinya.maff.go.jp/j/tokuyou/jyunkanriyou/index.html)

What it is: A Forestry Agency page on the broadleaf woods near villages: what they were used for before Meiji, and how they were cut and let regrow from the stumps every twenty to thirty years.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives, from the national forestry authority, both what the near woods were for and what they looked like - thin, low, many-stemmed trees regrowing from the stump - which is the look of a worked wood at a settlement's edge. It is written to promote the use of such woods today; its history is a sentence, undated beyond "before Meiji", and it describes Japan in general rather than any town's margin. The page dates only the uses to before Meiji; the twenty-to-thirty-year cycle and the thin, low look are left undated and are those of the charcoal woods managed into the twentieth century. Forest historians find that many hills near Edo-period settlements, in the crowded regions, had been cut down to grass, pine scrub or bare slopes by early Meiji. So a well-kept coppice at a town's edge is one likely state of the near woods, not the only one.

Used for: the near broadleaf woods used for firewood, charcoal, food and fertilizer before Meiji; and, undated on the page, cut and regrown from the stump every twenty to thirty years (towns)
