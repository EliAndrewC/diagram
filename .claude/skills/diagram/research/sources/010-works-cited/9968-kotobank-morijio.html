kotobank-morijio

Kotobank, "盛塩" (morijio), entries from Digital Daijisen and Seisen-ban Nihon Kokugo Daijiten (in Japanese; https://kotobank.jp/word/%E7%9B%9B%E5%A1%A9-646866)

What it is: Kotobank's page gathering two published Japanese dictionaries' entries on morijio, the small heap of salt set at a doorway, one of them with its earliest recorded use.

Why it applies, and its limits: The standard historical dictionary of Japanese places the custom at the doorways of the customer trades - restaurants, assignation houses and variety halls - and dates its earliest cited use to a popular novel of about 1838-40, which dates the practice to the late Edo period at least. Its list of trades is a modern dictionary's summary (the machiai, the assignation house, is largely a Meiji-and-later institution). The only period evidence is that one line of fiction, which sets the salt at the clean doorways of Sakai-chō, the theater-teahouse quarter of the city of Edo. So it attests an urban entertainment district, not a village or a road town. Both dictionaries give the purpose as drawing good fortune to the business (engi o iwatte), not warding off evil or purifying, so a salt ward needs another source. It calls the heap small but gives no shape or number, and says nothing about any residence, office or samurai household.

Used for: that morijio was set at the doorways of the customer trades, first recorded about 1838-40 (religion-and-death).
