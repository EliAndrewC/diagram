qimin-yaoshu-yangzhu

賈思勰 齊民要術 (Jia Sixie, Qimin Yaoshu, c. 540 AD), 卷第六, 養豬第五十八 (https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/%E9%BD%8A%E6%B0%91%E8%A6%81%E8%A1%93/%E5%8D%B7%E7%AC%AC%E5%85%AD)

What it is: The pig-raising chapter of the Qimin Yaoshu, the sixth-century Chinese agricultural treatise, on Chinese Wikisource. The same treatise's poultry chapter is used elsewhere in this record. The chapter is short and practical: how large a pen should be, what ground it wants, what to feed, how to manage a sow and her litter. The bracketed passages are the treatise's own interlinear commentary.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is by a long way the oldest and most direct evidence available on where a Chinese farmer put a pig pen, and it is PRIMARY rather than a modern reconstruction - which matters here because every other source on this question describes twentieth-century farms. It is also the only source we have that speaks to the instinct behind the question, and it answers in the opposite direction. Its limits are real: it is northern Chinese practice roughly a thousand years before the settlements these maps depict, it is prescriptive advice rather than a record of what any farmer did, and it says nothing about fish ponds at all, so it bounds the siting question without settling how a pig and a pond stood together.

Used for: the pen "not disliked for being small" and the place "not disliked for being filthy", with wallowing mud given as a positive - the only siting instruction the chapter carries, and no mention of water
