ja.wikipedia 稲荷神 ("Inari, the deity", title translated from the Japanese by this project) (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%A8%B2%E8%8D%B7%E7%A5%9E)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on Inari, the deity of rice and later of trade, and on the spread of its worship.

Why it applies, and its limits: It records the Edo saying that Inari shrines were among the three things one met everywhere in the city, which is the one statement read on how thick small street shrines stood in a Japanese town. Its limits: it is an encyclopedia article, and the saying rests on one footnote to a 2009 popular guide to Inari (中村陽, supervising ed., イチから知りたい日本の神さま2 稲荷大神), not on period records; a saying is not a count; it is about Edo, the largest city in the country, rather than a county town; and by the article's own account Inari shrines grew thickest in eastern Japan because the cult was an Edo fashion, so it overstates what a town in western Japan, or one on the Chinese model, would have had. The same article gives a smaller place's figure: six Inari shrines in early-Meiji Musashi Fuchū, a post town, and 566 household shrines (屋敷神) counted across the modern city in a 1982 local-history survey. Those are household shrines over a modern city's area, not street shrines.

Used for: small Inari shrines everywhere in Edo (religion-and-death)
