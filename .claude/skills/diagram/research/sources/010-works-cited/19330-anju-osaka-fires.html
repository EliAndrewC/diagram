anju-osaka-fires

Tani Naoki (director, Osaka Museum of Housing and Living), "Disasters in Edo-period Osaka: fire, earthquake, tsunami", Anju no. 84, Osaka Municipal Housing Information Center (in Japanese; https://www.osaka-angenet.jp/ange/ange84/3000347)

What it is: A short article by a historian of the Japanese town house, the director of Osaka's city museum of housing, in the city housing center's magazine, on the fires of Edo-period Osaka and how its town houses were built against them; it reads a one-sheet print issued after the fire of 1863, held by the museum, which lists the damage of three great fires.

Why it applies, and its limits: The print gives a count of earthen storehouses beside the counts of houses and households for the Osaka fire of 1724 (about 2,800 storehouses, 28,000 houses and 98,700 households), a premodern city-scale figure. It counts only what burned, not what stood, and a storehouse is built to survive a fire, so the ones that held are left out and a storehouse ratio taken from it runs low; the standard account has the fire taking about two-thirds of the city, not all of it. It is a popular broadsheet printed in 1863, 139 years after the fire, quoted in a magazine article, not a census, and its figures run well above the standard record: the Heibonsha encyclopedia gives 408 blocks, 11,765 houses and 1,097 storehouses burned, with deaths given from 293 to over 7,000 against the print's 30,000, and the Japanese Wikipedia gives 60,292 households. The two agree on storehouses to houses (about one to ten) but not on storehouses to households (one to 35 in the print, one to 55 in the record), so a household ratio should come from the record or be given as that range. Osaka, a merchant capital the article thinks probably the best guarded against fire in Japan, is not a provincial town. The article gives no issue date.

Used for: how many storehouses a premodern city held against its households, and where on the lot they stood (Urban fabric)
