daikan-jawiki

ja.wikipedia, 代官 ("daikan, a local magistrate", title translated from the Japanese by this project) (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/代官)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the daikan, the intendant who ran a territory's affairs in place of its lord, from the medieval estates through the Edo shogunate's intendants of its own lands, with their duties, their staff, their offices, a list of intendancies and how they governed from a distance.

Why it applies, and its limits: It says that the Edo intendants of the Kanto lived in Edo and went out to their villages only for land surveys, harvest inspections, tours of inspection and serious incidents, which is the historical form of an official seen rarely in the countryside. It is a tertiary summary, and the article carries Wikipedia's banner saying it cites no or too few sources, with no citation on this sentence. The sentence concerns the Kanto intendants of the shogunate's own lands in particular, not the daimyo domains. It says those intendants governed through contact with their tetsuke, the staff officers under them, so what was rare was a visit from the intendant himself, not all contact with the state. It gives no count of visits a year, so any frequency is our estimate. It is also the page read for the size of an intendant's staff: about ten shogunal retainers (tetsuki) and a few locally hired clerks (tedai), and at one late-Edo intendancy a single intendant, three tetsuki and ten tedai. It is an encyclopedia article flagged for missing citations. Its one scholarly citation (Nakata 2002) supports only its history of how the intendancy became a salaried office, from 1657 through the Kyōhō reforms (1725), and both staff figures are uncited; the Gojō count is given as hearsay ("it is said"). The counts are of officials and clerks only: the article says menial ashigaru and chūgen served beside them, so more hands worked at the office than the figures give. Its two figures do not agree on the mix (about ten tetsuki and a few tedai, against three tetsuki and ten tedai at Gojō). They are for the shogunate's intendancies, which varied in size: one staff ran a district usually of several tens of thousands of koku (Gojō's was 70,000), not a single town. Intendants near the Kantō lived in Edo and came only for surveys, inspections, tours and serious incidents.

Used for: how seldom an official came out to a village, as the reason the notice board stands in for the state (the notice board); the small staff of a daikansho, against the Chinese county's clerks (buildings)
