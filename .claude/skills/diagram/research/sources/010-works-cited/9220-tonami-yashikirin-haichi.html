tonami-yashikirin-haichi

Tonami City Institute for Dispersed Settlement Studies (砺波市立砺波散村地域研究所), 「4-1 The homestead grove that wraps the house」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「④－１家をつつむ屋敷林」), from 『砺波平野の散村』 (revised edition, 2001), as published on the Tonami City board of education's digital archive 砺波正倉 (https://1073shoso.jp/www/sankyo/detail.jsp?id=18168)

What it is: A chapter of the Tonami City Institute for Dispersed Settlement Studies' booklet on the dispersed farmsteads of the Tonami plain (revised edition 2001), published on the city's own archive, describing the plain's homestead groves (kainyo): how they began, the Kaga domain's 1717 protection of seven kinds of tree, the tree species found in them classed by height, which trees stand on which side of the house - tall sugi, kashi and keyaki from south to west, sugi, enoki and alder with bamboo from west to north, and little grove on the east, where the house fronts, but flowering and fruit trees - and what the grove did for the household.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a local research institute's account, drawn from survey, of where the trees stood around a farmhouse in the one Japanese landscape where a homestead grove is at its most developed, which is the question our grove and garden placement asks. Its limits are that it is a popular booklet that gives no survey date, sample or method beyond its 2001 publication; that it describes groves as they stood in the late twentieth century and notes itself that ornamental garden trees have lately been brought in and the bamboo groves have mostly gone, so part of what it lists is modern and part is past; that the sides it gives follow the Tonami plain's own winds - the strong south wind of spring and the winter monsoon - so they carry over only to a place whose winds come from the same quarters, and elsewhere the grove turns to face that place's winds; that the Tonami plain is the dispersed snow country of Hokuriku, with an isolated farmstead's grove at the developed end of the Japanese range, not a nucleated village's; and that it gives no distances between the house, its plots and the trees.

Used for: the east-facing house front with fruit trees rather than grove on the east; the sides of the house the tall trees and the bamboo stand on; the winds the grove held off; the bamboo stands once common in the grove, mixed among its trees, and their uses; the grove built round cedar with many kinds of tree (vegetation)
