jawiki-yashikigami

ja.wikipedia 屋敷神; minka-en.com 屋敷神; satologue.com 屋敷神様 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/屋敷神)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on 屋敷神 (yashikigami, the guardian deity of a household's own plot), together with two short pages on the same subject: the exhibit page of the Fukushima City Minka-en (福島市民家園, a municipal open-air museum of folk houses in Tohoku, minka-en.com), one sentence placing a stone, wooden or thatched hokora in a corner of the plot, especially the northeast 鬼門; and a page of Satologue (satologue.com), a hotel along the Ome line in western Tokyo opened in 2025, describing the small hokora in a corner of its own grounds. The Wikipedia article gives Naoe Hiroji's three types of household worship (every house keeps a shrine; only an old main house keeps one; the kin gather at the old main house to worship it), which come down to two patterns of who keeps a shrine - every household, or only an old main house - and it gives the form the shrine takes, almost always a small stone or wooden hokora rather than a building.

Why it applies, and its limits: It tells us the form of the household shrine - a small stone or wooden hokora, almost never a shrine hall - and where it commonly stood, which the page gives as the northwest or the northeast of the plot without preferring either; the page goes on to say that treating the northeast as the unlucky demon gate is a later overlay from Onmyodo and a comparatively recent custom, so our maps take a corner and not a compass rule. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary which Japanese Wikipedia itself flags as under-sourced and possibly original research, a heavier discount than the usual encyclopedia caveat; that it describes a folk custom that varied by region and household, so it gives a common pattern and not a rule; and that it dates none of it. It puts the changes in order - an old tree or stone as the yorishiro before any hokora; the hokora, and any hall or torii, a later change; the northeast as demon gate later still; the famous-shrine kami such as Inari a later substitution through folk religious practitioners, given as the state of things 現在では, today - but dates none of them beyond one painting of 1803, and its reading list (Yanagita; Naoe 1966; Sasaki 1983; Mori 1996) is twentieth-century folklore fieldwork. So it describes the custom as found in the twentieth century, and nothing here, Inari included, fixes the practice to the era our maps depict. It also says the custom is absent from Jodo Shinshu regions. The two companion pages carry less: the Minka-en page is one museum label from Fukushima, and it names the northeast 鬼門 as the usual corner, which the article calls a later Onmyodo overlay, so the two disagree and our maps follow the article; the Satologue page is a hotel's promotional page about its own shrine and supports nothing beyond a small hokora in a corner of the plot.

Used for: the two patterns; a small stone or wooden hokora at a corner of the plot (the northeast 鬼門 reading is, by the article's own account, a later overlay); the origin in the woods near the house, an old tree or stone as the yorishiro before any shrine (buildings); that a carefully kept household shrine may be given a hall on the scale of a subsidiary shrine and even a torii, and that Inari is very often the household's kami (religion-and-death).
