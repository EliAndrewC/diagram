kotobank-kido

Kotobank, 木戸 ("wooden gate", title translated) - 改訂新版 世界大百科事典 (Heibonsha) and 山川 日本史小辞典 ("Yamakawa Concise Dictionary of Japanese History", title translated) entries, with the Digital Daijisen and Seisenban Nihon Kokugo Daijiten definitions (https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%9C%A8%E6%88%B8-51154)

What it is: The kotobank reference page for the kido, the wooden gate that closed an Edo street block at night, carrying side by side the entries of five Japanese reference works, of which four are quoted: the Heibonsha World Encyclopedia, revised edition, the Yamakawa Concise Dictionary of Japanese History, and the Digital Daijisen and Nihon Kokugo Daijiten dictionaries, which place the gate at the boundaries between blocks on the main streets.

Why it applies, and its limits: It says what the block gates were for and who paid for them, from standard reference works rather than a hobby page, and it gives the machi-kido's hours. Its limits are those of an encyclopedia: it summarizes without citing the ordinances it summarizes, and its hours for the town gate differ from another page this record cites, which the note at that sentence discloses. It is also a great-city account. The Heibonsha entry, signed by Tamai Tetsuo, puts a gate on every block mainly in Edo and Osaka, and one gate for several blocks in small and middle-sized towns. The Yamakawa entry names castle towns, such as Sendai and Matsuyama, that had no gate on each block, only gates at the town's entrances and between the samurai and townsmen quarters. Its hours and the gate's form are Edo's. So a gate on every block fits our cities, and not a smaller town.

Used for: the block gate's role in public order, its upkeep from the block's own levy, and its hours; that it stood on the street at the block's boundary (government)
