Bureau of Land Management, National Historic Oregon Trail Interpretive Center, Wagons on the Oregon Trail (https://www.blm.gov/learn/interpretive-centers/national-historic-oregon-trail-interpretive-center/explore/wagons)

What it is: An interpretive page of the US Bureau of Land Management's Oregon Trail center describing the emigrant wagons of the 1840s-1860s overland trail, the oxen that drew them (it names no mules), and the routine of a travel day, including the midday halt called the nooning.

Why it applies, and its limits: It describes how long an ox-drawn train halted at midday and what the animals did while it rested, the one readable account found of a draft team's rest at a stop. It is nineteenth-century America, not East Asia, and an emigrant train rather than a post-station; we take only what an ox team at a halt did, and only as a rough bound. It is an unreferenced summary written for visitors, not a period diary or a count: its "an hour or so" is a generalization, and it says a train might stay several hours in hot weather. The length of the halt was set by the wagon leader to fit the day's travel, not by the animals alone. Its oxen grazed open range at camp stops in open country on a trek of months, not in a town yard where animals are fed fodder.

Used for: how long a draft team rested, unyoked, at a halt (stable yards)
