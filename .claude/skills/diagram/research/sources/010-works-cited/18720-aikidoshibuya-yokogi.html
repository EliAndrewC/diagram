aikidoshibuya-yokogi

Shibuya Aikido-kai (Yoshinkan Aikido), 合気道は剣の理合。なのに開祖はどうして剣を教えなかったのか？ ("Aikido follows the principles of the sword. So why did its founder not teach the sword?", title translated), blog post, October 30, 2021 (in Japanese; https://www.aikidoshibuya.tokyo/post/_riai)

What it is: A blog post by an aikido club in Tokyo, naming no writer, that quotes two of the founder Ueshiba Morihei's students, Tada Hiroshi and Saitō Morihiro, on striking a standing timber and a crosspiece of bundled branches, as they learned it at Iwama, where Ueshiba moved in 1942.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the earliest DATABLE description found of a bundle of branches laid on a stand and struck with a wooden sword: Saitō, who entered in 1946, built the stand of two posts and laid bundled branches on it. It is a modern practitioners' page quoting recollections of Iwama after 1946 printed in later books, not records of the time; Tada's line that the Satsuma samurai of old struck bundles of brushwood is his own hearsay, undated, and the post itself, citing a magazine of 2007, corrects him on which Satsuma school the crosspiece belongs to: Yakumaru Jigen-ryū, a Satsuma school of the late Edo period that still strikes a crosspiece today. So "earliest datable" means only that nothing earlier was found. The post shows that Tada's Satsuma line is hearsay, but it cannot rule out an Edo-period crosspiece in Satsuma, and no source read here dates the school's crosspiece either way.

Used for: that the earliest datable bundle of branches struck with a wooden sword is at Iwama after 1946, and that its Satsuma past is hearsay (buildings)
