kotobank-dozozukuri

kotobank, "土蔵造" (dozo-zukuri, storehouse construction) (in Japanese; https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%9C%9F%E8%94%B5%E9%80%A0-583695)

What it is: The Kotobank page reprinting Japanese dictionaries' and the Heibonsha World Encyclopedia's entries on dozo-zukuri, the thick plastered-earth construction of the storehouse, used also for townhouses.

Why it applies, and its limits: It names the construction as one of Japanese architecture's fire-resistant forms and dates Edo's spread of it for fire prevention to after the Kyoho era (1716-36). Its entries are short dictionary and encyclopedia summaries (tertiary), good for the construction's form and dates rather than for measurements. They differ on the policy: Heibonsha says the spread was forced, Yamakawa says it was encouraged, and the older Heibonsha says a 1720 edict permitted it. Mypedia instead puts the edicts after the Meireki fire (1657). The 1720 edict quoted in the Nihon Kokugo Daijiten is the firmest date. Its entries concern the construction and Edo's building policy; they say nothing of what a storehouse held or of spacing between buildings.

Used for: the plastered storehouse as a fire-resistant construction, spread in Edo for fire prevention (urban features)
