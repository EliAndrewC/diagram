kabu-ido-commons

Endo, Takahiro, "The Kabu-ido system and factors affecting local groundwater extraction control: case study of a customary groundwater management in Japan", Water History 14 (2022) 169-191 (https://doi.org/10.1007/s12685-022-00302-1)

What it is: A study of one Edo-period institution by which sixteen villages behind a single ring levee on the Noubi Plain limited how many wells they could sink, worked out of the villages’ own papers - the 1812 agreement between the upper and lower villages, and the Nishimatsu Diary, which records the well patrols year by year from 1821. It is the fullest account there is of a farming community policing its own water without an authority above it.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the clearest attested case of a community capping its own well count by agreement, which is exactly the practice this project draws more freely than the record attests, so it is evidence worth having against our own decision. Its limits cut that weight down a long way, and all four are the paper’s own. It is ONE district, not a country, and the paper says the institution is little known even in Japan. It is unusual rather than typical - the literature values it as a pioneering example. It runs from 1812 to the 1930s, the last half-century of the Edo period and the sixty years after it. And the wells it caps are ARTESIAN IRRIGATION wells whose free flow waterlogged the paddy of the villages downhill; the grievance is drainage nuisance, not scarcity, and a draw-well in a house yard raises none of it. One correction worth stating: the agreement’s only money runs the other way from a license fee - the upper villages pay the well inspectors a ryo a year, and no charge for holding a well appears anywhere in it.

Used for: the cap on well numbers, the permit, the villagers’ own patrols, and the government preferring an internal settlement
