dozo-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 土蔵 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%9C%9F%E8%94%B5)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the dozo, the thick-walled plastered storehouse: the kinds it is divided into by what it holds, the rice store among them, and a section on fire resistance giving wall thicknesses of about 300 mm and more, outer doors faced in earth and plaster, and an unfootnoted statement that interiors often escaped the Edo great fires and the modern air raids, which the article itself hedges (a plasterer sealed the gaps with clay when fire came near; a store opened while still hot could burn out by backdraft).

Why it applies, and its limits: It turns "fireproof kura" from an adjective into something a map can draw and a reader can check, and it names the rice store as one of the kinds such a building was built for, which is the thing our unwalled seat protects first. Its limits are that it is tertiary and Japanese. Of what we take from it, only its list of kinds, the rice store and the gunpowder store (硝煙蔵) among them, is footnoted, to a Kawagoe City Museum bulletin; that same footnote also carries its definition, its kinds by use and its Meiji history. Its whole fire-resistance section carries no footnote: the wall thickness of about 300 mm and more, the earth-faced doors and the survival of interiors alike. The fire door shut when fire came, the sliding door it calls the Osaka door (大阪戸), is named only in its opening paragraph, also without a footnote and with no era. It gives that thickness as what standing buildings "often" have, with no era attached, so it is a typical figure for the form and not a period specification; its history section, also unfootnoted, puts walls of 30 cm and more on castle turrets and keeps from the late Azuchi-Momoyama period into the Edo period, which dates the technique but not the storehouse figure. Its fire-survival claim is general, mixes the Edo great fires with twentieth-century air raids, and is conditional in the article itself, so it supports the form and not a survival rate.

Used for: the plastered storehouse as the fire-resistant form, its wall thickness and earth-faced doors, and the rice store among the kinds it held; the rice and unhulled-grain stores guarded against fire, damp and theft (buildings); gunpowder among what a storehouse held, and the fire door shut when fire came, against the charcoal yard's fire gap (urban features)
