dozo-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 土蔵 (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%9C%9F%E8%94%B5)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the dozo, the thick-walled plastered storehouse: the kinds it is divided into by what it holds, the rice store among them, and a section on fire resistance giving wall thicknesses of about 300 mm and more, outer doors faced in earth and plaster, and an unfootnoted statement that interiors often escaped the Edo great fires and the modern air raids, which the article itself hedges (a plasterer sealed the gaps with clay when fire came near; a store opened while still hot could burn out by backdraft).

Why it applies, and its limits: It turns "fireproof kura" from an adjective into something a map can draw and a reader can check, and it names the rice store as one of the kinds such a building was built for, which is the thing our unwalled seat protects first. Its limits are that it is tertiary and Japanese. Only its list of kinds, the rice store among them, is footnoted, to a Kawagoe City Museum bulletin. Its whole fire-resistance section carries no footnote: the wall thickness of about 300 mm and more, the earth-faced doors and the survival of interiors alike. It gives that thickness as what standing buildings "often" have, with no era attached, so it is a typical figure for the form and not a period specification. Its fire-survival claim is general, mixes the Edo great fires with twentieth-century air raids, and is conditional in the article itself, so it supports the form and not a survival rate.

Used for: the plastered storehouse as the fire-resistant form, its wall thickness and earth-faced doors, and the rice store among the kinds it held; the rice and unhulled-grain stores guarded against fire, damp and theft (buildings)
