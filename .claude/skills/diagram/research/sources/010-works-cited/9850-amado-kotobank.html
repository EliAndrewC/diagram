amado-kotobank

Kotobank, 雨戸 (Amado) - Kaitei Shinpan Sekai Daihyakka Jiten, Shimizu Hiromu; Nihon Daihyakka Zensho (Nipponica), Hirai Kiyoshi; Britannica Kokusai Daihyakka Jiten (in Japanese; https://kotobank.jp/word/%E9%9B%A8%E6%88%B8-27180)

What it is: The Kotobank page for the storm shutter, carrying dictionary and encyclopedia entries, among them the architectural historian Hirai Kiyoshi's in Nipponica, on what the amado is, how it runs and is stored, what it is for, and when it appeared.

Why it applies, and its limits: It establishes that a residence of the Edo period had amado along its openings, stowed in a tobukuro by day and shut against the night, weather and cold. Its limits are that these are tertiary summaries; that the Britannica sentence on daytime storage describes the modern house as much as the Edo one; that the dated examples are the rulers' great buildings (Hideyoshi's Jurakudai; the Ninomaru Palace of Nijo Castle as the oldest survivor), and none says when the tobukuro-stowed amado reached a lesser samurai house - the only humbler Edo-period form it names is the townhouse shopfront's drop-in boards, stowed behind a lintel; and that none says how a household used the shutters of rooms it was not living in.

Used for: what the amado and tobukuro are, their date, and the amado's use by night and storage by day (buildings)
