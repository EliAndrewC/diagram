sakamoto-tsubaki-1985-omoya-muki

Sakamoto Iwao and Tsubaki Katsuyoshi, "On the direction of the main house in villages on slopes and at the foot of hills: study on the plane composition of the village landscape in Okinawa, part 4", Transactions of the Architectural Institute of Japan (planning) 350 (April 1985) (in Japanese; https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/aijax/350/0/350_KJ00004073894/_pdf)

What it is: A field survey by two architects of Kyushu Sangyo University that tallied which way every main house faced, on a 16-point compass, in the most densely built part of each of 27 Okinawan villages on slopes and 15 at the foot of hills (shops left out), each surveyed in a year between 1974 and 1984, with the terrain, the coast and the curve of the roads as the causes they test; part 3 of the series did the same for villages on flat ground.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the one readable count found of how far the houses of one village spread in bearing, over thousands of houses. It covers Okinawa alone, whose villages were laid out under their own customs and against typhoon winds, it counts the houses standing when each village was surveyed, between 1974 and 1984, a generation after the Battle of Okinawa and the rebuilding that followed, with some main houses of reinforced concrete (the paper names them), so it carries the bearing that a lot and its lane gave a house rather than any house of a premodern date; its one-in-ten is the figure for the slope villages (3,267 houses: 87% on the commonest bearing and its two neighbors, 11% turned right, 1% left, 1% back), while the villages at the foot of hills spread wider (810 houses: 72%, about one in five a quarter turn off, 7% turned back); and its 16-point bins say nothing about the spread inside one point, so it bounds the spread from above rather than measuring it to the degree.

Used for: that the houses of one village spread over the commonest bearing and its two neighbors, and that about one in ten faces a quarter turn off it (homesteads)
