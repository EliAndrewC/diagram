Tanba Shimbun, 1つの村に14神社 地方に残る『神とのつながり』 明治政府の『合祀令』反発し現存 ("Fourteen shrines in one village: a 'bond with the gods' surviving in the country, still standing after resisting the Meiji government's merger order", title translated from the Japanese by this project), 16 October 2019 (in Japanese; https://tanba.jp/2019/10/1%E3%81%A4%E3%81%AE%E6%9D%91%E3%81%AB14%E7%A5%9E%E7%A4%BE%E3%80%80%E5%9C%B0%E6%96%B9%E3%81%AB%E6%AE%8B%E3%82%8B%E3%80%8C%E7%A5%9E%E3%81%A8%E3%81%AE%E3%81%A4%E3%81%AA%E3%81%8C%E3%82%8A-%E3%80%8D/)

What it is: A local newspaper article on Higashi-Ashida in Tanba city, Hyogo, a community of about 180 households with fourteen shrines - one for the whole and thirteen for its sections (koaza), one section keeping two - set against the city's usual one shrine a community, with the shrine priest's account of why and a shrine elder's guess.

Why it applies, and its limits: It shows, in one place, both forms below the village: sections with a shrine of their own and communities with none. It is a newspaper feature about present-day communities in one city, its counts cover only registered shrines, and its explanations are the priest's and a shrine elder's guess. Its fourteen shrines are listed in the shrine's 1879 register and most were rebuilt in the Edo period, so the section shrines reach back at least to the late-Edo village, though the newspaper reads that register at second hand. The communities with no shrine, though, are counted today, after the 1906 merger order, so they may be what the mergers left rather than what an Edo-period village held. It is one case, which the article calls exceptional, and it gives no share.

Used for: a village's sections each keeping a shrine beside the whole community's, and communities with none (archetypes)
