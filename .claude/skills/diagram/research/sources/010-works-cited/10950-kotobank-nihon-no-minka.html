kotobank-nihon-no-minka

kotobank 日本の民家 ("the folk houses of Japan", title translated from the Japanese by this project) (https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%97%A5%E6%9C%AC%E3%81%AE%E6%B0%91%E5%AE%B6-1616904)

What it is: The Kotobank reprint of the 日本の民家 article in Shogakukan's encyclopedia 日本大百科全書 (Nipponica), supervised by 工藤圭章: 100 houses chosen from the folk houses designated national Important Cultural Properties, listed region by region from north to south. Each has its place, its date, its roof form and material and a line on what makes it notable. They date from the fifteenth century to 1907 and include townhouses, a samurai row house and Okinawan houses as well as farmhouses.

Why it applies, and its limits: It shows how large the greatest farmhouses grew (the hereditary village headman's Hirayama house of 1769, 30 meters along its ridge; the district headman's Yoshimura house, over 40 meters across its front) and that some roofs now sheeted in metal were thatched first (the Takano and Nasu houses); the tiled or sheeted roofs it traces to an earlier covering were board, not thatch (the Kita and Ishiba houses). Its limits are those of any list of designated houses: they are the survivors, the large and the notable, so it says nothing of an ordinary house; and it is a tertiary summary. Each size it gives is one dimension, not a floor area. A district headman (大庄屋), who governed a group of villages, stands a scale above a village headman. Its dates run into the Meiji era (1901, 1905, 1907), so an entry's date must be read before its form is taken as premodern. Most of the re-roofings it records are twentieth-century repairs (one is dated 1963). The Agency for Cultural Affairs' designation database gives each house's dimensions from its designation record and is the closer source for a size.

Used for: the size of a hereditary headman's house, and roofs re-covered from thatch (homesteads)
