kotobank-inkyo

Heibonsha, Kaitei shinpan Sekai daihyakka jiten (entries by Nakamura Ken and Ueno Kazuo), Shogakukan, Nihon daihyakka zensho (Nipponica, the entry by Takeda Akira), and Heibonsha, Hyakka jiten Mypedia, s.v. 隠居 ("inkyo", retirement, title translated from the Japanese by this project), on kotobank (https://kotobank.jp/word/%E9%9A%A0%E5%B1%85-32812)

What it is: Several Japanese encyclopedias' entries on retirement gathered on one page: the legal sense, and the village custom by which the old couple handed the house to the heir and lived apart as a household of their own, often in a separate retirement house in the same plot.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is where the record finds that one farm family could live under two roofs, that the second roof mostly stood within the family's own yard, and in which parts of Japan the custom was strong or thin. These are encyclopedia summaries of folklore and family-sociology fieldwork, much of it of the twentieth century - three entries note the custom is still practiced, and one that building retirement houses had lately become a fashion in part of Shiga, and the page's Edo-period material is on warrior and townsman retirement, not the village custom - so they describe a custom rather than date it within the Edo period; they give its geography only as tendencies ("strongly", "common") and give no share of households that kept a retirement house. They say only that the retirement house stood mostly within the yard, with its own entrance, and was smaller than the main house. They do not say where in the yard it stood or give its size, and they note that in fishing and mountain villages the old couple often borrowed an empty house or part of another household's instead. Japan alone.

Used for: the retirement house in the family's own plot, retirement as the old couple moving out, and where in Japan the custom was kept and where the family stayed under one roof (settlements)
