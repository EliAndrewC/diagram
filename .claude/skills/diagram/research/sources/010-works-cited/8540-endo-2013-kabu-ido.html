endo-2013-kabu-ido

Takahiro Endo, "The Kabu-ido System: Innovations in an Indigenous Groundwater Management Institution", Digital Library of the Commons, Indiana University (2013) (https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/bitstream/handle/10535/8876/ENDO_0894.pdf)

What it is: A commons-studies paper on the kabu-ido, the institution by which the villages inside the Takasu ring levee on the Nobi plain limited how many wells could be sunk, with a dated table of the surveys, agreements and authorized well counts from 1854 onward.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the evidence that the number of wells in a farming community was a governed quantity fixed by the residents themselves - a permit bought with a fee, a survey of the wells standing, a cap negotiated between the upper and the lower villages, and patrolmen the villages provided for each other - with the local government expressly declining to interfere inside the levee. (The permit fee is this paper's; the sibling entry on the same institution records that the 1812 agreement carries no such charge and that the only money runs the other way, so the two accounts differ and the record should not lean on the fee.) It also corrects the reason: the quarrel was not scarcity but its opposite, the drainage the upper villages' artesian wells poured onto the lower villages' paddy. Its limits begin with the era: the institution the paper documents was established around the 1850s and ran to about 1905, so most of its recorded life is Meiji rather than Edo, and it reaches the setting's period only at the very end. Beyond that: it is one institution on one ring levee; that the cap is a total across the whole levee allotted among villages rather than a rule per village in the abstract; that it is Japanese; and that it is a working paper in a repository rather than a journal article, though a companion study of the same institution by the same scholar is registered here separately.

Used for: the kabu-ido cap and how it was administered; the 1854 survey and agreement and the 388 authorized wells of 1861; drainage rather than scarcity as the conflict it settled
