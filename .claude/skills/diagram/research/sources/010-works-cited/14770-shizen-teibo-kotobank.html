shizen-teibo-kotobank

Kotobank, 自然堤防 (shizen teibō, a natural levee), the entries of several Japanese dictionaries, the one used by Kaizu Masatomo in the revised Heibonsha World Encyclopedia (改訂新版 世界大百科事典) (in Japanese; https://kotobank.jp/word/自然堤防-73610)

What it is: Kotobank's page gathering the dictionary entries for the natural levee, the low sandy ridge a flooding river builds along its own banks; the entry used is the revised Heibonsha World Encyclopedia's, signed by a physical geographer.

Why it applies, and its limits: It says why the levee was built on and farmed dry - its deposits are sandy and it stands higher and drier than the ground around it - which is the reason the fields page gives for dry fields on the rise; the landform does not change with the century. It is a short encyclopedia entry, a modern geographer's summary of Japan's plains (its examples are the Nōbi and Tsugaru plains), so China's great rivers stand in only by the same landform - it gives their levees as up to 10 km wide and over 10 m high, against Japan's few hundred metres to 2 km and at most a few metres. It dates the land use only as "from of old" (古くから), against a recent change it names - paddies on the levees since modern irrigation and drainage works, and houses spreading onto the back marsh - so the older pattern is the one to draw. The Britannica entry on the same page names house land and orchards instead of dry fields, so it carries the principle and no proportion.

Used for: settlements, dry fields and roads on the natural levee because it is sandy, higher and drier (fields)
