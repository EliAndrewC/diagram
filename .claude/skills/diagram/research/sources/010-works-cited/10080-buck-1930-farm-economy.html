buck-1930-farm-economy

John Lossing Buck, Chinese Farm Economy: A Study of 2866 Farms in Seventeen Localities and Seven Provinces in China, University of Chicago Press, 1930, full text on the Internet Archive (https://archive.org/stream/chinesefarmecono0000john/chinesefarmecono0000john_djvu.txt)

What it is: The first of the two great farm surveys of republican China by John Lossing Buck and his students at the University of Nanking: 2,866 farms in seventeen localities of seven provinces, visited in 1921-1925, with tables of land, crops, livestock, labor and living standards.

Why it applies, and its limits: It counts, farm by farm, how many kept each kind of animal, which is the measure the record needs for how common the farmyard chicken was. Its limits are that it describes the 1920s, after the empire, read back here into the late imperial countryside - a countryside that by then also sold eggs to an industrial export trade through Shanghai's egg-processing plants, which Buck's East Central localities sat nearest, and which he himself says ("perhaps because of the nearness to markets") may be why chickens were commoner there, so its 82 per cent may run above an imperial-era share; that its farms were gathered by his students and field investigators, not drawn at random; that seventeen localities in north and east-central China are not the whole country; and that the Internet Archive's text is an OCR whose tables are garbled, so only its prose is quoted. Its grave figures carry the same limits and one more: they come from only five level-land localities for the share of farm land in graves, and from two small samples measured by students, and Buck himself says grave land is often reported inside the crop area.

Used for: the share of Chinese farms keeping chickens (homesteads); the share of Chinese farm land under graves, and where in the fields the graves stood (religion-and-death)
