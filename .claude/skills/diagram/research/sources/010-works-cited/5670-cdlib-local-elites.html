cdlib-local-elites

Esherick & Rankin (eds.), Chinese Local Elites and Patterns of Dominance: the introduction and concluding remarks, Timothy Brook's chapter 1 ("Family Continuity and Cultural Hegemony: The Gentry of Ningbo, 1368-1911") and Lynda S. Bell's chapter 4 ("From Comprador to County Magnate: Bourgeois Practice in the Wuxi County Silk Industry") (UC Press e-book, full text, https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/public/book/chinese-local-elites-and-patterns-of-dominance.html)

What it is: Chinese Local Elites and Patterns of Dominance, edited by Joseph W. Esherick and Mary Backus Rankin (University of California Press, 1990), a collection of eleven scholarly essays on how local elites held power in late imperial and Republican China. The editors' introduction and conclusion summarize the volume's findings - by Qing times much of the Lower Yangzi's gentry had moved into administrative centers and canal towns and drew their income from land they no longer lived on, and across most of China land was gathered slowly in scattered plots; Brook's chapter locates the home bases of Ningbo's gentry lineages, and Bell's reviews how Jiangnan landholding changed at the Ming-Qing transition.

Why it applies, and its limits: It bears on the question our hinterland has to settle, whether rich houses cluster along the road outside a city gate, but only in part: an urbanized, absentee landlord class collecting through agents leaves the open countryside without resident landlords' estates, yet Brook's own count for Ningbo puts twenty-four of forty-two greater gentry lineages in the city or its immediate suburbs (several named for the West and North Suburbs) and eleven more in villages within ten kilometers of the wall, some keeping a town house as well. So it argues against grand estates spread through the countryside, not against elite houses just outside the wall, and it says nothing of a gate road. Its limits are real: the absentee-landlord pattern it describes is the Lower Yangzi's (and Fujian's), and the volume itself says it was not general: in the southeast rural landlords stayed tied to their communities, Middle Yangzi elites kept closer rural contact, and North China had little landlordism at all, so a place not modeled on Jiangnan should not take it as the norm; the volume's weight is on the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, but the parts we use are not: Brook's chapter covers 1368-1911 from Ming and Qing records, the introduction's absentee-landlord sentence is about the Qing, and Bell's review is of the seventeenth-century transition, so only the concluding remarks' China-wide generalizations draw partly on later evidence; it is about China rather than Japan; and it describes where elites lived and how they drew rent rather than what their houses looked like. Brook's distances are one prefecture's, and are lineages' home villages rather than estates; Bell's account of the change in landholding is her summary of other scholars' work, for Jiangnan only.

Used for: "By Qing times, the substantially urbanized gentry were living the leisured life of absentee landlords in administrative centers or the many small towns that lined the canals"; absentee landlords acting through agents and rent bursaries; land accumulated in small pieces and scattered plots; the move away from the single resident landlord's estate at the Ming-Qing transition; Ningbo gentry villages within ten kilometers of the city wall
