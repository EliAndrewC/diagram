《周禮·冬官考工記》 (the Zhouli, Kaogongji chapter), Chinese Wikisource transcription (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%91%A8%E7%A6%AE/%E5%86%AC%E5%AE%98%E8%80%83%E5%B7%A5%E8%A8%98)

What it is: The artificers' chapter of the Rites of Zhou, a Warring States-era book of craft specifications (added to the Zhouli in the Han in place of its lost Winter Office chapter) that, among much else, prescribes the ideal capital - nine li square, three gates a side, nine avenues each way, the court before the market - and an ideal ladder of field channels, each rung twice as wide as the one below; here in a Wikisource transcription of the primary text, without commentary and without a stated base edition.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the primary source for the square ideal every discussion of Chinese city plans starts from, quoted rather than paraphrased. Its limit is that it is an ideal, compiled long before the cities our maps are modeled on, and says nothing about how far real cities followed it - which is why the essay beside it is cited for the shape's actual cause. The field channels are an ideal in the same way, and they carry two more limits. First, scale: each rung is the boundary of a unit in the ideal well-field division (the quan inside a plot, the sui at the field head, the gou between jing of nine farms, the xu between cheng ten li square, the hui between tong a hundred li square), so the xu and hui are district channels, not a village's, and the text never says how many were dug or how closely real ones matched. Second, the unit: its chi is the Warring States and Han foot of about 23 cm, not the Ming-Qing chi of about 32 cm or the Japanese shaku of 30.3 cm, so the one-chi quan is about nine inches and the two-xun hui about 3.7 m (12 ft).

Used for: the Kaogongji's square capital, its gates, its avenues and the court before the market; its ladder of field channels, each twice as wide as the one before, from the one-chi quan to the hui that joins the river (water); the finest channel as the cut of two spades and the one at the field's head as twice it, for the width a gathering drain begins at (fields)
