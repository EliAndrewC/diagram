guangdong-xinyu-22

Qu Dajun (屈大均), Guangdong xinyu (廣東新語, New Sayings of Guangdong), chapter 22, "Scale talk" (鱗語), the Chinese Wikisource transcription (in classical Chinese; https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/%E5%BB%A3%E6%9D%B1%E6%96%B0%E8%AA%9E/%E5%8D%B722)

What it is: The fish chapter of a late-seventeenth-century description of Guangdong by a scholar of the Pearl River delta, covering the pond fish of Guangzhou, the fry caught in the West River and sold by the households of Jiujiang in Nanhai, the dikes and ponds of the villages, and the fishery levies.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a primary account from inside the Pearl River delta at the time the dike-pond system was spreading there, so it speaks directly to what a premodern dike-pond village kept on its dikes and in its ponds. It is one observer's description, not a survey: its shares (how much of Jiujiang's pond water raised fry, how much of its land the fish and the ponds took) are his reckoning of one township, and one he himself calls exceptional (the only township with fry ponds, low-lying, where grain was hard to come by and few farmed), so they mark the far end of a dike-pond landscape rather than an ordinary village's; they are given in separate passages that do not say whether a pond's share counts its dikes, and a Wikisource transcription of a printed edition may carry copying slips (Wikisource files the page as 50% complete, and some characters do not render). It is also one region's practice: lychee, longan and citrus on the dikes, and a fry trade fed by the West River's spawning grounds, belong to the subtropical Pearl River delta, so for a dike-pond village elsewhere in the setting it carries the pattern of fish pond below and planted dike above, not these particular trees. The same chapter mixes plain observation with lore (dragons, fish that turn into birds and tigers); only its plain economic passages are taken.

Used for: that nursery ponds belonged to the fry township of Jiujiang, where most of the pond water raised fry, and that other places bought their fry and raised grown fish; that the villages of Guangzhou's large counties planted their pond dikes with fruit trees, lychee above all, and with tea and mulberry; that the fish took half of Jiujiang's land and its fish ponds eight tenths of the township, the oldest shares read of a dike-pond landscape's water, and that the larger ponds reached several tens of mu (archetypes)
