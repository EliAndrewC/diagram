kotobank-hitoyado

Seisenban Nihon Kokugo Daijiten, Kaitei Shinpan Sekai Dai-hyakka Jiten and Sekai Dai-hyakka Jiten, s.v. 人宿, on kotobank (https://kotobank.jp/word/人宿)

What it is: Kotobank's reproduction of the reference entries on hitoyado, the Edo-period placement brokers for servants, with excerpts from the older encyclopedia's entries on brokers and on servants. Both dictionaries also give 人宿 an older first sense, an inn or lodging house.

Why it applies, and its limits: It says the brokers grew up with the cities, that samurai households were most of their clients, and that samurai-household service gradually came to be done by peasants and townsmen hired through them and through day-labor guilds. That change was gradual and against the shogunate's policy, which from the Keichō era repeatedly banned one-year hired service to keep hereditary retainers, so a household kept its hereditary retainers alongside the hired one-year and half-year servants; and the hired men it names are those a samurai owed for military service, not the staffing of the whole household. It is written about Edo above all, the servants it names are mostly attendants and footmen rather than house staff, and it never says where in a town the brokers kept their premises, only that they put job-seekers up until they found a place. Its 1710 count of about 390 brokers describes the shogun's capital and should not be scaled down to a castle town.

Used for: how a samurai household hired its servants (government)
