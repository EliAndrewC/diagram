kikoba-kenchi

木古庭村 (Miura, Sagami) Bunroku 1594 and Genroku 1699 検地帳, 日本建築学会計画系論文集 222 (https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/aijsaxx/222/0/222_KJ00003747888/_pdf)

What it is: A 1974 research paper in the Transactions of the Architectural Institute of Japan (number 222), the second part of a study called "近世村方文書よりみた相模国三浦郡木古庭村の屋敷と家屋" ("The homesteads and houses of Kikoba village, Miura district, Sagami province, seen through early-modern village documents"). It works through that one village's own paperwork, including a 1594 land survey and a homestead-area register submitted to the magistrate in 1691, to reconstruct how large peasant house plots and houses were and who held them.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the one thing a homestead layout most needs and can least often find - the measured spread of house-lot areas across a whole village, from a register of 1699 - so the sizes and the spread of the plots on our maps rest on it; it also shows that the small end of the distribution was occupied, since every household in the village owned a main house. Its limits are that it is one village of 58 households in one province, so the spread is a pattern rather than a national rate; that the lots run from about 20 to 100 bu with peaks at 30 and 50, and the paper gives no separate figure for the great holders' lots - its "twice" and "three times" count buildings per household, not lot area - so any larger lot on our maps is our reading and not its measurement; and that the public copy is a scanned Japanese paper we read for its tables and its stated figures.

Used for: homestead-lot areas 20-100 bu with peaks at 30 and 50 - the right-skew corroboration (the paper's "2x" counts buildings per household, not lot area, so it gives no separate figure for great holders); and that by Genroku every peasant held a homestead, so the small end is occupied rather than empty
