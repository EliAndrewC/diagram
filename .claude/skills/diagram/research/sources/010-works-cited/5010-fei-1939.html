What it is: Peasant Life in China: A Field Study of Country Life in the Yangtze Valley by Fei Hsiao-tung (Routledge, 1939), the classic village ethnography of Kaixiangong, a silk-and-rice village on the lower Yangtze delta near Lake Tai. It records the place household by household: how the houses stand along the streams, what land a family held, boats, manure pits, and silkworms reared indoors on stacked trays.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a first-hand, room-by-room account of exactly the kind of settlement these maps draw, and almost everything we take from it - where the manure pit sits, that nearly every household kept a boat, that the village grew along its water, where rice was threshed - is arrangement rather than technology, and so belongs to the older village as much as to the one Fei walked through. The limits are real: it is 1936, with railways, motor roads and a silk economy already disrupted by mechanized filatures, and Fei chose this village because an industrial reform of its silk-making was under way there, which is a bias toward exactly the silk arrangements we take from it; his fieldwork was two months of one summer, by his own statement not a full year's cycle, so the seasonal claims we draw on are a summer observer's; it is one village in one delta rather than a survey; it is Chinese, standing beside the Japanese material for a setting built on both; and the copy we can read is a scanned text whose garbles the footnotes carry.
