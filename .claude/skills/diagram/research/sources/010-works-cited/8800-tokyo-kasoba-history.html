The Institute for Tokyo Municipal Research (公益財団法人東京市町村自治調査会), 『多摩・島しょ地域における火葬場の需給及び運営に関する調査研究報告書』 ("A study of the supply, demand and operation of crematoria in the Tama and island region"), March 2015, served openly as a PDF (https://www.tama-100.or.jp/cmsfiles/contents/0000000/470/all.pdf)

What it is: A planning study of where the Tama municipalities should build crematoria next, whose second chapter traces the law governing a cremation ground from the 1870s onward and whose case studies record the site areas of individual facilities.

Why it applies, and its limits: It carries the two things about a crematory that no premodern Japanese source states plainly, and both come with the same discount: they belong to a licensed modern facility rather than an open pyre. First a stated set-back rule: the model standard issued with the burial and graveyard regulations of 1884 put a cremation ground at least 120 ken, about 216 m, from dwellings and from places where people congregate, on ground that is not upwind, walled, with a chimney and a device against the foul smoke. Second the extents: recorded sites run from 10 tsubo 5 go (34.65 m2) at a village ground to 600 tsubo (1,980 m2) at a large urban one, which is the band our drawn cores sit in - though those are Taisho and Showa plants with buildings, furnaces and access inside the figure, a different KIND of place rather than a larger or smaller version of ours, and the 1884 rule is a sanitary regulation written for a walled ground with a chimney and a smoke device. It also records the premodern instance of the same hazard, the removal in Kanbun 7 (1667) of some twenty-odd temple cremation grounds in Asakusa and Shitaya after their smoke reached the shogun on his way to visit a grave. Its limits are that the rule is Meiji 17 and measures from dwellings and crowded ground rather than from a highway, and that the measured sites are Meiji through Showa municipal facilities offered as a scale anchor for a premodern setting rather than as premodern evidence; the 1667 removal is the one premodern datum among them.

Used for: the 1884 set-back of 120 ken from dwellings and from crowded ground; recorded cremation-ground site areas from 10.5 to 600 tsubo; the 1667 concentration of the temple cremation grounds; the open-air pyre as a stone-built bed with no superstructure
