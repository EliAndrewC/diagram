shukubamachi-kotobank

kotobank 宿場町 ("post town", title translated from the Japanese by this project), the dictionary and encyclopedia entries gathered on one page (https://kotobank.jp/word/宿場町-528292)

What it is: The kotobank page for the Edo-period post town, gathering four Japanese reference entries - the Digital Daijisen and Nihon Kokugo Daijiten definitions, the Yamakawa Nihonshi Shōjiten entry, and the Heibonsha World Encyclopedia (revised edition) article signed by the historians Kodama Kōta, Tamai Tetsuo and Yamada Yasuhiko, which carries the layout: how the town was laid out along its highway, how wide the highway ran through it, where the transport office and the lodgings for lords stood, and what marked its two ends.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a reference-work summary of the Japanese post town of the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries - a planned small town of the period whose street width is given in figures and whose lots and ends are described in plan - so it speaks directly to a town on a trunk road. It generalizes across the post towns rather than surveying one: the towns it names (Atsuta, Kuwana, Okazaki and others by their 1843 inn counts; Tsumago, Narai and Ōuchi as preserved) illustrate, and its layout figures - a street two to four ken wide, a typical Tōkaidō town of 300 to 600 houses, the largest over a ri long - are ranges tied to no one town, for a town of hundreds of houses, not a hamlet. The stone-walled or earth-banked mitsuke at the town's ends is stated only for the post towns of the five shogunal highways, whose byway counterparts it calls smaller and less organized, and its other entry gives a bend or a squared masugata turn at the entrance instead; its contrast between busy highways and byways is hedged as what was often the case.

Used for: the width of the street through a post town and the watercourse down it, the strip lots along it, the order of its buildings from the middle outward, and the barrier banks at its ends (ways)
