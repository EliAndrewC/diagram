Wang Jian (王健), "明清以来江南民间信仰中的庙界：以苏、松为中心" ("Temple boundaries in Jiangnan popular religion since the Ming and Qing, centered on Suzhou and Songjiang", title translated from the Chinese by this project), Institute of History, Shanghai Academy of Social Sciences (in Chinese; https://ih.sass.org.cn/_upload/article/files/85/9b/3c530d694fb39357215c6e822c8c/a8324f93-0201-498d-8e0f-c2086ec92055.pdf)

What it is: A research article by Wang Jian, then an assistant research fellow at the Institute of History of the Shanghai Academy of Social Sciences, published in the journal Shilin (史林) in 2008 (no. 6), on the territories served by earth-god temples and market-town temples in the Suzhou and Songjiang region from the Ming onward, drawing on gazetteers and on Hamashima Atsutoshi's fieldwork.

Why it applies, and its limits: It quotes the Ming statute that set one altar to the gods of soil and grain for every hundred rural households, and reports Hamashima's count of earth-god temples against villages - about one to every ten-odd lowland villages, one to about forty hamlets on the delta's slightly raised eastern land - the nearest thing to a count of earth-god temples per village that we have for any part of China. Its limits: it is one region, the Yangzi delta; the ratios count natural villages, and those differ greatly in size - about 113 households on average in the lowland case Hamashima gives (Hecheng), about 8 on the raised eastern land (Shengqiao), which is a slight rise in the delta, not hill country - so one temple to forty such hamlets is about three hundred households, not forty villages of our size, and the lowland ratio itself comes from a third place, Wuqing in Huzhou; the article also records that the Ming altars were already falling out of use in Changshu by the Hongzhi reign and in Taicang in the Jiajing reign, and were gone in many places by the Qing, their worship passing to earth-god temples with images, so an altar for every hundred households belongs to the first Ming century, not to the later village; the ratios are Hamashima's, reported rather than recomputed, and the author himself doubts them, because a township gazetteer could not record every small temple of its area; and the statute is an ideal of the early Ming rather than a measured pattern.
