mizu-shiba-izeki

"Tradition or modernization - the brushwood weir that must choose", Mizu no Bunka (Water Culture) no. 60, Mizkan Water Culture Center, editorial staff (in Japanese; https://www.mizu.gr.jp/kikanshi/no60/11.html)

What it is: A 2018 report in the Mizkan Water Culture Center's magazine on the Kawaharazono weir at Kanoya, Kagoshima, a brushwood irrigation weir said to be the last in Japan: its early-Edo origin, how the farmers build it each March from bundled brushwood, and the choice between keeping it and replacing it with a concrete weir.

Why it applies, and its limits: It places an irrigation weir of plants woven onto wooden stakes in the early Edo period, about 1638, with the Satsuma domain's new-field development, and says weirs of local plants were once found in many places. The early form is local tradition ("it is handed down"), given beside a stone-weir alternative; the weir described is today's, of bundled brushwood on a concrete base laid in 1950, on a river 43 m wide, larger than a hamlet's brook. The building each March from bundles of mateba-shii (an evergreen oak) is what the staff saw in 2016-2018, and the report does not say the early weir was rebuilt every year, so a yearly rebuild in the Edo period is our inference from a weir made of plants, not the source's statement. It is a magazine feature by the editorial staff and names no record for the early history, and it gives no date for the other weirs of plants it mentions. It is about Japan only.

Used for: an early-Edo irrigation weir said to have been plants woven onto wooden stakes, rebuilt of brushwood each year (water)
