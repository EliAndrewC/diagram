F. H. King, Farmers of Forty Centuries, or Permanent Agriculture in China, Korea and Japan (1911; Project Gutenberg; https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5350/pg5350-images.html)

What it is: The travel notes of an American soil scientist who toured the farms of China, Korea and Japan in 1909 and wrote down what he saw and what local informants told him, published after his death in 1911.

Why it applies, and its limits: King saw China in the last years of the Qing, before 1912, so his notes observe a countryside not yet industrialized (the book gives no year for the voyage, but dates observations on the trip to 1909). But the lower Yangzi he saw was the hinterland of treaty-port Shanghai, with commercial hatcheries selling chicks and an egg trade exporting to Japan, so the household flock is an old practice seen inside a partly modern economy. Its limits: what it says of chickens is hearsay, the word of Rev. R. A. Haden, of the Elizabeth Blake hospital near Soochow (Suzhou), whom King also calls Dr. Haden, in the lower Yangzi, one of the most commercial regions of China, and not King's own count; it is one traveler's short visit; and it says outright that there were no statistics on China's poultry, so "nearly every family" is an impression, not a figure.

Used for: that nearly every family in the lower Yangzi's cities and villages kept a few chickens in 1909 (homesteads)
