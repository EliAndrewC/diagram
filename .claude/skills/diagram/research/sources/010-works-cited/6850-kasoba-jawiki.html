ja.wikipedia 火葬場 ("crematorium", title translated from the Japanese by this project) (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%81%AB%E8%91%AC%E5%A0%B4)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the kasoba (火葬場), the crematory, covering the history of cremation grounds in Japan and their relations with the neighborhoods around them, including the Edo-period record for Asakusa and Shitaya.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives what our maps take about cremation grounds: that Edo's temples burned the dead in their own precincts, and that the shogunate moved those precinct crematoria together to a designated site near the Kozukappara execution ground, at the city's edge, in 1667 after the smoke reached a shogunal grave visit (for this the article cites the 1735 Zoku Edo sunago and Asaka and Yagisawa's 1983 monograph Kasoba); and the forms of the fire bed - most grounds open-air to the end of Edo, with a roofed bed and a walled snow-country hut appearing around mid-Edo. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary whose substance elsewhere is modern facilities and regulation; that its passages on the fire-bed forms and their mid-Edo dating carry no footnote of their own, so they are the article's word, likely drawn from Asaka and Yagisawa's Kasoba (1983), which it lists but which we have not read; that it describes Japan only, and says cremation before Meiji was not the national norm - common where Jodo Shinshu followers were many and in crowded cities, while elsewhere, and among the ruling class after about 1800, the dead were buried - so a place on our maps need not have a cremation ground at all; and that the episode is the shogunal capital's - twenty-odd temple crematoria in two wards, moved as a group by government order, in a city of a million - so a provincial town's one or two burning grounds is an inference from it rather than a reading of it.

Used for: by ~1650 the precinct crematoria of some twenty-odd temples in Asakusa and Shitaya; ~20 temple crematoria moved to the Kozukappara area in 1667 after smoke troubled a shogunal visit; the 1873 decree banning cremation over smoke and odor (repealed 1875); the roofed fire bed and the snow-country hut dated to around mid-Edo, and most grounds open-air until the end of Edo (religion-and-death)
