Ma, Brandt, Seipel and Ma, "Simple agents - complex emergent path systems: Agent-based modelling of pedestrian movement", Environment and Planning B: Urban Analytics and City Science (2023) - the version of record, open in the University of Gävle repository (https://hig.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1773446/FULLTEXT01); the publisher's page for DOI 10.1177/23998083231184884 refuses every automated fetcher

What it is: A peer-reviewed paper in Environment and Planning B by Lin Ma, S. Anders Brandt, Stefan Seipel and Ding Ma, which grows a network of desire paths from an agent-based model whose walkers aim straight at their destination and wear the ground as they cross it, and which reviews what is known about how a pedestrian picks a route. The published version is deposited openly in the University of Gävle's repository.

Why it applies, and its limits: It supplies the one thing our lane-bending rule needed and had been carrying as a guess: that a walker prefers the route that turns least. The paper reports it as Hillier and Iida's finding, that pedestrian movement across four areas of London tracks an angular measure of route choice because walkers have "angular preferences of fewest turns or least angle changes", and states the same preference again in its own agent, which keeps facing its destination and ends on the least detoured line. Its limits are that the turn preference is Hillier and Iida's 2005 result as this paper reports it rather than a page we have read ourselves; that the study is a simulation whose walkers are the modeler's abstraction, checked against modern campus and urban ground rather than village lanes; and that its subject is how a path network emerges, not how a premodern hamlet was laid out.

Used for: that a walker minimizes the number and severity of turns - which is why a lane runs straight between corners and a switchback within a few paces is a drawing artifact rather than a path
