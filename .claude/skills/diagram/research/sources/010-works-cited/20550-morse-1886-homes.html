morse-1886-homes

Edward S. Morse, Japanese Homes and Their Surroundings, Harper & Brothers, New York, 1889 printing, preface dated November 1885 (Project Gutenberg; https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/52868)

What it is: An American zoologist's illustrated account of the Japanese house, its rooms, gardens and approaches, written from what he saw and sketched in Japan, where he had been professor of zoology at the University of Tokio, as the title page names him.

Why it applies, and its limits: It describes how the houses of small villages stood along their road and how a crowded village reached the houses behind its street, and what a house's outer walls were made of and how its veranda and entrance were shut at night. Its limits: its walls and shutters are of Japan's houses in general, not said to be farmhouses', and of those walls the bark-slab one it places "in the southern provinces" and among "the poorer houses", and outside plaster it calls "not of a durable nature"; the one sentence it carries on farmers' houses' walls - plank walls on Hachijo where "on the mainland it is usual to have plastered wattles" - is Dickins and Satow's, quoted; it is one man's observation from his years in Japan before the 1885 preface, and it does not say when the villages he saw were built; and its crowded case is a single sentence that calls the alleys "the narrowest" but gives no width or count; its crowded case is Enoshima, a shrine island near Yokohama hemmed in by hills, not a farming village on open ground.

Used for: alleys to the houses behind a crowded village's street (Ways); board, plaster and bark outer walls (Homesteads); the veranda and entrance closed by storm shutters at night (Estates and other compounds)
