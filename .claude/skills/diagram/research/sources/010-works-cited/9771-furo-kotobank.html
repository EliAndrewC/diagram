furo-kotobank

Kotobank, 風呂 (Furo) - Kaitei Shinpan Sekai Daihyakka Jiten (in Japanese; https://kotobank.jp/word/%E9%A2%A8%E5%91%82-127856)

What it is: The Kotobank page for the bath, carrying among other entries the Heibonsha world encyclopedia's (revised new edition, 2007) history of bathing in Japan by the architectural historian Tamai Tetsuo, from the steam baths of temples through the public baths of the cities to the home bath of the late Edo period.

Why it applies, and its limits: It says who had a bath of their own in the Edo period: the upper-rank samurai's shoin-style residence had a yudono, while middle and lower samurai and ordinary people washed from a tub or used the public bath, and it says what made a home bath in a city costly - digging a well, and the fuel - calling it a great luxury, though it gives no figure. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary covering the whole country and period in a sentence; that its sentence on who had a yudono speaks of the cities (Kyoto, Osaka, Edo and the castle towns), not of villages; that before the late Edo period the word furo meant a steam bath so the page's terms shift with the era; that the late-Edo home bath it describes was a tub (the sue-buro), and the same article says a room set aside for bathing was still rare in the Edo period, a farmhouse's tub often standing unpartitioned in a corner of the earth-floored doma; and that it says nothing of where in a samurai house or compound the yudono stood - the only baths it places are the farmhouse's (in the doma and, in the Yamakawa dictionary's entry on the same page, near the stable or privy so the used water could go to the compost) and the Heian noble house's (a tub set in a partitioned corridor, with no fixed bath room).

Used for: the yudono of upper-rank samurai houses and the lack of one below them; the cost of a home bath; the spread of home baths late in the Edo period (buildings)
