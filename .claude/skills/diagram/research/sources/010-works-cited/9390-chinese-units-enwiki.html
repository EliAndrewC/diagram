en.wikipedia, Chinese units of measurement (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_units_of_measurement)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on traditional Chinese units, with a table giving, dynasty by dynasty, the length in meters of the foot (chi), of the pace (bu) at five and at six feet, and of the li.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the page read that turns the paces of a sixth-century treatise into meters, and it gives the two reckonings of the pace side by side. Its table has a Wei-Sui row (220-266 and 581-618) and nothing for the centuries between, so a foot for the 540s is carried across a gap the table leaves - and probably carried short: zh.wikipedia's own table (中国度量衡, itself unsourced by dynasty) gives the northern foot of the Qimin yaoshu's time as about 29.6 cm against this row's 25.5 cm, which would make feet reckoned from the row some 14% small. It gives the pace at five and at six feet without saying which held when. Its mu (614.4 square meters) and qing are from a separate table of the units promulgated in 1915, which the article says were based directly on the Qing definitions: the Qing builder's foot of 32 cm, a late-imperial measure, not an earlier dynasty's. A Northern Song area in mu put through them comes out about 8% larger than one reckoned from the article's own Song row (240 square paces of 1.54 m, about 569 square meters). The article gives no Ming or Qing mu of its own; its Ming foot of 0.3008-0.3190 m would make a mu of about 543 to 611 square meters, so the 1915 mu puts a Ming or early-Qing area such as the Bu nongshu's at the top of that range. The Ming row is itself a range (0.3008-0.3190 m). Its length table rests on one citation, but for a few Qin and Han li figures, Schinz's The Magic Square (1996), a book on the planning of ancient Chinese cities rather than a study of measures, and the article itself notes that it omits the several kinds of foot in use in some periods - for the Ming and Qing, the tailor's, the land-surveyor's and the builder's - so its Ming and Qing rows cannot say which foot a pond or a field was measured in, and fields, measured by the land-surveyor's foot and in Jiangnan by local mu, may not match either its rows or the 1915 mu.

Used for: the pace as five or six feet, and the Wei-Sui foot, to put the Qimin yaoshu bed in feet (Outside the walls); the Northern Song foot and pace, and the 1915 mu and qing, to put a pauper ground's area in acres (Religion and the dead); that the states kept their own measures until the Qin, so a classical width in chi assumes a foot (Fields); the pace as five or six feet and the Wei-Sui foot, to put the Qimin yaoshu's two-pace spacing of timber trees in meters (Vegetation and terrain); the Ming foot and pace, to put the Nongzheng quanshu's fry, middle and big ponds in feet and meters (Field archetypes); the 1915 mu, to put the Bu nongshu's garden of one mu to ten people in square meters (Homesteads)
