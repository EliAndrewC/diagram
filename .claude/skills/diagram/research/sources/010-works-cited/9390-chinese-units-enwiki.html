en.wikipedia, Chinese units of measurement (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_units_of_measurement)

What it is: The English Wikipedia article on traditional Chinese units, with a table giving, dynasty by dynasty, the length in meters of the foot (chi), of the pace (bu) at five and at six feet, and of the li.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the page read that turns the paces of a sixth-century treatise into meters, and it gives the two reckonings of the pace side by side. Its table has a Wei-Sui row (220-266 and 581-618) and nothing for the centuries between, so a foot for the 540s is carried across a gap the table leaves - and probably carried short: zh.wikipedia's own table (中国度量衡, itself unsourced by dynasty) gives the northern foot of the Qimin yaoshu's time as about 29.6 cm against this row's 25.5 cm, which would make feet reckoned from the row some 14% small. It gives the pace at five and at six feet without saying which held when. Its mu (614.4 square meters) and qing are from a separate table of the units promulgated in 1915, a Republican standard built on a 32 cm foot, not any dynasty's measure: a Northern Song area in mu put through them comes out about 8% larger than one reckoned from the article's own Song row (240 square paces of 1.54 m, about 569 square meters). The Ming row is itself a range (0.3008-0.3190 m). Its length table rests on a single citation, Schinz's The Magic Square (1996), a book on the planning of ancient Chinese cities rather than a study of measures, and the article itself notes that it omits the several kinds of foot in use in some periods.

Used for: the pace as five or six feet, and the Wei-Sui foot, to put the Qimin yaoshu bed in feet (Outside the walls); the Northern Song foot and pace, and the 1915 mu and qing, to put a pauper ground's area in acres (Religion and the dead); that the states kept their own measures until the Qin, so a classical width in chi assumes a foot (Fields); the pace as five or six feet and the Wei-Sui foot, to put the Qimin yaoshu's two-pace spacing of timber trees in meters (Vegetation and terrain); the Ming foot and pace, to put the Nongzheng quanshu's fry, middle and big ponds in feet and meters (Field archetypes)
