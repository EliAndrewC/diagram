edago-ja

"枝郷" (edago, branch village), ja.wikipedia (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%9E%9D%E9%83%B7)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article 枝郷 (edago, "branch village"), on the Edo-period relationship between an original village, the hongo, and the new settlement that split off from it. It explains that the branch's assessed yield was in most cases apportioned through the parent village's headman, and that there were nonetheless branch villages with their own headman and group elders, treated on a par with the parent.

Why it applies, and its limits: Our maps have to decide whether a small outlying hamlet has any officer of its own or is administered from the village down the road, and this is the attested form for exactly that, including the exceptions - which is what lets us mark our setting's flat rule as a deliberate departure rather than pass it off as history. Its limits are serious for a source doing that work: it is a short encyclopedia stub with no citations at all, and Japanese Wikipedia's own editors have flagged it as unverified and possibly original research, so the distribution it asserts ("in many cases ... but there were also ...") rests on nothing a reader can follow; the dictionary entry it links at Kotobank is the closer public source. It describes a particular Japanese tax and registration system, so we use it for the shape of the arrangement and never for a rate or a proportion.

Used for: the hongo/edago parent-and-branch settlement form, its commonness ("in most cases" the branch's assessed yield came from the parent's headman), AND the attested exceptions - branch hamlets with their own shoya and kumigashira - which is what makes Rokugan's flat "a hamlet has no headman" a deliberate deviation rather than a finding
