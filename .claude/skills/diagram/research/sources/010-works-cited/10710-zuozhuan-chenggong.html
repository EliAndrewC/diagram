zuozhuan-chenggong

Zuo Zhuan (春秋左氏傳), the chapters of Duke Cheng of Lu, second year (589 BCE), on Wikisource (in classical Chinese; https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%98%A5%E7%A7%8B%E5%B7%A6%E6%B0%8F%E5%82%B3/%E6%88%90%E5%85%AC)

What it is: The public-domain text of the ancient chronicle's account of the peace after the battle of An, in which the victor, Jin, demands that the defeated state, Qi, turn all its field rows to run east - which, Qi's envoy says, would serve only Jin's war chariots - and the envoy answers that the early kings laid the rows out by what suited the land. The event is dated 589 BCE; the chronicle was compiled perhaps two centuries later, and its speeches are the chronicler's.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the one read East Asian passage on which way neighboring fields' rows ran and what decided it: the land, as the early kings laid the fields out by what suited it, not one order imposed without regard to the land (here, for an army's chariots), with rows running one of two ways, south or east, in the envoy's reading of a line of the Book of Songs. It is a diplomatic speech of the sixth century BCE about the fields of the North China Plain, not a farming manual, and it names no angle, crop or field size; the rows it means are the ridges and furrows of that plain's dry fields, and it says nothing of paddy, whose rows are transplanting lines inside a levelled basin; it was a classic every educated reader in China and Japan knew, which makes it the principle an East Asian reader would have held, not a survey of any later village's fields.

Used for: field rows laid out by what suits the land, running south or east, and a demand that all run one way refused as disregarding the land (fields)
