New York Almanack, "The Big Boom: Old Hudson River Chain Recalls Logging History" (https://www.newyorkalmanack.com/2016/10/the-big-boom-old-hudson-river-chain-recalls-logging-history/)

What it is: An article by Glenn Pearsall, "The Big Boom: Old Hudson River Chain Recalls Logging History", published on 18 October 2016 in New York Almanack, an online magazine of New York State history and natural history. It describes the log boom that held floated timber on the Hudson above Glens Falls - hewn timbers bolted together raftwise, the groups joined end to end by chains running over friction rollers, each end anchored to a heavy bank abutment - and the 1859 break that scattered logs forty miles downriver.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for how a boom is built and held, which is what the log booms on our timber-town rivers are drawn from: a floating fence across a current, jointed so it rides the water, tied to solid ground at both banks. Its limits: it is popular history rather than a technical or scholarly account, with the scholarly source one step further out; it is about industrial-era logging in North America rather than East Asia, and its fittings - bolted timber groups, chains run over iron rollers - are nineteenth-century hardware a premodern river works would replace with lashings and rope, so what transfers is the principle of anchoring to fixed ground and not the fastenings; and the Hudson operation was far larger than anything at our settlements, so we take the arrangement from it and not the dimensions.

Used for: boom construction - hewn timbers bolted raftwise, chained end to end over friction rollers to bank abutments; the 1859 break that scattered logs 40 miles
