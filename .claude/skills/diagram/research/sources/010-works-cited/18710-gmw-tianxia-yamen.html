gmw-tianxia-yamen

Wanyan Shaoyuan, 奇文共赏八字墙 ("Strange writings on the splayed walls, shared", title translated from the Chinese by this project), an excerpt from his book 天下衙门 (China Archives Press), on Guangming Online, May 1, 2006 (in Chinese; https://www.gmw.cn/02sz/2006-05/01/content_437949.htm)

What it is: A book excerpt by Wanyan Shaoyuan, a Chinese writer on the history of officialdom, reprinted by Guangming Online, on the splayed walls that flanked the gate of a Chinese government office and what was posted on them in the Ming and Qing dynasties, with dated examples drawn from period writings such as Shen Bang's Wanshu zaji of the Ming.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a modern popular history, not a study with notes, but it places the posting of edicts, the county magistrate's own notices and bans, verdicts and tax-arrears lists on the office's gate walls. Its Ming support is thin: it quotes the monthly edicts of 1519 from Shen Bang's Wanshu zaji, which the Ming emperor gave out orally and the people received at the county gate, but that they hung on the splayed walls is the author's own framing, and his passage on verdicts and tax-arrears lists speaks of county offices of old without a date. His one dated official posting at a county office's gate is Song (the Green Sprouts law, "posted at the county gate"). His one dated Qing example is Shanghai in 1853, a notice on the county office's wall that he does not tie to the gate walls. It speaks of Chinese offices generally, not of any one county, and of walls rather than a freestanding board; the later use of the screen wall facing the gate is undated, and its Kangxi-era notice of 1686 illustrates how notices were signed, not where that one was posted.

Used for: that a Chinese county office posted its edicts, notices, bans and verdicts on the walls at its own gate in the Ming and Qing (buildings)
