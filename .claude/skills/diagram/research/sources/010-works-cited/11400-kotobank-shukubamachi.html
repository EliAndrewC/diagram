kotobank-shukubamachi

Kotobank, 宿場町 ("post town", title translated from the Japanese by this project) - entries from the Digital Daijisen, the Seisen-ban Nihon Kokugo Daijiten, the Kaitei Shinpan Sekai Daihyakka Jiten and the Yamakawa Nihonshi Shojiten (in Japanese; https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%AE%BF%E5%A0%B4%E7%94%BA-528292)

What it is: The Kotobank page for the post town of the Edo highways, carrying two dictionary definitions and one encyclopedia article in three signed sections (by the historian Kodama Kota, the architectural historian Tamai Tetsuo and the historical geographer Yamada Yasuhiko): on the station's institutions, on its form and plan (the long strip along the road, the relay office and lords' inn at the center, the road's width and channel, the strip lots and the gardens behind them, the bent entrances) and on its size on the Tokaido, with the Yamakawa historical dictionary's entry beside them.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the compact scholarly statement of what a Japanese road town looked like and how large it was, written by a historian, an architectural historian and a historical geographer, which is what a town map strung along a road needs. It is a set of tertiary summaries of Tokugawa Japan's official stations, strongest for the five great highways; it gives ranges and typical forms, not any one town's measured plan, and says nothing of China. Its size (300 to 600 houses, 30 to 60 inns) is the Tokaido's, the busiest road, and its inn counts are from an 1843 survey; it says stations off the great highways were smaller, so for a town on a lesser road the figure is an upper bound. The busy road's look, two-storey plank-roofed inns filling the frontage, is a late-Edo form.

Used for: the post town's form, size, center and ends, its lots and gardens, the two looks by traffic, and the street town that grows back streets (towns)
