mizu-no-bunka-28-suisha

Mizkan Water Culture Center, 水の文化 no. 28, 働く水車が伝える水のポテンシャル ("The potential of water that working water wheels convey", title translated from the Japanese by this project), with Kosaka Katsunobu (https://www.mizu.gr.jp/kikanshi/no28/03.html)

What it is: A feature in the Mizkan Water Culture Center's magazine, built on an interview with Kosaka Katsunobu, a Tachikawa primary-school teacher who researched the Tamagawa aqueduct and leads the water-wheel and mortar section (水車と臼分科会) of the Japan Industrial Archaeology Society (産業考古学会), on the history of power water wheels in Japan and a working mill in Mitaka.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the fullest readable account of who built a village mill, why and where: rice pounding driven by brewing and castle-town demand from the mid-Edo period, mills in the villages round Edo's highway gates, a permit needed for each on shogunal land, 33 on the Musashino plateau in 1788, owners mostly wealthy farmers and village officials, and one mill 200 m below the headman's. It is a magazine piece about the Edo hinterland, the most mill-dense country in Japan, and most of its figures are the interviewee's rather than footnoted (the 1788 count comes from the aqueduct record 上水記). Its mortar counts are all Meiji-era or later: 10 pounding mortars and 1 grinding stone at the Mitaka mill in 1903, the iron mortars there today, and 59 at Yodobashi after an 1887 rebuild. None of them is an Edo inventory. Its ownership pattern is Japanese: the article says, at second hand, that in China, as in Europe, mills were held by the powerful rather than shared, so it cannot stand in for the Chinese side of the setting.

Used for: what a village mill was for, who owned it, how many stood on one plateau, and a mill's mortars (water)
