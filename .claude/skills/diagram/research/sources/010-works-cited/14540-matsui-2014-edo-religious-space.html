matsui-2014-edo-religious-space

Matsui Keisuke, 「Discussions on the Religious Space of Edo City : The Landscape and Functions of Temples and Shrines」 (the paper's own English title; original: 「寺社分布と機能からみた江戸の宗教空間」), Journal of Geography (Chigaku Zasshi) 123(4): 451-471, 2014 (https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jgeography/123/4/123_123.451/_pdf)

What it is: A peer-reviewed geography review article (総説 - a synthesis of earlier work, not a new survey) on how Edo's temples and shrines were distributed and what they did for the city, drawing on the shogunate's surveys and on earlier studies of Edo's temple gate towns (Nishiyama 1983, Kaneyuki 2000, Ito 1995).

Why it applies, and its limits: It says Edo's new gate towns had rest teahouses and souvenir shops from the first half of the seventeenth century, gives 440 gate-town wards as the number the shogunate put under the town magistrates in the Enkyō years (mid-eighteenth), and prints the 22 households on the gate-town land of an average temple of about 1,000 tsubo in 1843, with their trades, in houses about 3 ken deep. It is about Edo, the largest city of its day and the one under the most pressure for land. It says (after Ito 1995) that the townhouse built on a temple's own land was not generally found in Kyoto or Osaka. But it also says (after Yamachika 1991) that in late-Edo Kyoto, temples rented out parts of their grounds where tenants built houses, shops and eating-houses, and that the gate town of Osu Kannon in Nagoya did the same - so renting out temple ground was wider than Edo, even if Edo's form was not. Its figures are second-hand: the 440 wards from Nishiyama 1983, and the 1843 list (Kyozenji temple in Azabu) from Kaneyuki 2000. The one temple's list is one ward in one year.

Used for: the trades and the size of an ordinary Edo temple's gate town (religion and death)
