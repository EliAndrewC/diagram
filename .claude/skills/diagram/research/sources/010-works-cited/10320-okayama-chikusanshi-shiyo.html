okayama-chikusanshi-shiyo

Okayama-ken Chikusanshi Hensan Iinkai (Okayama Prefecture Livestock History Editorial Committee), Okayama-ken chikusanshi (History of livestock in Okayama prefecture), 1980, web edition hosted by the Okayama Prefecture Livestock Association, part 1, ch. 1, sec. 1, "Keeping livestock" (in Japanese; https://okayama.lin.gr.jp/tosyo/history/1-1-1.htm)

What it is: The web edition of a prefectural history of livestock farming, compiled by an editorial committee of the prefecture and its livestock bodies and published in 1980, in its overview of livestock up to the end of the domain era: which beasts were kept, where they were bred, how they were kept, and who owned them, with figures for Bizen in the mid-seventeenth century and the cattle-lending of the Ando headman's household of Bitchu in 1818-43.

Why it applies, and its limits: It describes cattle-keeping in Edo-period villages of one western Japanese wet-rice region, where the draft beast was the ox, and it states flatly that the cattle shed was mostly inside the house. It is a compiled institutional history of 1980 that gives no source for each sentence and no date within the Edo period for the in-house shed. It covers Okayama alone. It also says that from about 1704-10 only about half of the farm households of Bizen and Mimasaka kept an ox or horse at all, and fewer later, so the in-house shed was the rule for the households that had a beast, not a feature of every homestead. And it and it names the lending arrangement (mayasaki) without describing where the lent beast stood, so it says nothing about a shed shared by several households either way.

Used for: that the cattle shed was mostly in the house, that most beasts were their keepers' own, and that large owners lent cattle out to tenants (homesteads)
