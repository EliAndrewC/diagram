Hokuriku Regional Development Bureau (MLIT), Aga River office, "The Aga River and traditional river engineering" (in Japanese; https://www.hrr.mlit.go.jp/agagawa/river/history_gijyutsu05.html)

What it is: The transcript of a lecture, "Traditional river engineering and its modern evaluation", given on 19 July 2001 by Chino Yasuaki, a lecturer in civil engineering at Nihon University, to the Aga River office's council on traditional river engineering, and published by that office of the national river administration. It describes the two standard pre-Meiji tools: the ushi, a timber trestle weighted with stone baskets, and the waku, a square mortise-jointed timber frame filled with stones thrown in from above. Its figures come from Edo-period manuals (Sanpo jikata taisei, Josui ki) and from Doboku ko yoroku, a Meiji-era compilation of traditional methods.

Why it applies, and its limits: It describes what the Edo-period frames were and says weirs were built of them, of stone and of reed facing. Its weir passage is about DIVERSION weirs on large rivers built to work in flood, not a village's intake on a brook; its definition of the square frame is given under groynes; its dimensions are for river-scale frames; and its Hamura weir is the intake of the Tamagawa Aqueduct, which carried Edo's city water, on a large river - built of the same frames, but at a city's scale, not a village's. It is a modern specialist's overview of period manuals and drawings, given as a talk, not a referenced paper.

Used for: that Edo-period weirs were built of timber frames, stone and reed facing; what a square timber frame (waku) was; the Hamura intake weir built of frames (water)
