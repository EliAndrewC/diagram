《周禮·地官司徒》 (the Zhouli, the Ministry of Education chapter, whose Suiren section orders the countryside), Chinese Wikisource transcription (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%91%A8%E7%A6%AE/%E5%9C%B0%E5%AE%98%E5%8F%B8%E5%BE%92)

What it is: The chapter of the Rites of Zhou on the officers of the land, in a Wikisource transcription of the primary text; its Suiren (遂人) section lays out the countryside as a ladder of channels, one between the allotments (fu) of single farmers, then one for every ten, hundred, thousand and ten thousand, each with a path or road along it.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a classical text, compiled long before the setting's period, that names a finite ladder of field channels and puts a path on every rung, so the ladder shape and the way along a channel are premodern ideas, not modern design vocabulary. It is an ideal scheme of the state, compiled around the late Warring States or early Han (roughly the 3rd to 2nd century BCE) for an idealized Zhou, not a survey of real fields, and it says nothing of how far any real countryside followed it. Its countryside is the grain land of the North China royal domain, not paddy; the rice officer (稻人) passage of the same chapter is the one on paddy water. It gives no widths. The Kaogongji (匠人, a separate Han-era work that stands in for the lost Winter Office section) gives widths for channels of the same names, but on a different spacing (a 溝 between nine-farmer jing, a 洫 between squares ten li on a side, a 澮 between squares a hundred li on a side), so those widths do not belong to this ladder rung for rung.

Used for: a finite ladder of field channels with a path along each rung (water)
