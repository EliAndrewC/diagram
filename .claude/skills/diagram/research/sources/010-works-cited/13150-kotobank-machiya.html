kotobank-machiya

Kotobank, 町家 ("townhouse", title translated from the Japanese by this project) - entries from the Digital Daijisen, the Seisen-ban Nihon Kokugo Daijiten the Kaitei Shinpan Sekai Daihyakka Jiten and the Britannica Kokusai Daihyakka Jiten Shokomoku Jiten (in Japanese; https://kotobank.jp/word/町家)

What it is: The Kotobank page for the machiya, the Japanese townsman's house, carrying dictionary definitions, a short unsigned Britannica entry on its history, and a signed article by Suzuki Mitsuru in Heibonsha's Sekai Daihyakka Jiten on its usual form (the ridge parallel to the street, a low storage story, the gable-fronted variant) and its regional looks: tile and plastered walls in the west, board roofs weighted with stones and exposed posts in the east, and the plastered storehouse style that spread in late-Edo Edo.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a signed scholarly encyclopedia's summary of what a Japanese town's houses looked like, with the east-west contrast a roof knob needs. It generalizes across the whole country and period and gives no counts, so it says what forms existed, not how common each was in any one town; its regional contrast is a broad tendency read from surviving houses, most of them late, and the page's own Britannica entry names Kyoto's and Takayama's townhouses as exposed-post, so a western town's houses need not be plastered; the plastered Edo front has no surviving example in Tokyo and is shown only by Kawagoe houses built after the fire of 1877; and it says nothing of China.

Used for: the low storage story, the roofs by region, the plastered front in Edo, and the ridge along or the gable to the street (towns); the townhouse's rooms in a row along its earth-floored passage (buildings)
