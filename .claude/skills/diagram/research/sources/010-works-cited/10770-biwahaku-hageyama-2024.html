Lake Biwa Museum, 「The causes of the satoyama of the Koto district of Shiga turning into bald hills in the Edo to Meiji periods, made clear」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「滋賀県湖東地域で、江戸～明治時代に里山が「はげ山」化した要因を明らかにしました。」) (https://www.biwahaku.jp/study/research/2024/06/1b5ff403a81ce34c22f878a5c7aaf1ffdaa743a5.html)

What it is: The Shiga prefectural Lake Biwa Museum's 2024 summary of a paper by Watanabe Keiichi, 「Bald hills and the village's management of its resources」 (translated from the Japanese by this project; original: 「はげ山とむらの資源管理」), in 『日本民俗学』 316 (2023), pages 35-70, built on the roughly 1,500 early-modern and modern documents of the village of Minami-Tsuda (Omihachiman), its early-Meiji cadastral map, old photographs and interviews.

Why it applies, and its limits: It measures, for one village's common hill, how far the stripping went: drawn as bald on the early-Meiji map, few usable trees, so little brush that over eight tenths of the brush the village burned in a year was bought in, and the shortage traceable to the Genroku era (1688-1704); and it finds the cause in ordinary, permitted brush cutting rather than a breakdown of the rules. It is one village in Omi, a crowded province next to the Kinai, and the bare state is measured as of early Meiji, at the very end of the premodern period. How bare the hill got was that village's own degree: the paper ties it to how many people cut brush, since the hill grew back once the village's population fell in the Taisho era and later. So it shows what a crowded village could do to its hill, not what every village's hill looked like. It is read through a museum's summary rather than the paper itself, and the author hedges the bare state and its cause as a reading of the documents.

Used for: how bare a village's common hill became under brush cutting (vegetation)
