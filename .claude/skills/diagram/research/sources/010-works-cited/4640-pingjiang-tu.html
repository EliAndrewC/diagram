pingjiang-tu

Pingjiang tu (平江图), the 1229 stone-carved plan of Suzhou, and its scholarship (https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%B9%B3%E6%B1%9F%E5%9B%BE)

What it is: The Chinese Wikipedia article on the Pingjiang tu (平江图), the plan of Southern Song Suzhou carved in stone in 1229 - the oldest surviving city plan of its kind in China - whose references are mostly web pages - a library page, two news sites, a local-history book and one 1982 urban-planning textbook (Tongji University's 中国城市建设史), and whose bridge count is footnoted to a news site's page. It describes the walled rectangle, long on its north-south axis, the broad moat, and the five gates, each a paired land gate and water gate.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is, by the article's account, the oldest surviving city plan in China, and the one dated plan of a whole Chinese prefectural city this project has read, and we take from it three things our city maps decide: that a walled city could be a long rectangle rather than a square, that a canal city's gates come in land-and-water pairs, and that such a city's canals were crossed by bridges in the hundreds (the plan marks 359). Its limits are that we read the carving through an encyclopedia summary rather than the object or a study of it; that it is one southern Chinese prefectural city at one date, a larger and wetter place than most of ours; and that a carved plan is a representation, so what it draws is not automatically what stood.

Used for: the walled rectangle long on its north-south axis; five gates, each a paired land gate and water gate; the plan's bridge count (Ways)
