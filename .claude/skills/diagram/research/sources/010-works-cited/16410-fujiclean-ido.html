Fuji Clean, 水の話: 井戸 ("stories of water: the well", title translated from the Japanese by this project) (in Japanese; https://www.fujiclean.co.jp/fujiclean/story/vol09/part102.html)

What it is: A popular column on the website of a maker of household water-treatment tanks, on how well-digging spread in the Edo period and how castle towns got their water.

Why it applies, and its limits: It reports, from a book of 1814, that digging a well once cost more than a samurai household could pay until a cheaper boring method brought the price down, and that most wells were shared. It is a company's column, not a history, and its one document is named without a title (other retellings identify it as Ogawa Kendō's memoir 塵塚談 and say its price was for a bored artesian well, 掘抜井戸). It speaks of Edo, where shallow wells on filled-in marsh gave salty water. So its 200 ryō, and the 3 ryō after, are the price of a deep bored well in Edo's low ground, from one anecdote, not the cost of an ordinary shallow well in a village or on higher ground. It gives no date for when the price fell, only that the book of 1814 reports it. Its guess that the cheaper method was Kazusa boring is its own, and that method took its known form in the Meiji period. It speaks of Japan only.

Used for: what a private well cost, and when it came within a samurai's reach (cities/government)
