hayashi-2007-shimomaruko

林和光 (Hayashi Kazumitsu), 近世・多摩川における河川氾濫と下流域農村に関する歴史人口学的分析 - 平川家文書からみた荏原郡・六郷領・下丸子村 ("A historical-demographic analysis of river floods and a lower-basin farming village on the early modern Tama River: Shimomaruko village as seen in the Hirakawa family papers", title translated), a 2007 research report published by the Tokyu Foundation (in Japanese; https://foundation.tokyu.co.jp/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/G172.pdf)

What it is: A study of one village on the lower Tama River from its headman's papers, written for a Tokyu Foundation research grant by a local resident who is not an academic historian (the title page names him deputy director of a road-traffic information foundation), and not peer-reviewed: the land registers of successive surveys rebuilt into the areas of paddy and dry field, the floods that moved the river, and the households.

Why it applies, and its limits: It sets a floodplain village long described as a typical paddy village against its own registers, which show its dry field at about a fifth of its cropland in the Kanbun survey of 1663 (about 6 cho beside about 20 of paddy, read off its chart), about equal to its paddy by the Genroku survey (1688-1704), and larger than it after the Kyoho survey (1716-1736), when its text says the dry field grew about 1.8 times. Its limits: one village near Edo, and the report's own reasons for the dry field's growth are the river's, not a market's - paddy lost to floods or turned to dry field as its soil declined, and new land taken from filled meander marsh and flood ground outside the levee under the shogunate's new-field policy - so its later share is a flood-prone village's, not a typical paddy village's; its areas are the taxed areas of the registers, read off a chart to about a cho, and the one number its text gives is that 1.8-fold growth; and of where the dry fields lay it says only that much of the new land was on the river side (outside the levee, on the old channel and its sandbars), and nothing of a row or of a homestead's own field.

Used for: the share of dry field in a floodplain paddy village (homesteads)
