kotobank-shikidai

Kotobank, 式台 (shikidai) (in Japanese; https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%BC%8F%E5%8F%B0-517766)

What it is: Kotobank's page for the word shikidai, gathering the entries of several Japanese dictionaries and encyclopedias - the Digital Daijisen, the Nihon Kokugo Daijiten and the Nipponica encyclopedia among them - with the Nihon Kokugo Daijiten's note on how the word's meaning moved from the Muromachi to the Edo period.

Why it applies, and its limits: It says what the genkan of a warrior's house was for - the Nihon Kokugo Daijiten's note names the projecting board floor where a palanquin was set down the genkan - and that the shikidai came to be the low board step where guests were bowed in and out. Its limits: it is a modern dictionary, defining words rather than describing a particular house, and the palanquin sense is the dictionary's own note rather than a quoted period text. The two senses belong to different times: the palanquin genkan, with the shikidai as the room where visitors were received, is the naming of the Muromachi period to the early Edo period, while the shikidai as the low step dates from about the middle of the Edo period, when the whole entrance had come to be called the genkan. It describes the houses of warriors - the Nipponica's example is the Ninomaru Palace of Nijo Castle - and not those of townsmen or farmers. It says nothing of what lay between the gate and the genkan; that the ground before the genkan was open is our inference from the palanquin, not the dictionary's statement. The page's modern housing and remodeling glossaries describe present-day houses and are not used.

Used for: the genkan as the projecting floor where a palanquin was set down, and the shikidai as the step where guests were bowed in and out (buildings)
