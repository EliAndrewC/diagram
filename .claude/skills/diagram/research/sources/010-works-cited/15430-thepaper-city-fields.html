thepaper-city-fields

Wei Zhou, 城市里的田园：都市为何离不开农村 ("Fields in the city: why the city cannot do without the countryside", translated from the Chinese by this project), The Paper, history column, 16 July 2016 (https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_1477443)

What it is: An essay in a Shanghai news site's history column written under the pen name 维舟, gathering evidence, from the Han to the twentieth century, that Chinese cities long held fields and gardens inside their walls and that city dwellers farmed them.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the readable page that says who worked the ground inside a Chinese city: its own residents. It is a popular essay arguing for gardens in today's cities, not a study; it quotes period texts, historians and travelers by title rather than giving sources in full, and its figures are reports passed on (Nanjing's three-fifths fields and waste land is what Akutagawa's guide told him in 1921). Much of its evidence is of war-emptied cities of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Its evidence before 1800 is of vegetables - officials' and great houses' plots, Tang offices' gardens, Song market gardeners, and Kaifeng's gardens lay around the capital rather than inside it; its rice inside a wall is Ming Beijing's Jishuitan and war-emptied Nanjing, so rice paddy within an ordinary, undamaged premodern city is our inference from it, not a thing it shows. It is about Chinese cities only, and notes that Japanese castle towns were laid out apart from the castle.
