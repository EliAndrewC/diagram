mukoyama-linear-borders

Mukoyama, Naosuke, "The Eastern cousins of European sovereign states? The development of linear borders in early modern Japan", European Journal of International Relations 29(2) (2023) 255-282 (https://doi.org/10.1177/13540661221133206)

What it is: An argument, in the leading European journal of international relations, that early modern Japan developed linear borders of its own rather than importing the idea. It gathers the evidence out of the Japanese historians - boundary disputes, the mounds and stones and trees domains built along agreed lines, the guardhouses that policed them, and the provincial maps the shogunate ordered five times between 1605 and 1835 - and reproduces two period boundary maps and photographs of markers still standing.

Why it applies, and its limits: No other readable source assembles the whole case for drawing a clan border as a LINE: the agreed line, the hundred-odd numbered mounds along 130 km between Morioka and Sendai, the guardhouse of 102 soldiers, the domain currency that stopped at the boundary, and the shogunate’s own instruction that borders be depicted unambiguously on the map. Three limits, each the author’s own admission. He reads the historians rather than the documents, so on any single number the record is at one remove. He states that the change was uneven - furthest advanced in the peripheral outside-lord domains, weakest around Kyoto and Nara, where dozens of lords held ground together - so a border near a capital has weaker warrant than one in the marches. And the paper is about Japan alone; it lists China among its open questions, so it licenses the Japanese half of this setting and not the Chinese one.

Used for: domains building a territorial order of agreed boundaries and mutual exclusion, and the line being drawn on a map as the authentic act
