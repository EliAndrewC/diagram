suido-ishizue-iseki

ARIC (Agricultural and Rural Integrated Information Center), Suido no Ishizue, "Taking water: the iseki" (in Japanese; https://suido-ishizue.jp/daichi/part2/04/03.html)

What it is: A page of "Daichi e no Kokuin" (Imprint on the Land), a popular history of Japan's land and water written in the agricultural-engineering profession and now on the web from ARIC, an association in that field (the site shows no author or date; a search result gives it as a 1988 book of an agricultural-engineering history study group, not confirmed on the page), explaining why irrigation weirs were built and how they developed, with captioned illustrations of a weir excavated at the Kodera site (Matsuyama) and a "grass weir" of stakes with kaya (thatching grass) woven between them, photographed still in use in Saga.

Why it applies, and its limits: It states the condition that decides between a bare intake and a weir, and it describes the earliest and simplest weirs, which were built on small streams - the scale of a hamlet's brook. It is a popular history from the modern irrigation profession, it gives no dates for most of its general statements, no dimensions, and no count of how many intakes took each form. Neither pictured weir is from the setting's era: the Kodera weir is an excavated work of the early Kofun period (about the 3rd-4th century), far earlier, and the grass weir is a present-day survival photographed in Saga, which the caption says is used where an agreement with users downstream forbids a permanent weir - a modern observation of an old form, not a dated record of it.

Used for: when a bare intake served and when a weir was needed; the earliest weirs, of nearby wood and stone, on small streams; the stake-and-kaya grass weir; the stake-and-log weir of the Kodera site (water)
