rots-2015-chinju-no-mori

Aike P. Rots, "Sacred Forests, Sacred Nation: The Shinto Environmentalist Paradigm and the Rediscovery of Chinju no Mori", Japanese Journal of Religious Studies 42/2 (2015), 205-233 (open-access PDF: https://nirc.nanzan-u.ac.jp/journal/6/article/1415/pdf/download)

What it is: A peer-reviewed article in the journal of the Nanzan Institute for Religion and Culture on how shrine groves came to be presented as Japan's sacred forests in the late twentieth century.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the picture of a shrine grove that the literature holds up - many shrines surrounded by small woods or flanked by forested mountains, the archetype "a clearly demarcated, broccoli-shaped patch" of natural broad-leaved forest - and, in the next sentence, corrects it: many real shrine woods are conifers planted in the premodern or modern period. Its limits are that it studies a modern discourse, not the groves themselves; that the patch is the literature's archetype, which the article is critical of, and a modern one - the image of ecologists writing from the late 1970s on (Miyawaki, Ueda), the phrase chinju no mori itself coined in the late nineteenth century - so it tells us how moderns picture a grove, not how people of our era did; that "broccoli-shaped" is the canopy seen from the side, and only "clearly demarcated" speaks to a grove's plan; that its correction lumps premodern and modern planting together with no count, and its dated examples are modern (Ise's forest planted in 1923, Meiji Jingu's in the 1920s), so it cannot show that groves had departed from the archetype before Meiji; and that it gives no measurements of a grove's plan.

Used for: the shape a shrine grove is thought of as having, and that real groves depart from it (religion-and-death.html).
