jinya-kotobank

Kotobank, 陣屋 - 百科事典マイペディア (Heibonsha), with excerpts from 世界大百科事典 (旧版); 日本大百科全書 (Nipponica), Murakami Tadashi (https://kotobank.jp/word/%E9%99%A3%E5%B1%8B-82659)

What it is: The Kotobank page for the jin'ya, carrying Heibonsha's concise encyclopedia (百科事典マイペディア; the 世界大百科事典 itself appears only as excerpts from its 代官, 大名 and 天領 articles) on the several kinds of seat the word covers, on the intendant's official residence also being called the daikan-jin'ya, on what stood inside such a compound - the residence, the office for public business, the staff houses, the rice and levy stores, the judgment yard and the gaol - and on the jin'ya-machi that grew around one; and Murakami Tadashi's Nipponica entry, which names the koya and nagaya of the tetsuki and tedai inside a daikan-jin'ya and says its boundary was usually a high white-plastered wall, a thicket, a ditch or a dry moat.

Why it applies, and its limits: It makes the identification our unwalled tax seat rests on a published one rather than this record's own guess, and it names the rice store inside the compound, which is the thing the fire posture says was fireproofed. Its limits are that it is a tertiary summary and that it describes Tokugawa Japan only, so the Chinese county seat our maps also draw on is not covered by it. Nor does it call the seat unwalled: it says a jin'ya had no high stone walls, that early ones had earthworks and moats like a castle, that they ranged from a bare office to a moated, turreted near-castle, and that a daikan-jin'ya was bounded by a high white wall, a thicket, a ditch or a dry moat - so it carries "no castle and no garrison", not "no wall". It names the rice store but says nothing of how it was built against fire.

Used for: the daikansho as the daikan-jin'ya, what its grounds held, and the jin'ya-machi that grew around one; the tetsuki and tedai housed in koya and nagaya inside the compound, and its walled boundary (buildings)
