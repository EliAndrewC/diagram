ja-tokyo-edo-yasai

JA Tokyo Chuo-kai (the Tokyo federation of agricultural cooperatives), 江戸東京野菜を語る ("Talking about Edo-Tokyo vegetables", title translated from the Japanese by this project) (in Japanese; https://www.tokyo-ja.or.jp/farm/edogatari/)

What it is: The introduction to the Tokyo farm cooperatives' oral-history series on the city's traditional vegetables, with a paragraph on how the land east and west of Edo was opened in the Edo period to feed the city.

Why it applies, and its limits: It sets side by side the two ways a great city's food ground came to be - reclaimed marsh and tideflat to the east, fed with the city's night soil, and new upland fields to the west - and how the produce went in, by handcart, ox cart and river boat. It is a modern farm-industry body's introductory paragraph, not a history, and it cites nothing: no figures, no distances, and one city - Edo, the largest city of its age at about a million people, so the scale of its night-soil trade and its ring of supply fields is a capital's, not a town's. And the western fields are a special case: the page says the Musashino upland had volcanic-ash soil, few rivers and no fields, and its farming grew once the Tamagawa aqueduct opened in 1653, so they are not a general pattern for upland new fields.

Used for: reclaimed ground east of Edo fed with the city's night soil and selling rice and vegetables in it; the western new fields and the carts and boats that carried their produce in (towns)
