bench-terrace-riser

FAO STI-portal and ICIMOD pages on traditional irrigated / rainfed paddy rice terraces, plus general bench-terracing references (https://sti-portal.fao.org/innovations/traditional-irrigated-rice-terraces)

What it is: A technology profile of traditional irrigated rice terraces on the Food and Agriculture Organization's Science, Technology and Innovation portal, compiled from survey data of WOCAT, the international database of soil and water conservation practices, and describing hand-built level bench terraces on steep slopes in Nepal, read alongside pages from ICIMOD and general references on bench terracing. It gives the dimensions of the structural face: risers 0.8 to 1.5 m high, their faces sloping at 80 to 160 percent on hillsides of 30 to 60 percent, earthen and compacted, protected by planted grasses, with stones worked in where available, with a small lip of 20 to 25 cm holding 10 to 15 cm of water on the platform.

Why it applies, and its limits: It supplies the physical distinction our terraced maps depend on, that the tall riser is a retaining wall holding up the soil while a separate low lip holds in the water, which is why micro-basins belong on a hillside and not on a valley floor. The clear limitation is place: these are earthen, grass-protected terraces in Nepal, so its 0.8 to 1.5 m is a low earthen riser, not the height of a stone-walled terrace; its figures come from the WOCAT record compiled by Ramanand Bhattarai, which the portal reprints, and the narrative sections around it are automatically compiled, so we use the reprinted record for the geometry of the structure and nothing from those sections.

Used for: the terrace riser as a structural retaining face (0.8-1.5 m, stone-faced where available, slope 80-160%) with water held by a separate 10-15 cm lip on the platform edge - the physical difference that makes micro-basins a terrace phenomenon and not a valley-floor one
