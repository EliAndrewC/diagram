Hong Kong Herbarium (AFCD), "An Overview of Fung Shui Woods in Hong Kong" (https://www.herbarium.gov.hk/en/special-topics/fung-shui-woods/an-overview-of-fung-shui-woods-in-hong-kong/index.html)

What it is: "An Overview of Fung Shui Woods in Hong Kong", a page published by the Hong Kong Herbarium of the government's Agriculture, Fisheries and Conservation Department. Fung shui woods are the patches of trees traditionally kept standing behind a village for the good fortune they were held to bring, and the page summarizes a field survey begun in 2002: about 116 such woods, averaging a hectare, ranging from 600 square meters at the smallest to 6 hectares at the largest, with 80% of them lying below 100 m elevation.

Why it applies, and its limits: The village grove is a feature we draw on nearly every map, and this gives the one thing hardest to guess - how big it actually is, and how much the sizes vary. The New Territories villages it surveys are Chinese villages, and their oldest wood predates British rule, so this is the Chinese custom our setting models, not a stand-in for it. It stands in only for Japan, whose village and homestead groves are a different custom that no public page we could read has measured; and the south China coast is subtropical, so the tree species differ from a northern or Japanese grove. The page says a wood is small because it is squeezed between the fields in front of the village and the hill behind, so these sizes are for villages backed by a hill; a village on open plain may have kept a grove of another size. It is a modern survey of the woods that have survived to be counted, not of what once stood; and the page rounds its figures and cites no study of its own, so where it disagrees with the underlying 2006 paper we follow the paper; and it gives the oldest wood's age of more than 300 years in a comparison table without saying how that age was established.

Used for: the survey summary - 116 woods, average 1 ha, 600 m² to 6 ha, 80% below 100 m; the 2006 paper's GIS table is primary where the two differ; that the oldest wood is over 300 years old
