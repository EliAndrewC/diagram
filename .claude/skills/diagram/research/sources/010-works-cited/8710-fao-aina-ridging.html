fao-aina-ridging

P. O. Aina (Department of Soil Science, Obafemi Awolowo University), "Rainfall runoff management techniques for erosion control and soil moisture conservation", FAO (https://www.fao.org/4/t1696e/t1696e02.htm)

What it is: An FAO chapter surveying the techniques a small farmer has for holding rain where it falls and keeping soil from washing off - ridge and mound tillage, tied ridges, terraces, contour bunds, contour hedges, straw barriers and grass buffer strips - and saying which suits what ground.

Why it applies, and its limits: It settles which ground contour ridging belongs to, and it settles it against the reasoning this record had been carrying: ridge-and-furrow aligned parallel to the contour has the dual purpose of erosion control and surface drainage, and its advantages are GREATER the less steep the terrain and the more permeable the soil, while for a smallholder with only hand implements or animal traction it is named the satisfactory method. The measures the same chapter assigns to steep cultivated land are a different family - terraces, contour bunds, contour hedges, straw barriers and grass buffer strips. So contour alignment is a supportable option on the gentle ground our maps draw, and varying the furrow direction between plots is a drawing choice for legibility rather than a correction of a misapplied rule. Its limits: it is a modern conservation-agronomy text, written largely from African smallholder practice, and it addresses conservation PERFORMANCE - what works - rather than what Chinese or Japanese farmers actually did, so it tells us contour ridging is supportable on gentle ground and never that it was universal there.

Used for: contour-aligned ridge and furrow as an erosion-control and surface-drainage measure whose advantage is greater on less steep ground, and the satisfactory method for a hand-and-animal smallholder; terraces, bunds, hedges and grass strips as what steep cultivated land takes instead
