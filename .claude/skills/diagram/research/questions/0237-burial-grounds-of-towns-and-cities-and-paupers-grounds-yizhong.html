Burial grounds of towns and cities, and paupers' grounds (yizhong)

In Japan, a settlement's dead commonly lay in a communal graveyard set apart from its houses, and in many castle towns before the Edo period the temples and their graveyards were gathered together on the town's outer edge, as a line of defense. A city's funerary grounds stood on the highways out of it, their entrance at or just past the edge of the city. How large a town's burial ground was in the Edo period, no page we read says.

China kept separate grounds for the dead too poor to be buried. Under the Song dynasty the state ordered pauper cemeteries of three to five qing, a Chinese land measure of a hundred mu - tens of acres in all - in the counties around its capital, and later counties kept several small charity grounds (yizhong) of a mu or a few each, from a twentieth of an acre to about an acre and a half.

  • In Japan, a settlement's graveyard was often a communal ground, set apart from the houses.
    Where the living settled for good, a communal graveyard often formed apart from the dwellings, and it was often one that belonged to each settlement. Where a village's dead lay is at Where a village buries its dead: its own ground, the temple yard, the fields or the home plot.
  • Castle towns gathered their temples and graveyards on the town's outer edge.
    In many castle towns before the Edo period, temples and graveyards were brought together on the edge of the town, meant as a line of defense against an attack.
  • After its siege, Osaka merged the graveyards scattered through the city into a few grounds on its rim.
    When the city was rebuilt after the summer siege of Osaka, its graveyards were gathered, for military reasons, onto its outer rim: four districts' grounds into one, the Uemachi plateau's several into another, and one quarter's divided among three. With two added later, they became the city's seven graveyards.
  • At Osaka, the temples came to the ground rather than the ground to the temples.
    The largest of the seven graveyards was laid out in 1621, and the page on it records two temples built there afterwards, in 1637 and 1648.
  • Did a city keep a burial ground for each of its temples?
    Nothing we read says so. A whole city burying its dead in seven grounds is Osaka's pattern, and no page we read gives a ratio of temples to burial grounds.
  • Japan's ancient law code kept the dead out of the capital and away from the roads, and set no distance.
    The Yoro code's article on the capital forbids burying the dead near the capital or beside the roads, and gives no distance for either.
  • Edo's execution grounds and Kyoto's burial grounds lay on the highways out of the city.
    Edo's two great execution grounds stood at the city's entrances: Kozukappara on the Nikko Kaido to the north, Suzugamori on the Tokaido to the south. In Kyoto, the street Senbon-dori takes its name from the thousand memorial tablets raised along the road to the burial ground of Rendaino, and the Rokudō crossroads lay on the road to Toribeno, the burial ground of the old capital.
  • Kyoto's funerary grounds were entered through a temple or a crossroads at their mouth.
    The temple Injo-ji stands at the entrance of Rendaino, one of the old city's three great graveyards, and is known as a place that leads through to the other world. The Rokudō crossroads was the entrance to Toribeno, held to be the boundary between this world and the other. The crossroads lies about 1,334 ft from Toribeno by this project's own measurement.
  • Kyoto's Toribeno was a ground where the dead were burned or left in the open.
    Toribeno was a ground of cremation and of exposure burial. Whether its dead were also buried there is on no page we read.
  • At Edo's north entrance, the execution ground and the temple that buried its dead stood together.
    Kozukappara was one of the city's two great execution grounds. In 1667 a priest founded a hall on the land beside it to bury the dead and pray for them; it became the temple Eko-in, and when a railroad later cut the grounds in two, the south half became Enmei-ji, within which the site of the execution ground lies.
  • Courtesans who died in Edo's earthquake of 1855 were buried at a temple near their pleasure quarter.
    Jokan-ji, the "throw-in temple" where they were buried as if thrown in, stands near the Yoshiwara pleasure quarter, about 1,527 ft from it by this project's measurement. No page we read places it on the same highway as Kozukappara.
  • How far past the city's edge did a funerary ground begin?
    At each of the Edo and Kyoto sites above - Kozukappara, Suzugamori, Rendaino and Toribeno - the entrance to the funerary ground is at or just past the edge of the city, and the field runs outward from it for another 1,000 to 4,000 ft. That is this project's reading of the cases; no page states it as a rule.
  • How large was a town's burial ground in the Edo period?
    No page we read gives the area of an Edo temple's or village's burial ground, nor the population it served. Digging at one site shows a ground that was cramped: at the Obodo site in Sagamihara, a city southwest of Tokyo, an Edo temple's burial area held many grave pits dug over one another, because the area was limited and the dead were buried in the same place.
  • No page we read gives the size of a Japanese mound for the unclaimed dead.
    The pages we read on Japan's unclaimed dead and their mounds give no measure.
  • The earliest allowance we found for paths in a burial ground's size is a modern one, from 1934.
    A factor of 1.5 to 2 for the paths and buildings of a whole ground, over the area of its graves, comes from a 1934 paper in Japan's landscape-architecture journal on the common cemeteries of Japan's cities, worked out from Tokyo's, and nothing earlier gives one.

China's grounds for the unclaimed and the poor are better recorded: first the great state cemeteries of the Song, then the small charity grounds of the Qing counties.

  • The Song dynasty ordered each county of its capital district to set aside three to five qing for the poor dead.
    The emperor Shenzong ordered each county of the capital district to measure out three to five qing of barren government land, where the coffins of the poor, left lodged at monasteries, could be laid to rest, with a monk in charge. In 1104 Cai Jing extended this into the louzeyuan, with a register, and graves three chi (~3 ft) deep.
  • How large was three to five qing?
    About 42 to 70 acres, a square some 1,350 to 1,750 ft on a side. A mu was already 240 square paces in the third century, and the Northern Song foot was 0.308 m (~1 ft), with a pace of five feet or six, this page taking the smaller five; taking a qing as a hundred mu, as the later tables do, gives the figure. It is this page's arithmetic, and it carries the later hundred-mu qing back to the Song.
  • No page we read gives the area of a louzeyuan outside the Song capital district.
    The three to five qing were ordered for the capital district's counties only.
  • Each burial in a louzeyuan was given eight chi of ground, about 8 ft.
    The edicts gave each burial eight chi of ground and two square bricks, and nine chi, about 9 ft, to one whose kin wished it buried there.
  • A louzeyuan stood on high, open, barren ground.
    The edict of 1104 called for high, barren ground, and a louzeyuan was generally sited on high, open, barren ground. The edict's words name the kind of ground, and give no distance from the town.
  • A louzeyuan was walled or fenced, planted with trees, and laid out in sections of graves.
    A louzeyuan generally had a fence built all round to keep out idlers. The Kuaiji gazetteer has the ground surrounded by a wall or palisade against intruders and those grazing livestock, and shaded with trees suited to its soil and easy to grow; Xu Du's notebook has the graves in groups of thirty with ditches, and a corner of the ground set aside for a Buddhist temple.
  • Buddhist monks kept the louzeyuan, and lived on them.
    From the Northern Song on, every louzeyuan was managed by Buddhist monks, and a house was built inside it for the monk who kept the ground.
  • No page we read places a crematory at a louzeyuan.
    The Chinese Wikipedia article on the louzeyuan says nothing of cremation; whether a crematory stood there is a GUESS either way.
  • Excavations have found nearly a thousand louzeyuan graves, laid in neat rows.
    They were dug at nineteen sites. The largest cemetery, near Sanmenxia, has yielded 849 graves, the dead laid in shallow pits in neat rows.
  • Under the Qing dynasty, a county kept several small charity grounds on the roads out of its town.
    Xuancheng's county gazetteer lists its charity graves by the road they stood on: two mu on Anle hill and one on Wangcheng ridge on the west road, three mu by the southern altar on the south road, one and a half mu on Jiezi ridge on the north road, and twenty-two mu more bought in 1805. At the mu of 614.4 square meters (~6,613 sq ft) fixed by the 1915 statute, taken here for the Qing mu, the four road grounds come to about 0.15 to 0.45 acre each, some 80 to 140 ft on a side, and about 1.1 acres together; the later purchase to about 3.3 acres.
  • How large was a Qing county's charity graveyard?
    From about a twentieth of an acre to an acre and a half. In Baoshan, a Jiangnan county, a count of the Guangxu reign (1875-1908) found each charity graveyard running from 3 fen to 10 mu, about 0.05 to 1.5 acres at the 1915 mu. These were grounds for the unclaimed and the poor, not for the whole of a county's dead, who were buried on family land (Where a village buries its dead: its own ground, the temple yard, the fields or the home plot). Neither source gives the population a ground served.
