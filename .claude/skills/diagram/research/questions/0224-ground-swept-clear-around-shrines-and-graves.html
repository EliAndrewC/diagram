Ground swept clear around shrines and graves

The one tending of this ground attested before Japan began to modernize in 1868 is of the grave itself: in Ming-dynasty Beijing in the 1630s, families at their graves on the spring festival of Qingming weeded them and heaped fresh earth on them. Japanese graves were cleaned at the start of Bon, and a village shrine's own households chose who cleaned it, but both are custom recorded in modern times with no earlier date. No page we read attests a cleared band of ground around a graveyard, or a swept ground around a shrine's hall or its arches before 1868.

A Japanese shrine does not stand in open cleared country: its setting is a preserved grove, the chinju no mori, kept around the sanctuary, the approach and the place of worship, so the precinct sits among kept trees. The grove itself is at Shrine woods and fences (chinju no mori, tamagaki).

  • In Ming-dynasty Beijing, families weeded their graves and heaped fresh earth on them at the spring festival of Qingming.
    The Dijing Jingwulue, a description of Ming Beijing and its customs whose author dates the preface 1635, counts among the families at their graves on Qingming day "those who bow, those who pour libations, those who wail, those who clear the weeds from the grave and add earth to it". The festival is kept so still: the young and the old alike kneel before the ancestors' tombstones, burn incense and paper, sweep the tombs and offer food.
  • Japanese graves are cleaned at the start of Bon, in custom recorded in modern times.
    The seventh day of the seventh month, which opens Bon, is when, region by region, the graves are cleaned - and, in one modern popular account, the paths to them as well. In some places the grass round the graves is cut before Bon, and in some it is cut so that the spirits of the dead can pass easily.
  • Was Bon grave cleaning kept before Japan began to modernize in 1868?
    No page we read dates it that early. The dictionaries and the library answer that describe it give no date, and the Kyoto and Edo almanacs of annual observances that might were not found in a readable text.
  • In the campaign's world, families weed and clean their ancestors' graves at the spring and autumn equinox festivals.
    The campaign notes give it: at the seven-day festivals of spring and autumn, families visit the graves of their ancestors, weed and clean them, and make offerings.
  • In the two-grave custom, the burying grave often lay in mountain forest, away from houses.
    The grave where the body was interred, mostly by earth burial, is often in mountain forest and the like, away from human habitation.
  • Was a band of ground around a graveyard kept clear?
    No source we read attests one. The weeding is of the grave itself, and how wide any cleared band ran is in no source read.

At a Japanese shrine the record says who did the cleaning and what the approach's gravel was for, but not what the ground around the hall was like.

  • A village shrine's own households chose who cleaned it, in folklore recorded in modern Japan.
    From among a shrine's parishioners, the ujiko, representatives were chosen as its caretakers - where the shrine was the center of the village's affairs, at a parishioners' gathering that doubled as the village gathering. Sometimes in rotation for a fixed term, they answered for cleaning the shrine, looking after its priest and running its festival. No page we read gives that cleaning before 1868.
  • A shrine precinct of swept, raked gravel or flagstones is this project's own guess.
    That the keidai, a shrine's precinct, was swept raked gravel or flagged is general reading, a GUESS: no page we read gives the precinct's surface.
  • The gravel laid along a shrine's approach was meant to purify the ground.
    The tamajari gravel laid along the sando, the approach, has the meaning of purifying the ground it covers.
  • An approach swept along its whole length, with the ground under each of its arches, is this project's own guess.
    That the path and the ground under each of its torii arches were swept the whole length of it is general reading, a GUESS: no page we read says so.
  • A tended verge, clear of scrub, right around a shrine or a grave, with the waste beyond left wild, is this project's own guess.
    That the ground immediately around a sacred or a funerary feature was a tended verge, clear of the scrub around it, is this project's reading, a GUESS; no page we read says so.

In south China the ground before an ancestral hall was not a swept verge but an open plaza with water in front of it, laid out on purpose.

  • The ancestral halls of Panyu, in Cantonese south China, mostly fronted a body of water and an open plaza.
    At the very front of the ancestral halls of Panyu lie mostly water and an open plaza, where the clansmen assemble and hold their ceremonies, an arrangement that answers both to fengshui and to drainage, fire protection and the raising of fish.
  • Before the hall of a Hakka walled house lay a drying floor and a half-moon pond.
    In front of the hall-and-wing house is a rectangular drying floor, the heping, for drying grain and other crops and for the gatherings of the new year, the festivals, the weddings and the funerals. Before it, as a matter of course, lies a half-moon fengshui pond that stores water against drought and for putting out fire.
  • A market or an opera on the plaza before a south Chinese ancestral hall, and such a plaza before a village earth-god shrine, are this project's own additions.
    No page we read gives them. The Hakka and Cantonese accounts give drying, gatherings, ceremonies, weddings and funerals, and neither a market nor an opera. Neither sets such a ground before a village earth-god shrine.
