How our maps draw a city's street front: rows of shophouses (machiya)

Our maps draw a city's commoner housing as rows of houses touching their neighbors, as the townsmen's houses stood with no gaps between them, and its market streets as lines of shops facing the street they front. The real shops touched as well; the maps leave a joint of about 18 ft between them so each one can be counted, a deliberate deviation. Only the samurai quarter keeps detached compounds in yards of their own.

  • Our maps draw commoner housing in rows, not as separate buildings in their own clearings.
    The homes of a residential row shared their walls, so the laborer, servant and burakumin homes and the small merchant houses are laid as touching rows. Obstacles break a row, and an occasional gap is kept, but a gap is not the usual pattern.
  • Touching homes keep a hairline seam between their outlines.
    They were separate houses built against each other, and the seam, a map drawing convention, lets each one read as its own.
  • Rows back to back keep the real lane between them.
    The lane between one back tenement building and the next was about 3 to 6 ft wide, with a drain down its middle that took the roofs' rain, so the maps keep that gap, for the eaves and the drain.
  • The samurai quarter keeps detached compounds with yards.
    A samurai residence was enclosed by its own hedge, wall and gate, and the contrast with the commoners' rows is what makes the rows read.

The rule the map follows - commoner rows:

  • at least 55% of a city's commoner homes touch a neighbor, and the median gap from one to its nearest neighbor is no more than 6 ft (a calibration against our drawn maps);
  • back-to-back rows keep a gap of about 3 to 6 ft (accurate);
  • an idobata court opens every second row, and the block's wells are drawn before the rows, so the rows break into courts around them (a calibration against our drawn maps);
  • a quarter's alleys are laid out first and lined closely with houses, while a main street keeps a strip clear for the shops that front it.

Along a market street the rows are shops, and the maps draw them a little apart where the real ones touched.

  • Our maps draw a market row as a line of shops, each facing the street it fronts.
    The shophouses of a busy street stood side by side with no gaps between them, so a market row is drawn as a line of shops, each facing the street.
  • Our maps leave a joint of about 18 ft between neighboring market shops, where the real shops touched.
    Real shophouses adjoined, the gable wall of one meeting the next, a gap of zero. The maps widen the joint, a deliberate deviation, so a reader can count the shops and see which way each one faces. The shops' relative sizes stay true; only the joint is drawn wider than life.

The rule the map follows - market rows:

  • neighboring shops in a market row are spaced along the row's own line, with a median joint of about 18 ft (a deviation);
  • a row follows its street through a bend in the stretch it fronts;
  • so far only the capital, Shiro Daika, is drawn this way; the provincial cities keep their earlier spacing.

A town's street front follows its own rule, which turns on its traffic: Does a town's street front stand wall to wall, or house by house?
