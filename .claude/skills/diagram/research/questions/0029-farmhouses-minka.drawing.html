How our maps draw farmhouses (minka)

Our maps draw each farmhouse longer than it is deep, about 46 by 28 ft for a well-off household, its size and proportion varied a little from house to house. Its plan and roof follow its region: in most villages a single thatched rectangle under one roof, elsewhere an L, two roofs or a court, and tile in a Chinese-leaning village. Each is turned from its village's common bearing, which is near south, by the curve of the lane it stands on, and its yard and garden beds turn with it. It stands 10 to 13 ft from the paddy, and never nearer than 6 ft.

  • Our maps draw a farmhouse 46 by 28 ft, the size of the better-off farmers' houses preserved today.
    That is about 1,290 square feet, some 36 tsubo (this project's arithmetic, at 35.6 square feet to the tsubo): the size of a headman's or a well-off farmer's house among the survivors, and about twice the usual house of one village in 1885.
  • How big should an ordinary household's house be drawn?
    Near 35 by 20 ft: the 20 tsubo of the commonest house of the Utsunomiya villages at the survivors' proportions. A poorer house is smaller (a 10-tsubo house is about 25 by 14 ft), and 46 by 28 ft and upward is kept for the headman and the few better-off. Carrying the survivors' proportion of 1.6 to 2.0 over to houses no survivor measures is a GUESS.
  • The headman's house is the largest on the map.
    Why, and what else a headman's homestead holds, is at What makes the headman's house different?
  • In a scattered village, a homestead's wealth scales its house and its grove together.
    Each homestead takes a standing - poorer, ordinary or better-off - so a richer farm reads bigger across the board. The scaling reaches what is drawn and not the ground kept for it, so it never moves a neighbor and never changes how many households fit on the map.
  • In a clustered village, a house varies along its length more than across its depth.
    No two farmsteads were copies of one another, so the houses on a map vary. A house's length varies by roughly 0.85 to 1.35 of the base and its depth by only 0.90 to 1.10, so the proportions stay inside about 1.3 to 2.5 long to deep, as a house that grew along its long axis would. The surviving houses under one roof run 1.6 to 2.0.
  • No farmhouse is drawn squarer than about 1.3 to 1 or longer than about 2.7 to 1.
    This is a check on the variation above, which itself stays within about 1.3 to 2.5; past 2.7 to 1 a house would be drawn wrong rather than drawn differently. The band is wider than the survivors' 1.6 to 2.0 on both sides, and its ends are a calibration, not a finding: no page we read gives how proportions spread across a village's houses.

The plan and the roof are regional forms, so a settlement's farmhouses share them. Most of our settlements are Japanese; a Chinese-leaning village, one our maps draw after Chinese rather than Japanese building, takes the Chinese forms instead. Where each plan and roof stood is at Farmhouses (minka).

  • Our maps give each settlement one farmhouse plan, from the plans its region used.
    Each plan is attested and each regional. How often each comes up is a GUESS: no page counts the share.
    • The straight house, under one roof, is the plain form.
    • The L-shaped house belongs in the north: the magariya in the horse-raising country of northern Miyagi and southern Iwate, most of all to the headman and the better-off, and the chumon-zukuri in Yamagata, Akita and Fukushima.
    • The two-roofed bunto house belongs in the far south of Kyushu and on the Pacific side.
    • The three-sided court (sanheyuan), or its one-wing and L forms, belongs in a Chinese-leaning village.
  • The roof shape is a knob, chosen per settlement and not per house.
    A hipped roof, a gabled roof or an irimoya, each common in its own regions; how often each comes up is a GUESS.
  • Each roof shape reads differently from above.
    Seen from overhead, a gabled roof is a rectangle split by a ridge running its whole length. A hipped roof has a ridge shorter than the house, with a slope running down from each end of it to the corners, so it reads as a long rectangle with a line down its middle and a diagonal into each corner. An irimoya reads like the hipped roof, with a small triangle standing at each end of the ridge.
  • Every farmhouse in a Japanese village is thatched, the headman's as the tenant's.
    The headmen's surviving houses are thatched too. Tile belongs on the storehouse (see How our maps draw farm storehouses (kura)).
  • How are the farmhouses of a Chinese-leaning village roofed?
    With tile, as they are in the one region read, Huizhou. No page we read says whether the poorest there went thatched, so a split between thatch and tile in such a village is a GUESS.

A farmhouse faced south or near it, toward its yard and the sun (see Farmhouses (minka)). A row of houses all square to the map would read as drawn rather than built, so each farmhouse is turned a little.

  • How near due south does a settlement's common bearing lie?
    Within about 11 degrees either side: half a point of a sixteen-point compass, so the bearing still reads as south. How near south is a GUESS; in the example a 1985 survey of farmhouse bearings in Okinawan villages gives, most of the houses on one hillside of a village faced a quarter away from the fall of its ground, on a slope our maps do not draw.
  • Each farmhouse turns with the curve of the lane it stands on.
    Neighbors along a bending lane face a little apart, never more than about 30 degrees from the common bearing - inside the roughly 34 degrees either side that three points of the compass span in the Okinawan survey, which puts the smaller turns down to curving roads.
  • How far is a house turned from its lane's own line?
    By up to about 12 degrees, most by much less, so a village reads as set out by eye rather than surveyed. How the turns spread inside the survey's range is a GUESS.
  • No farmhouse is drawn turned a quarter of the way round from its neighbors.
    The Okinawan survey found about one house in nine turned a quarter away, to the right of its village's spread, but that count is modern and from one island region, and no count from before 1868 was found. The curving street that the survey gives as the cause of the smaller turns is seen in a village moved to its site in 1736, so our maps turn a house by its lane and leave the quarter-turned houses, about one in nine there, undrawn.
  • A farmhouse's yard and garden beds turn with it.
    The yard is the ground in front of the house's south wall (see How our maps draw threshing and drying yards (niwa)), and a yard set square to the map beside a house turned four degrees would look like a mistake: a homestead was laid out as one piece. The homestead turns as one about the middle of the house, and the ground cleared for each part is the ground it is drawn on.
  • A yard's edge toward its house is straight and parallel to the wall.
    Its other edges wander a little, as a hand-laid plot's do.

No source states in feet how far a farmhouse stood from its paddy, so the least distance is built from three parts, two of them guesses.

  • The bund is taken as about 1.5 ft wide.
    That sits inside the one to two shaku the sources give (see Bunds between the paddies (aze)). Other bunds ran lower and narrower, or heavier, so 1.5 ft is one convention among several, and the 6 ft minimum below leans on it lightly.
  • The path along the bund's top is taken as about 1.5 ft wide.
    The bund was kept as a path for people and their tools; the path's width is a GUESS.
  • A thatched roof's eaves are taken to reach about 3 ft past the wall.
    No page we read gives the projection, so 3 ft is a GUESS: the gap our maps already keep between neighboring houses.
  • No farmhouse wall stands within 6 ft of a paddy.
    A wall nearer than bund, path and eave, 1.5 + 1.5 + 3 ft, would stand with its roof over the levee path and its drip line in the rice. The minimum is measured from each of a house's four corners, never from its center. Two of its three parts are guesses, so it is a soft threshold rather than a measured minimum.
  • Our maps draw farmhouses 10 to 13 ft from the paddy.
    That is well clear of the 6 ft minimum, and close enough that a household works its own ground, with the house's back to the hill and its face to the water and the fields.
  • No farmhouse is held to a maximum distance from its fields.
    A cluster farther from its paddy than some set figure is not wrong for that. One that reads as standing apart from its fields rather than beside them is, and that is judged by looking at the map, not by measuring.
