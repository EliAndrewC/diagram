How our maps draw village boundaries and their markers (murazakai)

Villagers felt their village began where each road from outside reached the houses, and a boundary god's stone usually stood there, so every lane or road that leaves a village for the fields or the next settlement has a boundary god's stone beside it where it passes the last house, with a small trodden patch of open ground beside the stone, where villagers gathered. Some villages add a rope across the road, a straw figure or serpent, or a great enoki tree, each picked at random for each village. No line between villages is drawn. A town on the Imperial road marks both ends of its street with a pair of earth banks, a dogleg bend in the road (masugata) and a tall post.

  • Every road out of a village has a boundary god's stone at its entrance.
    Its villagers felt the village's edge to be where each road from outside reached the houses, so there are as many entrances as roads out, and each takes a boundary god's stone at the roadside, where the dosojin usually stood.
  • A small patch of trodden ground beside the stone marks the entrance's gathering place.
    The entrance was a gathering place that served the village as a plaza, so a patch of trodden ground is drawn beside the stone. No page we read gives its size, and the size drawn is a GUESS.
  • Some villages add a rope across the road or a straw figure beside the boundary god.
    A village might cut the road with a rope or a straw serpent, or set straw figures at its boundary, so the form is a knob, chosen at random for each village among the forms on record. About half the villages have nothing more, about a quarter a rope stretched across the road there, and about a quarter a straw figure or serpent beside the stone. The shares are a GUESS: the record says which regions favored which form, not how many villages kept each.
  • About one village in two has a great enoki beside its boundary god.
    A village kept an enoki by the boundary god or a small shrine at its entrance, but no page we read says how many did, so the tree is a knob, chosen at random for about one village in two, a GUESS. It is drawn as one broad crown, larger than a farmstead's trees, beside the stone or small shrine where the lane leaves the settlement. No page we read puts a great tree at a crossroads or a well, so none is drawn there.
  • No line between villages is drawn.
    The line between villages seldom had a boundary god or a road-cutting at it and was not strongly felt as the village's edge, and these maps draw no administrative lines, a map drawing convention.
  • Where an Imperial road crosses a village's map, it may carry one boundary post.
    A highway's crossing of the line between villages was usually posted, so one tall wooden post may stand beside the road where it leaves the village's own fields for the next village's. Where that falls, and whether it falls inside the map, is a GUESS, chosen at random for each map. No post stands beside a lane.
  • A town on the Imperial road marks each end of its street with a pair of earth banks, a dogleg in the road and a tall post.
    The banks face each other across the road, each about 14 ft thick at the base and 8 ft high and fenced on top, the size recorded for the banks at the post town of Kanagawa. The road takes a dogleg bend (masugata) at that end of the town, and one tall boundary post stands beside the road.
  • An Imperial road may carry a milestone pair, but on most maps it does not.
    A milestone pair is two mounds about 30 ft square, one each side of the road, each planted on top with a tree, drawn as one crown, the tree chosen at random among enoki, pine and cedar in the recorded shares. A mound stood only at every ri, about 2.4 miles, so a map carries a pair only when a ri mark falls on its stretch of road.
