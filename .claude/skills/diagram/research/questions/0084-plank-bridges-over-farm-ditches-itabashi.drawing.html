How our maps draw plank bridges over farm ditches (itabashi)

Where a path along a bund meets a farm ditch too wide to step across, our maps lay a small crossing over it so a person on foot can keep going: square across the water, with a short abutment at each end. All the crossings on one map take the same form - a single log or board, logs under trodden earth, or a planked deck - picked at random for each settlement. A crossing is about 4 ft wide and spans about 8 ft, and it is laid over water 2 ft wide or more.

  • A crossing stands where a path along a bund meets a ditch.
    That is what it is taken to be for, a GUESS, so no path leads to one - the way to it is the bund.
  • A long ditch takes a crossing near its middle, and a very long one several.
    A crossing stands at about the middle of a long stretch of ditch, and a very long stretch takes further crossings at intervals along it.
  • Both ends of a crossing land on ground worth crossing to.
    That is cultivated ground, wet or dry, the settlement itself, or a walked dike, and never reed marsh, scrub commons, or the edge of the map. A ditch along a field's outer margin with nothing beyond it carries no crossing, and a crossing that can find no such spot is not drawn. A count of every crossing on every map found the ones with nothing beyond them over the drainage ditches at a field's foot and the diagonal drains along its outer edge, so those carry none; every field-to-field, dry-to-wet and settlement-to-field crossing stands.
  • A crossing never sits on a farmhouse.
    A crossing near the settlement's edge slides along its own ditch, staying over the water, until it clears every house.
  • Why is a crossing laid only over water 2 ft wide or more?
    A crossing is taken to be laid only over water a person cannot step across, a GUESS, and no page we read says at what width a farm ditch was bridged, so the line is this project's choice. It sits at about 2 shaku (~2 ft), the width a traditional farm water channel may have had - itself a GUESS - and at about the width at the head of our delivery ditches, which run 2.5 ft at the head and taper toward 1.2 ft, drawn no finer than 1.5 ft. So a standard farm ditch earns a crossing and the thin tails do not.
  • A ditch is judged by its widest point.
    A ditch wide at its head and a thread at its tail is worth a crossing somewhere, and the crossing is laid where the water is wide enough for one.
  • Where no wide spot is free, a crossing takes the widest spot left.
    Houses, crops, other crossings or the joins of ditches can rule out every wide spot; the crossing is then laid at the widest one remaining rather than left off.
  • On the Inashiro map, the 2 ft width line lays ten crossings.
    One stands on each delivery branch wide enough for one, each over 2.0 ft of water or a little more, and two more down the main ditch, over 2.8 and 2.4 ft; the two branches of 1.8 ft have none.
  • A branch's crossing may stand well down the branch.
    On Inashiro three of the branch crossings stand 160-250 ft below the heads of their branches, each where a bund crosses mid-field, because the head itself is a join with the main ditch. We leave them there: a crossing at the head would stand about 120 ft from the main ditch's crossing at the junction.
  • Branches with and without a crossing look alike.
    The unplanked 1.8 ft branches are drawn 2.7 ft wide, against 3.0-3.7 ft for the planked ones, too close to tell apart on the page, so the map shows some branches with a crossing and some without. Where two branches run 5-25 ft apart for 300 ft, one may have a crossing and its twin 12 ft away none.
  • A crossing is about 4 ft wide and spans about 8 ft, a map drawing convention.
    It is a single-file crossing with a short abutment at each end; a board any wider or longer would read as a jetty. It is kept narrower and shorter than a cart lane's deck on purpose: the deck that carries a way is as wide as the way, and a crossing that wide would read as a road bridge.
  • A lane or road that crosses water takes one planked deck.
    Where a way crosses a stream or a ditch, the map draws one timber deck as wide as the way, always planked whatever form the settlement's ditch crossings take, and never two decks at one point: a way that crosses a stream just where a channel joins it crosses on one bridge. This is a map drawing convention, and it has a cost: the one proportion we read, for river bridges, would make an earthen deck the likelier.
  • Why is the form of a settlement's crossings left to chance?
    All three forms were laid over small water (Plank bridges over farm ditches), and no page we read says which crossed a paddy ditch, so the form is a knob rather than a choice. A settlement's ditch crossings all take one of the three, each with an even chance. The even shares are a GUESS; the one proportion we read, for river bridges, would make the planked deck far rarer than logs under earth. Where a crossing is laid, and at what width, does not change with the form.
  • The planked form is drawn with its seams running across the deck.
    So a reader sees boards laid from bank to bank.
