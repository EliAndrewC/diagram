How our maps place travelers' inns and cheap lodging houses

A town on our maps keeps two grades of lodging: one caravan inn on the road its traffic uses, because a town is not a post station, and a cheap lodging house (a flophouse) where a late or poor traveler arrives. A provincial city keeps more: inns at the gates where its caravans stop (How our maps draw gate markets outside a town (guanxiang)), and a cheap lodging house inside its walls and outside every gate.

  • Every county seat on our maps stands on a trade route, so every town keeps a caravan inn.
    The town is drawn on a through road, the road its inn serves.
  • A town draws one inn, a calibration.
    The count is set against the towns our maps draw, not taken from a source: no page we read counts the inns of a county town that was not a post station. It is fewer than the lodging this project guesses a county seat kept, below, because the rest of that lodging is understood in the town's shophouses rather than drawn; a provincial city draws more, an inn at each gate where its caravans stop.
  • A town is not a post station, so the post stations' dozens of inns are a ceiling, not a target.
    In this setting the relay horses belong to the Imperial waystation (Relay stations: the Imperial waystation, the relay office and their stables (umaya, toiyaba)), not to a street of inns.
  • The inn takes one of two forms, chosen at random for each town: a single-story wagon inn or a two-story post-station inn.
    The record holds two historical models that disagree on the one thing a map can show, the inn's height: a north-Chinese wagon inn, as a modern newspaper recalls it, is single-story, and a surviving Tokaido inn of about 1836 is two stories, its upper floor the guest rooms. Neither is the better model, so the form is a knob, chosen at random from each map's seed with no bias between the two and never by the form of the settlement's houses (How our maps choose between the courtyard house and the Japanese house), because nothing we read says how common either was; a map may be set to either one. The single story rests on a modern account, but an inn must be drawn at some height, and the knob gives the other form, from a building of about 1836, an equal chance.
    • Was a wagon inn single-story before modern times?
      We GUESS so. The 1780 account of the north-Chinese inns gives no height, so the single story is carried back from a modern newspaper account of the wagon inn.
  • A wagon inn keeps its stable within about 150 ft of the inn, with open ground beside the stable.
    The north-Chinese inn kept a long stable and a broad yard for the carts, so no more than four dwellings stand within about 75 ft of the stable. The distances are calibrated against the drawn towns.
  • The maps draw no grooms' lean-to and no well at the inn.
    Only a 2026 newspaper's account of the wagon inn shows them, and this project draws nothing that only modern sources show.
  • A post-station inn is drawn without a stable or a cart yard behind it.
    No page we read shows either at a Japanese inn before 1868; the one picture of a large stable behind a travelers' inn is a sentence from a novel of 1913. The stable and the yard belong to the wagon inn, whose source before modern times shows them.
  • How wide is a post-station inn?
    It is a house on the street, and most were small, about 18 ft wide or less. At Mishima, a post station on the Tokaido, a large inn had 5 ken (about 30 ft) or more of frontage and a small one 3 ken (about 18 ft) or less, small inns were the commonest, and one inn at Hiratsuka, another Tokaido post station, was 5.5 ken (about 33 ft) wide. The footprint our maps draw for every inn, and the inn's own plan, are at How our maps draw an inn.
  • The inn fronts the road it serves.
    It stands within about 115 ft of the roadbed with no building between it and the road, its entrance turned to the nearest point of the road and its long side running along it, so a diagonal road gets a tilted inn. This is a convention of our maps: a caravan pulls up to the road it is already on, so an inn behind two ranks of shops is an inn no wagon can reach.
  • A walled town's inn stands in the gate market outside the gate its through road enters by.
    Inns and the other services for travelers and traders were set up outside the gates that gave onto long-distance routes, so the inn, and a wagon inn's stable, stand among the gate market's shops on that road (How our maps draw gate markets outside a town (guanxiang)). An unwalled town's inn stands along the road where the caravans pass.

The flophouse is the town's other lodging, for the poorest travelers and for the market crowd.

  • Every town draws one market-day flophouse unless its map is set to draw none, and a busy hub draws more.
    It serves the poor of the road and, by this project's reading, a GUESS, the market-goers from the far edge of a market's reach who come in the night before.
  • The flophouse is drawn as a plain thatched house with one shared room.
    It is drawn like the one in an Edo print, a thatched house marked by its name on the paper doors, with no awning or shop front, so it reads as nothing like a shophouse.
  • The flophouse is not drawn as a barn with a long row of doorways.
    No page we read, of any period, shows that building, or a house bedding dozens, and this project draws no form that no source before modern times shows.
  • A flophouse is drawn no bigger than a large house.
    This is a GUESS: no page we read measures one.
  • A walled town's flophouse stands in the gate market outside its gate.
    The gate market is where travelers arrive (How our maps draw gate markets outside a town (guanxiang)), and by this project's GUESS a county town's gate shut at dusk, so a peasant arriving late could not get in. That is our extension of the night closing recorded for Tang Chang'an and for the cities of the late Ming, a GUESS, since no page we read says so.
  • An unwalled town's flophouse stands along the road at the town's edge.
    So the kichin-yado stood at the edge of a post town.

A provincial city keeps more cheap lodging.

  • A provincial city draws one cheap lodging house inside its walls, and one outside every gate.
    The one outside stands within about 1,560 ft of its gate. Inns did gather outside a city's busy gates, but how many cheap houses a city held is on no page we read, so the symmetric count is a GUESS, a deliberate stylization on the generous side of a range nobody can bound.
  • A city draws a generous count because the setting is more attentive to its peasantry than its historical models.
    The count is drawn so the provision reads at a glance. This project declined two alternatives: putting the cheap lodging at the busiest gate alone, and drawing a separate cart-inn compound with an animal yard.
  • A city's cheap lodging house inside the walls stands in a humble quarter.
    It stands in the laborers' quarter, or the farming quarter where a city has one; never within about 330 ft of the temple, merchant or samurai neighborhoods, and never within about 450 ft of the burakumin quarter.
  • At a city gate where the caravans stop, the cheap lodging house stands beside the inn and the stable.
    There it is drawn with the gate's inn and stable as one group.
  • How much lodging do a town and a city keep that the map does not draw?
    A county seat of about 3,000 inhabitants on a highway, but not a post station, would run something like eight to fifteen lodging houses all told, by this project's GUESS. Our maps draw only a few of them as buildings of their own, and those counts are a calibration against the drawn maps: a town its one inn and its flophouse, a provincial city an inn at each gate where its caravans stop and a cheap house inside its walls and outside every gate, two to four of each on the cities drawn.
  • The rest of a town's lodging is small family inns in its shophouses, which the map does not count.
    In a seat of about 3,000 that would be five to eight family inns that were not separate buildings at all, by the same GUESS. These would be ordinary shophouses whose back rooms took lodgers, the household living on the meal trade, a field and porterage with lodging as the extra, and on the night a lord's procession stopped the whole town became the billet. So several of the shop rows on the road and at the gates are understood to sleep travelers, and nothing on the map counts them.
