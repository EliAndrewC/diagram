How our maps place a hamlet on its stream

A hamlet on our maps may stand astride its brook, as the villages of Xidi and Likeng in Huizhou, China, stand along both banks of theirs, or beside it, as Hongcun stands beside its stream, and each map takes the form its site gives it. The record ties the form to the size of the water: a settlement stands on one bank of a river, and its own small channel runs through the middle of it. A brook 7 ft wide, which the hamlet draws its water from, is in between, the size of a stream, where the record shows both forms and prefers neither. Each farmstead keeps the whole of its own ground on one bank.

  • The houses go on both banks only where the best site straddles the brook.
    Where the site that faces the fields and has its back to the winter wind is crossed by the brook, the houses are built on both banks.
  • A hamlet site the brook does not cross is preferred when two sites are otherwise equally good.
    That is this project's decision: a crossing is one more thing to build and keep.
  • The ways between the two banks cross the brook squarely, on a plank bridge.
    The maps draw each crossing as the board bridge a farm ditch takes (How our maps draw plank bridges over farm ditches), a convention, since no page we read says what a hamlet laid over its own channel.
  • Likeng, which straddles its stream, joins its banks with ten-odd bridges of stone, timber and brick.
    The maps do not copy them: Likeng is a village rather than a farming hamlet, and its one account, a visitor's, says nothing of how the village lived.
  • A way keeps to its own bank unless crossing saves it more than about 150 ft of walking.
    So the maps charge a crossing as that much extra walk. The figure, about the length of a row of houses, is a GUESS: no page we read prices a plank against a detour.
  • The maps set a crossing place every 160 ft or so along the brook.
    Each stands on a reach where the brook runs straight enough to cross it square. The spacing is a GUESS, since no page we read says how often a hamlet bridged its own channel.
  • Each farmstead stands whole on one bank.
    Its house, work yard, garden beds, storehouse and fixtures - the privy, the manure heap, the woodpile, the persimmon - keep to one side of the brook, so a household crosses the water to reach its fields, but never to reach its own vegetable beds or its privy. In Japan, at the village of Harie, the record shows a channel running past each house and its kabata, and nothing we read places a household's own buildings across a channel from its house; that they stood together on one bank is this project's reading, a GUESS. It is kept because a holding split by running water reads, on a map, as two holdings.

The rule the map follows: the straight line from a farmhouse to each part of its farmstead - the work yard, each garden bed, the storehouse, every fixture and the persimmon - crosses no reach of the brook.
