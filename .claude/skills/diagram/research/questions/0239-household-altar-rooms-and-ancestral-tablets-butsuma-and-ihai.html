Household altar rooms and ancestral tablets (butsuma and ihai)

The butsuma was the room of a Japanese house's Buddhist altar, where the household kept its Buddha images and its ihai, the memorial tablets of its dead. In farmhouses the altar was often built out beyond a room; sometimes it was set beside the tokonoma of the zashiki, or, where faith ran strong, given a small room of its own behind the inner room or the zashiki. In the last two places it stood by the formal rooms of the house.

In one of the two kinds of Japanese tablet veneration, the tablets were those of the house's own line: a house keeps, in principle, the tablets of its successive heads and their spouses, following the same line as its property.

  • In farmhouses the altar often stood in a place built out beyond the butsuma's walls.
    A Japanese encyclopedia says that in the butsuma of farmhouses the place for the altar is in many cases built projecting outside the room.
  • Some houses made their butsuma beside the tokonoma of the zashiki.
    The same entry says a place for the altar was sometimes made beside the zashiki's tokonoma and served as the butsuma.
  • Where faith was strong, a butsuma of about six tatami mats stood behind the inner room or the zashiki.
    The entry finds this above all in regions where Jodo Shinshu was strong: a room of about six tatami mats for a fine altar, set behind the oku-no-ma, the zashiki or the like.
  • In the imperial palace the butsuma was a building of its own.
    By rule it was built as a separate building beside the emperor's everyday residence hall.
  • Did a samurai house set its butsuma beyond its audience room?
    One surviving house suggests so, with a caveat. The Nomura house at Kanazawa, home of upper samurai with a stipend of 1,200 koku on a site of about 1,000 tsubo (about 0.8 acre), has its rooms run from the inner room past a waiting room for guests and an audience room for meeting superiors to the butsuma beyond. But that audience room was moved in from a shipowner's house in another domain, so the sequence is not the house's own.
  • Was a butsuma ever set among a family's private rooms?
    Not on any page we read. The nearest case is the palace's separate hall beside the everyday residence, and that is an emperor's.
  • In one of the two Japanese kinds of tablet veneration, a house kept the tablets of its own successive heads.
    A Japanese encyclopedia sorts tablet veneration into two kinds by whose tablets are kept. One keeps, in principle, the tablets of the house's successive heads and their spouses; it stresses the single line of the house through the male side, and follows the same line as the inheritance of the house's property. The excerpt we could read breaks off before the second kind.

Where a residence went with a government post, in Japan or at a Chinese county seat, the house rule leaves a question open: whose tablets did it hold? They could be the holder's own line's, or those of the officials who held the post before.

  • Did a government office, or the residence that went with it, keep tablets for the officials who had held the post before?
    No page we read says so, in Japan or at a Chinese county seat.
  • China honored its meritorious past officials in a state hall at the Confucian temple.
    The shrine of eminent officials (mingguanci) was part of the Confucian temple's buildings, and made offerings to local officials who had governed well. Most stood outside the temple's Dacheng gate, on the east, with a shrine of local worthies on the west. It was a hall of the state at the temple, not a shrine inside the county office.
  • Whose tablets would an official's residence hold when the post passed to a cousin, not a son?
    This is a GUESS from the house rule above: the tablets follow the house, not the post. When a post passes father to son, the past holders are the holder's own forebears. Where a cousin takes the post as heir of the same house, the house's tablets, and so any past holders who were its heads, come with them. Where the cousin heads a branch house of their own, their altar holds their own line's tablets, and the past holders of the post have no tablets at the residence. The pages we read do not say which held at any particular posting.
