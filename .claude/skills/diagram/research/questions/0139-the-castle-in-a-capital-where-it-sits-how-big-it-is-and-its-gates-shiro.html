The castle in a capital: where it sits, how big it is, and its gates (shiro)

A daimyo's castle was the heart of the castle town: the town was laid out around it, district by district, with the samurai nearest it and the townspeople beyond. It was not one building but a walled ground of several baileys ringed by moats - Hirosaki's covered about 50 ha (~124 acres), Himeji's larger still - and its keep, the tall tower most pictures show, was a small part of it.

It was at once the lord's fortress, laid out so that its garrison could fight off a siege, and the lord's residence: in Hiroshima's, most of the upper innermost bailey was taken by the palace.

  • A Japanese castle town was built with its lord's castle at the center.
    An English-language encyclopedia of Japanese history defines the castle towns as cities established "with the lord's castle in the center", and lays the town out around it in districts for samurai, foot soldiers (ashigaru), townspeople and temples.
  • The town around the castle was zoned in rings by rank.
    Retainers' estates stood in the samurai towns, and in general the higher a retainer's position, the closer to the castle; place names such as Sange and Kamiyashiki-cho survive from them. The townspeople's district lay outside the samurai towns, its merchants and craftsmen separated by trade, as in Gofuku-machi, the drapers' ward, and Kaji-machi, the smiths'. The low-ranked foot soldiers were placed outside the townspeople's district, in wards such as Ban-cho and Teppo-cho. The temple quarter (teramachi) stood at the outer rim, its spacious temples part of the town's defenses.
  • Main roads passed the castle's front, not its back.
    Whatever the lie of the land, the main roads ran past the ote side, the front, of the castle, not behind it, "to indicate the glory of the ruler".
  • Some castle towns walled in the whole town (sogamae).
    Enclosing the entire town with a moat and earth mounds grew more common, until castle towns began to turn into walled cities; Odawara and Osaka are the chief examples. Such a perimeter took in the whole castle town as well as the castle, closed with moats, stone walls and earthen ramparts, and one such ring of dry moats and ramparts ran for two and a half ri, about 9 km (~29,528 ft).

Chinese prefectural seats had no lord's castle, but they too walled their government offices apart from the town.

  • At Quanzhou, a Chinese prefectural seat, the administrative city stood inside an inner city inside an outer city.
    Quanzhou built an administrative city (yacheng) within its inner city (zicheng), giving it three nested walls with the outer city (luocheng).
  • In Tang-dynasty Yangzhou, the outer city was for living and trade, the inner city for the government.
    Yangzhou's outer city (luocheng) held its commerce, crafts and houses, and its inner city (zicheng) its government offices.
  • Did the seat of power usually stand at its town's center?
    In the Japanese castle town, by definition: the account read defines the castle town by its central castle. Of the two Chinese seats read, Quanzhou set its offices inside walls nested one within another, while the page on Yangzhou says only that its inner city held the government and its outer city lay below Shu Hill, not that the inner city stood at the core.
  • Where did a castle stand when it was not at the center?
    At the edge of its town, with a river or the sea guarding that side, in each case read. Castle towns used rivers and the lie of the land together with dug moats, earthen ramparts and stone walls; that a castle on a town's flank goes with water is this project's reading of the two cases below, not a rule a source states.
  • At Okayama, the Asahi River's channels were made the castle's moats on its north and east.
    Ukita Hideie set the castle's main bailey on the hill of Okayama. The Asahi River's branching channels were laid out to protect the castle's north and east sides and used as its moats, and the castle town stretched north to south between them.
  • Kitsuki's castle stands on a height between a river mouth and a bay.
    It was built on Daiyama at the mouth of the Yasaka River, a natural stronghold enclosed by the Takayama River to the north and Morie Bay to the east. The samurai houses stand on the high ground to its north and south, and the merchants' town in the valley between them.

The figures that follow are for a castle's whole walled ground, then for its parts.

  • How big was a castle's whole ground?
    From about 16 ha (~40 acres) to over 230 ha (~568 acres) among the castles read. Kawagoe's grounds were about 50,000 tsubo, about 16.5 ha (~41 acres), later enlarged to over 99,000 tsubo, about 32.6 ha (~81 acres). Nijo's measures about 500 m (~1,640 ft) by 400 m (~1,312 ft), about 20 ha (~49 acres) by our arithmetic. Hiroshima's ran about a kilometer (~3,281 ft) each way, 90 ha (~222 acres).
  • Hirosaki's castle, seat of a domain of 47,000 koku, covered about 50 ha (~124 acres).
    Hirosaki was the seat of the Tsugaru, a house of 47,000 koku. Its castle, completed in 1611, was six baileys about 500 m (~1,640 ft) east to west by 1,000 m (~3,281 ft) north to south, about 50 ha (~124 acres) in all.
  • Is Hirosaki's 50 ha (~124 acres) the castle as it stood, or the park it became?
    The castle. The park opens nearly the whole of the domain's castle, whose layout as first built survives entire, in about 49 ha (~121 acres) inside triple moats. A smaller figure, 385,200 square meters (~95 acres), appears undated on the same heritage page, and Japanese Wikipedia gives it for the castle as first built, 612 m (~2,008 ft) by 947 m (~3,107 ft). No page we read says what each figure counts, such as the moats or the outer baileys, or why they differ; both describe the castle as the domain held it.
  • How much ground did Himeji's castle, seat of a domain of 520,000 koku, cover?
    Up to 233 ha (~576 acres), though the page that gives the figure does not say what it takes in. Ikeda Terumasa was granted the whole province of Harima, 520,000 koku, for his service at the battle of Sekigahara in 1600, which brought the Tokugawa to power, and entered Himeji. The domain was later far smaller. The castle complex is given as 233 ha (~576 acres), and runs 950 to 1,600 m (~3,117-5,249 ft) east to west and 900 to 1,700 m (~2,953-5,577 ft) north to south. Its World Heritage property is 107 ha (~264 acres), and the ground within its middle moat is a Special Historic Site.
  • Hirosaki's keep covered a fraction of a percent of its castle.
    Hirosaki's keep (tenshu), three stories, built in 1810 and said to have been called a turret in its day, has a first floor of 5 by 6 ken, about 9 m (~30 ft) by 11 m (~36 ft), or 99 square meters (~1,066 sq ft) by our arithmetic - a fraction of a percent of its castle's 50 ha (~124 acres).
  • Not every castle had a keep.
    Some castles built the keep's stone base and never the keep (Tsu, Akashi, Ako); some had no base and used a three-story turret in its place (Yonezawa, Yamagata, Mito); and some had neither keep nor stand-in (Kagoshima, Hitoyoshi).
  • Turrets (yagura) stood at the corners of a castle's walls.
    Towers or turrets called yagura were placed at the corners of the walls, over the gates and elsewhere, and served a number of purposes.
  • How tall was a keep?
    The three measured here stand 15 to 27 m (~49-89 ft); the two whose plans are given cover about 200 to 350 square meters (~2,153-3,767 sq ft). Marugame's, the smallest keep that survives, is 15 m (~49 ft) tall. Kakegawa's wooden keep covers 209 square meters (~2,250 sq ft) and rises 16.18 m (~53 ft). Hiroshima's, a great domain's keep as rebuilt, covers about 354 square meters (~3,810 sq ft) and stands 26.6 m (~87 ft), 39 m (~128 ft) with its stone base.
  • How big was the innermost bailey (honmaru)?
    About 2 ha (~5 acres) in the two castles measured. Nijo's honmaru is about 150 m (~492 ft) square, about 2.25 ha (~5.6 acres). Hiroshima's upper honmaru covers 21,705 square meters (~5.4 acres), about 16,530 square meters (~4.1 acres) of it taken by its palace.
  • How wide were a castle's moats?
    About 20 m (~66 ft) at both castles read. Himeji's moats average 20 m (~66 ft) wide, 34.5 m (~113 ft) at the most, and about 2.7 m (~9 ft) deep. Hiroshima's outer and middle moats were about 20 m (~66 ft) wide, its inner moat 30 to 104 m (~98-341 ft). Whether a moat rings its walls or a river serves on one side is at City moats: how wide and deep, and whether they ring the wall (hori, chenghe).
  • How steeply did a castle's stone walls lean?
    Upright at first, then sloped, and more steeply after 1600. The first stone walls were low and mostly vertical. The daimyo of Toyotomi Hideyoshi's line, the ruler of Japan before the Tokugawa, built theirs at about 45 to 50 degrees, and after 1600 the lean grew steeper. The typical "fan batter" wall rose in a straight line at about 45 degrees for two-thirds of its height and curved over the rest.
  • How high were a castle's stone walls?
    The famous ones are the extremes. Osaka's honmaru east wall, the tallest in Japan, rises 34 m (~112 ft) from its buried footing and 24 to 25 m (~79-82 ft) above the water. Marugame stacked four tiers to 60 m (~197 ft) in all, its third-bailey wall alone 22 m (~72 ft) at its highest. No page we read gives the height of an ordinary domain seat's wall.
  • In 1644, early in the Edo period, the shogunate had every daimyo draw a plan of their castle's defenses.
    The shogun Iemitsu ordered the daimyo of the whole country to make plans of their castles, maps of the military works whose written notes were in principle limited to what mattered militarily. The instructions asked for the size of each bailey's stone walls, earthworks and moats, for the keep, the turrets and the walls to be drawn, and for every street of the town down to the narrowest. They named no residence, hall or storehouse.

A castle was entered through gates at its front and back, each crossing the moat before it.

  • A castle had a main gate at its front and a rear gate opposite.
    The main gate, the ote-mon, stood at the gate-mouth on the castle's front, in castles of the early modern period usually the south; the rear gate, the karamete-mon, at the gate-mouth behind. The karamete-mon opened opposite the ote-mon.
  • What was a castle's rear gate for?
    A way out: for the garrison to attack from, by one account, or for the lord to escape by, by another. A Japanese castle glossary says castles were designed so that, in a siege, the enemy was drawn in near the main gate and run down by troops sallying out of the rear one. The Japanese Wikipedia article on the rear gate calls it instead an emergency exit, by which the lord could flee the castle.
  • How many gates did a great castle have?
    Edo's had dozens. The "thirty-six watch gates (mitsuke) of Edo castle" is a popular count: the shogunate's works office recorded 26 gates with square gate courts (masugata) in the outer enceinte, and ten notable gates were added to make the round number, while other accounts give 66 or 90 watch posts. Many were built as one with a bridge over the moat.
