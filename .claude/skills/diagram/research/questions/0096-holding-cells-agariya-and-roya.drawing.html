How our maps draw holding cells (agariya and rōya)

Our maps give a county magistracy a small cell, where one or two of the accused wait for their hearing, and no prison block. The jails of the period held the accused until judgment, and a magistracy that judged cases kept a temporary cell inside its own compound for the prisoners called before its court, so ours does too. It is drawn about 12 by 10 ft in the office's outer court, with bars across its front.

  • A magistracy keeps a cell, not a prison block.
    Prison as a sentence was imposed only by exception, so a map needs no block for convicts: a county cell holds its prisoners only until their hearing. That is accurate.
  • How many of the accused does a cell hold?
    One or two, on our maps. The count is this project's GUESS: a county office holds only a few until their hearing, where the agariya rooms of the big city jails, such as Edo's Tenmachō and Osaka's, were as a rule shared by several.
  • How big is a cell on our maps?
    About 12 by 10 ft, a GUESS at the small end of the single cell rooms measured, which run from Osaka's 6 tatami (~12 by 9 ft) to Tenmachō's 18 (~18 by 18 ft). No page we read gives the size of a magistracy's temporary cell. A county cell belongs at the small end because it holds only a few until their hearing. A cell much larger, of about 22 tatami (~23 by 17 ft), would outgrow every single cell room measured; only the commoners' great cell of 30 tatami was bigger.
  • A cell may stand on any side of the compound.
    Chinese county offices set their jails to the south by rule, and Neixiang's stands on the west side of its first courtyard, but this setting keeps no such rule, so a map places its cell wherever the compound's plan wants it: a deliberate deviation.
  • Our maps draw no room built for interrogation.
    In this setting torture is unusual, though not strictly forbidden, so a magistracy keeps no room purpose-built for it, and questioning happens in the day office, where the office's daily business is done, or in the hearing court, like any other business: a deliberate deviation.
  • Where was a suspect tortured?
    Edo justice tortured a suspect who kept refusing to confess, though never in the hearing court, and at the Takayama magistracy office (jin'ya), whose examination room was roofed like its court, the torture is reported to have been done in the jail in the town.
