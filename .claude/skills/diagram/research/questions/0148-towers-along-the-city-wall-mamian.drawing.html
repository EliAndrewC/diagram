How our maps space and draw wall towers

Every city wall on our maps carries towers, drawn as rectangles standing out from the wall and turned square to it. How close they stand follows how much war the city has seen: about 200 ft apart on a city that has stood sieges, about 330 ft on a garrisoned one, and nearly 400 ft on one long at peace. Those spacings all fall within the ones the record finds on surviving walls, from about 160 ft at Pingyao to nearly 400 ft at Xi'an.

  • A city's defense setting is a knob, and follows the wars it has seen.
    A city that has been raided or besieged is towered densely, one long at peace sparsely, and the garrison setting is the default. That a region's raids drove its cities to build is in the record: each of Ming Shanxi's three largest waves of wall-building followed a Mongol raid. The barbican, too, was mostly a frontier city's device under the Han dynasty and the Tang, and was brought into the interior cities when the Northern Song set out to strengthen their defenses. That a border city was towered as hard as it could afford, and a city long at peace was not, is this project's reading, and how many towers a given history buys is this project's calibration: no source read counts towers against sieges.
  • On a city that has stood sieges, every point of the wall is within 197 ft of two towers.
    So its towers stand about that far apart, a dense ring for a border fortress. The reach is this project's calibration against Pingyao's 50 m (~164 ft) spacing, and is shorter than any bow's range in the record.
  • On a garrisoned city, the default, every point of the wall is within 328 ft of two towers.
    A looser ring. The reach rests on the Tang archers' opening range of sixty paces, about 92 m (~302 ft) at the old reckoning's five chi to the pace and about 30.7 cm (~12 in) to the Tang chi, rounded up by about a tenth. Applying the old reckoning's pace to the Tang chi is this project's assumption.
  • On a city long at peace, every point of the wall is within 197 ft of one tower, which sets the towers about 394 ft apart.
    The midpoints between towers are still covered by two. That is Xi'an's spacing.
  • A tower's reach is counted from its parapet, taken as 36 ft out from its center.
    The archers shot from the parapet round the tower's top, not from a single point. The 36 ft is this project's GUESS. Whether the towers reach the whole wall is checked about every 54 ft along it.
  • Four stretches of wall are left out of the coverage count.
    Each is, by this project's GUESS, already the most defended ground on the wall, and our maps seat no tower there, so the count leaves them out rather than demand towers no map could place:
    • within 390 ft of a gate;
    • within 165 ft of a gate's guard buildings;
    • within 135 ft of a water gate's opening, a grated passage flanked by two towers of its own;
    • within 186 ft of the gate of a walled ward where the ward's fence meets the wall, because the ward's gate was guarded. That a ward's fence runs up to the rampart at all is this project's GUESS, compared with the Tang walled wards' own form at The street along the inside of a city wall (shunchengjie)
  • Every city wall carries at least six towers, and no stretch without one spans more than about 75 degrees of the way round the city.
    This floor is a convention of this project's, and it holds even where the reach rule would be met with fewer towers.
  • The towers keep a regular rhythm.
    No gap between two towers on the open wall falls below seven tenths of that map's own median gap, the paired towers at gates and water gates excepted. The Song dynasty's sources give the towers fixed intervals, and a doubled-up pair reads as a mistake.
  • Each tower is turned square to the wall where it stands, within about 15 degrees.
    A tower on a stretch running at 30 degrees slants 30 degrees rather than sitting as a box squared to the map.
  • Every wall and gate tower is drawn as a rectangle.
    The tower's platform is wider along the wall than deep, about 1.7 to 1, near the proportion of Xi'an's, 20 m (~66 ft) wide and 12 m (~39 ft) out. The tower building set on it, and the gate towers, run nearer square, so no ratio is forced there.
  • Corner towers are drawn square too, a deliberate deviation.
    A study of Song dynasty city building finds a city's corner towers made curved on purpose, at the turn of the wall and at a barbican's angle; our maps square every corner with the rest of the ring and draw no round tower.
