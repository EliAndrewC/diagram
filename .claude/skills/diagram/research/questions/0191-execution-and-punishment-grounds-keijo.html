Execution and punishment grounds (keijo)

Edo, the shogun's capital, had two great execution grounds, Kozukappara to the north and Suzugamori to the south, each on the highway at one of the city's entrances. Anyone going in or out of Edo had to pass beside one, and the shogunate meant the sight to forestall crime. They were small: Suzugamori, in use for over two centuries, was a strip of about a third of an acre. China did otherwise: under the Qing dynasty an important execution ground of the capital, at a crossroads of Beijing, was called the Vegetable Market Execution Ground.

The lesser punishments were shown inside the town. In China an offender could be made to wear the cangue for months as a public humiliation, while beating with the cane or the stick was a punishment of the code, applied by the magistrate's own runners.

In Japan the execution ground stood outside the town.

  • Edo's two great execution grounds stood on the highways at the city's entrances.
    Kozukappara stood on the Nikko road at Edo's northern entrance, Suzugamori on the Tokaido at its southern one. The pages read call the pair Edo's "two great execution grounds".
  • Why were the grounds placed on the highways?
    To be seen, says a popular-history site: anyone entering or leaving Edo had no choice but to pass beside an execution ground, and the shogunate exposed so cruel a sight to forestall crime. No page read gives death pollution as the reason for the siting.
  • How far out of Edo did Kozukappara stand?
    A little under 2 ri, about 5 miles, from Nihonbashi. It was placed in the town just south of the post town of Senju, and Senju lay 2 ri from Nihonbashi.
  • Suzugamori measured about 74 m (~243 ft) by 16 m (~53 ft).
    A survey of 1695 gives its frontage as 40 ken and its depth as 9 ken, about a third of an acre.
  • Suzugamori was in use for 220 years.
    It opened in 1651 and closed in 1871.
  • How many were put to death at Suzugamori?
    The page on it gives 100,000 to 200,000 in its 220 years, but only as something "said". That would be some 450 to 900 a year.
  • The executioners were outcastes.
    The outcaste eta did lower-official work such as the executioner's and the arresting officer's. In this setting, every execution but a samurai's is done by the burakumin.
  • Outcaste quarters often stood at the village's edge or beside the river.
    They were often in ground unfit for farming, at the edge of the village or beside the river. Where their quarters stood is at Burakumin quarters and caste zoning.
  • Were the executed buried with the town's dead?
    Not at Kozukappara. There the bodies were left exposed, with only earth thrown over them, and in 1667 the abbot of the Ekoin temple founded a hall on adjoining ground to bury the dead and pray for them. The townspeople's own dead lay in temple graveyards, which many castle towns gathered at the town's outer edge as a line of defense.
  • A boundary stone marked where the settlement ended.
    Where a settlement ended, and so where "outside the town" began, was itself marked. The dosojin is a deity enshrined, mostly as a stone or a stone image, at the boundary of a settlement, between the village and the world outside, and at crossroads. Its other name, sae no kami, means "the blocking deity", and it is understood to keep intruders from outside.
  • Edo's main jail held the accused and the condemned.
    The jail at Tenmacho confined those awaiting judgment and held the convicted until their sentence was carried out. Imprisonment was imposed as a sentence only exceptionally, so the jail was nearer to a modern detention house than to a prison. Where a city keeps its jail is at Where does a city keep its jail, and how big is it?
  • Edo's jail carried out beheadings and floggings on its own premises.
    Beheading, flogging and tattooing were all done at the Tenmacho jail itself.
  • A village could banish one of its own.
    A villager who broke the village's own code could be banished from it.

Qing China executed inside its capital, and only once the court had reviewed the sentence.

  • Qing Beijing executed at a city crossroads.
    Caishikou, also known as the Vegetable Market Execution Ground, stood at the crossroads of Xuanwumen Outer Street and Luomashi Street.
  • A Chinese death sentence was reviewed each autumn at court.
    Under the Qing, the autumn assize decided each year whether death sentences were to be carried out. The Board of Punishments, the Court of Judicial Review and the Censorate heard the cases together, and the procedure applied to the prisoners held in the provinces awaiting execution.
  • Where in China was a confirmed death sentence carried out?
    At the county seat where the crime was done, in this project's reading, since a public death deterred where it was seen. It is a GUESS: the page on the autumn assize gives its outcomes and nothing on where a confirmed sentence was carried out.
  • How often would a small county have seen an execution?
    Perhaps once in five to ten years. This is a GUESS: at one to three executions a year for every 100,000 inhabitants, a county of 7,000 to 8,000 would reach the death penalty that seldom, and sweeps against bandits would add batches. No page we read gives a yearly count of executions.

The lesser punishments of Chinese law were given in the town.

  • The cangue was a public punishment.
    It was used for public humiliation and corporal punishment in East Asia, and could be worn for as long as several months in severe cases. One photographed cangue bears a label sealed by the magistrate of Shanghai County. No page we read says the offender's crime was written on it.
  • Beating was a punishment of Chinese law.
    Two of the Five Punishments, the five grades of penalty in Chinese law, were beating with a light bamboo cane and beating with a large stick.
  • Where in China was a beating given?
    At court, before the magistrate, in this project's reading. The magistrate's runners stood on both sides of the court during a trial and applied minor punishment, but no page read says where a beating took place.
