Who kept a southern Chinese village's fengshui woods, and what could villagers take from them?

In Huizhou, a hill prefecture where Anhui, Zhejiang and Jiangxi meet, one lineage's covenant of 1572 forbade any household to claim as its own the trees on the ridges running down to its village's houses and tombs, on the hill facing them and at its water mouth, and lineages closed their groves to cutting by rules they agreed in council and carved on stone. At one village a tree brought down by wind or snow went to common use, and one needed for a proper purpose was taken only after telling everyone; one who cut a tree paid for a performance of opera. In a Hong Kong village today the custom lets villagers into their wood for firewood on one or two days a year.

The rules below come from Huizhou, from its lineages' written codes and from its steles, mostly of the Qing dynasty (1644-1912); no older rule from the Pearl River Delta, where the record's measured groves stand, was found. Where the groves stood and what they were for is at Groves around a southern Chinese village.

  • Who owned a village's grove?
    Held in common, not by any household. The covenant the Chen lineage of Wentang, in Qimen county, drew up in 1572 calls the ridges running to its houses and tombs, the hill facing them and the water mouth the blood and veins of the ancestors, tells every household to nurture their trees, and forbids any to treat them as its own property or to dig, damage or steal from them; an offender was to be dealt with jointly, by a penalty laid by all.
  • Who made the rules, and who enforced them?
    The lineage itself, in council. Village associations, their lineage heads and the local elite proposed and debated the rules together, wrote them down once they agreed, and carved them on a stele, usually set in the ancestral hall; Yeyuan's stele of 1813 is set in the wall of the Wang lineage's hall, and the Wang living in the village oversaw it. Of the thirty or so Huizhou steles protecting forests, one is from the Ming dynasty and twenty-nine from the Qing; some were the villages' and lineages' own, others were issued by the authorities - the province, the prefecture or a county - on their own account or at the request of a lineage or the local gentry.
  • What did cutting a grove's tree cost?
    At Yeyuan, in Qimen county, the 1813 stele forbids cutting the trees sheltering the tombs and the water mouth, and fines an offender one performance of opera, a fine of an opera or of wine that the historian who collected the steles calls peculiar to Huizhou. In the Hong lineage's rules at Jiangcun, in Xiuning county, a member who cut a tomb hill's trees was expelled from the ancestral hall and punished besides. At Lai Chi Wo, in Hong Kong, a modern account of the village's custom, citing no older record, says anyone who damaged the wood was fined and publicly denounced.
  • What could villagers take from it?
    Little, and only with everyone's knowledge. At Yeyuan a tree brought down by wind or snow was announced to all and taken for common use, and one needed for a proper purpose was taken after all were told. At Jiangcun, under the Yongzheng emperor (1723-1735), even a dead tree on a tomb hill was left to fall of itself, and the fallen tree went to common use. Jiangcun's is a rule for tomb hills; outside Huizhou, only Lai Chi Wo's modern custom says what villagers might take from their wood: sticks for firewood, gathered on one or two days a year.
  • What happened when the rules failed?
    The grove was closed again. At Wangkou, in Wuyuan county, lawless men had been stealing the screen hill's trees since 1778, so in 1785 a licentiate and others agreed new rules and closed the hill once more, none ever to enter it and do it harm. Why Wangkou kept that hill planted is at Groves around a southern Chinese village.
