How our maps draw field drains (akusuiro)

Our maps draw a settlement's drains as dug channels, each a collector along the low line of its field, gathering what runs out of the paddies, and its run onward to the passing brook, to a pond at the field's foot, or off the edge of the map. Each starts as narrow as the smallest ditches that feed the paddies and widens to its outfall, and it runs across the fall of the land, as a collector does, so on some maps its outfall lies up the page.

  • The irrigation ditch and the drainage ditch are two things a reader can hover.
    The irrigation ditch is the head race, the supply canals and the delivery ditches that bring water to the paddies; the drainage ditch is the collector along the low line and its run onward.
  • The maps keep supply and drainage in separate ditches.
    A supply net above and one collector below is an Edo-period form at the scale of a district's trunk canals and its drain (the Minuma reclamation of 1728, at Irrigation canals and how they are laid out (yosuiro)), and only its field end, a ditch to every plot, is modern (Irrigation canals and how they are laid out (yosuiro)).
  • The older forms, where water passed from paddy to paddy over the bunds or one channel both fed and drained the fields, are not drawn as a field's layout.
    Both are attested (Field drains (akusuiro)). The maps lay every field out in the separated form, which gives every hamlet a ditch between its plots for a reader to hover. Below the last ditch drawn, though, the water passes from paddy to paddy as in the older form; that flow is too small to draw (How our maps draw where a field meets its ditch). One channel both feeding and draining the fields is not drawn at all.
  • A drain is a dug channel that reaches a watercourse, never a brook of its own.
    This is the map's rule rather than the record's. Where the passing brook comes near the collector's outfall, the drain joins it, as a district's drainage returned to the river to be taken up below; where it does not, the drain runs on as a ditch to the edge of the map, or to the pond at the field's foot where the map places one (How our maps lay out irrigation canals (yosuiro)).
  • A drain that ends in a pond of its own is our own guess.
    It is a GUESS: no page we read describes one.
  • About one hamlet map in three sends its drain to the brook or off the map rather than to a pond.
    Which way a hamlet's drain goes is rolled from the map's seed: about two maps in three lay a pond at the field's foot, and one in three send the drain to the passing brook or, where none is in reach, off the edge of the map. A map whose pond would have to sit far below the fields sends its drain off the map as well. The odds are a GUESS: no page we read describes a drain ending in a pond at all, so none says how often one did.
  • The pond at a field's foot covers about three parts in a hundred of the paddy it drains.
    In a hamlet of fifteen households it is about 230 by 150 ft, about 0.6 acre of water below about 20 acres of paddy, and it grows with the hamlet so that the share stays the same. That size is a GUESS too. The two or three tenths of its land that a Song-dynasty manual gives a pond is for a pond that waters its fields (How our maps draw reservoir ponds (tameike)), and a pond that only takes a field's runoff is one no page we read describes.

A collector crosses the slope of its field, and on a map whose land falls at a slant that can look wrong.

  • A collector runs across the slope, more than about fifty degrees off it.
    It has to gather the runoff of every column of plots, and the field drains of a drainage manual meet their collector at right angles at the field's lower end. That the collector then meets the stream at an angle is this record's own inference from those two facts.
  • The water only ever moves downhill.
    The end of a drain that lets its water go is its lower-ground end, never uphill of its head, and the brook that takes the water away heads downhill.
  • No rule keeps a drain running down the slope.
    Such a rule would ban the very collectors that drainage practice builds, so it was declined, a decision rather than an oversight. The two rules above say what is actually constrained: a collector crosses the slope, and its water only moves downhill.
  • On a map that falls at a slant, a drain's outfall can sit up the page.
    On a map high to the northwest, the collector runs from southwest to northeast, across the fall, and its outfall can sit at the northeast end - lower on the ground than the end it starts from, and also up the page, where the eye expects high ground. The water is not running uphill; the page is.
  • The brook curves out of the collector's exit, so the junction reads as a turn.
    Where a drain's outfall sits up the page and the water could seem to run uphill, the maps fix the reading rather than the hydrology. Contour lines were declined as awkward on a map already dense with line work, and flow arrows because the channels are drawn too thin to carry one. Instead the brook bends out of the collector's exit heading over the first stretch of its course, so the junction reads as the collector turning down the valley rather than meeting the stream at a hard corner. The turn is held under 65 degrees, each heading averaged over about 40 ft of its own line so a short, jittery segment cannot fool the measure.
  • The angle at which a dug outfall meets the brook is whatever the ground gives it.
    The turn held under 65 degrees is where a brook flows on out of a collector's own exit; where a drain instead runs on as a ditch and meets the passing brook, nothing here rules on the angle. The record has no figure for the angle at which a dug outfall was cut to meet a watercourse, and the maps' rule for a canal leaving its parent applies to an offtake, not to an outfall.

A drain gathers water as it goes, so the maps draw it narrow where it starts and full where it leaves the field.

  • A drain starts as narrow as the delivery ditches end, about 1.2 ft across.
    The delivery ditches taper to a thread as they give up their water to the paddies, so the drain that receives it begins as a thread and reaches its full width at the outfall, where it carries the runoff of every paddy it serves. On a hamlet map its head is drawn at the 1.5 ft floor that keeps a channel visible. How it widens is at How our maps draw a channel narrowing along its run, and its widths beside the other channels' at How our maps draw channel widths.
  • Why is a drain's head so narrow?
    The Rites of Zhou cuts its finest channel one chi wide and doubles it at the head of the field to two chi, about nine inches and about 1.5 ft on this record's reading of the chi. On this project's reckoning, a drain carries almost no water at its head, so its width comes from the spade that cuts it rather than from its flow, and the drawn head, about 1.2 ft (1.5 ft at a hamlet map's floor), lies within the span of those two classical channels.
  • A supply canal narrows the other way.
    It gathers no water along its run: it is widest at its head and narrows as its delivery ditches draw water off, from about 4.5 ft to about 1.5 ft along the upper edge of the fields (How our maps draw channel widths).

Every field on a map carries its own fall, and every drain is drawn to run the right way down the fall of its own field. Which end of a collector its water leaves by is what tells which way it runs.

  • Every discharge channel names the field it drains.
    A collector then matches its own discharge channels exactly, and nothing is inferred from where they lie.
  • The end of a discharge channel that counts is the one farther from its drain.
    A discharge may leave from partway along a collector: one map's west drain runs off the edge of the map from the middle of its run. So the end that counts is whichever of the channel's two ends lies farther from the drain.
  • Where a collector's water could leave at several places, the lowest is taken as its outfall.
    The west drain that runs off the edge of the map from the middle of its run also sends an overflow culvert back to the stream at its far end, and another drain runs off the bottom of the map at both ends. So every end carrying evidence of a discharge - a named channel, a brook alongside, the edge of the map - is gathered, and the lowest is taken. The rule then bites only where every piece of evidence puts the discharge at the drain's high end: then the water is running backwards.
  • An outfall at the edge of the map must be at its downhill edge.
    Nearness to an edge is evidence of a discharge only when that edge is downhill. One city's comb field has its head about 84 ft from the top of the map, inside the 96 ft within which a drain's end counts as reaching the edge and on the upslope side, so nearness alone would take that high end for an outfall, with the water running backwards to it. Nearness to a stream is evidence on its own, since a brook takes the water away wherever it lies.
  • A drain cut short inside a city wall is not judged as a collector.
    A drainage ditch inside a city wall is cut off short of the patrol strip and dropped through the wall into a culvert, as a walled city carried water through its wall in a conduit and let a river out by a water gate. What remains on the page is only the last leg to that outfall: one such drain is a single straight leg about 400 ft long, where every uncut drain on the same map runs 480 to 690 ft. Judging that stub's bearing against a collector's rule would be a category error, so a cut drain is exempt from it.
