How a town is zoned: shops on the street, housing behind

A town's street front was its commerce. Shops and workshops lined the high street, each facing the road on a lot narrow at the front and deep behind, and a merchant family lived in the same building as its shop. The poorer households lived behind them, in tenements on the back streets. The best-attested case is Kyoto, where in the Edo period the townsmen fell into three bands by how much street frontage their houses held, from the great merchant houses down to the small ones and the renters.

So the ground between a town's road and its fields was sorted by wealth, the shops on the road and the rented rooms behind, and each house's door faced its street. Most of that record is of the big cities, Kyoto above all; in a post town strung along a highway, the back of the lot was often the household's vegetable garden. How wide each band's frontage was in feet is at The town's street front: shop widths, how close the houses stood, stories and roofs (machinami); what one merchant's house holds from its shop front to the back of its lot is at Merchants' townhouses (machiya).

  • A merchant family lived in its own shop.
    The Kyoto townhouse is built on the coexistence of work and dwelling: its front, facing the street, is where customers are received and work is done, and a long, narrow earth-floored passage runs from the entrance through to the back. Its character was summed up as "work and residence in one".
  • A Kyoto townhouse lot was narrow at the street and deep behind.
    Because the plot was narrow at the frontage and deep, a Kyoto townhouse lot was called an "eel's bed". Many had a frontage of about 2 ken (~12 ft) and a depth of 10 to 12 ken (~59-72 ft).
  • The street front was held by the wealthier, the back lots by those a rank below.
    On each lot, the front part along the main street, about five ken (~30 ft) deep, held a shop, opened by the landowner or by a fairly wealthy merchant renting the ground; the back part was reached by the alleys between the shops. The shop-holders on the street were a stratum a rank above the common folk of the back tenements, roughly the wholesalers and brokers. Among the townsmen there was a gulf from the house-owning class with a shop on the main street down to the back-street tenants.
  • Most townspeople below the shop-holding merchants rented a tenement room on a back street.
    Merchant houses of the middle stratum and above kept independent shops on the main street; almost all the other townspeople and craftsmen lived as tenants in the row-houses of the back streets.
  • One kind of tenement row was named for the size of its rooms, about 9 by 12 ft.
    One named unit, the "nine-shaku-two-ken" tenement, was 9 shaku (~9 ft) wide and 2 ken (~12 ft) deep.
  • Kyoto's townsmen fell into three bands by frontage.
    They were divided into those holding large houses of ten ken (~60 ft) or more of frontage, those holding ordinary houses of four or five ken (~24-30 ft), and those holding small houses of one and a half to two ken (~9-12 ft), or renting. The division had been growing since the middle of the Edo period.
  • No page we read says how many households stood in each band.
    The pages give the bands of frontage and the main-street shops against the back-street tenants, but no numbers.
  • Where did a town's outcaste quarter stand?
    The outcaste settlements stood in many cases at the edge of a village, beside a river or on other ground unsuited to farming. No page we read places an outcaste quarter in a town's plan; that it stood at the town's edge on marginal ground is this project's reading of the village evidence.

The same order held in at least one Chinese county seat, though there the shop was the street-side building of a courtyard house.

  • In one Chinese county seat the shop stood in front, the workshop behind.
    The shops along the old street of a county seat mostly follow the northern plan of one or several courtyards: the building on the street is mostly two stories, the ground floor for trade and workshops, the upper floor a storeroom, "shop in front, workshop behind", with lodging.

A smaller town on a highway, a post town, is recorded differently: its houses faced the street as Kyoto's did, but what lay behind them was often a garden, where the record of the cities has tenement rows.

  • A town house's door faced its street.
    A townhouse usually ran its ridge parallel to the street, but some turned the gable to it. The two forms are named for which face of the building holds the main entrance, the long side under the ridge or the gable end, so either way the door is on the street.
  • A post town's lots were strips of nearly one depth.
    The lots on both sides of the highway were planned strips of nearly one depth and differing frontage. Whether the house stood right on the road or behind a front yard is at The town's street front: shop widths, how close the houses stood, stories and roofs (machinami).
  • Behind a post-town house was often its vegetable garden.
    Apart from the officials who ran the post station, the households usually farmed as well, the back of the lot often a vegetable garden.
  • Were a town's rows only one house deep?
    Often, it seems: behind the house was often its garden, not a second row of houses. No page we read counts the depth of a town lot's back rows, and the garden behind a post-town house is attested only as often, not always. That a town lot had a second row of houses only where a back lane reached it is our guess. In a city the tenement rows stand one row or two deep along a lane behind the shops, at which way a city house's door faces; where a town's laborers live at all is at Laborers' housing: back-lot tenements (uradana) and the master laborer's house.

A city sorts its shops and its poor housing the same way on a larger ground, at where the shops stand in a city; the walled compound of a city's richest merchants is at why a city has only one or two walled merchant compounds.
