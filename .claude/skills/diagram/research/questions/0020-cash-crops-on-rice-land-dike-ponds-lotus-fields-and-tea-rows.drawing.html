How our maps lay cash crops over a village's rice land

A village map may carry one cash crop over its rice, chosen settlement by settlement: dike-ponds, lotus or tea, or none. Each takes only the ground its crop needed in the history - the low, wet plots for ponds and lotus, the dry hillside above the rice for tea - and a map without that ground draws no cash crop at all, rather than scattering one across ordinary rice.

  • The ground decides which plots a cash crop may take, and a separate share decides how many of them it does.
    The share is of the eligible ground, never of the whole field. As a share of the whole field it would decide nothing, because the eligible ground is always the smaller, so every settlement would convert all of its low ground. A share well short of everything is what the history shows: the ponds of Longshan, an early pond township of the Pearl River delta, were 18% of its hill, dry land and ponds in 1581, and by Lake Tai mulberry stood on the polder's margin while rice stayed the main crop.
  • The low ground a cash crop may take is the two lowest rows of plots on the valley floor - a calibrated liberty.
    No page we read gives how wide a valley's wet bottom ran, and the estimate of a lotus-growing village's share, a few percent to about 10-15% of its field, is itself an interpolation. Taking only the single row of plots along the drain put lotus at about 2% of the field: inside that range, but so sparse that villages stopped looking different from one another, which is what the cash crop is drawn for. So the maps take the two lowest rows, the upper part of the range, for a reason that is not historical: so that the map reads.
  • Converted plots are set in patches rather than scattered evenly - a GUESS the map cannot show.
    Conversion is taken to have spread plot by plot from one household to its neighbors, though no page we read says how it spread, so the maps place converted plots in patches. On the map the eligible ground is always a thin, continuous strip along the wet bottom, where patches and an even scatter look much alike, so the patches are asserted from the history, not shown by the drawing.

Each crop then has its own rule.

  • Dike-ponds take some of a village's low plots, not all of them.
    Ponds stood among the rice rather than replacing it - as late as the 1980s a quarter of the Pearl River delta's dike-pond heartland was still in rice - so the dike-pond cash crop converts some of the low plots, in patches, and leaves the rest in rice.
  • A village whose ponds took nearly all its land is drawn as dike-ponds throughout, not held to the low ground.
    Where the ponds took nearly everything - 75% of the cultivated land at Longshan (Longshanbao) in the Qianlong reign, and mulberry ponds and no fish ponds at Longjiang and Longshan - the ponds had engulfed ordinary ground too, so a village laid out wholly as dike-ponds is not held to the low-ground rule. The two are one system at two scales. The dike-pond itself is at Dike-ponds: fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes (sangji yutang).
  • Whether such a village keeps a little rice among its ponds is rolled village by village.
    The records give both. At Longshan in the Qianlong reign "outside the dwellings all was pond", though the same account puts its ponds at three-quarters of the farmland, and in 1678 Qu Dajun puts Jiujiang's fish ponds at eight tenths of its land and its fields at two. So each village drawn as dike-ponds throughout takes one of two forms: every parcel a pond, or about one parcel in ten left in standing rice among the ponds. No page in this record says how many such villages kept any rice, so the even odds between the two are this project's GUESS. The one parcel in ten is a degree between none and Jiujiang's two tenths, a calibrated liberty.
  • Lotus is drawn in standing water, on low ground only.
    Lotus grows in standing water from about 30 cm (~12 in) to 2.5 m (~8 ft) deep, which only the wettest, lowest plots hold, so a lotus field never stands on high ground.
  • Lotus fields may be drawn larger than the record supports, as a map drawing convention.
    Lotus fields and lotus-growing villages both existed before modern times, as the history behind this section records, so the maps keep the lotus field; drawing it larger than the record supports, so that it shows, is a convention, not a modern form. A share in the upper part of that range fits a village in a lotus district, where rice was given up for lotus, better than an ordinary village with a few deep plots. The one share we read is modern: at Iwakuni, a lotus-root district in Japan, lotus stood at about six times its rice in 2015.
  • Lotus grown in turn with rice in ordinary paddy is not drawn.
    Lotus could be grown in turn with rice in the same field, a household's choice from year to year that the maps do not model, and drawn on ordinary paddy it would look like an even random scatter across the rice.
  • Tea is drawn as rows of bushes on the dry ground above the highest irrigation ditch.
    Tea grew on the lower hillside and never on the low land, so its lower edge is the highest ditch that waters a paddy, and a map draws tea only where it has dry hillside above its rice: a hill site, or the slope above a village's terraced paddies.
  • Tea is not drawn on contour terraces.
    The terraces of a slope are drawn for its paddy, not its tea: neat contour terraces of tea are taken to be modern, a GUESS.
  • Bund tea, the second form of tea, is not drawn yet.
    Tea had two forms: the hillside garden, which takes plots, and in Japan a single row along the field banks, which takes none. With two attested forms, the tea a village carries would be rolled between them; our maps draw only the hillside garden, so bund tea is a known gap.
  • On a rice polder, our maps put mulberry on the dike crest, not in the paddies.
    The record of the Wanchun polder has its mulberry in rows along the ridge of the dike, so a polder that is not a dike-pond village takes no mulberry into its paddies. Why its dikes were planted is at Polder dikes: what they were made of, how big, and what grew on them.
