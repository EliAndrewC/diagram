Grass hills and fodder meadows (kusayama, magusaba)

A Japanese village kept common grass ground, the magusaba or fodder ground, where its inhabitants cut fodder for their animals, the green manure their paddies needed, and fuel. It was far larger than the fields: grass ground more than ten times their area is said to have been needed. It lay beyond the fields, on hills kept bare of trees for their grass - the kusayama, or grass hills - and on river floodplains, beside the village's fuel wood (Village fuel woods and their coppice (satoyama)).

Cutting for grass, brush and wood left much of the hill country around Japan's villages bare or open by the early modern period, and the hills of China were stripped too. In south China a village's fengshui grove, protected by a taboo on cutting, stayed wooded, while beyond the immediate vicinity of the village's watershed the hills' vegetation was commonly disturbed by people and the higher slopes kept as meadow and scrub.

  • The fodder ground was usually one village's commons.
    A Japanese dictionary defines the magusaba as grass ground where fodder was cut, most of it a commons the inhabitants of the area used together. As a rule it was the commons of one village or hamlet.
  • The fodder ground went by many names.
    A Japanese dictionary also calls it the kusakariba, the kayaba and the "fertilizer-grass hill". In the Edo period it was a moor for gathering fodder and fertilizer, and the ground where the fields' green manure was cut was called by the same name.
  • At Fuchu, villages fought over the grass as the paddies spread.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on a dispute over the fodder ground at Fuchu says that as new paddies were opened, the ground for cutting fertilizer grass shrank, and villages scrambled against each other for what little was left.
  • How large was a village's grass ground against its fields?
    To get enough green manure and fodder, grass hills more than ten times the area of the fields are said to have been needed in the middle and late Edo period. A lecture handout from Takamori town repeats the same calculation. So wide grass ground was needed around the paddies, and the manure was cut on the hill and moor commons too.
  • A Takamori farm record of 1854 shows about two horse-loads of green manure put on every tan of paddy.
    A lecture handout from Takamori town cites a record for 1854 (Kaei 7) of about 12 to 15 horse-loads on 7 tan, about two to the tan.
  • A grass hill was cleared of its trees and kept clear for its grass.
    Its trees were deliberately felled so that the whole hill carried only grass, and it was kept free of trees to supply the dead grass the village's paddies needed for fertilizer.
  • What did a grass hill look like?
    Its cover varied, from open grass to a thin wood chiefly of pine up to 3 to 4 m (~10-13 ft) tall.
  • A small grass hill might serve one paddy alone.
    Sometimes a small grass hill was set aside for a particular paddy's sole use, and called a tazuke kusayama.
  • On the Kanto plain, a river's floodplain served as fodder ground.
    Villages whose own fodder ground ran short as new fields were opened came to depend on the floodplain of the Arakawa river.
  • A village with no hills borrowed another's.
    Villages without hills asked villages that had them, borrowed the hill, and took the grass and wood they needed there.
  • How much thatch meadow did a settlement keep?
    The same ground, or ground beside it, gave thatch: one of the fodder ground's names is the kayaba, the thatch-grass ground. In Aso, in Kyushu, a settlement of 50 to 60 households had to keep 20 to 30 hectares (~49-74 acres) of thatch meadow to meet its needs, cut in February and March once the leaves had fallen from the stems.

The grass hills were one part of a wider stripping of the hills around Japan's villages, for grass, brush and fuel.

  • The hill country around a Japanese village was a mosaic.
    The English Wikipedia article on the satoyama describes a mosaic of mixed woods, paddies and dry rice fields, grassland, streams and ponds.
  • By the early modern period most of Japan's village hills were red pine wood, grass hill or bare hill.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on the satoyama says so of most of Japan's satoyama.
  • In 1894, a generation after Japan began to modernize, a professor of agriculture reckoned that only 30% of its forest land was under trees.
    Shiga Taizan, of Tokyo Imperial University's College of Agriculture, put the other 70% as bare red hills and bald peaks.
  • Japan's forests were considerably degraded in the Edo period, says the Forestry Agency.
    Felling grew with demand for fuel, charcoal and building timber, and a hill in the south of Shiga Prefecture was so stripped it was called "the bald of Tanakami".
  • How did one Shiga village's common hill come to be bare?
    Probably by cutting brush. The map of Minami-Tsuda, on the outskirts of Omihachiman in Shiga, drawn at the start of the Meiji era, draws most of the village's common hill as bald. Felling standing trees there was forbidden but cutting brush was free, and brush cutting is thought to have taken the hill down to bare ground.
  • A Shiga village with a bare hill bought most of its brush from outside.
    At Minami-Tsuda brush ran so short that over eight tenths of the year's brush was bought in, and the shortage is traced back to the Genroku era (1688-1704).

China's village hills were stripped too, for fields as well as fuel, while a village protected its fengshui grove.

  • China's hill forests were cleared for fields, fuel and building.
    A Chinese forestry paper names fire clearing and fire hunting, farming, war, building and firewood and charcoal as the old causes, and says that under the Qing a fast-growing population sent great numbers of "shed people" into the mountain forests of central China to clear and plant the slopes.
  • Why was a south Chinese village's fengshui grove green when the hill beyond it was not?
    Because the grove was protected and the hill was not. A study of fengshui groves found villages punishing tree cutting by sacrificing the culprit's pig, while beyond the immediate vicinity of the village's own watershed the vegetation was commonly disturbed and the higher slopes often kept as meadow and scrub. Reading the taboo as the reason the grove is lush and the slope behind it is not is this project's reading of that study.
  • In the Great Leap Forward, some south Chinese villages spared their fengshui groves by cutting timber and bamboo outside them.
    The study of fengshui groves places a village's fengshui forest immediately behind and upslope from it, on its "master mountain", and, among the reasons groves survived the Great Leap Forward's peak demand for fuel wood, names bamboo and timber cut outside them.
