Keeping a village shrine or temple: its monk, the monk's dwelling (kuri), its records and its land

Before 1868, in the Edo period, a Japanese village shrine was kept in one of several ways. Until an order of 1868 separated the kami, the gods of the shrines, from the Buddhas, many shrines had a Buddhist temple attached, whose monk lived there and performed the shrine's rites in Buddhist form. Many small shrines had no priest of their own, and the parishioners took turns keeping them. Where a hereditary family of Shinto priests served a shrine, it often lived near it, in houses of its own. How often a village shrine had a monk of its own, no page we read counts.

A monk's home at a temple was its kuri, "the place where a temple's monks live". At an ordinary temple it was often much like an ordinary house, and often the temple's office as well; the kuri we found measured all belong to temples of standing. The temple certified its parishioners under the Edo registration of households, its precinct could be exempt from tax, and its parish households paid it for their rites of the dead.

  • Before 1868 many shrines had a Buddhist temple attached, whose monk kept the shrine.
    These shrine-temples, the jingūji, "remained common for over a millennium". At small shrines "the monks of the jingūji sometimes did the managing and performed the rites".
  • The shrine's rites were performed in Buddhist form, by a monk who lived at the temple.
    The shrine's rites, "such as sutra-reading before the kami, were performed in Buddhist form" by the bettō, "the head of the shrine monks", who lived at the temple. Some of the jingūji "in which the bettō resided" were called bettō-ji.
  • The shrine's monk was both a Shinto priest and a Buddhist monk.
    That monk "was tellingly called shasō (社僧) or 'shrine Buddhist monk', and was both a shrine priest and a Buddhist monk".
  • An order of 1868 separating the kami from the Buddhas ended the shrine monks' role.
    The first order separating the kami from the Buddhas, in April 1868, "ordered the defrocking of shasō and bettō (shrine monks performing Buddhist rites at Shinto shrines)", who "were told to become 'shrine priests' (kannushi) and return to their shrines". What it took to end the arrangement says how widespread it was.
  • In the purge of Buddhism that followed, an estimated 30,000 Buddhist buildings were torn down.
    The count covers temples of every kind, not only shrine-temples: "an estimated 30,000 Buddhist structures were demolished between 1868 and 1874".
  • How common was a monk at a village shrine?
    The sources say it in words, not numbers. An encyclopedia says that many provincial shrines of rank had a bettō-ji, which cannot show that a village shrine had one. A study of the Kanazawa area gives as the received view that shrines served by lay priests - Shinto priests who were not Buddhist monks - were, if anything, rare there before 1868, the many shrine monks and mountain ascetics becoming Shinto priests after it; the study itself questions that view in part. No page we read counts how many village shrines had a temple of their own.
  • Small Shinto shrines often had no priest of their own.
    "Small shrines often had no dedicated priest; the parishioners managed them in rotation", and "most village shrines during this period were too poor to have a dedicated priest".
  • A village's shrine guild, the miyaza, kept no full-time priest; its members took the role in turn.
    The miyaza "keeps no full-time priest; the members ... take the priest's role in yearly rotation".
  • A hereditary family of Shinto priests often had its houses near its shrine.
    Such families often "set up their houses near the shrine" they served.

The monk of a shrine-temple lived at the temple, and a temple's monks lived in its kuri.

  • A temple's monks lived at the temple, in its kuri.
    The Japanese encyclopedia defines the kuri as "the place where a temple's monks live".
  • The kuri began as a kitchen.
    In origin it was "a kitchen which prepared meals only for the abbot and his guests".
  • Many an ordinary temple's kuri was much like an ordinary house.
    At an ordinary temple, the encyclopedia says, many kuri are "buildings much like ordinary houses". Even the largest measured kuri resembles "the honmune-zukuri farmhouse characteristic of this region".
  • How large was a village temple's kuri?
    No page we read measures a small village kuri; those measured belong to temples of standing. Chōnen-ji's kuri, in Kawasaki, is "9 ken along the ridge and 5 ken across the beams", with "the head priest's living room ... a twelve-mat room", beside a main hall of "7 ken" by "7 ken". The largest, Tokuun-ji's, is "12 ken along the ridge by 8 ken".
  • Did a temple's hall and dwelling ever share one roof?
    In large temples the kuri is "a separate building". Once that we know of, hall and kuri were one, and it was called unusual. At Kaie-ji, a Zen temple in the city of Sakai, the "main hall and kuri" are "one building: ridge span 25.2 meters, beam span 8.0 meters on the left side, 13.0 meters on the right side" - 83 ft long by 26 to 43 ft deep, some 200 to 330 m² (~2,150-3,550 sq ft). "A main hall and kuri in one building is unusual for temple architecture of the beginning of the Edo period". No second example was found.
  • A domain lord's family temple had a main hall of about 84 by 53 ft.
    For comparison with Kaie-ji's hall and kuri together, a main hall alone at a temple of standing could be larger. The hall of a temple "protected as the family temple of the Ōzeki clan, who were the domain's lords" is "25.5 meters" by "16.3 meters".
  • Some temples had no one living at them.
    The encyclopedia's page on the head priest speaks of temples where "no one resides and a head priest from far away commutes".

A temple's records and its land were matters of the Edo state's registration and taxes.

  • At an ordinary temple the kuri was often the temple's office as well.
    "In ordinary temples it often doubles as the temple office that handles the temple's business."
  • At a small shrine, business was often done at the administrator's own home.
    An online dictionary of Japanese architecture says that at shrines "business was frequently carried on at the private home of the administrator ... especially at small shrines".
  • Under the Edo registration, each household's temple certified that the household was its parishioner.
    "The temple issued the terauke certificate to its parishioners, to prove that they were its parishioners", and "for travel or a change of residence its certificate ... was required".
  • The village headmen, not the temple, compiled each village's yearly register of households and their temples (shumon aratame cho).
    "The compiling of the register was to be done every year, town or village by town or village, by the nanushi or shōya headmen and the town elders".
  • A temple's or a shrine's precinct could be exempt from tax.
    Exempt land, jochi, was land "exempted from annual tax by the shogunate and the daimyo in the Edo period, other than the vermilion-seal and black-seal lands", among it "the precincts of temples and shrines and fields and homesteads holding a document of tax exemption".
  • The sealed land grants to temples and shrines ran from about 10,000 koku down to 1.
    Of the shuinchi, "Even the largest, Kōyasan and Nikkō Tōshōgū, were about 10,000 koku, and the smallest a mere 1 koku".
  • A parish household paid its temple for the rites of the dead.
    "A parish household belongs to a particular temple, entrusts all of its funerary and memorial rites to that temple, and pays fuse."

China, the setting's first model, had both forms side by side: village temples with keepers who lived at them, and small earth-god shrines too small to house anyone.

  • In Qing-dynasty Mianning, the village temples engaged monks and Daoists from elsewhere to live at them as keepers.
    The village temples of Mianning "generally engaged monks and Daoists to reside and keep watch, responsible for guarding the property, cleaning, and managing the temple's incense offerings".
  • In Mianning, the congregation elected its village temple's heads.
    There, "the association heads, as leaders of the village-temple organization, are chosen by election from among the congregation."
  • Temples to local gods were run by local managers and village communities.
    Such temples "are established and administered by local managers, village communities, lineage congregations and worship associations".
  • Did anyone live at the earth god's small shrine?
    Probably not at the smallest. An earth-god shrine runs "from a few square meters at the small end to over ten" - some 30 to over 100 sq ft - and at the small end it is too small to house anyone. That nobody kept one is a GUESS from its size: no source we read names a keeper at one, or says there was none.
  • An earth-god temple could be a hall: one founded in the Song dynasty keeps a main hall of about 33 by 23 ft.
    At Xietang, "first built in the Southern Song", the surviving main hall is "three bays wide and one bay deep, 10 meters long and 7 meters wide" - large only against the smallest earth-god shrines, a comparison the page itself does not make.
  • In Mianning, a village temple's land was given or bought for its deity's upkeep.
    Donors gave the temples land outright, or "partly bought and partly donated, to serve as a permanent resource for the deity's incense and lamps".
  • In China, villagers were counted for labor and tax through the lijia.
    The lijia's "greatest function ... lay in allocating corvée labor and collecting tax by head-count", and nothing we read puts a village temple in the registration business.
  • No page we could read says whether a Chinese village temple's land was exempt from tax.
    The pages we read on Chinese temple land say where it came from, not how it was taxed.
