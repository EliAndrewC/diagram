How our maps draw village burial grounds (bochi)

Each village on our maps draws one burial ground: an irregular patch of earth, from about a twentieth to a third of an acre, set with rows of low grave markers and a taller memorial stone or two. This setting cremates its dead and inters their bones, so the ground holds urns, and its size follows the number of inhabitants whose dead lie in it. It stands on the higher ground to hand, never upstream of the houses, with six small stone jizo at its entrance; a map may also draw a cremation ground beside it or on its own. Whether it lies in the yard of the village's shrine or on ground of its own is at How our maps choose where a village's and a hamlet's dead lie.

  • Every funerary feature is drawn at its real size.
    A burial ground or a cremation ground drawn at the wrong size reads as a different kind of place. So the maps do not enlarge one to make it legible; where a mark would vanish at a map's scale, it is drawn at the thinnest mark the map can carry, a map drawing convention.
  • A village's burial ground holds urns, not bodies.
    This setting cremates its dead and then inters their bones, as Japan did, so the ground is sized in urn plots.
  • How large is a village's burial ground drawn?
    It is sized to everyone whose dead lie in it: the village's own households and those of the hamlets that bury there. At 25 to 30 deaths in a thousand a year, the upper half of what Edo village registers give, with a plot reused after about 30 years, a population keeps 0.75 to 0.9 active urn plots for each inhabitant. At 10 to 20 sq ft a plot, that is 7.5 to 18 sq ft an inhabitant: some 260 to 320 plots and 0.06 to 0.15 acre for a village's own ~350 inhabitants, and 600 to 720 plots and 0.14 to 0.33 acre for a village and its hamlets together, ~800.
  • A grave plot is reused after about 30 years, a figure this project chose.
    No page we read gives a premodern term, so the figure is a GUESS.
  • Why is an urn plot taken at 10 to 20 sq ft?
    It is a GUESS: no page gives a premodern urn grave's ground. It is of the order of the graves excavated at an Edo temple ground, its jar burials and wooden coffins, the last of its four kinds of grave, mostly under 1.4 m (~5 ft) long. The step from a grave's size to an urn plot's footing is this project's own.
  • How much ground does a grave with a body in it take?
    No measured premodern urn plot was found, but graves with a body in them were measured, so they check the urn plot from the other side. This project reckons a full-body grave at four to six times an urn plot, 40 to 120 sq ft, a GUESS. The two premodern densities read fall inside it: about 43 sq ft a grave in an excavated Tokyo temple trench of coffins, and about 50 sq ft in a Qing charity graveyard for the drowned.
  • A modern cemetery planner's reckoning lands inside the size we draw.
    A 1934 paper sized a city cemetery by households: one family plot of about 36 sq ft for each household, times a site coefficient of 1.5 to 2. At five inhabitants a household (Households: how many live in a house, and under how many roofs (ie)), that comes to 10.8 to 14.4 sq ft an inhabitant - about 70 households and 0.09 to 0.12 acre for ~350, about 160 households and 0.2 to 0.26 acre for ~800. These are modern city figures, a check on the size we draw and not a ground for it.
  • Tokyo's cemeteries gave each burial about 18 sq ft in 1932.
    The same 1934 paper's table puts the ground per interment at about half a tsubo on average. It too is a modern city figure, a check and not a ground.
  • The burial grounds grow with the population they serve.
    A village's ground stays below a town's 0.25 to 0.75 acre, and a town's below a city's 0.75 to 2 acres, split across its yards. Even a village with its hamlets, ~800 inhabitants, serves fewer than a town's ~1,200. These sizes are this project's arithmetic from the plot figure and the population served. Whether a town's and a city's grounds were that size is at Burial grounds of towns and cities, and paupers' grounds (yizhong)
  • A village's burial ground is drawn as an irregular patch, not a ruled plot.
    No Japanese commoner's burial ground we read of was laid out to a surveyed plan, so the maps give a common ground an irregular edge.
  • A parish ground inside a town's or a city's temple precinct is drawn as a ruled plot.
    It is fitted to the precinct, a map drawing convention.
  • Rows of low grave markers fill the ground, with a taller memorial stone or two.
    The markers stand at about 9 ft spacing, each wholly inside the ground's edge.

Where the ground lies follows from how this setting treats its dead.

  • A village's burial ground lies within the village, beside its shrine or at the edge of the houses.
    Some Japanese villages, the two-grave villages, kept a burial ground for bodies on riverbeds, in the hills or on beaches, and a visiting grave at a temple or a hall within the village. A village that cremates and then inters needs no ground for bodies, so its one ground takes the visiting grave's place: within the village, with the hall that tends it - in this setting the village's shrine, where the history has a temple.
  • A ground apart from the shrine lies downstream of the houses, just beyond the last of them.
    It is never upstream of them on the village's stream. That side is attested only in today's two-grave villages, by a modern survey.
  • No set distance from the houses is drawn.
    The distances that modern surveys measured are of villages as they stand today, and the 60 ken (~360 ft) set in 1884 is a Meiji rule for new graveyards. No distance from before modern times was found, so the maps draw none.
  • Why is the ground drawn on the higher ground to hand?
    The evidence is Chinese: China's pauper grounds, where the unclaimed and the destitute were buried on government land, took high, open ground. Only the modern rule of 1884 asks for dry ground as well, so the maps do not rest on it.
  • A burial ground is never drawn in open water or in a flooded field.
    A grave there would fill with water. A paddy is a flooded field; a thin irrigation channel is not open water.
  • No set-back from water is drawn.
    The only distance found is a modern city's 20 m (~66 ft), so set-backs that widen with the size of the stream or river would rest on a modern rule alone. A ground placed at the village's edge keeps only a strip of bank, about 6 ft, between it and the water.
  • A cremation ground may stand nearer the water than the graves.
    Fire and its washing rites want water at hand, and a fire site does not flood out. This is a GUESS: no page we read places a Japanese cremation ground by the water.

The ground's entrance is marked by stone figures.

  • Six small stone jizo stand in a row at the entrance of a village's burial ground.
    They are attested at graveyards before modern times. A stone jizo is about 2 ft tall, and each is drawn at least large enough to be seen, a map drawing convention.
  • A cremation ground beside the burial ground shares its jizo.
    That the jizo at a burial ground's entrance serve a cremation ground beside it is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • A cremation ground standing on its own draws no jizo.
    No page dated before modern times puts six jizo at a cremation ground standing on its own.
