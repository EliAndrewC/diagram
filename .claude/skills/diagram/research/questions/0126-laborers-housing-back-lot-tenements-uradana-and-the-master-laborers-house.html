Laborers' housing: back-lot tenements (uradana) and the master laborer's house

A laborer in a Japanese city of the Edo period rented a room in a back tenement: a one-story row of single-room units on a narrow lane behind the shops on the street, each unit about 9 by 12 ft, with a well and a privy that everyone on the lot shared. In the great cities most residents rented, in the towns fewer did, and in a post town the households that supplied the station's porters and horses held plots of their own. How the city's tenement lanes run is at Which way does a city house's door face, and how deep do the rows stack?, and where a city puts its poor housing at Where do the shops stand, and where does the poor housing go?.

Above the ordinary laborers the setting sets a small band of rich master laborers. The master laborer is the setting's own figure; the nearest to it this record finds in history are the men who ran laborers rather than labored, above all the placement broker who found work for hired hands and lodged them until it was found.

  • A city's back tenements stood on lanes behind the shops, each lot with a shared well and privy.
    Within each lot, several row buildings stood on either side of a lane about one ken (6 ft) wide, and in one corner a well, a privy and a rubbish heap shared by the residents always stood together.
  • How big was one unit?
    An encyclopedia gives the standard unit as nine shaku wide by two ken deep, about 9 by 12 ft, and a rental-housing history calls that size the common one. In Edo, another entry says, units ran from nine shaku to two ken wide and from two to two and a half ken deep, about 9 to 12 ft by 12 to 15 ft.
  • A unit was an earth-floored kitchen and one room.
    The kitchen lay at the lane door, and beyond it a single room of four and a half or six tatami mats. In Edo the room had a board-floored part.
  • The common back tenement was one story.
    A rental-housing history calls the one-story unit the common kind.
  • Behind a narrow shop the units lined one side of the lane; behind a wider one, both.
    The back units all had the same plan, and they stood on one side of the lane where the shop in front had a frontage of about three ken (~18 ft), on both sides where it had about five (~30 ft).
  • No page we read gives the ground a whole tenement block took per household, its lane, well and privy included.
    The pages give the lot, the unit and the lane, but not how many households a lot or a block held.
  • Peddlers, craftsmen and day laborers lived in the back tenements.
    An encyclopedia names small traders such as peddlers, craftsmen such as carpenters and plasterers, and the simple laborers called day laborers.
  • A better-off small trader lived in the row facing the street.
    The street-front row was sunnier and roomier, with rooms of eight and four tatami mats and an earth floor, and some of its residents sold sundries and household goods from the four-mat room.
  • How many residents of Edo and Osaka rented?
    About seven in ten, by an estimate for the Edo period.
  • How many town residents rented outside Edo and Osaka?
    It differed by region: among the urban residents of early modern Japan the share who rented was 20 to 30% in the north-east (Ou) and on the Japan Sea coast (Hokuriku), and 50 to 60% around the Inland Sea (Setouchi). The shares are of all urban residents, not of laborers alone.
  • Where did a post town's porters live?
    Perhaps on plots of their own along the road. The households that made up the station were allotted house plots of roughly even size and, in return for supplying horses or porters, were often exempted from ground rent, so a post town's porters could come from households on allotted plots, not from a tenement behind them. That is this record's reading of the passage.

For China under the Ming and Qing dynasties the record is silent.

  • No page we read says where laborers lived in a Chinese county seat of the Ming or Qing dynasties.
    Nor in what kind of house.

Above the ordinary laborers stand the setting's master laborers, and in early modern Japan the men who placed, lodged or managed laborers.

  • The setting's master laborers are about one laborer in twelve in a town, one in eight in a city.
    This is the setting's canon: its budget for a town counts 12 master laborers among 144 laborers, and for a provincial city 150 among 1,200.
  • In the Edo period a placement broker guaranteed hired hands, found them places and lodged them until placed.
    The broker's house, the hitoyado, arose with the cities at the start of the early modern period, of necessity, because the many servants coming into Edo from the country needed someone to stand guarantor, find them a place and put them up until they had one. One encyclopedia article, which itself is marked as unsourced, likens the lodging to a laborers' bunkhouse.
  • In Edo the house manager of a tenement lot usually lived on the lot.
    The yamori collected the ground rent and house rent for the owner, registered the tenants and passed on the town's ordinances, and was the real ruler of the back-tenement residents, the day laborers among them.
  • A post town kept porters' offices along its main street.
    The relay office (toiyaba) and the lords' inns (honjin) stood at about the center, and before and after them the inns, teahouses and cook-shops of ordinary travelers, and the porters' and packhorse offices.
  • No page we read gives the size of a broker's house, a house manager's or a porters' office.
    The pages on the placement broker, the tenement lot and the post town say what each did, not how large its building was.
