Ferries and fords (watashi)

In Edo Japan (1603-1868) the shogunate restricted bridges over the great rivers, so a traveler on a highway crossed them by boat at a ferry, or on foot through the shallows at a ford; the smaller rivers were bridged. A highway ferry could be a large concern: the one at Rokugo on the Tokaido kept more than a dozen boats and two dozen boatmen, with a river office, a notice board and the boatmen's huts at its landing.

  • Why did the shogunate leave the great rivers unbridged?
    The reasons given are several, and debated: that a river served like a barrier station, that the shogunate meant to keep the post towns on both banks and the river porters in work, or that bridge building was not equal to it. In any case, a traveler crossed by boat or on foot.
  • Ferries ran where a bridge was hard to build or not allowed.
    Under the Edo regime ferries ran mainly where bridging was difficult, or where, as near a barrier station, bridges were not permitted for military reasons. On the Naka and Edo rivers no bridge was permitted, which made the ferries that joined the roads important.
  • Which rivers went unbridged?
    Only the great ones, on the Tokaido through Kanagawa (the modern prefecture southwest of Tokyo): the Rokugo and Banyu rivers were crossed by boat and the Sakawa on foot, while the Tsurumi, Katabira, Hikichi and other rivers were bridged.

A river with no bridge could be crossed three ways: by ferry, by ford, or on a bridge of boats.

  • A ferry crossed only at landings the shogunate designated.
    A ferry landing is where the ferry boats depart and arrive, and crossing anywhere else was strictly restricted.
  • At a ford, travelers were taken through the shallows on foot, on a litter or on horseback.
    This was the river-crossing system, used where no bridge was built.
  • On the Oi river, the ford closed when the water rose to about 1.4 m (~4.5 ft).
    At a depth of 4.5 shaku the crossing the article describes was stopped and the river declared closed.
  • On the Tokaido, the Abe, Oi and Sakawa were forded and the Fuji and Tenryu ferried.
    A Japanese encyclopedia's entry on the crossing names them.
  • A bridge of boards over a line of boats was the third way.
    On the Kiso rivers, generally crossed by ferry, a bridge of boats was laid only when the shogun or the Korean embassies crossed.
  • At Toyama a bridge of 64 boats crossed a river up to 430 m (~1,411 ft) wide.
    Posts were set on both banks and chained, and the boats were tied together and boarded over.

Two of the Kanagawa ferries, at Rokugo and Banyu, are recorded landing by landing.

  • The Rokugo ferry's landing held a river office, a notice board and the boatmen's huts.
    They stood on the Kawasaki bank, and the village of Hachimanzuka on the far bank had a notice board too.
  • The Banyu ferry kept its river office at one of its two landings.
    Its designated landings were at two villages, Yanagishima and Banyu, with the river office and the notice board at Banyu.
  • How many boats and boatmen did a highway ferry keep?
    The Rokugo ferry had 14 boats, 8 for horses and 6 for travelers on foot, and 24 boatmen. At Banyu, 16 boatmen were on duty day and night.
  • How large were a highway ferry's boats?
    The Banyu ferry's small boat for travelers was about 9 m (~30 ft) long and carried about 20, and its large flat-bottomed boats were about 13 m (~43 ft) long and 2.7 m (~9 ft) wide.
  • Who ran a ferry?
    The Rokugo ferry was contracted first to Edo townsmen or run by the village on the far bank, and from 1709 Kawasaki post town took the contract. The Yagiri ferry on the Edo river, designated by the shogunate, was run by farmers.
  • How many settlements kept a ferry landing?
    On the Tokaido through Kanagawa, four of 65: the road there passed nine post towns and 56 villages, and only the four on the banks at Rokugo and Banyu - a post town and three villages - held a landing. The count is ours, from the two ferries' accounts.
  • No page we read gives the boats of a ferry kept by one village.
    The boat counts found are those of highway ferries.
