Compound walls (neribei and tsuijibei)

A wall was a mark of rank as well as a barrier: in at least one castle town, samurai of middle rank and above walled their land with earth, and foot soldiers were allowed no wall at all. An earth wall carried a small roof of its own. It came in two forms. The lighter, the neribei among them, was laid up in clay without a frame or plastered onto a frame of posts, and stood about 1 to 2 ft thick. The heavy form, the rammed-earth tsuijibei, was as much as 1 m (~3 ft) thick.

  • An earth wall carried its own roof.
    Earth walls with a frame and without one alike were given a small roof, and many tsuijibei carry a simple roof frame on top, roofed with tiles or boards.
  • The neribei was built of old roof tiles and small stones laid in clay.
    The tiles and stones were stacked up, bonded with clay, and the whole was finished with a coat of earth plaster.
  • A lighter earth wall could instead be plastered onto a frame of posts.
    A sill and posts made its frame; lath was tied between the posts and earth plastered onto it to finish the wall.
  • How thick was a lighter earth wall, one not of rammed earth?
    About 1 to 2 ft. A castle's frameless earth wall, faced with plaster, was 1 shaku, about 30 cm (~1 ft), or more. The article gives that figure for the walls built on a castle's ramparts. A frameless neribei was perhaps 30 cm (~1 ft), thinner than a tsuijibei. A surviving late-Edo neribei, a dye shop's wall of head-sized stones laid in red earth and capped with tiles, measures 0.6 m (~2 ft) across its base and stands 1.6 m (~5 ft) high.
  • The tsuijibei was rammed earth, as much as 1 m (~3 ft) thick and 3 m (~10 ft) high.
    A form was made of boards, an inch or two of earth and sand put into it and packed down with a rod, and the work repeated until the forms came off a wall of that size.
  • Castle walls were built to stop bullets once firearms spread, late in the sixteenth century.
    By the end of the Sengoku period, the walls of castles were built 7 sun, about 21 cm (~8 in), or more thick, with bullets in mind.
  • In the castle town of Kanazawa, samurai of middle rank and above walled their land; foot soldiers were not allowed to.
    A sign in the city's samurai quarter says its earthen walls were rammed, heavy and solid, that foot soldiers were not permitted walls and used hedges, and that samurai of middle rank and above surrounded their residences with earthen walls.
