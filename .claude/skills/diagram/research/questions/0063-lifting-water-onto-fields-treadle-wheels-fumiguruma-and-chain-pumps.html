Lifting water onto fields: treadle wheels (fumiguruma) and chain pumps

Where a paddy's water lay below the field a farmer had to lift it, and in Japan that was seldom needed: the paddies were generally watered easily by channels or simple weirs. Where it was, a farmer carried a treadle wheel (fumiguruma) out to the paddy's edge and trod it, lifting the water about a yard at most.

In China the chain pump, trodden by people or turned by an ox, raised water from a canal onto the fields, and by a river a noria turned by the current watered fields day and night.

  • Japanese paddies depended little on lifting.
    An encyclopedia of Japan says they were generally watered easily by channels or a simple weir, so they had little need to lift water with wheels.
  • The chain pump is said to have come from China, and the treadle wheel drove it out.
    The "dragon-bone wheel" is said to have been invented in China. Two people facing each other trod it round. It was used mainly around the Kinki region early in the Edo period. It was hard to make and did not spread, and the spread of the treadle wheel drove it out.
  • One person worked a treadle wheel, where the chain pump had needed two.
    An encyclopedia of Japan says most chain pumps needed two people's labor, and the treadle wheel only one.
  • When was the treadle wheel first made?
    An agricultural tool book of 1822 says two craftsmen of Osaka made it in the Kanbun era (1661-72), early in the Edo period. An older encyclopedia entry has it appear in the eighteenth century.
  • How widely did the treadle wheel spread?
    An agricultural tool book of 1822 says it had spread through the provinces by 1751-1780. An older encyclopedia entry says it spread only in the reclaimed lowlands and new field districts downstream, where new channels were hard to dig.
  • The one-person wheel came in three sizes.
    It was made 4.5, 5 and 5.5 shaku (about 4.5 to 5.5 ft) across, beside a larger two-person wheel and a small hand-turned one.
  • A treadle wheel was carried to the field, not fixed in place.
    Used almost only for paddies, it was taken wherever it was needed.
  • How high could a treadle wheel lift water?
    About 1 m (~3 ft) or less, from the ditch to the paddy. For a higher paddy two or three were set in a row. A Settsu city catalog of old tools puts such a wheel's lift at a few tens of centimeters (about a foot or two).
  • A surviving wheel in Settsu is about 5 ft across.
    It measures 164 cm (~5 ft) across with 17 blades. Such a wheel is light enough to be taken apart and carried to the paddy by one person.
  • Larger wheels, trodden by two or six people, were used for drainage.
    In Settsu two-person wheels were used for drainage, and a rig of three wheels, one above another, trodden by six people survived until after the Second World War.
  • Without a wheel, two people swung the water up in a bucket.
    In regions and on farms with no treadle wheel, a bucket hung on two pairs of ropes was swung by two people to scoop the ditch water up into the paddy.

In China the same work was older and done on a larger scale.

  • The Chinese chain pump goes back to about the Eastern Han.
    A Chinese encyclopedia article dates it to about the Eastern Han, and says it improved and spread further in the Song dynasty.
  • How high did a Chinese chain pump lift water?
    About 1 to 2 m (~3-7 ft), or straight from a supply canal onto the fields. It suits the plains and was worked by people or animals.
  • A Ming encyclopedia credits a noria by a river with watering a hundred mu, day and night.
    The Tiangong kaiwu, a Ming encyclopedia of technology, describes a weir turning the current beneath the wheel, which fills its tubes and tips them into a trough that runs to the fields.
  • At a still pond, a chain pump was turned by an ox or trodden by several people.
    The Tiangong kaiwu says so of lakes and ponds whose water does not flow.
  • How long was a Chinese chain pump?
    The Tiangong kaiwu gives the pump's body as two zhang at the longest and half that at the shortest.
  • How much ground could one pump water in a day?
    The Tiangong kaiwu reckons a person's full day at 5 mu and an ox's at twice that. A small hand pump a few chi long waters 2 mu a day, and the well-sweep and the windlass less still.
  • No page we read counts the lifting devices a village owned.
    The pages describe the devices, their crews and their daily rates, and none gives a number for a village.
