City canals (horikawa)

A city canal was a channel dug through a town to bring boats to its doors and storehouses. They ranged from Kyoto's Takase canal, about 8 m (~26 ft) wide, to Osaka's Dotonbori, some 120 to 200 ft wide in 1787. At Suzhou, in China, their banks were faced in stone, and along them stood either the backs of houses with steps down to the water or a landing lined with wholesalers' storehouses.

Where a canal met the river it took one of two forms. At Suzhou the city's canals reached the moat, and through it the outside waters, by the water gates in the wall; at Osaka the canals were cut straight off the rivers. How a canal passes the wall is at Water gates and sluices (shuimen).

  • Kyoto's Takase canal was about 8 m (~26 ft) wide and normally about 30 cm (~12 in) deep.
    It was cut between 1611 and 1614, early in the Edo period, an entirely artificial channel.
  • How many of its boats did the Takase canal hold abreast?
    About four. Its boats averaged 13 m (~43 ft) long and 2 m (~7 ft) wide, so its 8 m (~26 ft) is about four boats wide, by this page's arithmetic.
  • The Takase canal had loading basins cut off its side.
    They were for loading and unloading goods and for turning boats: seven between Nijo and Shijo avenues (Kyoto's Second and Fourth) at first, nine by the end of the seventeenth century, and seven again by the middle of the eighteenth. The larger boat basins inside a city are at Wharves and landings: piers, quays and stepped landings (kashi, gangi).
  • In 1787, Osaka's Dotonbori canal was 20 ken across at its east end and 34 ken at its west.
    A document of that year, in the Edo period, gives the two figures, about 36 m (~119 ft) and 62 m (~203 ft).
  • Today the Dotonbori is about 30 m (~98 ft) wide in its eastern half and 50 m (~164 ft) in its western.
    The city of Osaka gives the two figures for its two sections.
  • Was a canal's width set by what it carried?
    This page guesses so, from the two figures: the narrow Takase carried boats 2 m (~7 ft) wide, and the Dotonbori was four to eight times its width. No page we read ties a canal's width to its traffic.

How a canal's banks were built is recorded at Suzhou and at Edo.

  • Suzhou's canals were faced in stone on both banks.
    They were for the most part cut by hand, both banks faced with solid stone revetments, some with wooden or stone railings along the top.
  • Edo's outer moat, also a canal, was walled in stone on the castle side and was a goods landing on the other.
    It was dug to defend the castle and served also as a drain. The goods landing on its far bank was the Shirobe landing.

What stood along a canal's banks took two forms, one recorded in a Chinese city and one at Japanese landings.

  • In Suzhou, many houses backed onto a canal, with stone steps down to the water.
    A house often fronted on a street and backed on the canal, and at its back a water landing had stone steps down to the water, for boats, for drawing water and for washing.
  • At a Japanese landing (kashi), wholesalers and their storehouses lined the bank.
    From the Edo period on, merchants with wholesale houses and their storehouses (kura) gathered at a landing and made it one commercial settlement. The landing's working face is at Wharves and landings: piers, quays and stepped landings (kashi, gangi).

A canal's mouth on the river is recorded at Suzhou and at Osaka.

  • Suzhou's canals reached the outside waters through the moat and its water gates.
    The water gate of the Pan Gate, a gate in Suzhou's city wall, joins the city's inner canals and its moat to the Grand Canal. A city plan of 1229, in the Song dynasty, shows a broad moat outside the wall, joined to the canals inside it through the water gates, its western stretch part of the Grand Canal.
  • At Osaka, the Nishi-Yokobori canal branched straight off a river.
    The Nishi-Yokobori, dug in 1600 by a timber merchant who was a town elder, branched from the Tosabori River just above a bridge and ran south into the Dotonbori.
  • Osaka's oldest canal, dug as its castle's outer moat, also branches straight off a river.
    The Higashi-Yokobori, dug in 1585 on Toyotomi Hideyoshi's order as the castle's western outer moat, branches south from the upper Tosabori River.
  • Could a walled city open a water gate straight onto its river, with no moat between?
    Osaka's canals were dug in a Japanese castle town, not beside a walled Chinese city's gate like Suzhou's, so they leave the question open. A water gate, by Wikipedia's definition, is a fortified gate leading directly from a castle or town wall onto a quay, a river side or a harbor, so a walled city could open onto its river without the moat between.
