How our maps draw where a field meets its ditch

Our maps draw a delivery ditch down to about 1.2 ft wide, near the width of a field ditch, and draw a paddy's bund unbroken along every channel it borders. Below the last ditch drawn, the water passes from paddy to paddy, the plot-to-plot watering (tagoshi) of the research section, and neither the openings in the bunds nor the flow between plots is drawn: both are smaller than a map can show. Beside a supply canal the fields stand back behind a bare earth bank about 5 ft wide, and at the foot of a field the lowest bunds are fitted to the drainage ditch, running along it and never into its water.

  • The finest ditch drawn is the tail of a delivery ditch, and it ends at a width of its own.
    A delivery ditch narrows from about 2.5 ft where it leaves its canal to about 1.2 ft and stops there rather than tapering away to a point, because, on this project's reasoning, a ditch dug with a spade keeps a width of its own down to its end. There the smallest ditches hand their water on to the inlets of the paddies they serve. On a hamlet map that last width is drawn at the 1.5 ft floor that keeps a channel visible (How our maps draw channel widths). The narrowing along its run is what makes that ending read, the stroke arriving at its last width by dwindling (How our maps draw a channel narrowing along its run).
  • No ditch finer than a delivery ditch's tail is drawn.
    The field ditch, the finest channel of the Rites of Zhou's graded network, is about a foot across or less (the quan of the Rites of Zhou, in the research section): a hairline on a hamlet map and invisible on a city's. A delivery ditch narrows to about that width and ends; past its tail the maps draw no finer ditch, and the water goes on from plot to plot.
  • On our maps, water runs on from paddy to paddy for several to about ten plots past where a ditch stops.
    So a ditch ending in the middle of a field is the handoff to the flow from plot to plot, not a line left unfinished. What is modern is a ditch reaching every plot, and the maps do not draw one (How our maps lay out irrigation canals).
  • The openings in the bunds that let water into and out of a paddy are never drawn.
    An inlet of at most 50 cm (~20 in), the modern design limit and the only size found, is a gap of a pixel or two in the bund on the finest map and half a pixel on the coarsest, which does not read as an opening at any scale. A legible one would be drawn larger than life on the most numerous feature of the map, one to a paddy on a map with hundreds. This is a map drawing convention.
  • Bunds are drawn unbroken along every channel's bank.
    A reader is expected to understand that water crosses it through openings too small to draw, as they understand that a drawn farmhouse has a door. The unbroken bund is not a claim that the paddies are unwatered.
  • How wide are the bunds drawn?
    About 1.5 ft between plots or along a delivery ditch, and about 3 ft along the head race or a canal - a large bund, inside the 80-150 cm (~2.6-4.9 ft) of the large bunds along the canal at the Hattori site. The 3 ft is a calibration against that one excavated site, not a measured Edo figure: no width was found for an Edo bund along a canal.
  • The 1.5 ft bund between plots falls within the bund widths recorded before modern times.
    The Hattori site's small bunds were 20-60 cm (~8 in to 2 ft), and an Edo survey reckoned a bund at 1 shaku, about 1 ft, so the drawn 1.5 ft lies inside the Hattori range and a little wider than the Edo reckoning.
  • A bare earth bank 5 ft wide runs between a supply canal and the fields beside it.
    A strip of earth beside a canal, probably walked on, is as old as the early Yayoi paddies and the classical Chinese field layout (the large bunds along the Hattori site's canal and the paths of the Rites of Zhou, in the research section), so the maps draw it. The drawn 5 ft is the top of the 80-150 cm (~2.6-4.9 ft) of the large bunds along the canal at the Hattori site, to the nearest foot. How the bank is measured from the water is at How our maps draw channel widths.

At the foot of a field, the drawn edge is a single line running along the drainage ditch (the drain), and the bunds that run down the slope between plots meet it end-on. Three rules keep that edge on the drain's line and out of its water.

  • Each plot in the bottom row is fitted to the drain at both of its own ends.
    A plot laid on the contour, while the drain runs at up to about nineteen degrees to it, would drift some 9 ft against the ditch across a 26 ft plot, starting above the drain and ending below it. Where that row holds most of the plots along the edge, the field's edge would read as a sawtooth, brown ticks poking into bare ground. So each plot of that row samples the drain at both of its ends, and runs both of its edges parallel to the ditch between them.
  • Bunds keep clear of a drain that widens downstream.
    A bund kept a fixed distance from the drain's center line takes no account of the drain widening: even laid at the right angle, it would sit inside the ditch's stroke along the lower half of its run. So each bund is kept back from the center line by half the ditch's drawn width at that point plus half a bund's width, and the two meet edge to edge instead of overlapping.
  • A straight bund is set back far enough to clear a wandering drain everywhere.
    The drain's line wanders on purpose, about 6 ft at a bend every 120-170 ft, so its stretches differ by a few degrees, and a paddy as our maps draw it has straight sides that cannot follow. A bund pinned only at its ends would cut the corner at every bend and land in the water, so each bund is moved upslope until it clears the highest point of the bank across its own span. A farmer laying a straight bund against a crooked ditch would do the same - this project's reasoning, a GUESS. The cost, which this project accepts to keep every bund out of the water, is that a bund can sit a few degrees off its own stretch of drain - about nine degrees on one map - while clearing it everywhere.
