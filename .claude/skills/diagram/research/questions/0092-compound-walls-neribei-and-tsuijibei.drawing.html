How our maps draw compound walls (neribei and tsuijibei)

A walled compound - a manor or estate, a governor's yamen, a merchant's estate, a mausoleum - appears on a settlement map as its wall, its gate and a blank court, because its interior is the subject of a building plan of its own. On the settlement map the wall and the gate are the whole feature, so they are drawn at the size the record gives them, never thinner than the sheet can show: the wall 1.5 to 2 ft thick, the gate 9 to 12 ft wide for a samurai residence and 18 to 24 ft for a yamen. A building plan draws the wall 3 ft thick, and treats it as a building whose ground nothing else may enter.

  • A settlement map draws a compound wall 1.5 to 2 ft thick, within what the lighter wall measured.
    A frameless neribei was perhaps 30 cm (~1 ft) thick, the ordinary lighter wall about 1 shaku, a foot, or more, a figure given for castle walls, and a surviving late-Edo neribei measures 0.6 m (~2 ft) across its base.
  • A wall too thin for the sheet is drawn at the thinnest line the sheet can carry.
    On a sheet where one pixel stands for 3 ft of ground, a wall 1.5 ft thick would be half a pixel wide and vanish; it is drawn two pixels wide instead, thicker than the real wall so that it can be seen - a map drawing convention.
  • A building plan draws the compound wall 3 ft thick, the heavier of the two real forms, so its stroke reads.
    This is a map drawing convention. The real form it matches is the rammed-earth tsuijibei, built as much as 1 m (~3 ft) thick; the lighter wall, at a foot or two, would draw too thin a stroke.
  • A building plan draws the compound wall as a dark, nearly black line.
    The dark stroke sets the wall apart from the buildings and the court on either side of it, a map drawing convention, not the wall's real color: the pages we read on earth walls, at Compound walls (neribei and tsuijibei), say how a wall was built and plastered, but none gives its color.
  • A building plan never draws a building on the compound wall.
    The wall is a thick structure, with its own roof, so its ground is taken: where a building and the wall compete for ground, the building gives way, and the wall is never thinned to make room. The wall is also painted last, over the outline of anything beneath it, so a building drawn across it would seem to melt into the wall.
  • How far off the wall does a building plan set a building?
    A foot or two, its eaves nearly touching the wall: the plan leaves that clearance behind each building that backs onto it. The spacing is this project's reasoning, a GUESS.
  • A gate is drawn at a real width, never a fixed distance on the sheet.
    The sheets are drawn at very different scales, so a gap fixed on the sheet changes size on the ground: 68 pixels, on a city sheet where a pixel stands for 3 ft, would open 204 ft, most of a compound's wall.
  • A gate post is drawn about 2 ft square.
    The figure is this project's own, a map drawing convention; like the wall, a post too small for the sheet is drawn at the smallest mark the sheet can carry.
  • How wide is a samurai residence's gate drawn?
    9 to 12 ft, within what surviving Japanese gates measure, from a gate of one bay of about 6 to 8.5 ft to a nagaya-mon opening two ken, about 12 ft; the widths and their sources are at The main gate and its gatekeepers (nagaya-mon) The official commentary on the nagaya-mon, the gate a samurai of middle rank and above was permitted, describes its servants' quarters and stable and gives no width.
  • How wide is a yamen's gatehouse drawn?
    18 to 24 ft, this project's own calibration on the drawn sheets, a GUESS. A yamen's gate was a roofed building of three bays, sourced at The main gate and its gatekeepers (nagaya-mon), but the best-preserved county magistrate's office in China is described by its forty thousand square meters (~10 acres) and its hundred-meter (~328 ft) axis, not by its gate, and no page we read gives such a gate's width.
  • Funerary features are drawn at their real size too.
    A funerary feature drawn to the wrong size reads as a different kind of place. Each is sized in its own section: the cremation ground and its fire bed at How large is a cremation ground, and what was its fire bed?, the bone mound at How large is a bone mound drawn?, and the single grave and the burial ground at How much ground does a grave take, and how large is a burial ground drawn?
