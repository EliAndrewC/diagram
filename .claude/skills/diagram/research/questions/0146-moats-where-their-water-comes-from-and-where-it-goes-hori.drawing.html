How our maps draw moat water

Every moated city on our maps shows where its moat's water comes from and where it goes. Our maps draw five moated cities: the provincial cities Tango and Nagahara, Minami, Shiro Daika, and the capital. A city's moat is about 66 ft wide and is fed from off the sheet by a watercourse nearly as wide; a closed ring that is fed has an outfall just as wide, leaving from its far side; a city on a river opens its moat onto the river; and the fields around a city draw water from the moat or drain into it. The castle's inner moat is still water joined to nothing.

  • A city's moat is about 66 ft wide.
    That is a provincial seat's moat, inside the range the measured ditches of the great cities give (How wide and deep is a city's moat?). It is a closed ring pushed outward from the rampart, with every point of the wall inside it.
  • Every moat has a feeder from a map edge.
    At least one watercourse reaches it from off the sheet. It may end at the moat instead of running on off the far edge. This holds for every moated city we add later, as for those drawn so far.
  • The feeder is drawn within a quarter of the moat's width.
    A trickle could not keep a moat full, so the feeder is drawn at the moat's own width rather than as a thread: a stream half a moat wide reads as a moat that must be nearly empty. That a feeder carries something like what its moat holds is a GUESS.
  • Moat water is drawn as solid color with a lighter sheen down its middle, never with a dashed centerline.
    A dash is this map's mark for a road's lane, so a dash on water would borrow from the wrong vocabulary - a map drawing convention.

A moat's water has to come in somewhere and go out somewhere.

  • Which way a moat's water runs is set by where it comes in and where it goes out, never worked out from the moat's shape.
    A ring has no upstream end, so "downstream of a moated city" is reasoned from those two points. An open moat cut from a river takes them from its two junctions with the river; a closed ring declares them, because the ring is dug before its feeder and outfall exist. Every rule about the current reads those two points, and none works a direction out again from the shape, so the map never holds two answers that could disagree.
  • A closed ring moat that is fed also drains, from the face opposite its feeder.
    A second off-map stream leaves the ring there, so the current flushes the ring corner to corner, as Beijing's moats took their water in from springs to the northwest and let it out to the east. It leaves by the low, downstream rim - on Tango, to the southeast. This holds for every moated city we add later, as for those drawn so far.
  • The outfall is drawn as wide as the feeder.
    The water that comes in has to go out.
  • Tango's moat is a stream turned into a ring and sent on its way.
    That arrangement has a history: Edo dug a stretch of its outer moat by moving a river's course, and part of Beijing's spring water ran on through the city and its palace moat into the inner city's south moat.
  • No trace of the stream's old bed is drawn through the city.
    Cities build over even their live channels - Japan's urban streams were culverted, and Beijing's moats covered, in modern times - and that an abandoned course is gone under centuries of building is this project's reading, a GUESS. Its absence is realistic, not an omission.
  • Irrigation taps are no substitute for the outfall.
    They are a seasonal second way out: a 21 ft summer tap cannot balance a 66 ft feeder that runs all year.
  • A moat with no feeder needs no outfall.
    A moat filled by springs or rain has no inflow to shed.
  • A city on a river opens its moat onto the river.
    An open moat cut from a river takes from it upstream and returns to it downstream. Minami's and Nagahara's moats back onto their rivers, so the moat's two ends where they meet the river are the connection, and nothing more is drawn.

A city near a river but not on it needs channels to reach the river; Shiro Daika is the one we draw.

  • A ring standing off its river is joined to it by two channels as wide as the moat.
    Shiro Daika's ring stands about 600 ft off its river bank, so a 66 ft sluiced feeder and a 66 ft drain join it to the river, as Tango's 66 ft feeder and outfall join its ring, and the moat reads as flowing through.
  • The feeder taps its river upstream, not where the river comes closest.
    On Shiro Daika the land falls from northeast to southwest, so water let in at the ring's low southeast corner, where the river comes closest, could not climb the east arc, and the whole north of the ring would be stagnant water. The feeder taps the river's upper reach, below the aqueduct's intake, and runs steadily down the map - every stretch west and south - into the ring's east arc.
  • On Shiro Daika, the drain rejoins the river below the river's bend, angled downstream.
    It leaves the ring's southeast arc onto ground that the land's fall puts below the arc.
  • On Shiro Daika, the river's towpath crosses the drain's mouth on a plank deck.
    A towpath was bridged where a side channel cut across it, as on British canals, the one tradition read on the point.

Where a channel meets the moat, its angle shows which way the water runs.

  • A channel meets the moat swept with the current.
    An offtake leaves pointing downstream, and a drain arrives pointing downstream: its heading where it meets the ring carries a downstream part. On the page, a tap that meets the ring square says nothing about which way the water runs. What hydraulics says of the angle at which channels part and join is at Which way water flows, and how channels bend and join, and how our maps draw those junctions at How our maps draw bends, junctions and the run of the water.
  • Every offtake on Tango and Nagahara leaves the ring at 47 to 48 degrees, and every drain arrives at 14 to 60 degrees.
    These are measured on the two drawn provincial cities, from the moat's downstream direction, so that a square junction is 90 degrees.
  • A tap drawn square to the moat is corrected by moving only its moat end.
    Its end on the moat walks along the rim - upstream for an offtake, downstream for an arriving drain - until its mouth is swept, measured by distance along the rim; the sluice does not move, so neither does the field it feeds.
  • Whether a field lies upstream of its tap is judged by the current's rough compass direction, not its exact heading.
    An irrigation offtake must not leave the ring from a point back upstream of the ground it waters. Measuring the ring's direction exactly at each tap is the physically correct picture, but an offtake leaves the ring roughly at a right angle, so its share along that exact direction is near arbitrary: on Tango and Nagahara it would flag some taps and spare their neighbors, on nothing more than which side of the tap the field happened to sit. The coarse test asks the real question, whether the field lies back upstream. A map drawing convention: a simplification chosen because the precise measurement answers a different question.

Around a moated city, the fields draw water from the moat or drain into it.

  • A moated city waters at least three of its large fields from the moat.
    Each tap stands about 90 ft outside the moat's rim, with a short stub bridging rim to gate, and the channel it feeds ends inside the field, not near it. Where a field lies upstream of the moat, the count of three moat-fed fields is made up from a field downstream.
  • A tap stands upstream of the field it serves.
    So the water runs downstream into the field. A channel whose field lay upstream of its tap would read as water running out of the field into the moat, backwards.
  • Irrigation taps may leave either side of a ring.
    The ring splits its feeder's flow into two arcs that rejoin at the outfall, so a tap on either side draws with the current.
  • A field upstream of the moat drains into it instead.
    Such a field takes its water from high ground off the sheet, or from the moat's source stream, and discharges into the moat below it, gated at the outfall and swept with the current; or it runs off the sheet with its water implied. Which way the water runs between a field and the moat is this project's choice, following the slope, not something a source sets; that moats both drained and watered the ground around them is attested (Moats: where their water comes from and where it goes).
  • A field's collecting ditch may empty back into the moat.
    Or it runs off the edge of the map.
  • A channel's mouth is laid onto the moat's open water.
    Its end is snapped onto the water's edge so that it covers the rim, rather than drawing its bed as a colored line across the water - a map drawing convention, the same one a channel meeting a pond gets.
  • No channel is drawn ruler-straight.
    Each is drawn with a bend.
  • A channel is drawn as a hairline, by convention.
    The hairline's width on the sheet is a drawing convention, not a measured ditch.
  • Farmsteads keep about 100 ft clear of each channel.
    The ground about 100 ft either side of each channel is kept clear of farmsteads.
  • A field inside the walls drains to the moat underground.
    It records the moat as where its water goes, but its ditch stops short of the patrol road: the runoff drops into an implied underground stone conduit that passes beneath the ring road, the rampart and the moat. No ditch is ever drawn running through a city wall; the water gate is underground.
  • On the capital's map, the narrow strip between the north wall and the map's edge stays unfarmed, for want of room.
    About 360 ft lie between the rampart and the edge of the sheet, and a field system of the kind the maps draw cannot fit there at any edge: a field's run downhill from its head channel, plus the strip that channel and its offtakes need along the top, is deeper than the strip. The current does not rule it out - a field there could drain into the moat like any other upstream field - the room does.

The castle's own moat is another matter.

  • A castle's inner moat is still water joined to nothing.
    It holds groundwater and rain (Moats: where their water comes from and where it goes), and no channel is drawn to feed or drain it. Still, standing water is how such a moat looked, not a lapse of the map.
