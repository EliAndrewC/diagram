How our maps draw stable yards and their troughs

Our maps draw the working yard of a gate stables, or of a caravan ground, as ground in use rather than blank parchment: beaten earth and straw litter, two or three hitching rails, a muck heap or two, and a cluster of troughs beside a well. No page we read describes the ground of such a yard, so its look is this project's reading of a painted city gate crowded with wagons and pack animals.

The troughs stand at the well because the water is drawn there by bucket, on this project's reading, and a train of a few dozen head drinks some hundreds of gallons in relays; the well, not the number of troughs, sets how fast that goes.

  • A gate's stable yard stands a little inside the town wall, near the gate, fronting the road.
    It stands behind the strip kept clear along the wall's foot, where the wagons pull in, never flush against the masonry and never on the rampart. The inn and stables stand inside the gate, for safe holding overnight, and the cheap lodging house outside it in the gate suburb. This siting is this project's reading.
  • The open ground kept clear around a gate's stables is about 255 ft across.
    The figure is this project's choice, not a sourced one: no page we read sizes such a yard.
  • No animals are drawn.
    Our maps draw no humans, so they draw no animals either. The rails, the litter and the troughs show the yard in use, as the empty streets already imply their crowds.
  • The yard is beaten earth, open to the road, never a lawn or a fenced paddock.
    A fenced paddock would be the least likely form: a rail fence belongs to an inn's private back yard or to a walled caravanserai, on this project's reading. The yard's edges are the town wall and the buildings beside it.
  • The ground is drawn as a scatter of earth and straw.
    The scatter is feathered over the open ground and avoids every building, road and field, so the yard reads as trampled ground, not as a filled shape.
  • The first hitching rail runs along the nearest road, set back from it.
    Its animals would face into the yard with their rumps to the road, which keeps tethered stock off the through road. The other rails stand at clear spots inside the yard.
  • No rail and no muck heap touches a road or the rampart.
    No rail tip and no heap edge may lie on a road, a ring road, a street or an alley, or on the strip kept clear at the foot of the rampart, so that tethered stock and dung stay off the ways that carry traffic.
  • A muck heap stands 72 ft clear of every hitching rail on the map.
    Both sides of a rail are working space where animals are tied, so a heap dumped against one sits where the animals stand. A heap nearer in, say 42 ft out, would have its edge about 8 ft off their rumps, still in the working row, and a heap can land as close to a neighboring yard's rail as to its own. At 72 ft from every rail, not only its own yard's, a heap's edge is about 38 ft past the animals, out of their way and still plainly that yard's muck pile.
  • A well, a trough cluster and a hitching rail may stand side by side, never on top of one another.
    A rail across a wellhead with the troughs stacked on both would be three marks on one spot that no reader could make out, and a layout nobody would build: water is not drawn through a rail, and no yard ties its stock over its own well. The rule forbids only overlap, with no clearance in feet, because the three belong close together: the troughs stand by their well for the bucket's pour, and the animals stand between rail and trough.

The troughs follow from how a train was watered.

  • A yard's trough is drawn as a plain one-sided trough, like a premodern stable's, not the two-sided street trough of modern cities.
    The free-standing watering trough is a modern form, first set up in Japan in 1906, while the troughs of premodern stable yards were fodder troughs. At map scale the two look the same; what differs is which trough the map claims to show, and the capacity below counts one side only.
  • Did premodern yard troughs hold water, stand by a well and stand in the open, as ours do?
    All three are GUESSES. No premodern animal trough found held water or stood beside a well, and the ordinary trough of a sixth-century Chinese farm manual seems to have stood under a shed, since the manual sets a trough in the open only to harden a horse for journeys.
  • A trough is drawn about 14 ft long, at about 2 ft of edge for each animal drinking.
    Both are this project's GUESS: no premodern trough found is sized.
  • A trough is drawn at least 6 ft wide.
    That is the least a mark must measure to be seen at all at city grain, the same floor the wellhead marker takes: a map drawing convention. A true trough, perhaps 2.5 ft wide by this project's estimate, would not show.
  • The troughs stand beside the nearest well, within 40 ft of it.
    Animals are led to the water in relays and watered at the well, not at the rail, on this project's reading, and no yard carries the hundreds of gallons a train drinks across a city block. The cluster is set only as far from the wellhead as it takes to clear the well-house's roof, in whichever direction it lies, on the yard's side of the well even where the well stands at or just past the yard's edge.
  • A yard with no well in reach digs its own.
    Where no well is near, or every side of the near wellhead is blocked, the yard sinks a well in its own courtyard and sets its troughs beside that. A caravanserai watered its trains from a well or fountain in its own courtyard, and a post station's yard, on this project's reading, did the same: having no well nearby is a reason to dig one. The dug well never lands on another wellhead, since two wellheads on one spot would be one unreadable mark.
  • The trough cluster stands on open ground.
    It is kept off every building, road, field and watercourse, the town wall and every other well-house's roof.
  • A water buffalo's wallow is left out of the yard.
    Where a train was drawn by water buffalo, in a hot climate it needed a wallow, a river or splashing water to keep cool, the wallow being a mud pond of perhaps 15 to 30 ft by this project's estimate, and on its reading a feature of China more than of Japan. It is left to a map whose ground and water suit it.

A train of several dozen oxen may pass through at once; the well, not the number of troughs, sets how fast it drinks.

  • A plain stables yard draws two troughs, and a caravan ground three.
    The number is this project's choice, made for how a train was watered.
  • How many animals can drink at once?
    A 14 ft trough at 2 ft a head, used from one side, waters about 7 at once, so a caravan ground's three troughs water about 20 and a plain yard's two about 14. This is this project's arithmetic on its own guessed sizes.
  • How fast could the troughs alone water a train, if they were kept full?
    About 12 to 18 minutes for several dozen oxen, if the well could keep up. At a modern feedlot's 1.1 to 3.7 gallons a minute, a working ox drinks its 5 to 8 gallons in about one and a half to seven minutes, so a caravan ground waters about 20 head every six minutes and clears several dozen in two or three relays. That fits a halt at which a team rested an hour or so, and several hours in hot weather, as on the American overland trails: watering was an unhurried chore for a couple of teamsters.
  • What limits how fast a train is watered?
    The well, not the troughs. A hand draw by bucket might give 5 to 10 gallons a minute, a GUESS: no page we read gives a rate. A train of 20 to 40 head drinks about 300 to 600 gallons, some 15 gallons a head, this project's arithmetic from the modern drinking figures; at that rate it takes half an hour to two hours of steady drawing, however many troughs stand there.
  • Why would more troughs not water a train faster?
    They would only hold more standing water: the troughs are a buffer, filled between arrivals so the first relay drinks at once while the drawing goes on. That is why a caravanserai could water its trains from one well or fountain in its courtyard; that it did so instead of multiplying basins is this project's reading.
  • Would a coaching inn's yard have had a single trough?
    Probably, this project's GUESS, which makes the maps' two or three the generous end. No page we read shows an inn yard's troughs.

The rule the map follows: a gate stables or caravan ground has a working yard of beaten earth and straw litter, drawn with no animals, with two or three hitching rails, the first along the nearest road and set back from it, and a muck heap or two 72 ft clear of every rail on the map; no rail or heap lies on a road or the rampart's clear strip, and a well, a trough cluster and a rail never overlap. The watering point is two troughs for a plain yard and three for a caravan ground, each a plain one-sided trough about 14 ft long, drawn at least 6 ft wide, beside the nearest well and within 40 ft of it, on open ground; a yard with no well in reach digs its own. The buffalo's wallow is left out.
