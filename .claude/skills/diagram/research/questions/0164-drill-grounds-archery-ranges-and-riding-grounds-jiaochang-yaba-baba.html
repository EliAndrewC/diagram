Drill grounds, archery ranges and riding grounds (jiaochang, yaba, baba)

A city's soldiers and samurai needed open ground to train on. A Chinese walled seat of county rank or above generally kept a drill ground, the jiaochang: a walled field of some acres with a review hall at one end, outside a gate or in a corner inside the wall.

A Japanese castle town of the Edo period trained instead on riding grounds, the baba, long and narrow, often some 600 ft long, because riders ran straight down them shooting from the saddle, and on archery ranges, the yaba, about 250 ft long.   • A modern Chinese history says nearly every walled seat of county rank or above kept a drill ground.
    It is the Chongming county government's history of its own drill ground, Chongming being a county seat.
  • The drill ground of Chongming, a county seat, was a walled field with a review hall at its north end.
    Founded in 1586, under the Ming dynasty, it lay outside the east gate, walled with earth on all four sides, with a three-bay review hall at the middle of its north end, a rear hall and a tea hall, a commander's platform, and one gate at the middle of its south wall.
  • How large was a drill ground?
    About 5 to 6 acres, on two cases. Chongming's is reckoned at 37.5 mu, about 2.5 ha (~6 acres). Fuzhou's, a provincial capital's, lay by the south gate and is given both as four li broad and as more than thirty mu (~5 acres) of low ground - figures that do not agree, a ground four li broad holding far more than thirty mu.
  • A drill ground lay outside the wall or inside it, and a city could keep one of each.
    Chongming kept its great ground outside the east gate and a small drill ground inside the city's southeast corner as well. Xi'an's garrison drilled on a ground inside the walls, on the site of an old prince's palace in the garrison quarter at the city's northeast corner.
  • A Chinese garrison quarter had arrow lanes and bow rooms of its own.
    The Chinese Wikipedia article on the garrison quarters lists bow rooms and arrow lanes among the buildings inside them.

In Japan, a castle town's samurai trained instead on horseback and with the bow.

  • Every early modern castle town kept riding grounds, inside the castle and outside it.
    A Japanese historical dictionary's entry on riding grounds says a castle town always had both.
  • The riding ground outside the castle stood in the samurai ground.
    The samurai living in the town did their riding training there.
  • How long and how wide was a riding ground?
    Long and narrow, often about 100 ken by 10 (about 600 by 60 ft), because many of its contests, such as mounted archery, ran a horse in a straight line while the rider shot. It was banked with earth and fenced.
  • A riding ground served riding, mounted archery and a ball game on horseback.
    Kumamoto's was for the lord's and their retainers' horsemanship and mounted archery, and on Sendai's below the main bailey samurai practiced riding, mounted archery and dakyu, a ball game on horseback.
  • Ako's riding ground ran along a river embankment from the castle's moat.
    It ran 2.5 cho (about 900 ft) north from the moat at the castle's main gate, 5 ken (about 30 ft) wide, with a small viewing stand of 3 by 2.5 ken (about 18 by 15 ft) below the bank south of its middle, and a tie-rail for three horses.
  • The riding grounds on record lay close to the castle and the great houses.
    Kumamoto's ran south from a residence called Hanabata, banked on its east side. One of Sendai's three lay below the cliff of the castle's main bailey. Daishoji's lay east of its two senior retainers' houses.
  • The shogun's riding ground at Takada in Edo was far larger than a domain's.
    Built in 1636, early in the Edo period, for the bannermen's training, it measured 6 cho by some 30 ken, about 2,100 by 180 ft, against a domain's usual 600 by 60 ft.
  • Among the samurai, an archery range was set at about 250 by 8 ft.
    Its length was set at 33 bow-lengths, about 76 m (~249 ft), and its width at one, about 2.3 m (~8 ft), with an earth butt, the azuchi, at its end to hold the target.
  • An archery range stood inside the castle, inside a residence, or in the thinly built outskirts.
    All three places are given.
  • Several domains built grounds to practice the long-shot contest.
    In the contest an archer shot arrows the whole length under the eaves of Kyoto's Sanjusangendo hall, about 120 m (~394 ft). The domains, among them Kishu, Owari, Sendai, Matsue and Kaga, built the simple origake, ropes strung between pairs of bamboo poles to stand in for the roof, and the fuller dogata, a timber copy of the hall, sometimes of only the highest stretch of the arrow's flight and sometimes of its whole length.
