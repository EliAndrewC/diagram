How our maps draw the castle in a capital

Our maps draw a capital's castle as its outer walls, its own moat and its two gates, with nothing inside. It stands at the center of the city, or at its edge where a river or the coast guards that side, and its ground is about 50 ha (~124 acres) - the size of Hirosaki's castle. What a castle held, its keep, its palace and its inner baileys, belongs to the castle's own building plan, not to the city map.

  • The castle stands at the center of the capital by default.
    Japanese castle towns were built round a central castle, and Quanzhou, a Chinese prefectural seat, built its walls nested one within another, so the centered castle is the default form and the castle at the edge the variant.
  • A river or coastal capital may set its castle at its edge instead - a knob.
    The castles found at a town's edge, Okayama's and Kitsuki's, had a river or the sea on that side, so a capital on a river or the coast may take either seat.
  • A castle at the city's edge has one moat on that side, not two.
    Where the castle takes the edge, its own outer moat becomes that stretch of the city's perimeter rather than a second line inside it.
  • One wall encloses the whole capital, as a sogamae did.
    Some castle towns enclosed the whole town, not only the castle, with moats, stone walls and earthen ramparts, so a capital drawn inside a single rampart is an attested form, not a convenience.
  • Our maps never set the castle at an edge without water.
    Without water on that side it would not be a variant but a weak wall, so a dry inland capital takes the center.

Each capital's castle is given its own share of the city's ground.

  • A capital's castle is about 50 ha (~124 acres), Hirosaki's size, unless the city is given a larger one.
    Hirosaki's castle covered about 50 ha (~124 acres), and our maps draw a capital's castle that size, about 2,550 by 2,100 ft, unless it is set larger. Taking the smaller of the two, Hirosaki's rather than Himeji's, as the standard castle and as the least the maps allow is this project's choice.
  • A capital may be given a castle nearly as large as Himeji's.
    The castle's area is set for each capital between 50 and 230 ha (~124-568 acres), a range topped by Himeji's 233 ha (~576 acres), so a great old clan's seat can have a castle near Himeji's. Our maps will not draw a castle outside that range.
  • Should our maps allow a castle smaller than 50 ha (~124 acres)?
    Real castles smaller than that are recorded: Nijo's, about 500 m (~1,640 ft) by 400 m (~1,312 ft), and Kawagoe's, about 16.5 ha (~41 acres), later 32.6 ha (~81 acres). Hiroshima's 90 ha (~222 acres) lies inside the range. The maps keep 50 ha (~124 acres) as their least castle for now; whether a smaller one should be allowed is not yet decided.
  • A capital's standard castle is about 85% the size of a whole provincial city.
    Tango, the provincial city our maps draw, has a walled interior of about 59 ha (~146 acres), against the castle's 50 ha (~124 acres).
  • A capital's wall is sized for its castle as well as its inhabitants.
    A capital holds roughly four provincial cities' worth of inhabitants, and its castle takes most of a fifth city's ground, so its wall cannot be sized by its population alone.
  • The castle's moat is drawn 80 ft wide, within the range of Himeji's.
    Himeji's castle moats average 20 m (~66 ft) wide and reach 34.5 m (~113 ft). Our maps draw the castle's moat wider than the city's own (~66 ft), about 60 ft out from the foot of the castle wall.

The castle is entered by two gates, front and back.

  • The castle's main gate faces south, as most did, and its rear gate faces whichever way each capital sets.
    A castle's main gate (ote-mon) stood on its front, usually the south, and its rear gate (karamete-mon) behind. Our castle's main gate faces south, onto the grand avenue (ote-suji) that is the city's ceremonial approach, and the rear gate's direction is set for each castle.
  • The rear gate has its own smaller tower, its own bridge and its own road.
    In Shiro Daika, a capital our maps draw, the castle's rear gate opens to the north, with a gate tower smaller than the main gate's and its own bridge over the castle's moat, and a rear approach road runs from the gate to a T-junction with the Imperial road where it heads for the city's north gate. The grand avenue stays the ceremonial front; the rear approach is the working back door.

The rule the map follows: a capital's castle is drawn as its outer enceinte only - the rampart as a doubled line for its battered stone face, its own moat, and its two gates - with nothing inside it. No keep, no residence, no granary, no armory, no barracks, no well, no garden: not one structure stands on castle ground on a city map, and the same rule governs every compound whose interior is implied rather than drawn, the governor's mansion and the magistrate's manor included.

  • Nothing is drawn inside, because a drawn interior would have to match the castle's own plan.
    The castle, like a governor's mansion or a magistrate's manor, is the subject of a building plan of its own. Any interior drawn on the city map becomes a constraint that plan must meet, with nothing to enforce it, so the two would drift apart and the map would assert what the plan contradicts. An empty castle asserts nothing and cannot be wrong. The blank costs legibility; detail would cost a contradiction no one catches. The blank stands until a check exists that sets the plan's buildings onto the map.
  • The castle plans the shogunate ordered in 1644, early in the Edo period, were asked for the defenses, not the halls and storehouses inside them.
    In 1644 the shogun Iemitsu ordered the daimyo of the whole country to make plans of their castles, maps of the military works whose written notes were in principle limited to what mattered militarily. Their instructions asked for the size of each bailey's stone walls, earthworks and moats, for the keep, turrets and walls to be drawn, and for every street of the town down to the narrowest, and named no residence, hall or storehouse. A castle plan that shows the defenses and leaves out the halls within has a historical precedent.
  • Leaving out the keep, the turrets and the inner baileys is a deviation.
    The 1644 plans drew all three; our maps draw none, a choice argued by what was tried, below, not by history.
  • Drawing the inner baileys was tried once, and dropped.
    Two renders were made. The first drew the baileys one inside another on the castle's own axes, and read as a bullseye, a target rather than a fortress. The second set them off toward the side far from the main gate, so an attacker would cross the whole works under fire, drew the walls heavier or lighter by rank and enlarged the walled court inside the main gate until it showed; it was better, and still read as rectangles nested in rectangles.
  • Why did the inner baileys not read as a castle?
    Rectangles inside rectangles read as abstraction however they are arranged. What makes a castle read as one is irregular outlines, broad water between the baileys and turrets (yagura) at the corners, and none of those survives being drawn as walls alone at 3 ft to the pixel. That judgment is this project's, from its two renders, not a finding of history; the switch that would draw the baileys stays, turned off, and a castle with truly irregular baileys could reopen it.
  • The outer stone rampart is drawn as a doubled line.
    A castle's walls sloped on battered stone bases, and a doubled line reads as that mass where a single stroke reads as a fence - a drawing convention, the one part of the experiment kept. It is the outer wall, so it adds no interior to keep in step.
  • The blank will be judged again once the city around it is drawn.
    It was judged inside a city not yet built, where everything read as empty. At the heart of a dense map it should read better; if it still reads as a void then, the rule reopens. That is why a capital draws its castle first, before the rest of the city, so the call can be made from an early render.
  • The castle's ground is budgeted in full, drawn or not.
    The ground a castle takes is set by its walls and moat, not by what stands inside them, so a blank castle takes as much of its city's ground as a fully drawn one.
  • The castle's own plan would draw its keep in one of four forms - a knob.
    A castle might have a keep, a keep's stone base and no keep, a three-story turret in its place, or none of them, so a castle's plan picks one of the four for each castle.
  • How large would the keep on a castle's plan be?
    When a castle has one, its size falls within the keeps read: from Marugame's 15 m (~49 ft), the smallest surviving keep, to Hiroshima's 27 m (~89 ft) as rebuilt, on a plan of about 200 to 350 square meters (~2,153-3,767 sq ft), in an innermost bailey of about 2 ha (~5 acres).
