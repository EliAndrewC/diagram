Water gates and sluices (shuimen)

A water gate was a passage through the thickness of a city wall for a canal or a river and the boats on it, shut at night. At Suzhou's Pan Gate it stands beside the land gate, a doubled passage about 24 m (~79 ft) deep closed by a sluice gate and a wooden palisade, and Kaifeng let iron-clad lattice gates down to the water over its East Water Gate at night. A city had a water gate beside every land gate, as Suzhou did, or one wherever a river crossed its wall, as Kaifeng did, six of them there.

A sluice (suimon) is a board set across a channel to control how much water passes. Where a moat drew from a river and drained back to it, its gates held its level. In this record's reading, a GUESS on no page we read, they stood partly raised in ordinary times and were moved only for a flood, a drought, a dredging or a siege. The sluices that let water onto fields ran by rule: on the Jing canal in Shaanxi, under water rules written down by the fourteenth century, each opened only on a permit and in its turn, and in Japan water was handed out by turns in a drought from the medieval period.

  • Suzhou's Pan Gate has a doubled water gate, closed front and back by a sluice gate and a wooden palisade.
    Its water gate stands on the south side of the land gate and, like it, is doubled, an inner gate and an outer. The passage is 24 m (~79 ft) deep, with a sluice gate and a palisade set front and back, and a hidden passage between the two gates leads up to the gate tower. The city's government gives the two gates as 4.6 m (~15 ft) apart and the whole as 24.5 m (~80 ft) deep, with revetments built along both sides of the channel between them.
  • Kaifeng let iron-clad lattice gates down over its East Water Gate at night.
    The gate, on the Bian river's lower course, spanned the river. At night its iron-clad lattice gates were let down to the water like a sluice, and a gate on each bank beside it let people through on foot.
  • Where a whole river entered a city, its water gate could be a row of arches.
    Nanjing's East Water Gate, where the Qinhuai river enters the city, was built three stories high with eleven vaulted openings in each. The lowest story carried the water, and the upper two hid troops and stored grain. The Chinese Wikipedia article on the gate dates the level-control sluice in the lowest story to the late Qing dynasty, so the gate's earlier closing gear is not described.
  • How wide was a water gate's passage?
    No page we read gives it. Pan Gate's depth and the spacing of its two gates are published, but not the width of its water passage, nor how many openings it has.
  • Was a boom or a chain laid across the water at a gate?
    None of the pages we read on the water gates of Suzhou, Kaifeng and Nanjing describes one. The closing gear they describe is the lowered grille, the sluice gate and the palisade.

How many water gates a city had followed one of two patterns.

  • At Suzhou every city gate had a water gate beside it.
    On the plan of Suzhou carved in stone in 1229, each of the city's five gates is drawn with a land gate and a water gate together, so the city's canals reached every gate.
  • At Kaifeng a water gate stood wherever a river crossed the outer wall.
    The Dongjing Meng Hua Lu, a memoir of the city, names each with the river it carries: two on the Cai river, beside the Chenzhou and Dailou land gates; the East and West Water Gates on the Bian; the Northeast on the Wuzhang river; and the Northwest on the Jinshui. Two stand beside a land gate, and the rest are listed apart from any. The memoir never totals them; this record counts six.

A moat fed from a river had two sluices: an intake, where it drew the river in, and an outfall, where it let the water back out.

  • How was a sluice board raised?
    In this record's reading, a GUESS from the gate's form, the board rides in grooves cut in its two posts. It is lifted by hand on a small gate, or by a windlass on a larger one, from a timber crossbeam spanning the posts above the water, the keeper standing on the beam or the bank. None of the Japanese encyclopedia articles we read on the water gate and the sluice gate describes the mechanism.
  • Were a moat's sluices kept open or shut?
    Neither, in this record's reading, a GUESS: no page we read describes how a moat's gates were worked from day to day. A wet moat loses water all the time to seepage and evaporation, and standing water fouls. So a moat fed from a river and drained back to it would keep both its boards partly raised, a small steady flow in and the same out, and its keeper's ordinary work would be trimming the opening by a few inches.
  • Was a moat's intake closed when the river was in spate?
    In this record's reading, a GUESS, it was wound down hard: storm water carries the silt that chokes a feeder channel, and a moat must not surge. A canal of the Yuan dynasty in Shaanxi was rested the same way when its river rose in the summer spate.
  • Was a moat's outfall shut in a drought?
    In this record's reading, a GUESS, it was, to hold what water the moat had.
  • Were a moat's sluices shut in a siege?
    In this record's reading, a GUESS, both were, to hold as much water as the moat could.
  • Guangzhou's moat silted up and was dredged again and again.
    The gazetteer of Guangdong records Guangzhou's moat silting up over the years and dredged by successive officials, between 1041 and 1048 and again in 1210.
  • How were a moat's sluices set for a dredging?
    In this record's reading, a GUESS, the two worked against each other: the intake shut and the outfall wide open, to draw the water down for the diggers.

The sluices that let water onto fields did a different job: a moat's gates held a level, while a field's sluice delivered each field its share, and it ran on a calendar.

  • Under fourteenth-century Chinese canal rules, a field sluice opened only on a permit and in its turn.
    The water rules of the Jing canal in Shaanxi, written down under the Yuan dynasty, let a sluice open only when the office had issued its clerk a permit. The water ran at the appointed time, and the sluice was shut as soon as the watering was done, the water handed on to the sluices above it. The work's preface is dated 1342, and its rules cite an order of 1274.
  • The Jing canal ran for a season each year and was repaired between seasons.
    Water was let in from the first day of the tenth month and stopped in the seventh, and the canal and its weir were repaired in the eighth and ninth months, by the lunar calendar.
  • In Japan, water was handed out by turns in a drought (bansui) from the medieval period.
    Two Japanese reference works describe it as a way of sharing irrigation water in a drought, the fields drawing water in a fixed order, kept as unwritten custom until the late medieval period, when its rules came to be written down. Irrigation districts today still sometimes switch to it in a severe drought.
  • In what months did a rice field's intake open and shut?
    No page we read from before Japan began to modernize in 1868 gives the months. One district's present-day schedule opens its water on 21 April and shuts it on 28 September, while the records we read of turn-by-turn delivery and of water disputes before 1868 give no opening or closing month.
