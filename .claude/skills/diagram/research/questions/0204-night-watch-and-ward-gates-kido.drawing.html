How our maps draw the night watch and ward gates (kido)

Our maps bar a county seat's main street at night with a ward gate at each end of the built core, a keeper's hut beside each gate and a watch house at one end. A city draws watch houses for its commoner wards and guard boxes at the crossings of its samurai quarter, and a village may keep a single watchman's hut where its lane comes in. The maps draw the huts, not their keepers. Outside Edo many of the watchmen were of hinin status, and in the setting the keepers are the hinin.

  • A county seat draws a ward gate at each end of its main street, where the road leaves the built core.
    That is the post town's form, a gate at each end.
  • How many more gates does a county seat draw between the two at the ends of its main street?
    At most one more, across the street between them. Each county seat's number is chosen from the map's seed, within a small town's spacing of one gate to several wards.
  • A county seat draws a keeper's hut, about 6 by 9 ft, beside each ward gate.
    That is the fixed size of an Edo gate keeper's hut.
  • How big are the watch houses our maps draw?
    From about 9 by 12 ft up to 12 by 18 ft, chosen for each settlement from the map's seed. The source's "some 2 to 3 ken" does not say which side it measures, so reading the larger house as 2 by 3 ken is a GUESS.
  • A county seat's watch house stands at one end of its main street, against the gate.
    That is where a ward's watch house stood in Edo.
  • A county seat draws no mitsuke banks.
    The earthen banks at a town's entrance are recorded at post towns, and our county seats are not post towns.

A city draws more watch houses, at the same sizes, and adds guard boxes in its samurai quarter.

  • A city draws one watch house to each commoner ward, or one to two or three small wards.
    That is Edo's rule, which a provincial city takes over at its own number of wards. Each stands against the gate at the end of its ward's main street.
  • A city's samurai quarter draws guard boxes, about 12 by 9 ft, set into a compound's wall.
    That is the Edo guard box's usual size and seat.
  • A samurai quarter draws a guard box at each crossing of its streets.
    This is a GUESS: no page we read gives how many stood to a ward.

A village's watch is a single hut, where the village keeps one at all.

  • About one village in two keeps a hired watchman and draws his hut.
    Whether a village keeps one is a knob, chosen for each village from the map's seed. The share is a GUESS, since no page we read says how often a village kept one.
  • Where does a village's watch hut stand?
    Where the lane enters the village. The one placement found is a watch hut at a village's entrance, and a watchman who keeps the night watch and controls vagrants is best placed there, in this project's reading.
  • A village's watch hut is about 9 by 6 ft, and a hamlet keeps none.
    Both are a GUESS: no page we read gives the hut's size or says which villages kept a watchman.

The rule the map follows: whether a village keeps a watchman is chosen from its seed (about one village in two, a GUESS); one that does draws a single small hut, about 9 by 6 ft (a GUESS), beside the lane where it enters the village. A hamlet keeps none (a GUESS).
