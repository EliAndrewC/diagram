Groves around a southern Chinese village: the fengshui woods (fengshuilin) and the dooryard copse

Villages in the hills of southern China kept groves of trees to shape the flow of wind and water through their site - the fengshui woods, 風水林 (fengshuilin, "wind-water forest"). A village did not ring itself with them: it kept them at particular places in its landscape, chiefly a back grove on the slope behind the houses and a grove at the water mouth, where the village's stream ran out of it. Trees of practical use, fruit trees and bamboo among them, were planted in the woods as well. Among the houses themselves stood more bamboo and fruit trees, the dooryard copse - a GUESS, since no page we read describes them. Village custom protected these groves for centuries, which is why they survive as the region's most intact forest.

A village in Fujian commonly kept about two such groves. A Hong Kong village's back grove covers about a hectare (~2.5 acres) on average, though some in Fujian run to more than twenty hectares (~49 acres), and no ordinary Chinese water-mouth grove has been measured. The groves are centuries old, but every size known was measured on a grove that survives today.

  • A village's groves stood at chosen places in its landscape, not in a ring.
    A paper on the fengshui forests of southern Chinese villages, in its idealized schema of a typical village, places them at particular topographic points - mountain gaps where the wind is strong, cutbanks where the water is - with the rice paddies in front of the village. The field studies behind it covered 57 villages in Fujian in 1994 and 1995, and villages in Guangdong, Fujian, Jiangxi, Hunan and Anhui between 2011 and 2017.
  • The back grove stood immediately behind the village, upslope of it.
    The groves typically stand on the slope behind the village, on what is called its master mountain, and the Chinese Wikipedia article on fengshui woods says villages were often built beside dense woodland so that it became a green screen at their rear. Hong Kong's woods are seen today behind many villages, typically in a crescent shape.
  • The paddies lay open to the sun in front of the village, and the hills behind it kept off the winter wind.
    Direct sunlight favors rice, ideally grown on a broad floodplain south of the village, while the mountains behind it, to the north, block the cold winds of the winter monsoon.
  • Did the back grove also shelter the village from the winter wind?
    In this project's reading, yes: the grove stands on the slope behind the village, and that high back, to the north, is what blocks the cold winds of the winter monsoon.
  • What did a grove's keepers say it was for?
    Warding off misfortune. A stele of 1785 at Wangkou, in Wuyuan county, says the lineage living there leaned on the hill facing it as its screen, but that hill's cliffs pressed so close that left unplanted it would mostly portend misfortune, so trees had always been tended and raised on it in dense shade. Who kept such groves, and what a village could take from them, is at Who kept a southern Chinese village's fengshui woods.
  • The water-mouth grove stood at the village's low entrance, where its stream flowed out.
    A study of seven Fujian villages places this grove near a village's entrance and exit, to protect it from evil qi, where the stream flows out of the village, and finds the province's village fengshui forests mainly near village entrances, ancestral halls and temples.
  • A village could have further groves along its river, above and below the houses.
    The caption to a photograph in the paper on southern Chinese fengshui forests notes several more groves of this kind, out of the frame, along the river above and below the village it shows.
  • The back groves are centuries old.
    In Hong Kong the Herbarium dates the oldest fengshui wood back more than 300 years, while a 2025 newspaper piece says many have stood for well over 300 years and some more than 600, while much of the forest around them grew back only after the Second World War.
  • Most of Hong Kong's fengshui woods have lasted outside any modern reserve.
    Sixty per cent of the known woods lie outside any legally protected zone.
  • In Huizhou, villages were planned around their water mouth by the mid-sixteenth century.
    In Huizhou, in the hills of southern Anhui, a village laid out between its stream's source and its water mouth was an important feature of village plans by the middle of the sixteenth century at the latest.
  • Eighteenth-century verse shows Huizhou's water mouths under dense trees and bamboo.
    One eighteenth-century poet describes the water mouth under dense shade, another its screen of bamboo and trees.
  • Fruit trees and bamboo were planted in the woods for their use.
    The Chinese Wikipedia article on fengshui woods says villagers planted trees of practical value in them, such as fruit trees, banyan, camphor and bamboo, so that the woods had real economic value.
  • This project takes a southern Chinese village's houses to have stood among scattered bamboo and fruit trees.
    Litchi, longan, persimmon, citrus and mulberry by the doors, and some villages ringed in bamboo, would make the village leafy rather than bare. It is a GUESS from general reading. The dooryard copse as the Japanese record knows it is at How big was the village's dooryard copse? and Groves of trees around farmhouses (yashikirin).
  • Did a clustered village shelter behind one shared wood rather than a grove for each house?
    This project takes one village-scale wood to be the dominant southern Chinese pattern for a nucleated village, and a grove round each house to be the dispersed farmstead's form. It is a GUESS.

Which sides of a village its back grove took, what trees it was planted with and how a lane passed through it are at Shelter belts on a village's windward side (bofurin).

  • How many groves did a village keep?
    About two, where they have been counted. A study of Fujian reports that most villages there keep two fengshui forests on average, a figure it takes from an earlier survey and gives for the province or for Youxi County within it - the passage read does not say which. Two Buyi villages in Guizhou each hold two parcels of fengshui forest.
  • How dense were the groves?
    Densely: about one stem for every 32 sq ft, the density of a mature broadleaf forest. Thirty-two well-protected groves in the Pearl River Delta, sampled with one 1,200 m² (~12,917 sq ft) strip each, averaged about 3,400 stems a hectare (~1,377 an acre) and a basal area of 49 m² a hectare (~213 sq ft an acre), values their authors call consistent with well-developed evergreen broadleaved forest. Five fragments in Guangzhou had a basal area of 30.0 to 52.5 m² a hectare (~131-229 sq ft an acre).
  • How did a fengshui wood look?
    Tall, dense and dark within. The Hong Kong Herbarium describes a mature wood's canopy as dense, its top story of very tall trees, often over 20 m (~66 ft), whose crowns show plainly from outside while inside only their trunks can be seen; within, most of the sunlight is blocked out.
  • How large was a village's back grove?
    About a hectare (~2.5 acres), though they vary widely. Hong Kong's survey of 115 village woods, each boundary drawn on a map and measured, gives a median of about 1 ha (~2.5 acres) and a mean of 1.1 ha (~2.7 acres): half are under a hectare, four in ten between one and two, one in ten at two or more, and they range from 0.06 to 5.3 ha (~0.15-13 acres). The median, mean and shares are this project's arithmetic over the survey's table.
  • Zhongshan's groves average about 4 hectares (~10 acres).
    In Zhongshan, in the Pearl River Delta of Guangdong, a 2016 survey counts 66 fengshui groves in 66 villages, 269.85 ha (~667 acres) in all - about 4.1 ha (~10 acres) each, by this project's arithmetic. The passages read give no grove's own area, so the spread behind that mean is not known.
  • Some Fujian groves are far larger.
    In Youxi County two villages' fengshui forests are listed as over three and over six hectares (~7 and ~15 acres), and Lingtou's cover over 20 ha (~49 acres), kept for over 800 years. A 22 ha (~54 acre) back grove in Nanjing County, in Fujian, was saved in 1958-59 and made the Letu Subtropical Rain Forest Nature Reserve by 1963, and another paper gives a 133 ha (~329 acre) lineage forest at Letu - probably the same forest, sized differently by the two papers, a GUESS from the shared name.
  • A typical village grove is nevertheless small.
    The paper on southern Chinese fengshui forests calls them typically small in area.
  • How many trees did a grove hold?
    Perhaps a hundred to a few hundred canopy trees over a dense understory - a GUESS, since no source we could read counts a Chinese back grove's trees; only two exceptional water-mouth groves, at Huangling and Xin'anyuan, are counted below. Korean village groves hold from fewer than 10 to more than 100 trees each, and at the Pearl River Delta's density a grove of 1 to 2 ha (~2.5-4.9 acres) would carry some 3,400 to 6,800 stems of every size.

Korea's village groves include a kind that stands where the Chinese water-mouth grove does, at the village entrance, and these have been measured.

  • How big was a water-mouth grove?
    Probably a fraction of a hectare - a GUESS from the Korean analogue. The village-entrance grove (donggusup) was the commonest type in a Korean field survey, 42 of the 100 groves surveyed, averaging 7,149 m² (~1.8 acres) and standing about 727 m (~2,385 ft) from the village center. Twenty-four village groves in Namwon City, South Korea, average 0.22 ha (~0.54 acre), from 0.025 to 0.63 ha (~0.06-1.6 acres).
  • One Chinese water-mouth grove, at Xin'anyuan in Anhui, is reported at about 7 hectares (~17 acres) and called the largest in East China.
    A Huangshan Daily piece puts the water-mouth grove of Xin'anyuan village, in Xiuning County, Anhui, at over a hundred mu - about 7 ha (~17 acres) - with nearly a thousand ancient trees, and calls it the largest in East China: an extreme rather than a type.
  • Huangling's water-mouth grove holds over a hundred trees, most over five hundred years old.
    At Huangling, in Wuyuan county (in Jiangxi today), the water-mouth grove is mainly Chinese yew, mixed with camphor, ginkgo, pine and others.
  • Were the groves as large before 1912, when China's last imperial dynasty fell?
    No old record says. No page we read gives an area, a width or a tree count for a back grove or a water-mouth grove as it stood before 1912; every size above was measured in the twentieth century or since, on a grove that survives. The one partial answer is Huangling: trees that old stood there before 1912, so its grove already held something like a hundred trees then - a GUESS from their ages, since trees lost or planted since are not recorded. That the surviving groves keep the extent they had before is a GUESS.
