How our maps decide where a farming hamlet grows its coarse grain

Where an alluvial fan's middle is left wild, the dry fields along its toe are far too small to grow a hamlet's coarse grain. Each map therefore draws that grain in one of two places: on the paddy, as barley sown after the rice where the paddy drains, or in fields cleared up into the fan's wild middle where it does not. Which of the two a settlement takes is its winter crop, chosen at random (at How our maps show the paddy through the rice year).

  • Where a hamlet's paddy carries barley over the winter, its grain is drawn there and its dry fields stay on the toe.
    Barley followed the rice on a paddy that drained, never on a wet one, so only the drained plots count.
  • A drained paddy's winter barley is counted acre for acre against the dry field it spares.
    That is a GUESS: no page we read gives a winter barley crop's yield.
  • Where a hamlet's paddy lies bare over the winter, its dry fields are drawn climbing into the fan's wild middle.
    The dry plots climb from the toe into the middle, plot by plot, out as far from the canal as the ground runs, until they hold the grain the hamlet needs: the middle is cleared as far as the grain required and no further. A fan's middle, once cleared, held wheat-and-barley fields among its uses.
  • The clearing starts from the plots nearest the toe.
    That order is a GUESS: no page we read says from which end the clearing went.
  • How much dry field does a hamlet's grain need?
    About 0.85 acre a household, beside its 1.3 acres of paddy, a GUESS resting on attested rates. By this project's reckoning a household ate about half as much coarse grain as rice, a third of its diet against two-thirds. The land surveys rated each grade of field by the grain it was expected to yield: a middle-grade paddy at 1.3 koku, and, at an Edo-period rate, a middle-grade dry field at 1 koku. Half the paddy's ground, at the dry field's lower yield, comes to about 0.85 acre.
  • A bare winter paddy is drawn only where the fan's dry ground can grow the hamlet's whole grain need.
    The toe and the whole wild middle together must be able to hold it. On a fan whose middle is already cleared, the cleared ground is drawn only as the hem along the whole canal, which cannot hold the whole need, so such a hamlet is always drawn growing its barley on its paddy.
  • No grain is drawn in the island fields inside the paddy or on a levee.
    The island fields are recorded with cotton, mulberry and figs, not grain, and the levees are recorded as the dry fields of the low, wet alluvial lowland. Nothing we read puts the grain of a hamlet at a fan's toe in either.
