How our maps lay the lanes inside a clustered settlement

Inside a clustered settlement, the lanes form one network reaching every farmhouse but the few reached across a neighbor's land.

  • The farmhouses are placed first, and the lanes are drawn among them after.
    A village lane is a footpath worn by villagers walking between their houses: in this setting the homesteads were built first and the lanes came after, as a worn path forms (Village lanes), so the maps seat every farmhouse before they lay any lane, and each lane follows the houses rather than the houses following it. An Imperial road is the exception, a planned work that houses were then built along, so it may be laid before the settlement that fronts it.
  • Neighbors stand a lane's width apart, and the lanes are laid in the gaps between them.
    A clustered settlement parts every two homesteads by a lane's room of 18 ft - 7 ft at each garden fence and a 4 ft tread between - and a 2 ft parting between the two holdings, 20 ft in all - a map drawing convention. A household reached across a neighbor's yard stands 2 ft from it. No way is sought or kept while houses are seated; once the last house stands, the track out's start is set on the cluster's edge and each household's way is laid from its dooryard along the middle of the gaps to the track out or an earlier way, joined at a T. That a clustered village's lanes are the gaps left between its house plots is this record's own reading, a GUESS (Village lanes). A village-scale map, unlike a hamlet's, finds each path as it seats a house, and faces each farmhouse along the field's edge, not a lane - two DEVIATIONS.
  • A few farmhouses may be reached across a neighbor's land, with no lane of their own.
    In Japan a household whose land had no way of its own to the road crossed a neighbor's land by custom, and in Echigo the way ran on across two plots in a chain (Village lanes). So each clustered settlement rolls a share of its households that may be reached that way, from none up to a quarter, rounded down to whole households. The share is a GUESS: the record shows the custom but not how common it was, and the clustered villages it describes, Enoshima and the surveyed Manchu village, reach their houses by alleys. Such a household is seated only where it has no way of its own to the lanes - the custom's own condition, historically accurate. A way of its own here means a dooryard that opens onto the ground the lanes are laid in, connected to open ground on the track out's side; no path is looked for while it is seated. Cutting through a neighbor's yard was no great matter in this setting, but the map counts any lane ground a dooryard opens onto, however far round, since a way is laid to each - a map drawing convention. A walk from its dooryard to the neighbor's threshing yard must keep to the two households' land and cross no house, garden bed, shed or fixture of either. Its land must adjoin the neighbor's, no more than 3 ft apart: the 2 ft parting it stands from that neighbor, and a foot more. That margin is a GUESS, since the custom is land against land, never a walk across open ground. A household may be reached across a neighbor who is itself reached across another, and no farther - a GUESS resting on the Echigo chain, the longest the record reads. The walk is not drawn as a lane: dooryard and threshing yard are open ground, and the walk a custom, not a way - this record's reading, a GUESS. So it shows no lane of its own, standing against its neighbor, whose own way is always drawn. Whether it has a way of its own is judged by its whole holding: if any layout of the homestead would leave it one, it has one. Once the ways are laid, one that a way reaches is given it and is no longer reached across its neighbor; no house is moved to find one. It is placed only on its neighbor's threshing-yard side - a map drawing convention.
  • Every other farmhouse is served by a lane of its own.
    Its way leaves its dooryard round its own garden beds and fixtures - a map drawing convention. Where none can be laid that keeps the rules below - a pocket the field, the water, the map's edge or its neighbors close off - the household is reached across the yard of its nearest neighbor with a way, the walk not drawn: a household cut through a neighbor's yard in a pinch.
  • Past its last farmhouse, a lane ends at the dooryard or runs on to something a reader can see.
    That may be a field path, a bund or another way; a lane end that reaches nothing is pulled back to the last house it serves. No page we read says how far a lane ran past its last house, so the rule is a GUESS, and so is how close counts as serving a house.
  • Nothing is built on a lane.
    Farmsteads front a lane, and no corner of a house stands on its tread.
  • Which of two forms a settlement's lanes take is a knob, chosen at random for each map.
    Either side lanes branch off the main lane between the house plots to reach the row behind, or back lanes run the length of the cluster behind its ranks of houses, parallel to the field's edge. A back lane implies a planned village and side lanes one that grew over time, in the record's reading (Village lanes), and a hamlet in this setting could plausibly be either, so the maps vary it between settlements rather than settle it once. Both forms are laid after every house stands and every household's own way is laid in the gaps; they differ only in which way they cut through the cluster. A back lane is laid so:
    • it runs the length of the cluster at one distance back from the field's edge, following the edge's curve, in a gap between two ranks of houses at least 18 ft wide - the widest such gap where several would do;
    • as few back lanes are laid as bring every farmhouse within 100 ft of one, but a household reached across a neighbor's yard;
    • each runs only along the houses it serves, and about 30 ft past the last of them;
    • cross lanes through the gaps between houses, about one to every 300 ft of the cluster's length, join the back lanes, so that they make one framework rather than parallel lanes that never meet - the framework the planned English villages' back lanes made with their main street (Village lanes);
    • a stretch that would cross the field, a crop, wet ground or a homestead is cut out, and a stretch that joins nothing of the network is not drawn.
    How many back lanes, where they run and how often they are tied is a map drawing convention, and the 100 ft reach a GUESS at how close counts as serving a house.
  • A dispersed hamlet has no lanes between its farmhouses.
    Its farmhouses stand scattered among the fields, so a lane runs to the hamlet and nothing runs between the houses - a value of the settlement-form knob.
  • A clustered settlement's lanes are one network, joined where their treads meet.
    A lane that runs along a garden fence to reach its junction belongs to the network, because the plot fronts the lane.
  • A lane's middle keeps at least 7 ft clear of a garden fence.
    The gap is a map drawing convention, not a measured figure. The 7 ft is measured from the fence to the middle of the lane, so the edge of a 3 ft footpath may pass 5.5 ft from the fence - a DEVIATION.
