Samurai country manors (bushi yakata)

In this setting a samurai family may keep a country estate close to farmland, but the estate is never part of the village. History offers two forms of such a house. The older is the medieval warrior's moated residence, a square enclosure of about 1 to 3 acres inside a moat and an earthen bank, where the warrior lived among the paddy and fields that bondservants farmed before its gate. The later is the Edo-period lord's suburban residence, a second house on the edge of town: a garden for the lord's leisure, whose vegetable plots and bamboo groves supplied the main residence.

The medieval residences are known from excavations, a deed and one scholars' reconstruction.

  • How big was a warrior's moated residence?
    Commonly about one cho square - about 109 m (~358 ft) a side, near 3 acres - enclosed by a moat and an earthen bank. The measured ones read here run smaller: a fourteenth-century residence in Toyama sat inside a square moat about 62 m (~203 ft) by 80 m (~262 ft), about 1.2 acres, and 2.5 m (~8 ft) deep, with an earthen bank inside built of the moat's spoil, and a castle site whose dig found a moat, wells and household goods is put at about 90 m (~295 ft) square, about 2 acres.
  • A medieval deed of 1310 hands over a warrior's paddy together with a house site of a little over an acre, holding several buildings.
    It transfers five cho of paddy together with a dwelling on a house site of five tan - roughly half a hectare, a little over an acre, on the later measure of the tan - and the site held more than one building, among them a separate kitchen house.
  • What did a large warrior's residence hold?
    Scholars' reconstruction of a large Kanto warrior's residence, paraphrased by one town's history, fills in the parts. It had a near-square site, a moat dry or wet, a bank one or two meters (~3-7 ft) high and a fence running round, and a gate with a lookout over it. Before the gate stood the thatched row houses of the servants, with the gate paddy before them, and inside the gate more servants' houses and a stable. The master's house stood at the center, roofed in thatch or board, with a riding ground behind it or outside the residence, and a storehouse for the rice, barley and beans paid in as rent.
  • How big was the large Kanto warrior's residence the town history describes?
    About 150 to 200 m (~492-656 ft) a side, about 5.5 to 10 acres. The town history suggests its own district's residences, if it had any, may have been smaller versions of it.

The Edo-period form was a lord's or an upper retainer's, and a garden rather than a fortified farm.

  • A lord's suburban residence at Edo was a garden estate that also supplied the main house.
    It held gardens for the lord's leisure, and vegetable gardens and bamboo groves that supplied the main residence in the city.
  • Lords and their upper retainers kept such residences in other castle towns too.
    Domain lords, or their upper retainers, are found setting one up in castle towns other than Edo, besides the castle residence or their main house.
  • Some lords' residences on Edo's outskirts stood on farmland bought from commoners, and still paid that land's tax.
    A residence a lord built on land bought from commoners, mostly in Edo's outskirts, still bore the rent and levies laid on that land.

    The pages read give sizes only for estates kept by lords.
