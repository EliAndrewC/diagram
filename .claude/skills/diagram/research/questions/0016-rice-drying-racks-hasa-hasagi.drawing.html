How our maps draw rice-drying racks (hasa, hasagi)

On our maps a rice-drying rack stands by every farmhouse where the settlement's harvest weather is changeable, along one side of its yard, and by none where the weather is settled. Racks out on the paddies stood only for the month or so after the harvest, and our maps do not yet show a particular season, so none is drawn on the fields.

  • A settlement's harvest weather, not chance, decides whether its houses have racks.
    Which way rice was dried changed with the natural and social setting of the land that took it up, and in the record it differs between regions, not between neighboring villages in one climate - this record's reading - so our maps never pick it at random village by village. It follows the harvest weather the settlement's own description states, settled or changeable, and neighboring settlements in one climate draw the same.
  • Where the harvest weather is changeable, each farmhouse has a rack beside its yard.
    Racks were built together near the house on the changeable San'in coast, so that the threshing could be done at home.
  • Where the harvest weather is settled, did the racks stay out on the fields?
    That is this project's reading, and our maps draw no rack at the house there: Nishimura and Makino's 1959 survey names racks gathered by the house only for the changeable San'in coast, and racks were mostly built on the fields.
  • A settlement whose description gives no harvest weather is drawn with settled weather.
    Racks gathered by the house are recorded for one region's changeable coast, so a settlement whose weather is not stated takes the other case - this project's decision.
  • Which side of the yard does a rack take?
    Along one side, in the half nearest the house where that leaves it room, and never in the yard's southern half, so that it shades the ground north of it and never the drying floor, which needs the sun from the south (How our maps keep yards and gardens in the sun). No page we read says which side of the yard a rack stood on, so this is a GUESS.
  • A rack by the house runs from the yard's edge to its middle.
    No page we read gives how much rack a household put up, so this length is a GUESS: from the yard's edge by the house to its middle, or along the whole side where the near half has no room.
  • A rack is drawn thicker than its poles, so that it can be seen.
    Its poles at their real width would be too thin to see at the map's scale - a map drawing convention.
  • Our maps draw no racks on the fields, where they stood only in the month after the harvest.
    The sheaves hung usually twenty to thirty days, and our maps do not yet show a particular season. A map of that month would draw them as thin lines on the reaped paddies, the long single-level racks reaching about 330 ft.
  • A row of trees planted as rack posts (hasagi) along a lane is not drawn.
    Such a row can be a modern arrangement: the one row whose planting we found dates from 1943-1945, when a district's consolidated paddies were laid out.
