Writing schools (terakoya)

A terakoya was a private school of the Edo period where a writing master taught the children of commoners, townsfolk and farmers alike, the first steps of reading, writing and reckoning. It needed only a master and one room to teach in, and there were said to be about as many of them as there were villages in Japan. A school held anywhere from 10 to 100 pupils. A domain's own school for its retainers' sons, the domain school (hanko), was another thing, with buildings of its own.

  • The name points to the temple.
    A report quoted in the article says the schools were called terakoya in the Kamigata region because many children studied at the nearby temple.
  • Who kept the schools?
    In one area of Bingo province the seven schools of the late Edo period, not all open at once, were kept by one village headman, two village officials, one doctor, two monks and one other.
  • Where was a village's school held?
    Most likely wherever its master lived: in the headman's house, a doctor's, an official's, or the temple. That is an inference from who the masters were, a GUESS - no page we read says where a village's school was held. On the village temple, see whether a village keeps one and what stands in its precinct.
  • How many schools did a village keep?
    In what is now Hannan city, one terakoya each in four villages and two in a fifth are recorded for the end of the Edo period: one or two to a village that had a school.
  • How many writing schools did Japan have?
    About 15,500 to 16,500 were counted, and the count missed many regions altogether. The survey behind it, published as the Nihon kyoikushi shiryo ("Materials on the history of Japanese education"), is read as 15,560 schools in one book and 16,560 in another retelling. The popular figure of about as many schools as villages, some 60,000, is far above any count.
  • How many writing schools did the city of Edo have?
    The same national survey, the Nihon kyoikushi shiryo, counts 400 to 500 large ones there, and 1,000 to 1,300 counting the small.
  • No page we read counts the writing schools of a provincial castle town or a country town.
    The pages we read give counts for Edo, for the nation and for a few villages only.

In China, schooling for a family's children or a county's poor was likewise held in a building that stood for other purposes, or was set up by the state.

  • A Chinese family's ancestral hall served as its children's school.
    The hall held its rites in spring and autumn, and in ordinary times served as a private school (sishu) for the family's children.
  • The Qing court ordered charity schools for orphaned and poor children in 1712.
    In the 51st year of the Kangxi emperor (1712; the page gives 1713) it ordered provinces, prefectures and counties to set up many charity schools that gathered the orphaned and poor and hired teachers for them.
  • Charity-school pupils were mostly six to eleven years old.
    So says the Chinese Wikipedia article on charity schools.
