Sunlight and shade on the farm

In the northern hemisphere the midday sun stands to the south, so anything tall throws its shadow to its north, east and west, and the ground to its south keeps the sun. A sixth-century Chinese farm manual warns that no grain grows in the elm's shade, and reckons a tree's shade to reach about as far as the tree is tall. The groves around farmhouses commonly stand some 10 to 20 m (~33-66 ft), and the vegetables of a kitchen bed want about six hours of direct sun a day.

Where a farm's trees stood followed the wind as much as the sun. In Tohoku many homestead groves stand on the north and west of the house, while on the Tonami plain the tall trees stood from the south round to the west, against the spring south wind, and the east front of the house held little grove. Which sides a grove took is at Groves of trees around farmhouses (yashikirin), and how the yard dried its grain in the sun at Threshing and drying yards at farmhouses (niwa).

  • A sixth-century Chinese farm manual says an elm's shade reaches as far as the tree is tall.
    The Qimin Yaoshu says the elm's shade reaches, on the east, the west and the north, as far as the tree itself, by its height and breadth, and tells the planter to set elms on the north edge of the garden ground - the side from which no noon shadow falls on it.
  • How tall were windbreak groves?
    The modern surveys run from about 9 to 28 m (~30-92 ft), most of their figures between 10 and 22 m (~33-72 ft). A Sendai planting list counts cedar, zelkova, black pine and hinoki as tall trees of 15 m (~49 ft) and more, with a lower layer of trees of about 10 m (~33 ft) and shrubs of about 3 m (~10 ft). Two surveys of Tohoku groves measured them at 11-22 m (~36-72 ft) and about 9-16 m (~30-52 ft). A 2022 paper describes one 10 m (~33 ft) high on the windward side. Tonami's trees stood 11-28 m (~36-92 ft), about 15 m (~49 ft) on average.
  • Okinawa's fukugi groves are lower, about 7 to 10 m (~23-33 ft).
    A survey of the old fukugi trees lining the houses of five Okinawan villages found mean heights of 7.1 to 9.9 m (~23-33 ft).
  • Were the groves cut back to a fixed height?
    Only one kind is recorded as kept to one: the pines round the houses of the Izumo plain, clipped flat at a fixed height, though no page we read gives the height or how often. Elsewhere the groves are pruned of their limbs - at Sawadaue, in the Tohoku city of Osaki, the 23 households whose groves remain prune them and clear the fallen trees in common - and no page we read speaks of cutting their tops.
  • How tall was a windbreak that a farm kept in use?
    About 10 m (~33 ft), against 15-25 m (~49-82 ft) for a mature stand left untended. This is a GUESS: the surveyed heights include both kinds, and no source says which is which.
  • Were the groves as tall before Japan began to modernize in 1868?
    No page from before 1868 gives a grove's height. The Edo record of the Tonami grove speaks of large cedars sold from one, but not how tall they were, and every height found is from a survey of the 1980s or later.
  • How many hours of direct sun did a kitchen bed need?
    It depends on the crop. Vegetables fall into three classes by the direct sun they need: sun crops want direct sun all day - about six hours or more - and will not grow in shade; half-shade crops do with three to four hours, or with light dappled through leaves all day; shade crops grow on one or two hours. Daikon, eggplant, cucumber and the beans are sun crops; turnip, komatsuna, taro, negi and ginger are half-shade crops; mitsuba, shiso and myoga are shade crops. A farm cooperative's lists put the same crops in the same classes, though they name neither turnip nor shiso. This is modern advice for modern cultivars: no Edo farm manual we could read says where a household set its vegetable bed for the sun.
  • Daikon, the kitchen bed's autumn crop, needs six hours of sun in the season of the longest shadows.
    Daikon is a sun crop, and autumn daikon is sown in early autumn and pulled from late autumn into winter, when the sun stands lowest and throws the longest shadows.
  • On the Tonami plain, the tall trees stood south and west, and fruit trees at the open east front.
    A survey of the plain's groves found many tall trees - cedar, oak and zelkova - from the south round to the west of the house, and cedar, hackberry and alder, often mixed with bamboo, from the west round to the north. The east, the front of the house, had little grove, and flowering and fruit trees were often planted there. The grove held off the strong south wind of early spring and the cold seasonal wind of winter. The pages that place these trees describe the groves as they stood in the late twentieth century.
  • Accounts differ on where a farm's bamboo stood.
    The Tonami survey puts it in the grove from the west round to the north; the Japanese Wikipedia article on homestead groves puts Tonami's storehouse, barn, fruit trees and bamboo on the south side. The Qimin Yaoshu sets a garden's bamboo in its northeast corner, because bamboo spreads to the southwest and will fill the garden of itself in some years.
