How our maps draw ancestral halls in south-China villages (citang)

On our maps a south-China village has one ancestral hall, a row of them, or none, as such villages did. A hall stands at the front of the village facing its pond, three bays wide and three halls deep, the commonest form of a hall.

  • A knob chooses one of three layouts for a village's halls.
    One hall at the village's center, a row of halls across its front, or none: the three layouts a historian of these villages finds.
  • A hall stands in the village's front row, facing the pond.
    Where the village has a hall and a pond, the hall stands in the front row of buildings with its door to the water, and the drying floor, the heping, between them is left open, as the half-moon pond's own forecourt is left open (How our maps draw and label the half-moon pond (banyuechi)).
  • Every hall is drawn three bays wide and three halls deep.
    Its three halls stand one behind another on one axis. That is the commonest form of a hall of standing. The two-hall form of a small, less wealthy lineage's hall is not drawn: taking the one form for every hall is a simplification where the record has two.
  • How big is a hall drawn?
    About 110 ft wide and 74 ft deep, and that size is a GUESS. The only measured depth we found is the 8.5 to 9 m (~28-30 ft) of the rear buildings of several halls at Langtou, in Guangzhou prefecture; no page we read gives the whole width and length of a common hall on the ground.
