How our maps draw the street along the inside of a city wall

Every walled city on our maps runs a patrol road as a closed loop just inside its rampart, as Chinese cities ran a street along the inside of their walls, one of which, at Tongzhou, began as a road ridden to patrol it. The road runs about 100 ft inside the rampart and is about 20 ft wide. The city's quarters are packed inside the road rather than up to the wall, and nothing but the wall's own works stands on it. Where the fence of a walled ward reaches the wall, the fence crosses the road through a gate and ends on the rampart itself.

  • How far inside the rampart does the patrol road run, and how wide is it?
    On our maps it runs about 102 ft in and is about 20 ft wide. Both figures are GUESSES: the one wall-side street measured, at Tang Chang'an, a capital, was 66 to 82 ft wide.
  • Nothing may stand on the road's bed.
    No house, no ministry, no governor's compound, no temple, no field. The road is kept clear so that troops could be moved along the wall wherever they were wanted, which is this project's reading of what the street was for, a GUESS.
  • The wall's own works may line the patrol road.
    The gate guard houses, the inspection stations and the mural towers stand beside it.
  • Houses are held off by a clear corridor along the road.
    The corridor reaches half the road's width plus 63 ft from its center, so that even a large or turned house's nearest corner stays well clear of the bed.
  • The patrol road need not be built up.
    Unlike an ordinary city street, it is exempt from the rule that a street must be lined with buildings, its wall side being bare by design.
  • A street or alley running at the patrol road ends on it.
    It reaches the road's bed and ends there, neither stopping a sliver short in open ground nor poking a stub out the far side. A street that genuinely crosses the road and carries well past it is a different thing, and is fine.
  • Keeping the patrol road and its margin clear costs a city about eight percent of its building ground.
    Pulling the built ground in off the wall costs about one perimeter row of buildings, by this project's own measurement.
  • Our maps make up that ground elsewhere.
    A map that adds the patrol road makes the ground back, usually by a small rise in density in one or two quarters, rather than quietly housing fewer inhabitants than the city is given.

Where a ward's fence reaches the city wall, our maps end it on the rampart.

  • A ward fence ends on the rampart; it does not cross it.
    On our maps the rampart is what seals the ward: the fence's ends abut solid wall so that the ward gate cannot simply be walked around. A fence continuing out through the rampart would enclose nothing on the far side, and would draw as two walls crossing rather than one wall closing against another. It is the convention these maps follow wherever two lines meet: one ends at the junction, as a street ends on the patrol road and a channel joins another at a T or a Y.
  • Did ward walls really run up to the city wall?
    Not at the one city whose wards the sources describe. At Tang Chang'an every ward was walled on all four sides and a wall-side street ran along all four sides inside the outer wall, so no ward wall met the city wall at all. Ending a ward fence on the rampart is a GUESS that runs against that evidence, chosen for how the map draws a closed ward; the Tang form would close the fence along the inner edge of that street instead of borrowing the rampart as the ward's fourth side.
  • Each fence end is snapped onto the wall before it is drawn.
    Measured on the three drawn cities, fence ends laid by hand stood 3 to 15 ft off the wall: too little to see, but enough to leave a stub of fence outside the rampart. Each end is snapped onto the wall's centerline, trimmed or extended along its own last segment to where that line crosses the wall's line; a snap straight to the nearest point would kink the fence's last stretch off its line. A last segment running parallel to the wall never meets it, and falls back to the nearest point on the wall's line.
  • A fence end more than 72 ft from the wall is not moved.
    That is not a junction but a fence failing to reach the rampart, a real hole in the ward, and dragging it onto the wall would hide it, so it is left as an error to fix.
  • A fence end only a few feet past the wall's line shows as a stub outside the rampart.
    The rampart's stroke covers only its own half width, about 16 ft, while the fence line is drawn with a rounded end that inks about 7 ft past its last corner. An end 15 ft out, inked 7 ft further, against the rampart's 16 ft, leaves about 6 ft of palisade standing on the moat's bank outside the rampart, at three feet to the pixel.
  • Our maps judge a fence by where its ink ends, not where its line ends.
    Both stroke widths are read from the drawing's own records, so that what is drawn and what is judged cannot drift apart.
  • A fence may not cross the wall mid-run either.
    Its interior corners are measured the same way: a fence that dives out through the wall and back is the same crossing, further from its end.
  • A fence end may fall up to about 30 ft short of the wall or beyond it.
    Our maps accept a fence end within about 30 ft of the wall's line either way. A fence stopping further short leaves a gap the commoners could walk around.
