Farmland around towns and cities

A market town sat in the middle of the countryside it served: in China the villages that traded at one town lay within a walk of a few miles of it, and cities, too, grew up to serve the rural population around them.

The ground closest to a town was worked hardest. A Japanese farming manual of 1707, in the Edo period, sets out a ladder of manures around the castle town of Kanazawa, from urine and abundant manure within one ri to bushes and grasses in remote places, because night soil is heavy, and carrying it far cost too much.

  • How large was the countryside a Chinese market town served?
    A study of the markets of rural China finds that in the typical case a town's marketing area was just over 50 square kilometers (~19 square miles), market towns stood less than 8 km (~5 miles) apart, and the longest walk to town was about 4.5 km (~3 miles): some 1,500 households in eighteen or so villages.
  • In China, cities grew up to serve the countryside around them.
    A study of China's walled capitals says that cities developed to serve the needs of the rural population, and so grew up along the waterways that linked them to rural communities and to other cities.
  • The wall of the Chinese city of Suzhou still enclosed farmland in the twentieth century.
    Suzhou's city wall still enclosed large tracts of farmland in the Republican era, after 1912.

In Japan the record shows how hard that farmland was worked, how that changed with distance from town, and which ground became paddy and which dry field.

  • Around Kanazawa, the nearer a field lay to the castle town, the richer its manure.
    A farming manual written in Kanazawa in 1707 gives the ladder: within one ri, about 4 km (~2.5 miles), of the town the fields got urine and abundant manure; within about three ri, manure, rapeseed cake and dried sardine; beyond four ri, manure, ash and dried and raw sardine; and in remote places bushes and grasses as well.
  • Farmers near Kanazawa traded vegetables for the town's urine every morning.
    For the farmers within one ri, urine was the main fertilizer, and every morning they brought vegetables into town to exchange for its households' urine.
  • Night soil was heavy, so the cost of carrying it limited how far it went.
    It is mostly water, and it cost much more to carry and handle than the commercial fertilizers.
  • How far would the heavily manured ground reach around a town smaller than Kanazawa?
    Less far than around Kanazawa, by this project's reading. The ladder is a castle town's, and it measures the kind of manure a field got, not how much of the ground was farmed.
  • Was the land left fallow far from town?
    No page we read says. The account of the manure ladder around Kanazawa says nothing of fallow at all.
  • In a wet lowland, settlements and dry fields stood on the river's natural levee, and the back marsh became paddy.
    A natural levee is a slight rise along a river's channel, and within the low, wet lowland around it the levee drains well, so the old settlements of such a plain sit first of all on it, and it is also used as dry field. Behind it the flood water drains poorly and the ground turns to wetland, and where wet rice predominates, as in Japan, that back marsh was reclaimed as paddy.
