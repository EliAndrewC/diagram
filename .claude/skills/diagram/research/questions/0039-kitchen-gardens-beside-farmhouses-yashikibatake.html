Kitchen gardens beside farmhouses (yashikibatake)

A Japanese farm household grew vegetables for its own table on a plot of its own, the yashikibatake, the "homestead field", which went by other names from region to region. It might lie in the yard before the house, all round the house, or on spare ground away from it. An early Edo-period farm book lists, month by month, the greens, roots, beans and gourds a household ate. No page we read measures the bed itself; where it lay by the house, it shared a house lot of a few hundred square meters (a few thousand square feet) with the buildings and the work yard. Where the bed stood for the sun is at Sunlight and shade on the farm.

  • Where did the bed lie?
    The sources place it in four ways, and none says which was usual. On the Shonai plain of Yamagata, much of a farmhouse's narrow front yard was often planted with fruit trees or made into flower beds or vegetable beds. On a hillside in Ehime, one house had its vegetable field all round it. In a town on the Kanto plain, the front vegetable field was kept near the house: a near field was said to bring pests into the house, but a far one made the harvest hard work. And the Japanese article on the household kitchen garden says the plot was often on spare ground away from the house - a corner of land joined to the house lot, an empty space beside the fields and paddies grown for sale, or a corner of a river's floodplain.
  • Did the bed have an edge of its own?
    Not at the one house a source describes: the hillside house in Ehime had vegetable ground round it and set no special boundary. The same account says that walls closing a house lot off are a recent trend, and that the lot was traditionally a space open to the outside. What ringed a farmstead is at Groves of trees around farmhouses. No page we read describes the bed's surface, its rows or its ridges.
  • What vegetables did an early Edo-period farm household eat, season by season?
    The Seiryoki ("Record of Kiyoyoshi"), a chronicle of a warlord who held his castle at Mima in Kita-Uwa county, thought to have been written between 1629 and 1654 and known as Japan's oldest farm book, lists what to eat in each month. For the first month its list of greens to eat includes daikon, water dropwort, shepherd's purse and burdock; for the sixth, taro stalks, cowpeas, bottle gourds, eggplant and chives; for the tenth, taro, burdock, daikon, turnips and yams.
  • Do the lists say what grew in the bed?
    No. The second month's list is headed for vegetables and the kitchen garden, yet it also holds horsetail shoots and mud snails. Of the book's list of seasonal crops, the prefecture's editors note that many plants now treated only as wild greens were then grown as crops. Vegetables were a crop of the household's dry fields too, and no list says which grew beside the house. That the bed held the daily greens while the bulk crops grew in the fields is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • Later accounts say a household's vegetables were its own growing.
    In Ozu, in Ehime, vegetables came only from the household's own growing: about ten kinds, among them greens, eggplant, squash, daikon, carrots, burdock and green onions, with wild greens besides, dried or salted for the months when vegetables were scarce. In Masaki, daikon was cut and dried for the gap between harvests. In Masaki, too, soybeans planted on the paddy bunds were eaten as homemade tofu or boiled beans. These are accounts, undated save that Masaki's tells of the years before bought fresh fish grew common, from the end of Meiji into Taisho; beyond the soybeans they do not say whether the vegetables grew by the house or in the fields. In the Kanto town, the front vegetable field grew everyday leaf greens, soybeans, sweet potatoes and taro, and held mulberries while silk farming flourished.
  • How big was a whole farm house lot?
    In Ehime, about 3 se, some 300 sq m (~3,203 sq ft), was the usual house lot across the prefecture: about 4 se in the farming villages of the plains, 2 to 3 in the mountains, and 1 to 2 on the islands and in fishing villages. A survey of 1924 in one district of Imabari found an average lot of 128 tsubo, about 423 sq m (~4,554 sq ft); on fifty of its lots, buildings took 45% and the drying yard, which was also the work yard, 20%. A tsubo is about 3.31 sq m (~36 sq ft), and a se is 30 tsubo, about 99 sq m (~1,067 sq ft).
  • How big was a Japanese farm's kitchen bed?
    From a few tsubo up to about 1.4 se, roughly 10 to 140 sq m (~108-1,507 sq ft), a GUESS: no page we read gives the area of a Japanese bed. The 1924 survey bounds it in one district only. What its average lot had left after the buildings and the drying yard, about 148 sq m (~1,593 sq ft), held everything else, and the surveyor wrote that the vegetable ground was small; more than half the households had a vegetable plot or planted trees. That puts the guess's upper end near what one district's lots could hold in the twentieth century; nothing found sets its lower end.
  • No page we read says who worked the bed or what it yielded.
    Nothing found names who in the household tended it, or gives its harvest or the share of a household's vegetables it grew; the later Ehime accounts say only that the vegetables were the household's own growing.

The bed's name and these accounts are Japanese. A farm book from Zhejiang, in China, describes a household garden of its own.

  • A seventeenth-century Chinese farm book tells a farmer to keep a fenced garden, beside the house where the site is broad.
    Zhang Lüxiang, who lived in Tongxiang county and dated his Bu nongshu ("Supplement to the Farm Manual") in 1658, writes that a household farming fields and mulberries must keep a garden as well, fenced, both to feed itself and to keep out thieves. Where the house site is broad, he puts the garden at its side, with trees in front, bamboo behind and fruit trees in the courtyard. In his own district, he says, the garden was opened on ground of its own, apart from the threshing floor, or failing that vegetables were sown under the mulberries, to the mulberries' cost. He faults this as wasted land and idle work, and urges the ancients' way of one ground for both, threshing floor at harvest and garden in spring, which he says was still often seen in the villages of Huzhou. The layouts he sets out are advice on how a farm should be kept; beside them the book reports some practice as it was, among it the common hibiscus hedge, his own district's separate garden and the Huzhou way.
  • The book wants a thorny hedge round the garden.
    The common hedge of hibiscus is easily grown, Zhang writes, but of little use and weak; better to set trifoliate orange in it, with acanthopanax and goji, three thorny plants that keep out intruders, and to plant day lilies all along its foot, their flowers picked and dried as a vegetable.
  • How big was the Chinese garden?
    Zhang gives a rate, not a plot: each mu of garden gives a household of ten its vegetables in all four seasons without going out of the door. He does not give his mu's size. On the standard the Republic of China set in 1915 a mu was 614.4 sq m (~6,613 sq ft), so a household of five on his rate would want about 307 sq m (~3,307 sq ft), more than twice the top of the Japanese guess - a whole vegetable supply rather than a small bed. No page we read measures an actual Chinese farmhouse garden.
