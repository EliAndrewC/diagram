How our maps hang a capital's time bells (toki no kane)

Every walled seat on our maps draws one bell-and-drum tower at its main crossing to sound the hours. A capital, the largest seat, keeps that same single station for its time signal, or in its place a separate drum tower and bell tower, and never draws a second station elsewhere in the city.

  • A capital draws one time signal station, where Edo kept a relay of many bells - accurate.
    A castle town such as Iwatsuki, the seat of a lord's domain, kept a time bell of the domain's own, and the relay of many bells belonged to Edo, the shogun's city, far larger than the seats these maps draw. That comparison is this project's, not one a source states.
  • A time drum inside a capital's castle is not drawn - a convention.
    Edo castle kept a drum for its own time signal, but it stood inside the castle, and the castle is drawn blank.
  • A capital may instead draw a separate drum tower and bell tower, as the Chinese city of Xi'an had - a knob.
    Xi'an kept a drum tower and a bell tower apart, reporting the hours one by drum and one by bell, the bell tower, when first built, standing opposite the drum tower. So a capital draws either the single combined tower every walled seat draws or the pair, chosen at random for each capital from the map's seed.

The rule the map follows: a capital draws exactly one time signal station at its main crossing - either the single combined bell-and-drum tower every walled seat draws (about 36 ft square), or, chosen at random for each capital, a drum tower and a bell tower of that size facing each other along the main street, one on each side of the crossing. It never draws a second station elsewhere in the city.
