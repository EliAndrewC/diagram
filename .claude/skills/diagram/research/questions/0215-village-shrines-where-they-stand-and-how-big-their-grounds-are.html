Village shrines: where they stand, and how big their grounds are

A Japanese village kept a guardian shrine for its own ground, its chinju. It stood within the village or at its edge, in a wood kept around its hall and its approach, and its ground was where the village gathered for its main observances. That ground, its precinct, typically ran to a few hundred tsubo, roughly a tenth to half an acre, and the halls on it were small and covered only a small part of it.

The Chinese earth god's small shrines stood in temples and under house doors, and one stands inside the entrance gate of a Hong Kong walled village. Some village temples were built at the water mouth, the point where a stream enters or leaves the village.

In Japan, the records show which communities kept such a shrine.

  • Nearly every Japanese village had a guardian shrine of its own.
    The clan gods of ancient belief came to be identified with the chinju and with the god of one's birthplace, and a shrine of that kind is found in every village today. The village as its unit is old: once the village system was settled in the Edo period, the idea of a village's own god, with the villagers as its parishioners, spread over nearly the whole country.
  • Did parts of a village keep shrines of their own?
    Some do today, and some communities have none. In Tanba city, in Hyogo, most communities have one shrine each, and some have none at all. One, Higashi-Ashida, of about 180 households, has fourteen: one for the whole community, and the rest each the shrine of one of its sections. Its priest puts the number down to the villagers' refusal to merge their shrines under the order of 1906. These are communities of today, counted by a modern newspaper, and the sections that kept their shrines did so by resisting a merger; that an Edo-period village held such sections' shrines as well is this project's reading, not something the article states.
  • Did a branch hamlet have a shrine of its own?
    No page we read says. Shrines of hamlets within a village are on record (one Saga district's, below), but the entries we read on the edago, the branch hamlet, give its founding and its taxes and say nothing of a shrine, and no page read says how many hamlets had one.

The size of a shrine's precinct is given shrine by shrine in the registers the government kept of them after 1868.

  • How large was a village shrine's precinct?
    Typically about 150 to 650 tsubo, some 5,000 to 23,000 sq ft. That is this project's reading of the medians and ranges of three districts' registers, in the bullets below.
  • In one Saitama district, fourteen village shrines had 55 to 708 tsubo.
    The village-rank shrines, the sonsha, of Hiki district run from 55 tsubo to 708 tsubo, with a median, by our arithmetic, of 403 tsubo, about 14,300 sq ft, the area of a square some 120 ft on a side. Left out are the district shrine's 3,373 tsubo and Sugaya Shrine's 2,499, swollen by a state reservoir's land absorbed in 1908.
  • In one Tochigi district, fifty village shrines had 142 to over 2,700 tsubo.
    Their median, by our arithmetic on the fifty entries of the Sano district register that give an area, is 655 tsubo, about 23,300 sq ft.
  • In one Saga district, nine hamlet shrines had 50 to 662 tsubo.
    Nine shrines one step below the village shrines, unranked ones, the mukakusha, and a few "miscellaneous" ones of Kanzaki district, from the registers of about 1880 and 1915, have a median, by our arithmetic, of 148 tsubo, about 5,300 sq ft.
  • Some village shrines held over 2,000 tsubo.
    The guardian shrine of Ishigami village, in Saitama, had a little over 2,000 tsubo with its grove at the end of the Meiji era.
  • Most shrines in Nagoya today have grounds of under about 760 tsubo.
    Of 92 Nagoya shrines that answered a survey, 21 have under 1,000 m² (~10,764 sq ft), about 300 tsubo, 37 more under 2,500 m² (~26,910 sq ft), about 760 tsubo, and the other 34 larger, 8 of them over 10,000 m² (~107,639 sq ft), about 3,000 tsubo.
  • A register can understate an Edo precinct, because the state took shrine lands in the 1870s.
    The lands that temples and shrines had held in the Edo period were confiscated by the orders of 1871 and 1875. In Kanzaki, the land-tax reform of 1876 split a shrine's ground into precinct and outside land and put the outside land into state hands. A return the prefect of Nagasaki signed in 1877 (Kanzaki then lay in Nagasaki prefecture) keeps 1 tan 13 bu (about 313 tsubo) of one shrine's former 7 tan 5 se 27 bu (about 2,277) as its present precinct.
  • A register can also overstate an Edo precinct.
    Some precincts were later enlarged with land the state had taken: a Hiki shrine was permitted in 1903 to add 1 tan 5 se 1 bu (about 451 tsubo) of surrendered forest. Others grew by mergers: Sano's Wakukama Shrine grew fivefold in 1911 when another shrine's ground was joined to it.
  • Were shrine precincts of 150 to 650 tsubo found before 1868?
    Yes, as far as two kinds of Edo record show. The Saga domain's figures for hamlet shrines cover the lower part of the 150 to 650 tsubo band, and the exempt lands of two villages' shrines near Edo its upper part and beyond, as the next three bullets set out.
  • The Saga domain's Edo-period records, 1663 to 1859, give hamlet shrines' grounds of about 45 to 540 tsubo.
    For several of the same Kanzaki hamlet shrines, the domain's temple-and-shrine returns give each site in se and bu, from 1 se 15 bu to 1 tan 8 se, four of the six entries quoted under 1 tan (300 tsubo), a se being 30 tsubo. The largest is an exempt land rather than a site.
  • Near Edo, village returns gave their shrines' exempt land, from about 10 tsubo to over 3,000.
    A village's return, the report its headman made to its lord on the village's land and holdings, listed its shrines, most with their exempt land, the jochi, which the lord took no tax on. Kami-Nerima's return of 1821 gives its guardian shrine, a Hachiman shrine, 3 tan 8 se 15 bu, about 1,155 tsubo. It gives its lesser shrines "about 10 tsubo" or "about 20 tsubo", outside the tax survey, and one Inari shrine 1 chō 1 tan 1 se 18 bu, about 3,350. Doshida's returns of 1804 and 1831 give its shrines 7 se 6 bu to 6 tan 6 se 20 bu, about 216 to 2,000 tsubo, beside halls as small as 4 shaku square.
  • An exempt-land figure is a ceiling on a precinct, not its measure.
    Exempt land was a shrine's or a temple's precinct, but also fields and house plots held under a document of exemption. A shrine's could be far larger than any precinct: Doshida's return of 1814 lists 5 chō 6 tan 6 se 20 bu, some 17,000 tsubo, as land exempted for a kami's shrine; that it was fields is this project's reading. The Saga figures, sites but for the largest, are the nearer measure of a precinct.
  • How big were a village shrine's halls?
    Small, at the shrines we read. Hie Shrine in Kurihara, ranked a village shrine in 1874, keeps a worship hall of 3 bays by 2, thought to be late Edo, and a sanctuary of 2 bays by 1, early Edo at the latest. At a bay of 6 shaku that is about 18 by 12 ft and 12 by 6 ft - this project's conversion, since the page gives bays only and real bays varied.
  • How much of a shrine's precinct was built on?
    Between about a fortieth and a seventh. Rokusha Shrine in Hiroshima, a village shrine from 1872, lists a worship hall of 9 tsubo, an office of 30 and a sanctuary of about 3.4 on a precinct of 285 tsubo, about 15 per cent built. The Tochigi register's Wakukama Shrine has a worship hall 5 ken by 2, a sanctuary under 1 tsubo and an offering hall of 1 ken by 1 and a half, on 457 tsubo, under 3 per cent.
  • Was the rest of the precinct the shrine's wood?
    Mostly, as a GUESS: no source measures it. The registers single out old, dense trees - one unranked shrine's 1,848 tsubo are praised as unmatched in the region for their "luxuriant, dense trees" - and a shrine's wood, the chinju no mori, is by definition kept around its sanctuary, its approach and its place of worship. No page we read splits a precinct into buildings, wood and open ground.

In Chinese villages, the earth god's shrines and other village temples are recorded at a village's entrance and, for some deities, at its water mouth.

  • In one Hong Kong walled village, the earth god's shrine stands inside the entrance gate.
    That is one instance, not a rule: the same page puts the god's other shrines inside temples and houses, beneath the main altar or below the door.
  • In the fengshui of village groves, a village's entrance is its head and its exit its mouth.
    Both ends are filed as the water gate, the shuikou, which can mean the water at either end.
  • One account of fengshui woodland files the earth god's shrine grove with the temple groves.
    It divides fengshui woodland into funeral, village and temple groves, and lists the earth god's shrine grove under the temple groves, not the village ones.
  • The temples of one Hakka deity were usually built at the village's water mouth.
    The temples of Minzhu Gongwang are usually built at the village's shuikou, and a named one, built in 1760, stands on a stream bank in what is now New Taipei.
  • In some of Zhejiang's old villages, temples stood at the water mouth or at the village's entrance.
    A mountain-god temple stands at one village's water mouth, a temple to the deified general Guan Yu at another's head, its entrance end, and a local temple at a third's entrance. A survey of those entrances reads their siting as borrowing the idea of an "auspicious site" from the worship of nature spirits, and its table puts the earth god's own temple at one village's entrance.
  • Did the earth god's shrine stand at the water mouth as a rule?
    No source we read says so. The water-mouth siting on record is of a Hakka deity, not of the earth god.
  • One Zhejiang village temple's compound covered about 2.5 mu, some 500 tsubo.
    Gantang Temple, the village temple of Shui in northern Zhejiang, stood on about 2.5 mu, by the town gazetteer.
