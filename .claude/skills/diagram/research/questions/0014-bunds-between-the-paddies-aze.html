Bunds between the paddies (aze)

A bund, aze, is the small embankment of earth heaped up between one rice paddy and the next. It holds each paddy's water, and it serves as a boundary and as a path; where the owners on its two sides differ, it is often the bund that divides their land. Every year before the paddy was flooded it was re-plastered with mud, so that it held the water.

Before modern land consolidation, a land survey for tax reckoned a dividing bund at one shaku, about a foot, wide, a field replanned in 1869, just after the Edo period, laid it at two, and the one old height found is a few inches to a foot; the bunds farmers walked along ran wider. Such a bund had no set shape or size, and the old paddies it enclosed were irregular plots of every size. Modern land consolidation remade those plots into uniform ones, and today's standard bund stands about a foot high.

  • An excavated early Yayoi paddy had small bunds under 2 ft wide and large ones 2.5 to 5 ft.
    At the Hattori site the small bunds, 20-60 cm (~8-24 in) wide, were mounds of the tilled soil made to hold standing water, not to be walked on; the large bunds, 80-150 cm (~2.5-5 ft) wide with flat tops, divided the paddies into large blocks and seem also to have served as paths for farm work. The site long predates the Edo period.
  • In the small paddies of the Yayoi and Kofun periods, the large bunds were both path and dike.
    Many small bunds, the smallest plots about 2-3 m (~7-10 ft) on a side, stood in rows inside large bunds that served as both azemichi and dike, and they were built throughout the country.
  • The Edo land survey reckoned a bund at one shaku.
    A survey for tax took the bund to be one shaku wide, about 30 cm (~1 ft), and took a further shaku of field off each side of it; a dictionary's earliest example of the word is an order of the shogunate of 1726. A present-day column reports the same allowance, hedged and without naming a source. It is a reckoning for tax, not a measured bund.
  • A paddy replanned just after the Edo period, in 1869, had bunds two shaku wide.
    Nishikimi Chodayu replanned some 43 cho at Inazato, in Hozumi village, Gifu, from the autumn of 1869 until the rice was planted the next year: each paddy 10 by 30 ken, the farm roads and the ditches 1 ken wide, and the bunds 2 shaku. It was a replanned layout, not a measurement of an old field.
  • How high did a bund stand before modern times?
    A few inches to a foot, on the one site that gives a figure: Hattori's small bunds stood 5-20 cm (~2-8 in) high and its large bunds 15-30 cm (~6-12 in). No height for a bund of the Edo period was found, in a farm manual, a survey or a dig.
  • Today's standard bund is about a foot high and a foot across its top.
    A modern design standard makes it a trapezoid 30 cm (~1 ft) high and 30 cm (~1 ft) across its top, on slopes of one to one; in cold regions it is enlarged to about 50 cm (~20 in) across and 40 cm (~16 in) high. A prefecture's standard sets its height at 30 cm (~1 ft) above the higher field, and a bund newly built under land consolidation has a crest of 300 to 600 mm (~12-24 in). So it is about a foot high and about 3 ft across its base. These are figures for modern, consolidated paddy.
  • How wide was a bund built to be walked on?
    Wider than a dividing bund, but no page we read says by how much. What is called an azemichi, the path of farm work, is wide, a bund doubling as a private farm road, but no page gives its width. The one wider way read is the 1869 replanned field's farm road of one ken, about 6 ft.
  • Among the paddies, the way to the fields ran on along the bunds.
    A farm road is the way made for farm work between the farmhouses and the fields. A bund that is also the way round the paddies is called an azemichi or a nawate. Farmers walk the bunds to weed and to fertilize, and rest on them, and some bund paths were once village roads. How a lane runs across the dry fields is at Village lanes.
  • The path between paddies has had its own word, nawate, for more than a thousand years.
    It is in a Japanese dictionary of about 934, the Wamyosho, as a road between paddies.
  • Was a gap left in a bund for walkers?
    No page we read says so, nor that the way to the fields stopped at the field's edge; the pages we read put the walking on the bunds themselves.
  • Every year before the water went in, the bund was re-plastered by hand (azenuri).
    The weeds were stripped from the bund's face and kneaded mud plastered on, so that less water seeped through it, and a modern engineering page still calls for it every year before the paddy is flooded. An Edo-period farm calendar from Echizen, now northern Fukui, has last year's bunds shaved from early May and the plastering finished by late May, before transplanting in early June. It was hand work with the hoe, and the hoes differed from province to province.
  • In haiku, bund-plastering is a season word of late spring.
    It means firming the bunds with plaster before rice planting, so that water and fertilizer do not seep away, and one verse filed under it sets a farmer rinsing a straw raincoat in the running stream.
  • Did two neighboring paddies share one bund?
    On this project's reading, yes: the bund is the one wall between two paddies, and it is built once. A second ridge beside the first, with a strip of ground between, would double the yearly re-plastering - on this reading the largest upkeep the bunds carry, a GUESS - for no gain; it would hold no water the first does not; and it would leave idle a strip of watered rice land, worth more to a household than any dry field. No source states the shared bund itself; it follows from how the bund was built and kept. So a stretch of paddy was one connected network of bunds, its lines meeting at T-junctions, not a set of separately walled plots.
  • Outside modern works, a bund had no set shape or size.
    Its dimensions varied by region and soil. Most paddies were opened in the age of the domains, and few were laid out as neatly as the ancient jori grid: most were irregular plots of every size. Modern land consolidation remade the narrow, irregular plots handed down from the early modern period into uniform ones, and after the Second World War fields shaped to their slope were squared into large rectangles, for machines.
  • On the plains of western Japan, the old jori grid survived.
    There regular plots laid out up to the Meiji period, the jori division among them, were widespread, each square unit of the grid divided into ten equal parts, so on a plain whose fields descend from it the bunds run in straight, evenly spaced lines.

Why a hand-piled bund wavers is written down nowhere we looked. What follows is this project's reading of how piled earth behaves, each point a GUESS.

  • Did a hand-piled bund's corners wear round?
    On this reading, yes: a right angle in soft earth is the thinnest, least supported point of the ridge: it slumps under its own weight and under every rain, and it is the one spot every foot, hoof and carrying pole cuts across. The spring re-plastering restores the ridge, not its geometry, so a corner wears to a walked-and-slumped curve a few feet across - the corner on the walked side to a broad sweep, while one behind a neighbor's bund keeps its angle for years. No page we read says why a mud corner rounds, or how wide the curve is.
  • Why did a hand-piled bund's line wander?
    A run was paced and eyeballed between two corners, and re-cut a little differently whenever a holding was split, sold or re-plastered: the bund was repaired every year before the water was let in, and it often marked the boundary between two owners. So a straight hundred-foot bund bows by a foot or two: straight in intent, and to the eye at a distance, but never to the ruler. Neighboring bunds, laid by eye by different households in different decades, were not parallel either: they drifted, converged and opened out again, so a strip between two was wider at one end than the other.
  • On this project's reading, a bund junction was the most worked point in a field.
    Four paddies push water at it, it carries the crossing foot traffic, it is where someone stands to open and close the water, and it is the first place to slump and be re-piled. No page we read speaks of the junctions at all.
  • Did a bund step sideways and carry on?
    No source speaks to it, and on this reading it did not. A bund could bend and a paddy be a trapezoid, since a bund had no set shape, and, on the shared-bund reading above, a bund could end at a T-junction against another. But a bund's cost is its length, re-plastered along its whole run every year before the water goes in, so a sideways step pays for the extra length between two corners for nothing. It also adds two corners, where a puddled ridge slumps and is re-plastered hardest. And the ground a sideways step trades is worth nothing to either owner: it lies at the same level, floods from the same offtake and is reached from either paddy, so no farmer pays a wall's upkeep to move a boundary a few feet along its own line.
  • Where a real boundary did step, on this project's reading, something stood at the corner.
    A ditch, a path, a rock or an inherited holding - a GUESS. The only stepped line on any page we read is a whole jori grid offset at a district border.

By high summer, the bund was no longer bare mud.

  • A paddy's bunds carried grass and beans through the growing season.
    The grass on the bund was cut from of old for manure or fodder, and beans were often grown on it. It grows hard: farmers today keep the grassland on a paddy levee down by mowing, two to five times a year in the paddies one study followed. By midsummer a grassed and planted bund all but vanishes into the green of the rice - this project's reading, a GUESS; no page we read says so.
  • The beans on the bunds were soybeans, azemame.
    Soybeans called azemame, "bund beans", were sown on the bund after transplanting and harvested at the same time as the rice. A photograph on the Asuka Historical Museum's page shows them planted in a row along a bund. Whether they fed the paddy is at Rice paddies and their plots (suiden).
  • Bund beans, once grown across Japan, mostly disappeared with modern farming.
    Land consolidation, herbicide and damage by animals took most of them; they are still grown in Asuka village.
  • A soybean plant is a knee-to-waist-high bush with medium-green leaves.
    Mature plants generally stand 50 to 125 cm (~2-4 ft) tall. The plant is erect and bushy, branching from a central stem, with broad, pointed, medium-green leaflets; the leaf color is from a trade site, the encyclopedia giving none. No page we read gives how far one plant spreads on the bund: the encyclopedia gives its height, and the trade site its height and habit but no width.
