Teahouses, eating houses and drinking houses (chaya)

A teahouse (chaya) began as a tea seller's stand at a temple gate, where travelers and worshippers stopped. By the Edo period, before 1868, public eating and drinking went on in a run of such houses - the tea stall, the cooked-food shop, the drinking house and the restaurant - and the city of Edo alone is said to have had well over a thousand drinking houses in the early nineteenth century.

They gathered where people passed: at the gates and in the precincts of temples and shrines, in the busy quarters of the cities, along the highways and at the ends of the post towns, and on the bridges and the canals. China had the same run of houses, and its great cities were thick with them.

  • The earliest recorded teahouses were tea sellers at temple gates, in the fifteenth century.
    Tea sellers gathered before the gates for those traveling the highways and those visiting temples and shrines, and the first record of them is the tea seller at the south gate of the temple Toji in 1403.
  • In the Edo period the tea stall served passers-by tea and a place to rest.
    It stood at the roadside and in the precincts of temples and shrines.
  • Most tea stalls stood apart from their keeper's house.
    A few were run from the keeper's own house, but most were detached stalls, with the dwelling somewhere else.
  • Some tea stalls were temporary, open only at blossom time.
    There were permanent stalls, and temporary ones such as those open only at blossom time.
  • The cooked-food shop sold simple meals with tea or sake.
    That is how a Japanese encyclopedia defines it.
  • Simple eating houses stood in the cities and along the highways.
    The Edo period's cooked-food teahouses and rice houses, which served simmered food and other simple dishes with rice, are held to be the origin of the modern popular eating house.
  • When did the drinking house appear?
    Probably by the middle of the Edo period, branching off from the highway tea stalls, which already sold sake and food in the early Edo period, and from the cooked-food shops of the cities. That is a dictionary's reckoning, not a dated record.
  • The drinking house (izakaya) took its name from sake drunk at a sake shop's storefront.
    Such sake was formerly called izake, as a Japanese dictionary explains the name.
  • How many drinking houses did Edo have late in the Edo period?
    More than 1,800 around the Bunsei era, 1818 to 1830, is the figure the Japanese Wikipedia article on drinking houses gives, and it gives it as hearsay.
  • No page we read counts the eating and drinking houses of a county town.
    None of the dictionaries, encyclopedias or Song records we read gives a count for a town of about 1,000 to 3,000 inhabitants.
  • Drinking houses reached the farming villages only in the later eighteenth century.
    The countryside was the last place they spread to.

In Japan the same few places recur.

  • Teahouses gathered at the gates and in the precincts of temples and shrines.
    In Edo from the 1750s tea stalls sprang up one after another before the gates and in the precincts of temples and shrines, as well as in the busy quarters. Kyoto's well-known restaurants included the two teahouses before the gate of the Gion shrine.
  • Travelers' teahouses moved from the hills between post towns to the post town's ends.
    They first opened in the hills and other places away from the post towns, as rest stops on the way, and gradually moved in as far as the edge of the post town.
  • Tea stalls moved into the gate towns and the busy quarters of the cities.
    They were of the same kind as the travelers' tea stalls: a set of tea things behind reed screens, and cheap tea at one copper coin, a sen, a cup.
  • Cooked-food shops multiplied in Edo after the Meireki fire of 1657.
    They fed the laborers who came in from the provinces to rebuild.
  • An essay of 1810 found Edo's Ryogoku quarter crowded with cooked-food shops past counting.
    The essay miscellany found them beyond counting in Ryogoku alone.
  • In Osaka drinking houses are said to have set up even on the bridges.
    By the later Edo period they seem to have been everywhere.
  • Kyoto's river-fish restaurants stood in numbers along the Takase canal.
    They stood around Sanjo and Shijo, two of Kyoto's main east-west streets.

In China the wine shop led the run.

  • The Chinese wine shop spread with the towns and market towns of the Song dynasty.
    The wine shops of Kaifeng and Hangzhou were famous.
  • Under the Yuan, Ming and Qing dynasties the wine shop stood in every part of the country.
    Its business had settled into its form under the Southern Song, and it became a place of rest for the common people.
  • A Chinese wine shop was marked by a wine flag, generally blue.
    The wine flag showed where a shop was.
  • Kaifeng's wine shops dressed their doors with gateways of colored silk.
    Every wine shop of the capital bound a decorated tower and a welcoming gate of colored silk at its door.
  • In Kaifeng, the Northern Song capital, every lane off Maxing Street had its teahouses and wine shops.
    The lanes and courtyards were crowded door to door.
  • Kaifeng's trading households in the Song dynasty bought their meals at the market shops.
    They bought their food at the market shops as they needed it, and kept no vegetables at home.
  • A wine shop stood among the houses on Kaifeng's imperial avenue in the Song dynasty.
    Just past the Zhou bridge, where both sides of the avenue were residents' houses, stood the Zhang family's wine shop.
