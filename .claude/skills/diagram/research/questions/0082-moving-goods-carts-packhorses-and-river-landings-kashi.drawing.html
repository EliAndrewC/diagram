How our maps draw moving goods: cart lanes and boat landings (kashi)

Freight on our maps goes by water where there is water to carry it, as Edo Japan's tax rice went by river, and by cart where there is not, since this setting puts carts on its roads. A settlement on a navigable river or the coast has a boat landing; a compound that ships or receives bulk goods has a cart gate, a cart yard and a cart lane; and no transport canal is drawn unless the map is set where the setting has one.

  • A settlement on navigable water has a boat landing or jetty as its main freight link.
    The river or the coast is its main way to the world, as Edo Japan's tax rice went by river landing. A settlement with no navigable water beside it moves its freight by its land road, and that road is its main link.
  • Our maps draw no transport canal just because a settlement moves goods.
    In the setting a canal is a domain's work, dug only where an inland river is worth canalizing, as the Lion Clan's canal from Toshi Ranbo to the Drowned Merchant River is. The setting's example is the Lion Clan's, so this project draws a canal by default only in Lion Clan lands, and on any other map only where it is asked to carry one.
  • A compound that ships or receives bulk goods has a cart gate, a cart yard and a cart lane.
    Rice bales and charcoal come and go by cart, and the road outside the gate carries carts. This is a deviation: Edo Japan kept its carts to the towns (Village lanes), and the setting puts wagons and carts on its roads, a charcoal merchant's cartload among them.
  • How wide is a cart lane?
    About 6 ft, a GUESS. The Edo hand cart's bed was 8 shaku long and 2 shaku 5 sun wide, about 8 by 2.5 ft, but how much wider its wheels ran is on no page we read. Six feet is our allowance over that bed: room for the wheels outside it, with some to spare. How wide the road is at a compound's gate is at How our maps draw and label the highway.
  • An Imperial road is drawn as a kept road, wider than a village track.
    The setting's Imperial roads run some 50,000 miles, with about 5,000 staffed waystations and no tolls, tolls having been outlawed by an Emperor, and they are repaired by corvee labor from the counties along them. So where a map shows one, it is drawn as a maintained road of earth or gravel, wider than a village track. Its width and surface are a map drawing convention.
  • A settlement's own lanes, and the road joining it to the Imperial roads, stay humble tracks.
    The setting's Imperial network is sparse, within a domain a through-route and the odd spur, so most settlements stand off it, and this project draws their own lanes as the village lanes the history records (Village lanes).

A water town, where a map takes that form, strings its houses along a canal so that they front the water.

  • A water town is drawn only in the Lion Clan's lands or on a map that carries a canal.
    The setting's canals are a domain's work, the Lion Clan's its example, so the form follows the canal. The generator can draw it, but no settlement in the pool uses it yet.
  • The houses stand on both banks, alternating from one bank to the other, evenly spaced along the canal, each beside its fields.
    Around Lake Tai the houses stood along the streams because the boat was how the villagers traveled (The dike-pond hamlet), and in the Lower Yangzi's water towns nearly every riverside house had a small landing at its door (Washing places at the water's edge). No page we read gives a water town's plan - which bank its houses took, or how far apart they stood - so the alternation, the even spacing and the seat beside the fields are a GUESS.
