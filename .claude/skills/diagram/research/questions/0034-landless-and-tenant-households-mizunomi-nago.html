Landless and tenant households (mizunomi, nago)

In a Japanese village before 1868, in the Edo period, the farmers who held land on the village's tax register were its full members. Below them stood the mizunomi, households with no land of their own, who had no voice in running the village and made their living however they could. Some, the nago, rented even the house they lived in.

Many such households had no house plot of their own. They lived in a hut or a gate-hut inside a richer farmer's enclosure, under that household's protection and control; in one village in Tosa, on the island of Shikoku, only a third of the registered cultivators held a house plot. No page we read says where a landless household's own house stood; the one rule we found, for new branch households in the northern Nanbu domain, kept them off the cultivated land and sent them to vacant plots, the edge of the fields or the roads at the foot of the hills.

  • The landless were not full villagers.
    An encyclopedia entry on the mizunomi sets them against the landholding farmers: they owned no fields, were not counted as full farmers, were not allowed a part in village government, and lived as tenants, or by migrant service and day labor, or by a craft, the poor of the farming village.
  • A nago rented the house plot and the house as well as the fields.
    Where the mizunomi was a tenant who rented fields, the nago is distinguished by renting the house and its plot too.
  • Dependents lived in huts inside a master's enclosure.
    The dependent farmers called kadoya took the name from the gate-hut on their master's plot where they lived. Many of the house plots registered in the land surveys held, inside the plot's enclosure and beside the main house, huts, gate-huts and retirement houses, where weak dependent farmers lived while the master's family lived in the main house.
  • Sukumo's town history says most cultivators without a house plot lived on a powerful farmer's plot.
    The land surveys of Sukumo, in Tosa on the island of Shikoku, register many cultivators with no house plot. Most of them, by the town history's account, were kin, retainers or nago of a powerful farmer, living in a separate building or a hut on the head household's plot.
  • In one Sukumo village, only 120 of 360 registered cultivators held a house plot.
    In the village of Yamana, the town history counts 120 holders of a house plot against 360 registered cultivators.
  • The Nanbu domain gave a nago no house plot as a rule.
    By its land survey of 1737 the Nanbu domain, in the north, gave a nago no house plot as a rule.
  • In the Nanbu domain, a new branch household had to build off the cultivated land.
    Branch households were not landless households, but the rule shows where a domain let a new house go. Nanbu's order of 1715 forbade a branch household to take its house plot out of the cultivated land, and allowed it on a vacant plot, at the edge of the fields, or along the roads at the foot of the hills.
  • In Shiwa district, a branch household was given at least a house, a stable and a privy.
    By the custom of Shiwa district, in the Nanbu domain, the least a branch household was given was three buildings - a main house, a stable and a privy - as one hearth, that is, one household.
  • No page we read places a landless household's own house in the village.
    The encyclopedia entries and local histories above place dependents inside a master's enclosure and branch households at the field edge, but none says where a landless household with a house of its own stood.
  • How small were the houses of landless and tenant households?
    An eighteenth-century count of house sizes near Utsunomiya, north of Edo, found many houses under 10 tsubo (~356 sq ft) and some of only 4 or 5 (~142-178 sq ft); it is at How big was an ordinary farmhouse, and how much bigger was a well-off one? No page we read ties those small houses to landless or tenant households by name.
