Market days and the market ground (ichi)

In the setting, the county town holds a market day about once a week, when farmers from all over the county come in to buy, sell and barter, since the town is the smallest kind of settlement that has merchants. The Japanese periodic markets it is drawn from kept a calendar of days in the month instead of a week: three market days a month, and later six, every fifth day. A market in Qing China drew a farmer from a walk of an hour or two.

On the day, the market met along a town's main street, on a strip kept clear between the street and the house lots, or before the gate of a temple or shrine. Who stays in town the night before is at Travelers' inns and cheap lodging houses (hatago, dian, kichin-yado); the standing shops outside a walled town's gate, which serve the road's traffic every day, are a different thing, at Gate markets outside a town (guanxiang).

  • Japan's periodic markets met three times a month, and later six.
    The older periodic market, the three-day market, met three times a month; with the growth of trade, markets came to be held six times a month, every five days - the rokusai-ichi - on pairs of days such as the 1st and 6th or the 2nd and 7th, two days in each ten.
  • Is the setting's weekly market a historical schedule?
    Not in its count: about four market days a month falls between the three-day and the six-day market, so the weekly market is a deliberate deviation in its count and not in its kind - a market held by a town that is the only one its villages have.
  • The six-day market lasted in Tohoku and Kanto, while around Kyoto and Osaka it grew into country towns.
    In the Edo period the six-day market stayed the center of a region's trade in Tohoku and Kanto; in the Kinai, the region around Kyoto and Osaka, many such markets grew into country towns and the name died out.
  • Neighboring Japanese markets set their days so as not to compete.
    In each region the market days were adjusted, for instance by setting them against the days of the region's central market.

China's periodic markets kept a schedule of their own, and a study has measured how far their customers walked.

  • A Chinese periodic market opened every few days, for the villages around it.
    It opened one day in every two or several, or three times in ten days, and its buyers and sellers were chiefly the villagers nearby.
  • How far did a Chinese market's customers walk?
    In the mid-Qing, a 2000 study of the market network finds, China's markets stood one or two to every 100 square kilometers (~24,711 acres). A market drew on 40 to 60 square kilometers (~9,884-14,826 acres) on the plains and over 100 (~24,711 acres) in the mountains. Its customers walked 3 to 5 km (~9,843-16,404 ft) on the plains and 5 to 7 km (~16,404-22,966 ft) in the mountains, so a small farmer's trip to market took one or two hours, there and back in half a day. That is the farmer's walk to the kind of market the setting's county town holds.
  • Are the mid-Qing market figures measured walks, or averages?
    They read as averages, not paced roads: each area looks like a province's land, hill and water included, divided by its count of markets, with the walk worked out from it - a GUESS.
  • A twentieth-century model of rural China puts the walk to market inside the mid-Qing range.
    In the anthropologist G. William Skinner's typical case, a market town's area was just over 50 square kilometers (~12,355 acres), market towns stood less than 8 km (~26,247 ft) apart, and the longest walk in was about 4.5 km (~14,764 ft), for some 1,500 households in eighteen or so villages.
  • How far did a Japanese market town's customers walk?
    No page we read says. The nearest figure is a spacing: in Settsu and Kawachi near Osaka, a country of cotton and other commercial crops, where the Kinai's country towns grew up in the late seventeenth century, a town had formed every few kilometers. That is of the same order as the Chinese reach, though it is a dense case, not a typical one.

Where the market met took two forms in Japan: along the main street of a market town, and before a temple's or shrine's gate - and China's temple fair met at a temple too.

  • In the market towns of Edo-period Kanto, the market met in a strip along the main street.
    The market ground was a strip one to two ken deep (about 6 to 12 ft), set between the town street and the house lots and kept for putting up stalls on market day.
  • At a Kanto market town, the lot owner ran the stalls inside the house lots, and the market's head the middle stalls out in the street.
    The stalls inside the lots, which took in the market strip, were under the lot's owner; the middle stalls out in the street, which used it as the selling floor, were under the market's head.
  • What were the stalls built of, and did anything of them stand between market days?
    No page we read says.
  • Where did the market god's stone stand?
    Most Japanese market sites were on old highways, and the market god, the ichigami, was worshipped at the roadside, sometimes at the boundary of the market or of its ward, or between the market settlement and its neighbors. It often stands at a place that marks an edge - a village boundary, a landing, a bridge foot, a crossroads.
  • Historians read a market god's stone as a sign that a market once met there.
    The god's body was most often a round natural stone, and a market is inferred from where one stands.
  • In medieval Japan, the market met mostly before temple and shrine gates.
    The medieval three-day market was held mainly before the gates of temples and shrines, at hubs of land and water transport, and near an estate's office or its steward's residence.
  • In China, the temple fair met at a temple and took in trade.
    The temple fair, the miaohui, gathered near a temple for offerings to the gods, contests and performances, first on its religious festivals and later on fixed dates, and gradually took in market trade.

Earlier Chinese law kept the market behind a wall of its own, and the record finds that wall only in the capitals.

  • The Han dynasty's capital had walled and gated market squares, with a tower for their officials.
    Each was walled and gated on four sides, with a five-story tower at its center from which the market's officials looked down over it, and storehouses behind the shops against the market wall.
  • Under the Tang, a market could be set up only at a prefecture or county seat.
    A 2009 essay on the Tang market quotes the rule.
  • A Tang market kept set hours, opened and closed by drum and gong.
    It opened at midday to three hundred drumbeats and dispersed before sunset to three hundred strokes of the gong, by the rules the same essay quotes.
  • Even a Tang county's market was laid out in rows by goods.
    Not only the great cities: a county's market too was divided into rows - the rice row, the silk row, the iron row.
  • The Tang capital's two markets each measured over 3,000 ft on a side.
    Each took the ground of two wards, about 1,025 m (~3,363 ft) north to south and 927 m (~3,041 ft) east to west.
  • Was a county seat's market walled?
    No page we read says whether a county seat's market had a wall of its own, or how big it was.
  • The Song dynasty ended the walled market.
    The Song abolished the Tang's ward-and-market system and its night curfew, and commercial streets, entertainment quarters and night markets took its place.

The market day brought a crowd, and a festival brought another.

  • The setting's acting troupes make most of their money on market day.
    The rest of the week they frequently travel through the county's village districts to perform. The stage they play on is at Theater stages on shrine ground (miyaji shibai)
  • A Japanese shrine's procession rests at an otabisho.
    A shrine's festival generally carries the god out in a portable shrine, which rests or stays the night on its way at, or sets out to reach, a place called the otabisho. That may have a shrine building of its own, a temporary altar, or be the house of the representative of the shrine's ujiko.
  • The market's own festival was the market god's, at the first market of the New Year.
    A Japanese encyclopedia says it was customarily held then.
  • Did a town keep a square or festival ground open for the crowd?
    No page we read describes one, Japanese or Chinese. The pages place the festival at the shrine, the temple and the route of the god's procession, and the temple fair near its temple.

On a market day a provincial city draws the same crowd on a larger scale, and its gates meet the caravans of the road besides; the caravans, and what a post station kept standing for the road's traffic, are at Relay stations: the Imperial waystation, the relay office and their stables (umaya, toiyaba).
