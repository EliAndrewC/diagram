Verandas and storm shutters (engawa and amado)

The veranda, the engawa, ran along the outside of a Japanese house's rooms. In a shoin-style house it came in two layers, an inner veranda inside the building line and an outer one on its edge, usually a step lower. The inner one was a passage along the rooms, and on the garden side a wide veranda served as a secondary space of the main room. In the widths we found it ran half a ken to one ken, about 3 to 6 ft, and it wrapped all four faces of the great daimyo mansions of the early seventeenth century, but only two or three faces of the houses that took it up late in the period.

Outside a house's openings stood its storm shutters, the amado: board doors sliding on a one-groove sill, kept against wind, rain and the night, and stowed by day in a box at the end of their run, the tobukuro.

  • There were two kinds of veranda, one inside the building line and one on its outer edge.
    The one inside the building line was the irikawa, in recent years itself called the engawa; the one on the outer edge was the en or nure-en, its floor usually a step below the floor within.
  • In a shoin-style house, a wide veranda stood between the main room and the garden.
    It served as a secondary space of the main room.
  • In the great daimyo mansions of the early seventeenth century, verandas ringed the rooms on all four sides.
    The mansions built in every region in the years 1596-1624 had their main halls in the developed shoin style, their rooms accompanied on all four sides by wide and inner verandas.
  • Farmhouses took up the veranda from the middle of the seventeenth century.
    Varying by region, they set a veranda first outside the zashiki, from about the middle of the seventeenth century, and then along the front of their main rooms from the middle of the eighteenth, with white paper screens lined up behind it.
  • Late in the Edo period, some houses ran a veranda along two or three sides.
    Farmhouses, townhouses and the houses of middle and lower-ranking samurai had almost no corridors. At the end of the period houses appear with a continuous veranda along two or three sides, used as the way to the privy and the bath.
  • The dictionary width of the inner veranda is one ken, about 6 ft.
    A Japanese dictionary defines the irikawa as a passage one ken wide between the zashiki and the veranda.
  • How wide was a veranda, face by face?
    Half a ken to one ken, and it could change from one face of a house to the next. At Katsura the Ko-shoin has a veranda one ken wide on the east of its main rooms that turns the corner and narrows to half a ken on the south, and the Naka-shoin carries a matted inner veranda half a ken wide round its east and south faces.
  • How wide was the outer veranda?
    No page we read gives a general width for the nure-en. The one outer-veranda width we found is Katsura's own: the Ko-shoin's wide veranda above, one ken on the east narrowing to half a ken on the south.

The storm shutters closed the house along those same edges.

  • The storm shutter was devised in the late sixteenth century.
    It dates from the Momoyama period, and its use at Toyotomi Hideyoshi's Jurakudai palace is an early example.
  • The shutter box came in the seventeenth century.
    In the sixteenth century the shutters were board doors sliding past each other between the posts at the veranda's edge; in the seventeenth the form with a tobukuro, running in a one-groove sill and lintel, was made.
  • The shutters kept out wind and rain, guarded the house at night and kept it warm.
    Those are the purposes a Japanese encyclopedia gives them.
  • By day the shutters were stowed in their box.
    Two encyclopedia entries say so: one that the shutters are stored in the tobukuro in the daytime, the other that they are usually kept there when not in use.
  • Did a household keep the shutters shut on rooms it was not using?
    The pages we read say when the shutters were closed, at night and against wind and rain, and that they were stowed by day; none says a household kept those of unused rooms shut.
