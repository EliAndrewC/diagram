How our maps draw gate markets outside a town (guanxiang)

A walled town on our maps draws a small gate market outside each gate that carries a road: a few shops strung along the approach road, with an inn among them where the gate opens onto a through road. A gate market's size follows the traffic through its gate, not the town's population, so the largest stands at the gate the main traffic uses. Each is drawn whole inside the town's map.

  • Every walled town draws a gate market, except a military fort or a town whose gates have emptied.
    By the nineteenth century few walled Chinese cities lacked a suburb outside at least one gate, so only a purely military fort, or a town whose gate is depopulated or suppressed, draws none.
  • How many premises does a gate market hold, and how far out does it run?
    Typically four to eight permanent premises, never fewer than three, along the approach road within about 420 ft of the gate. The numbers are set against the towns our maps draw, and are a GUESS: no page we read counts a county seat's gate market or says how far it ran.
  • A gate market stands outside every gate that carries a road, and its shops sell to the villagers.
    The ground outside a gate drew markets and businesses serving the country people.
  • An inn stands among the shops only where the gate opens onto a through road.
    Inns stood only outside the gates that opened onto long-distance routes.
  • The gate on the town's main through road has the largest gate market.
    That is the gate the traffic uses: a suburb whose own wall grew to rival its city's lay in the direction of the main traffic.
  • Each gate market lines the road that runs on through its gate as the town's main street.
    This is a convention of our maps: the shops face the traffic coming through the gate.
  • A town's gate market sits whole inside its map.
    This is a convention of our maps: a real gate suburb could run on well past what we draw. A city's map is cropped, so its gate market shows that by running off the edge; a town's map is the whole view and keeps its buildings clear of the edge, so the approach road running off the edge says by itself that there is more beyond.
  • A larger gate market does not change the town's population.
    Our maps count only the merchants' own houses in a gate market as homes; every other building in it is drawn as a shop, which our maps count as housing nobody, so a longer gate market adds no one to the town.
  • The town's laborers and servants are never moved outside the wall to make room.
    That would invert the real pattern, in which the wall is what the commoners live inside: under the ward-and-market system a city's residents lived in walled wards within it.
