Shrine gateways and the approach to the hall (torii, sando)

A torii is the open gateway that marks where a shrine's sacred ground begins, and the approach to a shrine, its sando, passes under one or more of them on its way to the hall. The first stands where the approach enters the shrine's grounds. Most shrines, we GUESS, had a single arch; where an approach has two or three gates, they are numbered from the outermost in; the hokora, small shrines below the rank of a shrine, had none, or a very small one.

An arch was a gift. Worshippers gave arches in thanks for a prayer answered, a custom that spread from the Edo period, so a shrine's count of arches records its patronage rather than its size. At the great Inari shrine of Fushimi, in Kyoto, the gifts have grown to some 10,000 arches along the mountain's paths.

  • A torii marks the boundary of a shrine's sacred ground.
    The Association of Shinto Shrines says a torii is put up at the boundary dividing the inside of the shrine from the outside, and the ground inside it is revered as the kami's.
  • The first arch stands where the approach enters the shrine's grounds.
    The approach "is almost always straddled by one or more torii", its point of origin "usually straddled" by one. Where there are several, the first, and the largest, "stands at the approachway to the grounds of the shrine", and further arches may stand within the precinct as one nears the sanctuary.
  • Several arches on one approach are counted from the outermost in.
    They are called the first, second and third torii, the ichi-no-torii, ni-no-torii and san-no-torii. At Meiji Jingu, a modern shrine in Tokyo with eight arches in all, the first stands at the entrance to the southern approach, the second where the southern and northern approaches meet, and the third just before the worship hall.
  • An ordinary shrine's arch could be the gift of its parishioners together, or of one sponsor.
    The oldest stone arch in the city of Kudamatsu, of 1679, standing on the front approach of Kudamatsu Shrine, was built by the shrine's parishioners at a priest's solicitation, and one of 1701 by a single donor who "gave his wealth".
  • How many arches did a village shrine have?
    Most likely one. A proper village shrine had a single arch, and one with none was rare. No census of shrines' arches exists, so this is a reconstruction from what the pages state and the examples they give; the share of shrines with one arch, and that one with none was rare, are a GUESS.
  • How many numbered gates did a great shrine have?
    Two or three. The pages name a first, second and third gate on a long approach and give no count of shrines; three as the usual ceiling is a GUESS.
  • The hokora, small shrines below a shrine's rank, had no arch or a very small one.
    Unlike a shrine, a hokora has no torii, or only a very small one. That the hokora far outnumbered true shrines is a GUESS.
  • How many arches stood in a row of donated arches?
    From a handful to thousands. Fushimi Inari has around 800 arches on its main path and some 10,000 on its mountain. Rows of dozens or more belong to the Inari shrines; a handful to a few dozen at a modest rural one, and hundreds only at famous sites, are a GUESS.
  • Did a household shrine have an arch?
    Seldom, on the one page found. Most household guardian shrines are small shrines of stone or wood; one that is carefully kept may be given a hall the size of a subsidiary shrine and "may even have a torii". An arch there is a mark of care, not a fixture.
  • Two shrines on one ground may share one arch, or each have its own.
    The separate arch is attested; the shared one follows from where the boundary stands. The precinct's arch marks the ground of everything inside it, and a precinct keeps small subsidiary shrines besides the main hall, so a second shrine inside the same ground stands behind the same arch. But a subsidiary shrine may also have an arch of its own: the stone torii of Banshi-sha, a subsidiary shrine within the precinct of Kitano Tenmangu in Kyoto, is among the noted arches of the city.
  • No page we read gives how far an arch stood before its hall.
    Since the arch stands at the edge of the shrine's ground (Village shrines: where they stand, and how big their grounds are), the distance is how deep that ground is in front of the hall. No page gives it for any shrine, household or village, nor the length of a village shrine's approach, nor how far its innermost arch stood from the hall.

Where arches stand in a row, the spacing of the ones that could be measured falls into two kinds: donated rows packed almost post to post, and a great shrine's numbered gates strung far apart along a whole approach.

  • How close together stood the arches of a donated row?
    From almost touching to about 4 ft apart on average. At Yutoku Inari in Saga the arches of one packed stretch "appear to be lined up at intervals of almost 10 centimeters" (~4 in). Over the shrine's whole climb, 223 arches on the 300 m (~984 ft) path to its inner shrine come to about 1.3 m (~4 ft) apiece - this project's arithmetic on the page's figures.
  • How far apart stood the arches of a small rural row?
    About 3 to 4.3 m (~10-14 ft), on one estimate. At Manzo Inari, a remote mountain shrine with no house in sight, a visitor walked about seven minutes up a mountain path from the entrance before the view opened up, and counted 114 arches on the way back - a count that may take in arches beyond the hall, where the row goes on. No page gives the path's length; if the row runs the whole walk and no farther, an ordinary walking pace gives the figure above - this project's arithmetic on a walking time, not a measurement, and at a mountain shrine, not a village's.
  • How far apart stood a great shrine's numbered gates?
    Hundreds of feet, on the two examples found. At Nagao Shrine in Nara the first gate stands about 300 m (~984 ft) from the second, at the entrance of the precinct. Meiji Jingu's first gate stands at the entrance to its southern approach and its third just before the worship hall, and the walk from each entrance to the main hall takes about ten minutes.
  • Nagao Shrine's approach is a little over 7 m (~23 ft) wide.
    It was once a course for mounted archery.
  • Great Buddhist temple precincts of the Nara period measured some 700 to 1,500 ft on a side.
    A shrine's was not the only kind of great precinct with room for a long approach. Sanuki's provincial temple, its kokubunji, stood on 240 m (~787 ft) by 220 m (~722 ft), and Shimotsuke's on 457 m (~1,499 ft) by 413 m (~1,355 ft) - sizes that show the scale of a great precinct, not where any arch of theirs stood.
  • Were arches ever spaced between a donated row's few feet and the numbered gates' hundreds?
    No page we read gives one. The encyclopedias, the shrine association's explainers and a study of the great shrines' approaches attest the donated row and the numbered gates and give no spacing between them.

Rows of donated arches stand at Fushimi Inari, and the pages date them there.

  • Fushimi Inari's donated arches go back to the Edo period.
    The shrine says the devotion of giving arches along one of its approaches "arose already in the Edo period", and, more narrowly, that the donations "are said" to have begun around the late Edo period. The national library's reference service puts the first of its rows "from the Edo into the Meiji period".
  • Each arch at Fushimi Inari bears its donor's name.
    Each is given in thanks that a wish "passes through" or "has passed through", and carries the name of the one who gave it.
  • How thickly did Fushimi's arches stand before modern times?
    Perhaps thinly. A book by Kyoto teachers, as a sightseeing blog reports it, says the shrine's own drawing of its precinct in the first years of Meiji shows only six arches, with their bark on, and not one vermilion - a report at second hand of a drawing that may leave out the mountain paths.
  • At ordinary shrines, the early dated arches are single gates.
    A single stone arch at a country shrine in Kato is thought to date from as early as the late Muromachi or early Edo period. One city's inventory counts 64 stone arches and lists its dated ones up to the Kansei era (1789-1801) year by year, each arch listed on its own.
  • Did a village shrine have a row of arches before Japan began to modernize in 1868?
    No page we read shows one. Every dated arch found at a village shrine, or any ordinary shrine, before 1868 is a single gate, and the present-day rows at Yutoku and Manzo Inari are dated by no page we read. A row at an ordinary shrine is attested only in modern times.
