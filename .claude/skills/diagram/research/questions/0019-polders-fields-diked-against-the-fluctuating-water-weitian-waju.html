Polders: fields diked against the fluctuating water (weitian, waju)

A polder is low, wet ground walled in by a dike and drained so it can be farmed. In China it was the 圩田 (weitian, "polder field"): wetland enclosed with dikes to cut it off from the rising and falling water around it, then drained by hand. Fan Zhongyan, a statesman of the Northern Song, described the polders south of the Yangtze as each tens of li square, like a great city, with channels inside and gates through the dike outside, opened in drought and shut in flood. Japan had its own polders, the 輪中 (waju, "inside the ring"): ground enclosed by dikes, and the settlement within it, on the Nobi plain along the Kiso, Nagara and Ibi rivers; their defense against the flood is at Flood defenses of river-plain villages: ring dikes and refuge mounds (waju and mizuka).

A polder's floor comes in time to lie below the water outside it some or all of the time, and it drains by opening its sluices when the water outside is low; that its dike therefore runs all the way round, the water crossing only at gates, is this project's reasoning. The water outside rose and fell with the seasons, on one great Chinese lake by as much as forty feet in a year, and the rain that fell inside had nowhere to run, so every polder had to be drained: through its gates when the water outside fell, by pump, or by raising the rice above the wet.

  • A polder's dike encloses it completely.
    Full enclosure is what makes a polder: the ground is diked to cut it off from the water around it, and then drained. A polder comes in time to lie below the water around it some or all of the time, is at risk of flooding at all times, and drains by opening its sluices when the water outside is low. That a gap on any side would flood the whole block at the next high water is this project's reasoning from those lines, which describe polders in general rather than the Chinese one.
  • By the early twentieth century the old polders had been cut into smaller ones.
    They were divided into still smaller, islet-like polders, called fish-scale polders. So, in this project's reading, the water outside one polder's dike was often a creek with another polder across it.
  • The water outside rose and fell with the seasons.
    The level of China's Dongting Lake swings by up to 12.9 m (~42 ft) in a year, and its surface shrinks from thousands of square kilometers (several hundred square miles and more) in the flood season to a few hundred (a hundred square miles or so) in the dry, so at low water much of the ground outside a lakeside polder lay bare. That it lay as mudflat and reed, and that a dike was built against the flood rather than the ordinary day, is this project's reading; no page we read says either.
  • What lay outside the dike?
    In this project's reading, the lake and creek network the polder was dug from, and reed marsh and mudflat where reclamation had not yet reached; and, at the edge of a reclaimed district, the natural shore, since reclamation advanced outward from the shore. Both are GUESSES: no page we read says what lay outside a polder.

The farmers of a polder lived on it or beside it.

  • Where did a polder's houses stand?
    Along the water, and on the dike itself. In Kaixiangong, the village the anthropologist Fei Xiaotong studied, houses had to stand near the water, so villages grew up along the streams; and along the margin of each polder a strip 10 to 30 m (~33-98 ft) wide was left for mulberry, with wider ground for houses, and that higher margin also served as the farm's dike. Where a polder met the natural shore, its village standing on that dry ground is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • How old were the houses on a polder's dike?
    No page we read dates them. One account dates the large dyked-canal (tangpu) polders of the lower Yangtze to the eighth century, under the Tang dynasty, but says nothing of houses on their dikes.
  • This project guesses that each polder's own village kept up its dike.
    In that reading the polder's community was often one extended family, and named its polder. This is a GUESS; no page we read says who kept a polder's dike.
  • This project guesses that ten to twenty households shared a small polder, behind a dike about 1.5 m (~5 ft) high.
    Both figures are a GUESS: no page we read gives either for a small polder. The dike heights that were read are at Polder dikes: what they were made of, how big, and what grew on them.

Inside the dike, the water ran in channels of its own.

  • The lower Yangtze polders were laid out on a planned grid of channels.
    Under the Five Dynasties and the Song dynasty, the polders of the Lake Tai plain grew into the tangpu system, which joined dredging, dike building, sluices and water transport, with a cross channel every five or seven li and a long channel every seven or ten li. A second account dates the large tangpu polders earlier, to the Tang dynasty by the eighth century.
  • Seen from above, polders look like chessboards.
    A Chinese geography magazine's feature on polders says that, seen from above, polders large and small look like so many chessboards.
  • Did a ring canal run round the inside of the dike?
    Channels inside the dike and gates through it are recorded; a single canal ringing the field at the dike's inner foot is not, and no page we read names one or gives its line a set form. How channels meet and bend is at Which way water flows, and how channels bend and join.
  • Outside the dike lay the water it held back.
    A polder's dike enclosed the fields within and held the water without. That no channel of the polder's own ran outside it is this project's reasoning.

Closing a waju's ring dike on its downstream side as well made its foul water hard to drain, and from then on the waju suffered from its own standing water as well as the river's. Three answers are recorded.

  • A waju drained by gravity, through culverts opened when the river outside fell.
    With no power to pump, a waju could not drain whenever it liked, because the river outside often stood higher than its fields; so it drained through a hakehi, a culvert through the dike, shut while the river was high and opened when it fell.
  • Water came in at the top of a waju and left at the bottom.
    The intake was generally set at the upstream head of the ring and the outlet at its downstream tail.
  • How many openings did a waju's dike have?
    In the one waju whose count we found, many, and drains outnumbered intakes. Between 1789 and 1872 the villages of the Fukutsuka waju had 34 hakehi and one water gate for drainage, against four intake culverts.
  • A waju had few intakes, because a safe one was hard to build.
    A gate strong enough to let river water in safely was very hard to build through the dike. After one blew out in the flood of 1722, the Fukutsuka waju set no other for a long time, and surprisingly many waju paddies waited on the rain.
  • A Chinese polder's gates, too, were opened and shut with the river.
    The Wanchun polder, whose restoration was pressed for in 1061, had five water gates. They were opened in drought to let the river in and shut against it in flood, and, the page's own gloss adds, when the polder was waterlogged and the river outside fell below the gate, it could be opened to let the water out.
  • A polder on the sea could lose its outlet as the tidal flat grew.
    On the Ariake Sea in southwest Japan, where the tide rises and falls about 5 m (~16 ft) and the flats run out 5 to 7 km (~16,400-23,000 ft) at low water, a reclaimed field's outlet opened onto the flat. As the flat grew, the older land behind it lost its outlet and could no longer drain, and the way out was to reclaim the flat beyond it.
  • Was a sea polder's outlet opened on the ebb and shut on the flood?
    That such an outlet was opened on the ebb, the falling tide, and shut on the flood, the rising tide, as a river culvert was opened and shut, is this project's reading; no page we read says it.
  • A chain pump could lift water out of a polder in flood.
    The chain pump, a chain of scraper paddles that drags the water up, worked where the water had to rise only a little; besides raising water into the fields in drought, it could throw water out in flood, which helped the Jiangnan polders spread. Where on a polder the pumps stood, and how many, no page we read says.
  • Where the water would not go, the rice was raised above it: the horita.
    In a waju that stayed waterlogged, some paddies had part dug out and the mud heaped on the rest to raise the planting surface. Chronic poor drainage gave the waju this look, and the dug strips between became channels traveled by field boat. A waju of horita looked like a field of long ridges drowned so that only the ridges showed, spread across the whole view.
  • The horita spread across the waju in the Edo period and was gone by the late twentieth century.
    It spread from about 1753 and had vanished by 1975.

Some polder ground was dug into fish ponds, in the dike-pond system (at Dike-ponds: fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes (sangji yutang)).

  • Were the fish ponds of the Lake Tai plain laid out as a chessboard before today?
    Not that any record found shows. The grid the sources give is the canal lattice; the only chessboard of ponds found on the Lake Tai plain is the view from the air of Digang, on the south shore of Lake Tai, as a reporter saw it in 2023.

The old layouts of the ponds on record are in the Pearl River delta, in the far south, and they are of two kinds.

  • Dike ponds were dug out of the low fields, and their mud raised the dikes.
    The fields were dug deep into ponds, and the mud spread round them to raise the dike.
  • A modern essay describes the Pearl River delta's ponds as a mosaic.
    A landscape-architecture essay describes the historical delta as mosaic-like constructed ponds among meandering natural rivers, the boundary between the made and the natural blurred.
  • In 1678 Qu Dajun likened the pond land of Jiujiang, a delta township, to a chessboard.
    He says its land was like a chessboard, its black lines the dikes and its squares the ponds. So a grid of ponds is recorded before modern times as well as the mosaic. The two accounts differ in kind: the mosaic is a modern essay's view of the whole delta's past, the chessboard a seventeenth-century writer's likeness for one township, Jiujiang in the Pearl River delta (Dike-ponds: fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes (sangji yutang)), and how even his squares were, a likeness cannot say.
  • Most of the ponds were rectangles.
    They were mostly narrow rectangles stacked side by side, with shorter rectangles, squares and irregular shapes pressed in to fit the ground. Why rectangles is this project's GUESS: straight dikes are the easiest to pile, walk and plant mulberry on.
  • How large and deep was a dike pond?
    Most were 0.4 to 0.6 ha (~1-1.5 acres) and 2 to 3 m (~7-10 ft) deep.
  • In one Pearl-delta village, regular pond shapes are a plan of today.
    In Ruxi, the government is now planning to reshape all ponds into regular shapes.
  • How wide was a dike between ponds?
    In Ruxi, as a 2024 study of the village reports for the decades since the reform and opening-up, dikes that used to be 20 m (~66 ft) wide were eroded to less than 4 m (~13 ft) as the ponds grew and the fish farming narrowed them. It is one village's account, not a measurement across the delta.
