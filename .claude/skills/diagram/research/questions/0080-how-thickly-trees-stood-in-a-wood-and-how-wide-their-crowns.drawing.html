How our maps stock woods and size crowns

Our maps draw every canopy tree of a wood at its real size, on real spacing, so a wood is a dense mass of crowns. A hill wood is stocked at about 600 trees a hectare, one to every 13 ft, its crowns about 17 ft across; a village's coppice more thickly, about 1,700 a hectare on 8 ft centers, its crowns 8-9 ft across. No page we read gives a crown's width, so every crown size is a GUESS.

  • A hill wood is drawn at about 600 trees a hectare (~240 an acre), on centers about 13 ft apart.
    That is the middle of the 500-800 canopy trees a hectare (~200-320 an acre) guessed for a mixed hill wood, a figure on no page read, so the density is a GUESS. A hectare is 107,639 sq ft, so 600 a hectare is one tree to about 180 sq ft, a mean spacing near 13 ft.
  • Each crown in a hill wood is drawn about 17 ft across, some smaller and some larger.
    The mean crown radius is 8.5 ft, at the low end of the 5-8 m (~16-26 ft) guessed for a canopy crown, and each crown is drawn between 0.75 and 1.4 times that.
  • The crowns overlap, so the wood reads as closed.
    Crowns about 17 ft across on 13 ft centers overlap. Some classifications divide woods into open and closed by their crown cover, and this project reads a closed wood as one whose crowns meet, so the packed look is meant.
  • No crown is enlarged so that it can be seen.
    At one foot to a pixel a crown is drawn 12-24 pixels across, and it shrinks with the map at coarser scales, as the buildings do.
  • A windbreak's crowns are drawn the same real size as a hill wood's.
    One crown size serves the hill woods and the windbreak's cedars alike: on the Inashiro map the windbreak's crowns have a median radius of 7.6 ft, about 15 ft across. No page we read gives a crown width for a cedar windbreak.

One rule keeps the crowns of a wood from piling on one another, and one allowance lets them touch.

  • No canopy tree is drawn under another's crown.
    A crown is drawn only if its center lies outside every crown already on the map, and no drawn crown's center lies inside it. A crown wholly inside another's is not part of the canopy layer, and the canopy layer is what the map draws: a drawing convention.
  • Two crowns may still overlap at their edges.
    Two crowns whose centers stand closer than the sum of their radii are drawn overlapping at the edge. That overlap is a drawing allowance, not a claim about real trees: in some forests the crowns of fully stocked trees keep a gap rather than interlacing. On the Inashiro map, of 787 crowns none stands under another and 737 (94%) still touch a neighbor, so the wood still reads as closed.

A village's coppice stood more thickly than a hill wood, and the maps stock it from modern counts of konara woods.

  • How thickly is a village's coppice drawn?
    Our maps draw it at about 1,700 crowns a hectare (~690 an acre), on centers near 8 ft. That is the count of a planted konara stand of 29 years at Tsukuba, inside the 1,460-2,063 a hectare (~590-830 an acre) of woods left to grow to 26-31 years, one crown to about 63 sq ft. Those stands stood at or just past the old end of the cutting cycle the research section gives, so the figure is probably thinner than most coppices at their cut, and the coppice may read a little more open than it stood.
  • Why not the 2,560 a hectare (~1,040 an acre) of a younger stand, nearer the age many coppices were cut?
    The 23-year Kiso stand's 2,560, on centers near 6.5 ft, is younger, but it is one stand, counted unthinned as found. This project keeps the 1,700 until a coppice still being worked is counted at the age it was cut.
  • The maps do not draw a coppice at its younger, thicker stage.
    A wood cut more recently stood thicker still, to about 5,000 stems a hectare (~2,020 an acre) at 15 years.
  • Each crown in a coppice is drawn about 8-9 ft across, so that neighbors just meet.
    That is a GUESS, sized from the spacing, because no page we read gives a crown width for a konara, a kunugi or any tree of a worked fuel wood.

Only the village coppice is drawn from figures of its own; every other wood, the windbreak among them, keeps the hill wood's spacing and crowns until it is researched in its own right.

  • No wood is drawn at the densities timber was planted at in the Edo period.
    The Edo-period figures we read are for timber seedlings on the day they were planted, most often about one to the tsubo, not for a grown wood, a village's fuel wood or its windbreak. A rule practiced in one Rikuchū village planted coastal sugi thicker where the wind was fierce, a denser planting, which says nothing of a wider crown. So every density the maps draw rests on modern measurements or on none, and is labeled so.
