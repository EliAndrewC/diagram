How our maps draw bamboo, when one culm is too small to see

A culm of madake, the common timber bamboo, is at most about 10 cm (~4 in) across, a fraction of a pixel at our maps' scale of one foot to a pixel, so a stand drawn stem by stem would show nothing. Our maps draw each bamboo stand as an area whose place and size are to scale, marked inside with paired culm strokes and a leafy fork in pale yellow-green, jittered on a 7 ft grid with no fill beneath them. The marks are a map drawing convention: they stand for the bamboo, they do not count it. Such stands appear on about three farmsteads in five, as a strip beside the house or a patch inside the farm's own grove, and in a thicket of the village's own.

Our maps show only what existed before 1868, when Japan began to modernize. Bamboo stands did; the mark that stands for them comes from later maps.

  • Bamboo grows as a stand of its own, not as single stems among trees.
    A stand is in most cases of nearly one species, so our maps draw it as one area, not as culms mixed through a wood.
  • Japan's modern topographic maps give bamboo a symbol of its own.
    The legend of the national mapping agency gives bamboo groves an entry and a symbol of their own, beside broadleaf and conifer forest, and separate from, though like, the symbol for low bamboo grass. So the three can be told apart at map scale.
  • How old is the map symbol for bamboo?
    The earliest we found is on the national survey's maps of about 1910, and no page we read dates its introduction or traces it to a map from before 1868, in the Edo period.
  • How did older maps and pictures show bamboo?
    A village map of 1801 draws a local lord's house surrounded by a thicket, as a feature of its own, though the page does not say what the thicket was. An early-Edo screen paints settlements ringed by bamboo groves, and the pictorial maps of the period more often than not carry no legend for what their colors mean.
  • Our maps keep the modern mark, as a convention.
    A stand's place and size are historical; its mark is taken from a modern map legend, knowing where it comes from. The mark only stands for the stand, and the old maps offer no symbol to put in its place, only a picture of the growth. The marks are one small block of them repeated across the stand, the way a map fills a kind of ground with the one symbol its legend gives it, rather than a mark set down at each spot.

Where a settlement's bamboo stands follows the two places the record finds it: on the farmsteads, and in a thicket of the village's own.

  • Whether a settlement has bamboo, and where, is a knob.
    It is chosen at random for each map: no bamboo, bamboo on the farmsteads, a thicket of the village's own, or both. The record shows both places, and moso and madake stopping where the winters are hard (Bamboo groves (chikurin)), so two maps may honestly differ.
  • How many farmsteads keep their own bamboo?
    About three in five, on a map whose farmsteads have bamboo. This is a GUESS: no page we read gives a share, and bamboo named as one of several lesser plants of the grove reads as common but not universal.
  • A farm's bamboo is a strip of 22 by 16 ft.
    Its size is a GUESS, no page giving one.
  • Which side of the house does a farm's bamboo strip take?
    The strip is seated with the sheds and gardens, so the lanes and wells keep off it. Its side is chosen at random for each farmstead: behind the house most often (35 in 100), then on the windward side (30), beside the shed (25), and least often on the other flank (10). The sources give bamboo with the storehouses, at a wet edge, and from the west round to the north of the house in the grove; the seat behind the house is one no page we read names, and every weight is a GUESS.
  • On the Inashiro map, 8 of 15 farmsteads keep a bamboo strip.
    Six stand at the windward northwest corner and two on the far flank. Every shed on that map stands on the north side of its house, so behind the house and beside the shed are one place, and no strip found room there; a strip given either takes the windward corner.
  • A farm drawn with its own grove keeps its bamboo inside that grove.
    There it is a patch of culms the strip's size, 22 by 16 ft, on the house side of each windward band of the grove, as bamboo grew among the Tonami grove's lesser plants. The patch's size, its seat on the house side and a patch in every windward band are each a GUESS.
  • In the Tonami grove, the bamboo and the tall windbreak trees shared only the west side.
    The grove held its bamboo from the west round to the north of the house, while its tall trees stood from the south round to the west. A patch in every windward band, as our maps draw it, goes beyond what this one grove shows.
  • A grove's bamboo shows only between its crowns and along its edge.
    Bamboo filled the low part of a grove, under the trees, and a crown is drawn from above, so bamboo under one is hidden. Where a bamboo plant stands in the open it is drawn as the culm mark, beneath the crowns.
  • About one plant in twelve of a windbreak clump is bamboo.
    The share is a GUESS, no page giving one. It holds in the farm's grove and in the village's shelter belt alike; the dooryard copse and the alder at a marsh's reed edge carry none.
  • Where does a village's bamboo thicket stand?
    On dry ground just beyond the back row of houses. An early-Edo screen paints settlements ringed by bamboo groves, and a sixth-century Chinese farm manual says bamboo suits high, level ground and dies in a low field once it gets water. Its side of the settlement and its being held in common are each a GUESS.
  • A village's bamboo thicket is 84 by 58 ft.
    That is a working stand, cut and left to regrow. Its size is a GUESS, no page giving one.
  • A stand narrower than 14 ft is not drawn.
    Below 14 ft on its short side a stand does not read at fit zoom; a farm's strip, 16 ft deep, does. The floor is a convention.
  • No bamboo stand is drawn on a paddy.
    The farm manual that wants bamboo on high, level ground says it dies in a low field once water reaches it, and a paddy stands in water for the rice.
