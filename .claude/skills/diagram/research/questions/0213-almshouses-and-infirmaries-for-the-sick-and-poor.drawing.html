How our maps draw almshouses and infirmaries

A relief house - a poorhouse for the poor or an infirmary for the sick - took two forms, and our maps give each city one form or the other, a knob. In the form of China's Song dynasty, every county seat keeps both a poorhouse and an infirmary, as the law ordered, and so does a capital, as the Northern Song capital kept its almshouses. A provincial city keeps them too; that is a GUESS, since the orders name the counties, and a city above a county is taken to keep at least what a county seat keeps. In the Japanese form, a capital keeps one: a single longhouse standing in the ruler's herb garden, as Edo's infirmary did. A provincial city has none; that is a GUESS, since no page we read describes a relief house in a provincial castle town.

  • Our maps draw a pharmacy as one of a city's shops, not as part of a relief house.
    The Song dynasty's official pharmacies in its capital, Hangzhou, are named among the city's famous shops, so in either form the map draws a pharmacy as one of the shops. For the Japanese form that is a GUESS by analogy.
  • How big is a relief house on our maps?
    One longhouse about 25 ft deep and 110 ft long, and in the Song form each of the pair is drawn at that size. It is a GUESS, reckoned from the Edo infirmary's forty beds: forty sleepers at two tatami each (a mat is about 3 by 6 ft), doubled for the rooms and passages, since no floor area was found for any relief house.
  • Our maps draw the Chinese poorhouse and infirmary side by side in one walled yard.
    That is a GUESS: the Song dynasty's orders put both in every county but say nothing of where they stood or whether they shared a wall.
