How our maps place a village to match its name (chimei)

A place name in Rokugan usually says something about where the settlement sits relative to its fields, its water or its ground, so our maps place a settlement where its name says it stands, reading the name before placing it. Japanese names were often made the same way, from the lie of the land. The program that draws our maps does not read names; the name is read by whoever lays out the map.

  • A village whose name describes its ground is placed where the name says.
    The worked example is 上田 (Ueda, "upper paddy"), whose paddies are the upslope ones, so its dwellings stand downslope of them, facing up-valley toward the fields. On that map's ground, high to the northeast, the cluster sits on the lower, southwest flank with most of the paddy rising above it.
  • A village whose name says nothing about its ground stands where our maps usually place one.
    That is a clustered village on the drier, higher side of its valley, close against its paddies - the reverse of a name like Ueda ("upper paddy"), whose houses stand below their fields.
  • The maps read these parts of a name as its ground.
    Each cue reads its character's own meaning onto the map; the list is this project's choice of cues, not a historical one.
    • 下 (shimo, "lower") says the reverse of Ueda: the settlement stands above its paddies, the same as the usual placement.
    • 川 (kawa, "river"), or -gawa at a name's end, puts the settlement hard by the watercourse.
    • 山 (yama, "mountain"), 坂 (saka, "slope"), 原 (hara, "plain or moor") and 浜 (hama, "shore") each name the ground the place stands on.
    • 口 (kuchi, "mouth or entrance"), or -guchi at a name's end, puts the settlement at the way into a valley or onto a road.
    • 谷 (tani, "valley"), or -dani at a name's end, names a valley.
    • 谷 (ya, "valley") names a valley too, commonly a shallow one cut into uplands or low mountains.
    • 沢 (sawa, "mountain valley or marsh") names one or the other, by the rule below.
  • A sawa name is read as a valley or as a marsh, and whoever lays out the map chooses which.
    The word means a mountain valley in eastern Japan and a marsh in western Japan today. This project has not tied any part of Rokugan to eastern or western Japan, so the reading is a guess made for each map from its own ground.
