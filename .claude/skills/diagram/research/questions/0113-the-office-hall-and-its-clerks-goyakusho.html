The office hall and its clerks (goyakusho)

The office hall was the working building of an intendant's office (jin'ya), the office where its public business was carried on. At the Takayama Jin'ya, which governed Hida province for the shogunate, the office's main rooms, among them the examination room where hearings were held, were rebuilt in 1816, on this project's reading as one block. Its floor came to about 8,100 sq ft, while a small branch office's whole central building was about 2,850 sq ft. A county magistrate's office hall, the kind this setting's counties keep, falls between the two, on this project's reading.

In Japan the clerks called tedai were chosen from the peasants and townsmen versed in rural administration, under officials sent by the shogunate, and at Takayama the officials hired mostly from the district, the jiyakunin, worked in a room partitioned from theirs. A Chinese county office worked through six chambers, by its authorized staff of two or three men each. This setting's clerks are three or four heimen scribes who live in town.

  • The Takayama intendant's office, which governed a whole province, had a floor of about 8,100 sq ft.
    Its office block, the goyakusho, measures 754.75 m² (~8,124 sq ft). It dates from 1816, in the Edo period, the year the office's entrance hall, examination room, working office and great hall were rebuilt; this project reads them as that one block. The hearing room inside it is at The hearing court (shirasu).
  • A small branch office, at Joge, had a central building of about 80 tsubo, some 2,850 sq ft.
    The Joge branch office was an outlying office set up away from the intendant's own, to which three officials came at the end of the shogunate. A plan drawn in 1869, soon after the shogunate fell, shows its buildings roughly as they stood at its end.
  • How big was a county magistrate's office hall?
    A county magistrate governs one county of this setting. On this project's reading its office hall lay between the two measured offices: larger than a three-man branch office and far smaller than an office governing a whole province. Neither source, nor any other page we read, gives an office hall's length or depth - only a floor area.
  • Japan's tedai were chosen from among peasants and townsmen.
    The tedai were chosen from the peasants and townsmen versed in rural administration, with the shogunate's finance office's formal permission. Most were the children of village and town officials, hired first as scribes and promoted to tedai and then to chief tedai.
  • At the Takayama intendant's office, the clerks worked in rooms of the office hall.
    Beside the room where the officials sent from the shogunate worked, and partitioned from it, was the goyoba, the workroom of the officials hired mostly from the district. A room used only for writing, the kakiyaku-beya, was where the documents sent to the shogunate were drawn up, and they were bound in the room beside it.
  • At Edo's town magistracy, two of its sections kept their duty rooms among the rooms of its court.
    The duty rooms of two of its sections, the shacho-sen'yokata and the reikurikata, stood in the same quarter of the compound as its court, among the rooms given to suits and examinations.
  • An intendant's staff, the tetsuki and tedai, lived in huts and row houses inside the office's compound.
    An intendant's office took in, beside the office itself, the huts and row houses that were the official houses of its tetsuki and tedai.
  • Did a Japanese intendant's clerks ever have a building of their own to work in?
    No page we read gives them one.

A county office in China organized its clerks differently, and in practice kept far more of them.

  • A Chinese county's clerks worked in six chambers on either side of the office's second courtyard.
    The chambers, one for each of the court's six ministries, were set on the east and west sides of the county office's second courtyard.
  • How many clerks did a Chinese county office keep?
    Its authorized staff was two or three men to each chamber. In Ming and Qing times the chambers often ran over that number, even to more than a hundred men.
  • In China's capital, a few families filled most of an office's clerkships, and the clerks grew rich.
    The clerks of the silver treasury were mostly of one surname, and the tax clerks of the Chongwen Gate, one of the capital's gates, of a few, entrenched there. While the court's officials were often too poor to support themselves, the clerks amassed vast fortunes.
  • What did permanent clerks mean for a county office?
    This project's reading of the entrenched clerks of the Chinese capital is that, as permanent locals, clerks were an office's memory under one magistrate after another, and its natural pressure point for tax-farming.

A county of this setting is much smaller than a Chinese county, and its clerks are few.

  • In this setting, clerks are heimen scribes who live in town.
    Scribes are heimen artisans by caste, hired locally from the county town; they live in town and come in each day to the magistrate's compound, where the office hall stands.
  • How many clerks would a county office in this setting keep?
    Three or four, each paid about 4-5 koku a year: that is this setting's own figure, the county budget's line for its heimen clerks. That is well under the two or three men to each of a Chinese county's six chambers, and a county of this setting, of about 6,800 inhabitants, is far smaller than a Chinese county, of about 100,000-200,000 on a figure of this project's own, which no page we read gives.
