How our maps keep yards and gardens in the sun

Every threshing yard and kitchen bed on a map keeps clear ground to its south, where a neighbor's house would shade it, and to its west and southwest, where the village's windbreak throws its afternoon shadow. No source measures how far a farm's plots stood from its trees, so the distances are worked out from where the sun stands at 38 degrees north - the temperate middle of the empire our maps are set in - late in autumn, when the harvest is threshed and dried and the autumn vegetables are in the ground. They come to 39 ft of clear ground to the south of a yard or bed, and 50 ft to its west and southwest.

  • How tall is a farmhouse, up to its ridge?
    About 20-22 ft. A thatched roof has to be steep, and steeper the coarser its stems, and in the gassho farmhouses of the snow country the steep roof holds a large loft. A farmhouse on our maps is 46 by 28 ft, so a roof pitched at 45 degrees rises about 14 ft above a 7 ft eaves wall, to a ridge of about 20-22 ft. The pitch and the height are a reconstruction: no page we read gives either.
  • How much clear ground does a yard keep to its south?
    39 ft. Threshing and drying follow the autumn harvest, and at that latitude a 20 ft ridge casts, late in autumn, the shadows below. The drying day that matters is taken, as a GUESS, to run from nine to three, so no neighbor's farmhouse may stand within 39 ft to the south of a yard.

  • No tree of a neighbor's grove stands in a yard's sun.
    A grove there would shade the yard as a house does. Every crown keeps the clear ground every canopy tree keeps (below), and a farm is seated, where it can be, with its grove's bands clear of a neighbor's yard, so the grove stands whole.
  • Rows of homesteads leave room for the sun.
    Rows are set 92 ft apart, and a house (28 ft deep), its yard (about 26 ft) and 39 ft of sun come to about 93 ft; the rule is what keeps a neighbor out of the gap.
  • The clear strip is a rectangle, not the wedge the moving sun sweeps.
    It is measured across the yard's own width - a simplification, taken knowingly.
  • The maps may set a neighbor off to one side instead.
    The rule forbids the shadow, not the neighbor, so a map may stagger houses east to west to keep a yard out of the shade. Whether real homestead lots were staggered this way is a GUESS.
  • A kitchen bed keeps the same 39 ft.
    A bed's autumn daikon, and in this project's reading its other autumn greens, are in the ground in the same months as the yard's work, under the same low sun, so a farmhouse shades a bed 39 ft off just as it shades a yard. The summer's high noon sun throws only 7-12 ft, so autumn decides. The rule holds whichever is placed first, the bed or the house.
  • A bed may stand to the southeast, southwest, east or west of its house.
    So a bed whose seat to the southeast would lie in shadow moves to the other flank.

The morning sun comes from the east, where a neighbor's grove may stand against that neighbor's west wall - just east of a bed.

  • A bed shaded from the east by a neighbor's grove is moved a little south.
    Once every farm's grove is drawn, an east-shaded bed is nudged south into open ground, so the trees fall to its northeast. It is a best effort, not a rule every bed meets: a bed hemmed in to the south by paddy, lane or neighbor stays where it is.
  • The village's shared copse keeps the clear ground every tree keeps.
    So a bed beside it keeps its morning, its midday and its afternoon sun.

In the afternoon the sun moves round to the southwest, and on a map whose wind comes from the west, that is where the village's windbreak stands.

  • How far does a windbreak's afternoon shadow reach?
    Late in autumn at that latitude, the sun at three in the afternoon stands 27 degrees high in the southwest, so a windbreak throws a shadow about twice its height to the northeast: one of 10 m (~33 ft) casts about 64 ft, of which about 50 ft reaches east, and one of 15 m (~49 ft) about 97 ft, 75 ft of it east. In midsummer the sun is higher and the shadow shorter, so autumn decides.
  • No windbreak tree stands within 50 ft west or southwest of a yard or bed.
    The clear ground runs from a plot's north edge to 50 ft below its south edge, since the afternoon shadow comes from the southwest. It is kept as the windbreak is planted: a clump of trees that would shade a plot is refused and set deeper.
  • No canopy tree stands in a yard's or bed's sun, whatever stand it belongs to.
    No crown of the windbreak, the copse, a farm's own grove, a wood, the woodland commons, a persimmon, a scrub pine, or a dike's willow or fruit tree stands within 50 ft east, west or south of a yard or bed, from the plot's north edge down, its own farm's or a neighbor's: the morning sun throws a tree's shadow to the northwest as the afternoon sun throws it to the northeast, so a tree east of a plot takes its morning as one west of it takes its afternoon. The 50 ft is the windbreak's, which reckons every tree at a working windbreak's 10 m (~33 ft) - the least height the record draws a tree at, below the 12 m (~39 ft) of a full-grown persimmon - a GUESS. The record gives the sides a farm's grove stood on but not how far it stood from the farm's plots, so holding a farm's own grove to the rule is this project's choice, a GUESS. On the Tonami plain the flowering and fruit trees stood at the open east front of the house, but no source we read says how near a drying yard they stood, so a farm that dries its grain in a front yard keeps its persimmon behind the house (How our maps draw farmyard fruit trees).
  • Bamboo may stand closer, and a dike's coppiced mulberry and clipped tea are not held.
    Bamboo is left out of the rule - this project's choice, a GUESS, and not because its shade is slight: madake grows a tall culm in close thickets (Bamboo groves). The mulberry on a dike is not a canopy tree: cultivated mulberry is "for the most part trained as low bushes", pruned back each year. A tea dike's hedge is clipped low too, its form, height and width a GUESS. Neither throws a shadow near the 50 ft a tree does.
  • The windbreak is drawn at its working height of 10 m (~33 ft).
    That is the height of a windbreak a farm keeps in use and tends. One surveyed stand averages 15 m (~49 ft); that the taller figure is a stand left untended is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • A taller windbreak would stand off the edge of the map.
    At 15 m (~49 ft) it would need 75 ft of clear ground - so much that the windbreak stands off past the edge of the drawn map, which is set by the houses, yards, beds and roads. Tried on the Inashiro map, the windbreak fell from 131 clumps of trees to 38 and broke apart; at 50 ft it stands whole, 81 clumps.
  • The windbreak's inner face sets the map's edge.
    The face keeps the same margin from the edge that every other feature keeps, and the windbreak's depth runs off the edge.
  • The 50 ft is wider than the one tree's height an old Chinese farming manual allows for a tree's shade.
    The Qimin Yaoshu's reach, one tree's height, is 10 m (~33 ft) for the windbreak we draw; the maps keep the wider 50 ft that the afternoon sun's geometry gives.
  • The windbreak's clear ground is a rectangle, not the wedge the afternoon sun sweeps.
    It is simplified as the yard's is.
  • The windbreak's face keeps a small gap from the plots.
    The gap is an average plot's overhang; what guarantees the plots their sun is the refusal of any clump that would shade one.

Two kinds of map stand apart from these placement rules: the older hand-drawn maps, and the building plans a person draws.

  • The older hand-drawn maps are exempt from the sun rule.
    Every hand-drawn clustered map breaks it - 45 of 85 yards shaded at noon on the Ueda map, 31 of 70 on Hoshigaoka's, 27 of 55 on Kikuta's, 21 of 36 on Ubame's, 19 of 44 on Hoshizora's, 3 to 10 on each hamlet's, a neighbor's wall commonly 2-8 ft off a yard's edge - while the provincial cities, with their farms spread loosely around them, are clear. Re-packing a finished map to satisfy a rule written after it would move every feature on it, so the rule binds a map when a generator draws it, and on the hand-drawn maps a shaded yard is drawn anyway.
  • A kitchen bed on a hand-drawn building plan must get six hours of direct sun in autumn.
    A kitchen bed is a bed of sun crops such as daikon, which want about six hours or more of direct sun. Counting the hours in the autumn months is this project's reading, a GUESS, and so is counting an hour when at least half the bed is lit. The scripted maps keep the placement rules above; this rule checks what a person draws by hand.
  • A bed of half-shade crops needs three hours - a knob.
    A plan may declare a bed of half-shade crops instead, which does with three hours of direct sun or with light dappled through leaves; it then needs three hours in which nothing but a tree's leaves stands between it and the sun - a GUESS at how to count dappled light.
  • The six-hour check casts each shadow at the least height the record gives each thing.
    A building at the farmhouse ridge's 20 ft, a compound wall at the 1.6 m (~5 ft) of the surviving earth wall (Compound walls (neribei and tsuijibei)), a tree at the 10 m (~33 ft) of a working windbreak. Taking the least height is a GUESS, chosen so that a bed the check fails is surely shaded, an untended stand being taller still.
