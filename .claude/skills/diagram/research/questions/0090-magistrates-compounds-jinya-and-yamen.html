Magistrates' compounds (jin'ya and yamen)

A magistrate in Japan and China worked and lived in one walled compound. In Japan, in the Edo period, the shogunate's intendants governed its own lands from office compounds called jin'ya, more than fifty of them across the country; the one that survives is at Takayama, the office the shogunate set up to govern Hida province; in the towns, a town magistrate's office held their administration and their courts. In China a county magistrate governed from a compound called a yamen, laid out by regulation.

Both put the magistrate's household inside the working compound, so that the office was also a home. In China, by regulation, the office stood in front and the home behind it. The two differed most in scale: an intendant governed with a dozen or so staff, where a large Chinese county had up to a thousand clerks.

  • A Chinese county yamen was laid out by regulation, on a central axis.
    The yamen at Neixiang, in Henan, follows the Qing dynasty's regulations for local government offices in its overall layout, with its main hall, second hall and third hall on the central axis.
  • Was a jin'ya laid out on a set plan, as a yamen was?
    No page we read gives a jin'ya a central axis, a symmetry or a prescribed arrangement. That the Japanese form had no codified plan is this project's reading of that silence.
  • In China, the office stood in front and the residence behind.
    Neixiang's layout of office in front, residence behind is the ancient order of court in front, sleeping quarters behind, and the regulation it follows puts the court in front, the sleeping quarters behind and the jail to the south, the whole sitting in the north and facing south.
  • Did Japanese offices put the office in front and the residence behind?
    No page we read says so. At Takayama the intendant's official residence stood on the lot immediately to the west, beside the office rather than behind it.
  • Takayama's office was rebuilt in 1816, and its residence restored from a plan of 1830.
    The office's entrance, examination room, office and great hall were rebuilt together in 1816, and the residence and inner rooms were restored in modern times from a plan of 1830.
  • Takayama's designated site covers about 2.8 acres.
    The site measures 11,219 square meters (~120,800 sq ft), about 9,800 square meters (~105,600 sq ft) of it without the plaza in front of the office.
  • The magistrate's household lived inside the compound.
    A Chinese official and their immediate family commonly lived in a residence attached to the yamen, and a Japanese town magistrate's official residence stood inside the magistracy.
  • A small domain's jin'ya held its lord's residence, offices and storehouses inside earthen ramparts and a moat.
    Jin'ya also named the seat of a small domain, the territory of a lesser daimyo. Ranked below a castle, it had earthen ramparts and a moat but no high stone walls, and inside them a residence hall, offices, the retainers' residences, earthen storehouses and a drill ground.
  • In a yamen, an inner residence gate divided the office from the magistrate's home.
    Neixiang's five gates end at an inner residence gate. That the gate was guarded is this project's reading, a GUESS: no page we read says so.
  • A yamen's main court was where the magistrate tried cases.
    In Neixiang's main court the magistrate tried criminals and held major ceremonies, and two kneeling stones of the Ming dynasty mark the places of the plaintiff, to the east, and the defendant.
  • A yamen kept shrines inside its walls.
    Behind Neixiang's guesthouse is a courtyard for the worship of the god of the land and the god of the government office. Japanese offices did the same (Shrines kept inside government offices and houses (yashikigami)).
  • A yamen had its own prison, a walled courtyard of its own.
    Neixiang's prison, the last of the Qing dynasty's to survive in China, stands southwest of the main court. More than twenty rooms survive, in inner, outer and women's prisons, enclosed by high walls, with a temple to the prison god inside.
  • How large was an intendant's jail?
    No page we read gives its size or layout; a visitor's account of Takayama mentions its jail only in passing. That it was smaller than a yamen's prison is this project's reading, against that silence: set beside a yamen prison of more than twenty rooms, an intendant's office had a staff of about a dozen.
  • This project guesses that a yamen kept a granary and a records archive.
    It is a GUESS: the pages we read on the Neixiang yamen list offices, storerooms, account rooms and the prison, but neither.
  • An intendant governed with about ten retainers and a few local hires.
    Under an intendant were about ten retainers of the shogunate and several locally hired assistants.
  • A scholar of the late 1700s wrote that a large Chinese county had up to a thousand clerks.
    Hong Liangji, writing in the late 1700s or early 1800s, said that in the larger departments and counties the clerks reached a thousand.

The smallest Japanese office on record is a branch post at Joge, in what is now Hiroshima prefecture.

  • Even a branch office of three officials was a walled compound with a court for trials.
    The shogunate's post at Joge was an office set up in territory away from its intendant's office, something like a branch, and in 1867, at the end of the shogunate, three officials were posted there. A drawing of it made in 1869 shows shrines inside its walls, among them an Inari shrine and a Tenjin shrine, though excavation of the site did not turn up the shrines, which rest on the drawing alone; it also shows a central building, and a court spread with white gravel where trials were held. Its main gate is at The main gate and its gatekeepers (nagaya-mon), and the entrance facing it at The formal entrance and a guest's arrival (genkan)
  • The Joge post's central building was about 80 tsubo (~2,850 sq ft).
    So the city of Fuchu's pamphlet on the site gives it.
  • The Joge post's site measured about 230 by 89 ft.
    Excavation from 2008 to 2010, with the drawing in hand, found its stone steps and stone walls, ditches thought to have bounded its buildings, and their foundation stones, and put the site at about 70 m (~230 ft) by 27 m (~89 ft), close to the drawing's 35 by 14 ken (~207 by 83 ft).

An intendant's office was paid for out of the rice it collected, and at some offices that did not stretch.

  • The intendants first paid for their offices out of a rice surcharge (kuchimai).
    The surcharge was collected with the tax at a fixed rate, and in the early Edo period it went wholly to the intendant's office, spent at the intendant's discretion on the staff's wages and on brush, ink and paper.
  • Did the surcharge cover an office's costs?
    Accounts differ. One encyclopedia says the costs came in below the surcharge, while another says that at offices where it yielded little, with no prospect of more, it became one cause of spending the main tax on the office, and of bribery.
  • From 1725, a century into the Edo period, the shogunate paid the offices' costs.
    The surcharge went into the shogunate's storehouse with the tax, and the office's expenses were paid separately.
  • How many intendants were in debt?
    A popular account written from two books on the intendants says, without giving a date, that the wages and travel costs of the office's staff were paid to the intendant and were not enough, and that nine intendants in ten carried a debt, called hikioi, from spending tax they had collected. The account hedges both sentences, and neither book was read.
  • A gundai's letters from Takayama tell of a drafty house, not of want.
    The same account quotes the letters of Toyoda Tomonao, gundai at Takayama, to a father at home: drafts came through the double shoji, and on a snowy day the least wind piled snow on the veranda. It is a cold house in a snowy province, laughed off with the family, and not in itself a sign of want.
  • No page we read shows what a short-funded office looked like.
    What was read shows that the money ran short, not what the shortfall looked like.
