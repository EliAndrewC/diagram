How our maps draw a village shrine's monk, the monk's dwelling (kuri), its records and its land

On our maps a village shrine is kept by a country monk who lives there, as a shrine with a Buddhist temple attached was kept until an order of 1868 separated the kami from the Buddhas. By default the monk's dwelling is one end of the worship hall's own building, under the same roof; where it is a house of its own beside the hall, it is the size of a farmhouse. The district's records, which in the setting are the country monk's to keep, are kept in a writing room in that dwelling, since a small shrine's business was often done at its keeper's home, and no office building is drawn. The shrine's land and its income are not drawn at all.

  • The game's published setting puts caretakers at its shrines and a resident monk at most temples.
    The setting's shrines have keepers who look after them, which the resident monk fits. As its fan wiki summarizes the rulebook, "There was no village without some manner of shrine", and "Priests were the shrine's administrators and primary caretakers". Of temples, the smallest "relied on visiting monks, priests, shugenja, or even local volunteers", "but most had at least one resident monk".
  • Our maps have the country monk live at the shrine.
    The setting's fusion of the Fortunes and Shinsei is read as Japan's fusion of the kami and the Buddhas before 1868, when shrine-temples "remained common" and a shrine's monk lived at the temple and performed the shrine's rites in Buddhist form. So the form is accurate. No page we read counts how common it was in the villages, and the received view in one region puts shrines served by lay priests rather than monks as the rare case.
  • Our maps draw no shrine kept by its parishioners in turn, and no temple served by a visiting priest.
    History had both: a Shinto village shrine with no one living at it, kept by the parishioners in turn, and a temple with no resident, served by a priest who comes from far away. The game's setting has the second at its smallest temples. This project gives every village shrine a resident monk instead, the form that matches the setting's fusion of the Fortunes and Shinsei. The choice is ours, not a count of which form was commoner.
  • By default the dwelling shares the worship hall's roof, at one end of the building.
    A temple's main hall and dwelling under one roof is attested once, and called unusual there, so the form is accurate but rare. This project makes it the default because at a village shrine one building serves as both the monk's home and the place the villagers come to. No page we read counts how common the form was, so the default is our choice. The building's size is at How our maps draw a village shrine's precinct: halls, basin, sacred tree and offerings (keidai).
  • Where the dwelling is a house of its own beside the hall, it is drawn the size of a farmhouse.
    A country shrine's plan is drawn by hand, and its author may give the dwelling this form, the ordinary parish temple's, in place of the one-roof building. It is drawn at the size of the map's farmhouses, 46 by 28 ft. An ordinary temple's kuri was much like an ordinary house, and one measured kuri resembles its region's farmhouse, so the form is accurate. The one small kuri we found measured, at a Kōshin hall in a Fukuoka town that had once belonged to the shugen order of mountain ascetics, was 2 ken wide and 2.5 deep, about 12 by 15 ft, far smaller than a farmhouse (Village temples (tera)). No page we read measures an ordinary village parish temple's kuri, so the farmhouse size is a guess.
  • The district's records of births, deaths, marriages and travel are kept in the monk's dwelling, never in an office building.
    In the setting our maps are drawn for, these records are the responsibility of the country monk of each village district, so they are kept where the monk lives. A small shrine often had no office of its own: "business was frequently carried on at the private home of the administrator". So the records are kept in a writing room or a record chest in the dwelling, and no office is drawn (accurate). History divided the work differently. Under the Edo period's temple registration (terauke), a temple certified each of its parishioners as its own, but the yearly register itself was compiled by the village headmen, so the monk keeping all of the district's records is a deviation of the setting's. The writing room itself, its size and its place along the dwelling's front are a guess.
  • Nothing about the shrine's land or its income is drawn.
    What the record holds of them - a precinct's exemption from tax, the sealed land grants, the fees a parish paid its temple for the rites of the dead - are rights and payments, not buildings, so there is nothing on the ground to draw.
