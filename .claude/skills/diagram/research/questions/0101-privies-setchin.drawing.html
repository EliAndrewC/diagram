How our maps place privies (setchin)

On our maps a samurai residence's privies are built into the house, as a samurai house's were, never a hut in its yard. The house has two, the guests' and the family's, and the rest of the compound has about one to each of its parts, toward the service walls where the night-soil collectors reach them from outside.

  • Our maps give a residence two privies, the guests' and the family's.
    A samurai house might keep two, and a magistrate's house whose main house dates to 1794 counts two, though its record does not date them.
  • How do our maps place the guests' privy?
    On the formal side of the house, at the rear of the reception room, reached along a board veranda. A house with a guest parlor put a privy at its rear, and the Sayama domain's council hall gave its two upper privies board verandas; neither shows the whole arrangement in one house, so the combination is this project's reading of the two together. On the magistrate's compound our maps generate, it is attached to the house's rear face, centered behind the reception room.
  • Where do our maps put the family's privy?
    Apart from the guests', toward the living rooms - a GUESS. It is drawn from the urban house after Meiji, whose family privy jutted out near the living rooms, and from the row houses of a daimyo's retainers, since no page we read places a family privy in a samurai house before 1868. On the generated compound it is attached at the house's rear corner by the family's rooms, as a privy built in a corner of the corridor, its pit toward the rear wall.
  • How many privies does a compound have?
    About one to each part of it, three or four at a county magistrate's compound, the count following how many live and work there - this project's choice, a GUESS. The generated compound sets them beside the barracks, the stables and the servants' row, and a servants' row that lodges by the stables shares the stables' privy. That lodgings shared a privy building of their own nearby is recorded for a compound's row houses.
  • The privies away from the house stand against the compound's service walls, near a gate.
    Night soil was bought and carted away by collectors from outside, and a merchant house on a back street kept a back gate for them; no page we read names such a gate at a samurai house, so setting the pits where a collector's cart can reach them from a gate is this project's reading of the trade.
  • No night-soil collector has to cross the inner court.
    That is this project's own rule, not a finding: the pits are never set so deep in a compound that a collector would pass through the court where the household lives.
  • No privy stands in a courtyard.
    That is this project's own rule: a privy is never set in one of the courts the compound's buildings are ranged around.
  • A privy against the compound wall is emptied through a hatch in it - a GUESS.
    The hatch is a small opening through the wall behind the privy, level with its middle, the collector's way in from outside. The house's own privies, attached to the house, have none.
  • A privy is drawn 5 ft square, a one-seat privy - a GUESS.
    No source we read gives a privy's size; this is this project's choice.
  • A privy stands at least 15 ft from any well - a GUESS.
    The record holds no separation distance.
