Merchants' townhouses (machiya)

A merchant's townhouse (machiya) in a Japanese town stood on the street: the shop at the front, where it opened onto the road, and the family's rooms behind it in one row or two along a long, narrow earth-floored passage - a plan much alike across the country. In Kyoto, the old imperial capital, most held three rooms in a row, with a tiny garden set into the house for light and air, and the storehouse stood at the back of the lot. Many had a low upper story used for storage.

How wide a shophouse's frontage was, and how high a town's street front rose and what it was roofed with, is at The town's street front: shop widths, how close the houses stood, stories and roofs (machinami).

  • Most Kyoto townhouses had one plan: a long earth-floored passage with three rooms in a row beside it.
    A long, narrow earth floor (toriniwa) was lined by three rooms: the shop room, the kitchen, and farthest in the most formal room.
  • Kyoto townhouses set a small garden inside the house for light and air.
    It was about one tsubo, about 36 sq ft, set at the back or the middle of the building.
  • The back of the lot held detached rooms, outbuildings and the storehouse.
    In Kyoto the storehouse was as a rule never built on the street but at the back, and the storehouses at the backs of a block's lots together made its boundary and a kind of firebreak.
  • Townhouses in the provincial towns followed one of three plans.
    Castle towns, post towns, ports, temple towns and country market towns built Kyoto's through-passage plan; Edo's plan, with its earth floor across the front of the house, made a little wider; or a fusion of the two.
  • Towns near Kyoto mostly built the through-passage plan, towns farther off the other two.
    The through passage was common near Kyoto, the front earth floor or the fusion farther away.
  • A form common in Kyoto split the house into two buildings.
    The shop stood on the street and the dwelling behind it as a building of its own, an inner garden between them and a narrow entrance wing joining them.
  • Wealthy townsmen of the Edo period built their dwelling apart, behind the shop.
    From the second quarter of the seventeenth century such a house set its dwelling in the middle of the block, joined to the shop on the street by a veranda and an inner garden, its entrance off an alley.
  • Through the Edo period most Kyoto townhouses had only a low half-story upstairs.
    It served as a storeroom and as rooms for live-in servants; only the large merchant houses built it up further.
  • When did Kyoto townhouses gain a full second story?
    At first the upper floor was built only over the street front, under low eaves; as time went on it grew to a full story, nearly as large as the ground floor, used for guest rooms. The source gives no date for the change.
