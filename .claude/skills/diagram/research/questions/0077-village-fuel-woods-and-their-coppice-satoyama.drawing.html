How our maps bound and draw the coppice woods

Our maps draw a village's woodland commons as a few patches of coppice on the nearest rising ground beyond its fields, because the record's village runs from its houses out to its fields and then to the hill and wild land beyond. Each patch has a smoothly wandering edge, grass reaching a few feet in under its first crowns, and no brush or pines inside, because a worked wood's floor was kept open by cutting and raking.

  • Where on the land does a village's coppice wood go?
    On the nearest slope beyond the fields from the houses, on ground higher than the fields beside it - not on whatever open ground lies nearest the houses. The record's village runs from its houses out to its fields and then the hill and wild land, and where the record ranks the hills, in the Yoshino mountains of Nara, the satoyama is the nearest and lowest hill ground, on the slopes around the settlement. Reading "beyond the fields" as "higher than the fields next to it" where the ground slopes is this project's reading of those slopes.
  • Where the map has no higher ground, the wood goes on the far side of the fields on the level.
    The record's village runs from its houses out to its fields and then the wild land beyond, and that order holds where there is no hill to climb: the wood keeps to the far side of the fields from the houses.
  • Low ground by a river or marsh is left to grass, not wood.
    A wood seated below the houses, past the fields and beside a river or marsh, would stand where the record puts a village's grass and riverbank commons, not its forest. That the edge of a reed marsh went with the grass commons too is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • Why does a coppice patch have a wandering edge, never a square?
    A boundary fought over at law was fixed as a drawn line that bent, its seals set at its ends and bends, and no page we read describes a coppice lot laid out as a surveyed rectangle. So each patch is drawn as an outline whose edge wanders smoothly, as a wood's edge wanders rather than serrates. The wandering edge itself is a map drawing convention.
  • Where a patch comes near a brook, a lane or a field, its edge runs alongside.
    Within about 45 ft of one of those, the patch's line keeps off the feature and follows it, rather than wandering near it or across it. That a wood's edge followed what bounded it on the ground - ridge, stream and path - is a GUESS: no page we read says so.
  • The straight strip woods of the planned upland villages are not drawn.
    In those villages each household's strip ran from house lot to fields to flat-land woodland, so the wood was a strip with straight sides. That is a form of the whole settlement - a planned dry-upland strip village with no paddy - not a shape for a hillside coppice, and drawing it would change the houses, the fields and the lanes together. A wet-rice hamlet at the foot of hills, like Inashiro, draws the hillside form.
  • No brush or pines stand inside a coppice wood.
    A worked wood's floor was kept open by cutting and raking, with herbs under the canopy, not brush, so the brush and the lone pines our maps scatter over scrubland stop at the wood's edge.
  • A village's windbreak grove is kept clear underneath as well.
    A windbreak grove is a managed wood too, its trees planted and kept up, so the same rule holds for the village's shelter belt as for its coppice patches.
  • How far does grass reach in under a wood's edge?
    About 8 ft, and there it stops. A worked wood's undergrowth was cut and carried off, and the light after cutting let the plants of its floor grow, and that this floor is grass at the wood's edge, thinning out over the first few paces under the crowns, is this project's reading, a GUESS. The 8 ft is a calibration of those few paces, narrower than the margin our maps draw at a marsh's edge, where reeds and grass interleave over about 46 ft.
  • An abandoned coppice's brush is not drawn.
    A wood left uncared for grows poorer in plant species, and that it grows brushy too is this project's reading, a GUESS; so a brushy wood would be a second form, for a settlement abandoned for years. That an inhabited settlement never shows it is this project's inference from the contrast between managed and uncared-for stands, not a finding.
