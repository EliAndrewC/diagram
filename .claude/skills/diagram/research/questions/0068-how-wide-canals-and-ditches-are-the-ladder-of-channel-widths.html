How wide canals and ditches are: the ladder of channel widths

Before modern times - before 1868 in Japan - the water of a rice-growing district ran through channels of a few set widths, each dug for the ground it watered: a field ditch for a single paddy about 0.3 m (~1 ft) wide on this record's reading of a classical scheme and an intake, a distribution lateral about 1 m (~3 ft) on this record's reading of an excavated drain and an intake, and a main canal (yosui) a few meters across or more. The moats round castles ran wider still, to about 90 m (~295 ft) at Osaka. From the finest ditch to the widest moat is a span of about three hundred to one.

A channel also narrows along its run, because each branch it feeds draws off part of its water. An engineering law for earth canals puts a channel's width at the square root of the water it carries, so a ditch keeps most of its width while it still has most of its water, and narrows fast over its last stretch.

  • The Rites of Zhou set out a ladder of five field channels, each twice as wide as the one below it.
    The Chinese classic's account of the crafts names them from the finest, one chi wide - "the cut of one ou", two spades working side by side - through two, four and eight chi to the widest, two xun, which leads straight into the rivers. On this record's reading it describes an ideal division of land, not a survey, and never says how many such channels were dug.
  • How wide was a field ditch that watered one paddy?
    About 0.3 m (~1 ft), though no source measures one directly. The finest channel of the Rites of Zhou is one chi wide. In 1657, in the Edo period, a branch of the Tamagawa aqueduct that served one village, Sunagawa, was taken off through an intake seven-tenths of a shaku square, about 21 cm (~8 in) - the opening of the intake, not the ditch below it. A modern Chinese design standard makes 0.3 m (~1 ft) the narrowest bottom width a canal of any grade may be built to, so that it can be kept up - a floor, not a measurement.
  • How wide was a distribution lateral?
    About 1 m (~3 ft), on this record's reading of figures none of which is a lateral's own channel. At the Hattori site in Shiga, a paddy from the very start of the Yayoi period, a drain 0.5 to 1 m (~1.6-3.3 ft) wide ran east-west, and the central canal was 1.4 to 2 m (~5-7 ft) wide. It is a single excavated site, far older than the Edo period. A branch of the Tamagawa aqueduct that served fourteen villages, the Mita canal, is listed under 1722, marked for a time suspended, with an intake three shaku square, about 91 cm (~3 ft) - again the intake's opening, not the channel below it. A modern Chinese district's canal works are designed with laterals 1.0 and 1.2 m (~3-4 ft) wide, and farm canals 0.4 and 0.6 m (~1-2 ft).
  • How wide was a main irrigation canal (yosui)?
    Up to about 11 m (~36 ft) for a large one, by the size of the district it watered, in this record's reading; the record has no figure for a small one's own channel. On the Echizen plain the rules agreed in 1537 for dividing the Jugo canal's water named, among their very detailed figures, a channel width of just over six shaku, about 1.85 m (~6 ft), without tying the figure to any one channel. The Minuma canal, whose cutting began in 1727, ran past its split from the Hoshi River in a new channel about six ken wide, about 11 m (~36 ft). This record takes about 5 m (~16 ft) as a middling district's main canal - a GUESS at its middle.
  • This record's widths for a village brook and a town river, about 2 m (~7 ft) and 20 m (~66 ft), are GUESSES.
    Brooks and rivers are natural watercourses, not things anyone built, and no record from before modern times that we read gives either a width. No modern page we read gives a typical width for either.
  • How wide were the canals cut through cities?
    From about 8 m (~26 ft) to 62 m (~203 ft) in the two we read. Kyoto's Takase canal, cut from 1611 to 1614, was 8 m (~26 ft) wide and about 30 cm (~12 in) deep. Osaka's Dotonbori measured 20 ken across on its east side and 34 on its west in 1787, about 36 and 62 m (~118-203 ft). What faced a city canal's banks is at How wide is a city canal, what faces its banks, and what stands along them?
  • How wide were castle moats?
    From about 12 m (~39 ft) at Himeji to about 90 m (~295 ft) at Osaka. At Himeji the inner moat runs 12 to 34 m (~39-112 ft) wide along its circuit and the middle moat about 20 m (~66 ft); the outer moat is about 14 m (~46 ft). Osaka's outer moat reached about 90 m (~295 ft) at its widest.
  • Do the old figures agree with the modern ones?
    Yes, on the size of each channel. Every kind of channel that was dug by hand has a figure from before modern times that this record reads its width from - for the field ditch and the lateral a classical scheme, intakes and an excavated drain rather than a measured channel of their own kind - and the modern design documents agree with those readings.

How fast a channel narrows follows from three things: that its branches draw off its water, how its width follows the water it carries, and where along its run the water leaves.

  • A main canal narrows as its branches draw off its water.
    A study of Japanese paddy irrigation says the main canals gradually decrease in size as they are tapped by branch canals, and that part of one branch canal may serve most or all of a village's land.
  • Lacey's regime equation for earth canals puts a channel's width at the square root of the water it carries.
    For a channel in alluvium, the wetted perimeter is 4.8 times the square root of the discharge, and width, depth and speed go as the discharge to the powers 0.5, 0.33 and 0.17. It is a modern engineering law, cited for the physics of an earth channel, which has not changed.
  • Does a delivery ditch lose its water evenly along its run?
    Nearly, in this record's reading. A modern Japanese design rule is one inlet from the ditch to each plot, so a ditch running past a row of like paddies gives up about the same water along each stretch; a drain gathers water the same way in reverse, through one outlet from each plot.
  • How does a ditch's width fall along its run?
    As a square root, not a straight line: where a ditch gives off its water evenly along its run, the width squared is what falls evenly, because it is what tracks the water. A ditch keeps most of its working width while it still has most of its water to deliver, and dwindles hard over its last stretch.
  • Does a drain widen the same way, in reverse?
    By the same arithmetic it gains width fastest over its first stretch. That it also gathers the largest share of its water there is this record's reading, a GUESS.
