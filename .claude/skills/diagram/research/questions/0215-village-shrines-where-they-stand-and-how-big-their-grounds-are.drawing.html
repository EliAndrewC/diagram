How our maps site and size a village shrine

A village on our maps has one shrine. It stands at the village's water mouth, inside its own wood, fronted by arches (torii) facing the village, and it is drawn small: a modest hall that holds, under one roof, both the villagers' place of worship and the home of the country monk who keeps it, on a precinct of a few hundred tsubo, inside the band that shrine registers compiled after 1868 give, a band Edo-period figures also span.

  • The village's shrine stands at its water mouth.
    On our maps the village's entrance is its shuikou: the point where the water leaves and the track sets off for the road, and where the water-mouth grove already grows (the village's groves). In Chinese villages, temples are on record at the water mouth or the entrance (where village shrines stand); that the earth god's shrine took that site as a rule is this project's reading, a GUESS. Japanese villages kept their guardian in a tutelary shrine in its own wood, within the village or at its edge. Our maps join the two, and seat that shrine in its wood at the Chinese site.
  • How large is a village shrine's precinct drawn?
    About 150 to 650 tsubo, the band the prefectures' shrine registers give. Those registers date from after 1868 and can err either way against an Edo-period precinct. But Edo site figures and the figures for tax-exempt shrine lands span the same band, so the band holds before 1868 as well (accurate as a range).
  • How does the shrine precinct on our Hoshigaoka village map, about 860 tsubo, compare with real ones?
    Hoshigaoka is one of our village maps, and its precinct is larger than most. Measured from its well to its outermost arch, it is larger than the usual band and than the largest village shrine in the Saitama district's register, but inside the Tochigi district's range of 142 to over 2,700 tsubo. It is larger than every Edo-period site figure found, and inside the figures for tax-exempt shrine lands, which may include fields. The range is accurate; the precinct's place in it is a GUESS.
  • How much of the precinct does the wood cover?
    Some of it, or nearly all. The rest is open ground. That the unbuilt ground was mostly wood is a GUESS; where the wood stands, its outline and why no fence bounds it are at How our maps draw shrine woods and fences (chinju no mori, tamagaki).
  • The hall and its arches stand in a cleared opening in the wood.
    They are drawn clear of the canopy, not under it - a map drawing convention. The bare ground is a small clearing at the hall and at the sanctuary, the small inner building that houses the god, not a yard.
  • How big is the drawn hall?
    About 60 by 48 ft, some 275 m² (~2,960 sq ft), two farmhouses' worth under one roof. Worship hall and monk's home share one roof, and the only building of that form we know of is a temple. That building, the hall and priest's quarters of Kaie-ji, a Zen temple in the city of Sakai, measures 25.2 m (~83 ft) by 8.0 to 13.0 m (~26-43 ft), some 200 to 330 m² (~2,153-3,552 sq ft), a form its own heritage listing calls unusual. The drawn hall lies inside that band (accurate; the country shrine's halls, the monk's dwelling).
  • No village shrine's hall is drawn larger than about 600 m² (~6,458 sq ft).
    The grandest village hall our maps draw runs to about 490 m² (~5,274 sq ft). Halls of 1,000 m² (~10,764 sq ft) and up, the size of a monastery's or a large temple's, are drawn only in towns and cities, so a village's stays under 600 m². These three figures are this project's own calibration: no source read gives a Japanese village shrine's hall a ceiling.
  • Hie Shrine in Kurihara, a village shrine on record, has a worship hall far smaller than the drawn hall.
    Its worship hall comes to about 18 by 12 ft (a village shrine's halls); the drawn hall is larger because it holds the monk's home.
  • The hall stands beside a lane, never on one.
    A building sits off the way. The arches are the deliberate exception: each straddles the approach, and the way runs under it.
  • A shrine's stone basin, for washing before worship, is drawn beside the approach.
    It stands beside the approach (what else stood in the precinct), never on the hall and never under an arch.
  • The hall and its arches carry no label.
    A vermilion hall with arches in front of it reads as a shrine on sight, as the rows of markers beside it read as a graveyard, so neither is labeled - a map drawing convention.
  • Our maps call a village's shrine a shrine, never a temple.
    Where our map text uses the word temple of a village site, it means the shrine.
  • A hamlet draws no shrine of its own.
    The record shows both a part of a village with a shrine of its own and one with none, which would ordinarily make it a knob. But the counts that show small communities with their own shrines are modern, and reflect a wealthier, even post-industrial society, so this project draws a hamlet without one. A few of a hamlet's homesteads keep a household shrine of their own, and those it keeps. There is no knob (what each size of settlement draws).
