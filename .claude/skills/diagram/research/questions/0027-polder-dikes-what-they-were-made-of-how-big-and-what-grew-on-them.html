Polder dikes: what they were made of, how big, and what grew on them

A polder's dike was a bank of earth heaped round fields reclaimed from a lake, a river or a marsh, to hold the water out. In the dike-pond country of the Pearl River delta, the dikes between the ponds were made of the mud dug out of them. The one premodern polder dike whose size we found, a great one of the Song dynasty, was about five times as broad as it was high, and a river dike in Edo-period Japan stood about 3.6 m (~12 ft) high on a base of about 5.5 m (~18 ft). A village's own dike, in this project's reading, stood lower than either.

The Chinese dikes whose planting is recorded were planted, or planned to be planted, along their length: willows on the side facing the water, whose roots bind a bank against the water, and, where silk was made, mulberry along the crest. Willows could be cut back for their shoots, and willow, mostly, and mulberry stand bare in winter, so in this project's reading the dike's trees did not do the work of the tall groves that sheltered farmhouses from the wind.

  • The only premodern polder dike whose size we found is a great one: the Wanchun polder's.
    Shen Kuo's record of the Wanchun polder, whose restoration was taken up in 1061, gives its dike as six zhang broad and one zhang two chi high, and 84 li long, with a road inside it 22 li long, wide enough for two carts and lined with willows, and five water gates. So the dike was five times as broad as it was high.
  • The Wanchun dike was planned broad at its foot and narrow at its crown.
    To the fear that wind and water would wear the dike away, its rebuilders answered that its foot would be several zhang broad and its crown, the flat top, only a few chi.
  • How large were polders?
    From a few tens of mu to over a hundred thousand mu. In the Song dynasty Fan Zhongyan described each of Jiangnan's polders as several tens of li square, "like a great city", with channels inside and gates outside.
  • Did a polder's outer dike follow the water's edge?
    This project's GUESS is that it did, in gentle curves and bends, while the ditches and paddies inside ran straighter. Polders took the shapes the ground gave them, square, round, oblong, many-sided or irregular, but no page we read says that an outer dike traced a lake or creek shore, or that the fields inside ran straighter.
  • By the early twentieth century, the small polders round Lake Tai were divided into still smaller "fish-scale polders".
    They were named for their shapes as seen on aerial photographs.
  • Around Lake Tai, rectangular polders are a modern form.
    After China's economic reform of 1978, the organic forms of the Lake Tai polders were replaced by standardized rectangles for industrial farming.

Japan's records give the river dikes, and the ring dikes of the waju, the diked communities of the Nobi plain (Polders: fields diked against the fluctuating water (weitian, waju)).

  • Early Edo river dikes in Echizen were about 3.6 m (~12 ft) high on a base of about 5.5 m (~18 ft).
    The dikes built on the Kuzuryu river in Echizen, today's Fukui, in the early Edo period were, with differences from place to place, about 3 ken across the base, 1 ken across the crown and 2 ken high: 5.45 m (~18 ft), 1.82 m (~6 ft) and 3.64 m (~12 ft).
  • Up to the Edo period, Echizen's river dikes were chiefly open-ended dikes and ring dikes.
    Some ran for kilometers along the river: one built early in the period ran 5,700 ken, about 10.4 km (~34,000 ft).
  • The Mino side's dikes were held lower than the Oakoi, so the Mino side built waju to defend itself.
    It was settled that the dikes on the Mino side should stand 3 shaku, about 1 m (~3 ft), lower than the great dike called the Oakoi, so the Mino side had to defend itself locally and built waju.
  • Inside a waju, the banks raised against the fields' own standing water were smaller than the ring dike.
    They were built as flood defense required, and went by many local names.
  • How thick was a great waju's ring dike at its foot?
    Perhaps of the order of 40 m (~131 ft). The shared drain of eighteen villages in one waju, on the Ogure river, was seven culverts, each 41.1 m (~135 ft) long, passing through the dike. That the dike there was about that thick at its foot is this project's reading: a culvert may run out beyond the dike's toes, the feet of its two slopes, on either side.

Neither country's records give a small village polder's dike outright, but they bound it.

  • How high and how broad was a village polder's dike?
    No page we read gives a small polder's dike height. The one breadth read is a Lake Tai village's, where the raised margin of each polder, ten to thirty meters (~33-98 ft) of land where it held mulberry and wider where it held houses, served as the farm's dike. The heights read bound it from above: in this project's reading a village's own ring stood far below a great official polder's dike like Wanchun's, and below a domain's river dike like Echizen's.

What a dike was made of is recorded best in the Pearl River delta, where the fields were dug into fishponds and the dug earth heaped into the dikes between them.

  • A dike-pond's dike was the mud dug out of its pond.
    A Shunde account has the mud taken and spread round the sides to make the dike, the hollow sunk in the middle being the pond.
  • A pond's dike was raised and repaired with pond mud two or three times a year.
    Each time a pond was drained, the mud at its bottom was dredged up onto the dikes, raising them and fertilizing their soil. That this made the dike the best dry planting ground in a landscape of water is this project's reading.
  • An aged pond's dike tended to collapse near the pond gate in heavy rain.
    An FAO account of the mulberry-and-fishpond plots says so of the older ones.
  • Today's pond dikes are narrower and barer than they were.
    In one modern study, dikes that used to be 20 m (~66 ft) wide have been eroded to less than 4 m (~13 ft) as the ponds were enlarged, the cane and mulberry that once stood on them have declined, and the government plans to make all the ponds regular in shape.

The Chinese dikes whose planting is recorded were planted, or planned to be planted, along their length, the willows on the water side to bind the bank.

  • Willow roots protect a bank against the water.
    Willows are often planted along streams so that their interlacing roots protect the bank. That a bare bank of heaped mud gullies in the rain, is gnawed at its foot by the waves and slumps into a breach is this project's reasoning.
  • On the Wanchun polder, rows of mulberry flanked the dike's ridge, and its rebuilders planned rows of willows along it, with reeds below.
    The record counts some tens of thousands of mulberry trees on the dike, and, in the rebuilders' answer to an objection before the work, has willows and reeds keep the water a hundred paces off.
  • In the Pearl River delta, the pond dikes were planted with mulberry, fruit trees and sugarcane.
    Mulberry was planted on the dike and fish stocked in the pond, and the dug earth was built into dikes with fruit trees and cane on top. Mulberry leaves fed the silkworms and the silkworms' droppings fed the fish.
  • Which trees stood on which side of the dike?
    Willow by the water and mulberry on the crest, in this project's reading of the Wanchun record, whose willows had reeds below them and whose mulberry flanked the ridge. Willow grows on moist ground.
  • Dike willows were cut for their branches.
    Willow's rod-like shoots, often grown on pollards, were woven into baskets, fish traps and fences, and a Ming river manual has the long willows along a dike yield big branches every year for fascines.
  • In one Lake Tai village of the 1930s, each polder's raised margin was both its dike and its mulberry ground.
    In the village the anthropologist Fei Xiaotong studied, the land along the margin of each polder was left for mulberry, with a wider space for houses, and this higher margin served as the farm's dike.
  • How close together were the willows in a dike's row?
    No page we read gives a tree spacing for a polder's dike. The nearest figure comes from the Willow Palisade, a border earthwork of the Qing dynasty in the northeast whose levees were planted with rows of willows: there three willow stakes were planted every 5 chi, about 1.67 m (~5.5 ft), along the embankment, each four cun, about 13 cm (~5 in), thick and six chi, about 2 m (~7 ft), long. It is an analog, from a border embankment, not a figure for a polder's dike.
  • How far apart were a dike's rows of willows?
    About 3 chi, roughly 1 m (~3 ft), on the great river dikes of the north. Pan Jixun's Ming river manual plants lying willow densely about 2-3 chi from the dike's foot and long willow about 5-6 chi from the dike, both on the ground off the dike's foot rather than on its faces. The distance between the rows is this project's arithmetic from those two figures, and the manual is for the Yellow River, an analog for a polder.

In Japan, farmhouses were sheltered from the winter wind by groves of their own, and in this project's reading the trees on a dike could not do that work.

  • Could the dike's trees shelter a village from the wind?
    Very little, in this project's reading. A windbreak slows the wind for up to 30 times its height downwind, and prevents wind erosion for 10 to 20 times its height, so its shelter grows with its height. In this project's reading, willows cut back for their shoots stand low, and a single row of them is thin. That dike willows were cut so, and what follows for shelter, is this project's reasoning.
  • Willow and mulberry stand bare in winter, the season of Japan's northwest wind.
    Willows are typically deciduous, and so is the mulberry, while in Japan the northwest wind prevails in winter. That winter is the season when shelter matters most is this project's reading.
  • On the Sendai plain, many homestead groves stand on the north and west, of cedar, pine, hinoki and zelkova.
    Many igune stand on the north and west sides of the homestead as windbreak and snowbreak groves, and their trees are sugi, pine, hinoki and keyaki.
  • Does a sheet of ponds slow the wind less than land?
    In this project's reading, yes. Over open water the wind slows near the surface far less than over land: by 20 to 30 percent, against 40 to 50 over rough ground. That a sheet of ponds does the same on a smaller scale, so a village on the downwind dike gets the wind little slowed, is this project's reading.
  • Dike trees and homestead groves are recorded in different landscapes.
    The Pearl River delta planted mulberry on its pond dikes, and inside a Japanese waju a homestead had a windbreak grove to its north and a flood-protection grove to its south. No page we read shows both in one landscape: they are different plantings for different jobs.
