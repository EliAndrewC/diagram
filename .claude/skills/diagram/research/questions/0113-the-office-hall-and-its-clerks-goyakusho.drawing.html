How our maps draw the office hall and its clerks (goyakusho)

Our maps draw a county magistrate's office hall as one long building in the office's outer court, sized between two offices whose buildings were measured, with a floor of a few thousand square feet. The clerks' room is a room inside it, beside the day office, where the magistrate's own samurai staff do their daily business. The clerks have no building of their own at the office, to work or to live in.

  • How big is the office hall on our maps?
    Between about 1,600 and 6,750 sq ft. That is always well below the 8,100 sq ft of the Takayama intendant's office, and, except at its low end, above the about 2,850 sq ft of a small branch office's whole central building. The ceiling is accurate, calibrated against the two measured offices; the floor, below the smaller of them, is a GUESS.
  • How long and deep is the office hall on our maps?
    80 to 150 ft long and 20 to 45 ft deep. The smallest and largest halls multiply out to the floor above (80 by 20 ft is 1,600 sq ft; 150 by 45 ft is 6,750 sq ft), but the proportions are a GUESS: no page we read gives an office hall's length and depth.
  • Behind the magistrate's dais, our maps draw a day office and an official study.
    The dais is the front of the office hall, above the hearing court (How our maps draw the hearing court (shirasu)). A dais standing alone would leave the magistrate and the samurai staff nowhere to do their daily paperwork, and, on this project's reading, the private study in the residence stands on the household side of the line between office and home, the wrong place for official business. So the dais is drawn as the front of a deeper hall with those two rooms behind it. That is this project's reading of what the block must hold, a GUESS: no page we read places those two rooms behind the court.
  • Our maps put the clerks' room inside the office hall, beside the day office, as at the Takayama Jin'ya.
    There, the workroom of the officials hired from the district was partitioned off beside the room where the shogunate's own officials worked.
  • Our maps give the clerks no building of their own to work in.
    No page we read gives an office's clerks a building of their own to work in, so a separate clerks' building would be a GUESS; they work in their room in the office hall.
  • Our maps house the clerks in town, not at the office.
    An intendant's staff did have housing inside the compound, apart from the office hall: huts and row houses for its tetsuki and tedai. This setting's clerks are heimen scribes hired from the county town, who live at home in town and come in each day, so our maps draw no housing for them at the office. That is a deliberate deviation from the Japanese record.
