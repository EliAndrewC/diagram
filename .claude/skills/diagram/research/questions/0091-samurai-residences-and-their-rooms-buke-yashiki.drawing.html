How our maps lay out samurai residences and their rooms (buke yashiki)

Every residence our maps draw is the house of the magistrate who holds the office, set in the private court of their compound. It is sized to the house of a district magistrate built in 1794, in the Edo period, and its rooms and its massing follow the forms samurai houses are known to have taken: rooms running from the reception at one end to the family's at the other, and one block under one roof or a few halls stepped back one behind the next. It keeps its guest rooms inside the house, as samurai houses did, and keeps its garden on the sunny south and its service on the shady ground behind it.

  • The house is measured against a district magistrate's house of about 49 tsubo (~1,740 sq ft).
    That is the Yokota house at Matsushiro, built in 1794, of a 150-koku man who served as district magistrate, the one house measured from before 1868 whose holder held the office a county magistrate holds. On the county magistracy's plan the house, its kitchen and its bath come to about 1,780 sq ft.
  • The 67-tsubo (~2,380 sq ft) samurai house at Matsue is not used as a measure, because its size is that of a later restoration.
    It measures a house restored from drawings of the Meiji period, after 1868, not the house as it was built.
  • How big is the house of a samurai of about a thousand koku?
    The one larger samurai house measured is the Kuchiba house at Hagi, whose holder had several times the Yokota household's 150 koku. It is anywhere between about 41 and 74 tsubo (~1,470-2,630 sq ft), as its two official measures stand. Our maps size no house from it.
  • How big is a kitchen?
    A fraction of the house, its size a GUESS. The one kitchen measured, the Kuchiba house's east wing, is about a sixth of the house by one of its two measures, and no other house read gives its kitchen's share. A plan whose kitchen approaches the size of the whole house has its sizes wrong, and How our maps size a compound and its buildings checks for it.

Inside the house, the rooms follow one of the two orders the record holds.

  • The room order is a knob with two forms, one of them read from a single house.
    The palace order runs from the reception in front, through the master's rooms, to the family's rooms beyond. The small house's order sets the formal zashiki deepest in, the living rooms between it and the entrance. The second rests on one house, as this project reads its plan.
  • Our maps always set the reception room at one end of the house.
    It stands at an end in both known forms, so a reception room set between the master's rooms and the family's is drawn nowhere.
  • The plans drawn so far follow the palace order.
    The reception stands at the end nearest the approach, the master's rooms beside it, the family's beyond, and the inner rooms at the far end.
  • Each labeled room stands for several rooms.
    The master's rooms, the family's rooms and the inner rooms are each a suite compressed to one label, a drawing convention.

How the house is massed is the second knob.

  • The residence is one block, or two or three halls in echelon - a knob with two attested forms.
    The ordinary form is one block, its rooms under one roof, and middle and lower-ranking samurai houses had almost no corridors. Halls stepped back one behind the next are Katsura's form, and each map takes one of the two.
  • On the county magistracy's plan the house is one block in two rows.
    It is massed in two rows, front and back, as the Kuchiba house's rooms are, with the reception at its east end, the full depth of the house, nearest the middle gate.
  • A residence drawn in echelon reads as a house that was added to.
    At the Katsura villa that is the form a house took as it grew hall by hall, over about half a century, so a plan drawn this way shows a house its holders have built onto.
  • Is each hall joined to the last by a corridor?
    On our maps it is, this project's reading, putting the great houses' corridors between buildings together with Katsura's echelon. No page we read says how Katsura's halls are joined.

A guest is lodged in the house.

  • A magistrate's guest quarters are the zashiki and the rooms beside it, inside the residence.
    That is where a samurai house kept its guest space.
  • A guest house drawn as a building of its own is a GUESS.
    None was found at a samurai house, and the nearest attested form, the honjin's suite of honor, belongs to a post town's lodging house, not to an office. The county magistracy's plan is the one that departs from this: it draws a guest house of its own, facing the garden beside the reception rooms, a GUESS.

Around the house, the garden takes the sunny south and the household's work the shady ground behind it.

  • The garden takes the sunny south, before the reception rooms, where the plan's buildings allow.
    The formal garden lay south of the formal rooms. Where a plan's buildings do not allow it, the reception faces the court or the service ground before its block instead, short of that ideal.
  • The shady north band behind the residence is a service strip, or narrowed to an alley.
    It holds the servants' rowhouse, a utility building, the well and the family privy attached to the house, a GUESS, or it is narrowed to an alley of about 6-10 ft for carts and servants, its width a GUESS. On the county magistracy's plan the house stands 10 ft off the north wall, with the family privy flush to the wall in the alley.
  • Why put the servants, the well and the privy at the rear?
    By reasoning from where the garden stood, a GUESS: with the garden in the sun to the south, the service went behind the house. The storehouse is the one service building a page places there, and no page we read places the family privy anywhere in a samurai house's grounds, or a servants' range behind the house. The one samurai house's well we found placed stands at the front, through the gate of the Aoyagi house at Kakunodate, so the well drawn at the rear is a GUESS that example does not support.
  • A wide empty band behind the house is not drawn.
    It would be dead ground inside the walls, and it would leave the household's servants, about ten on the grounds by the plan's own count, a GUESS, drawn nowhere.
  • The household's storehouses stand behind the house, about 12 by 18 ft each.
    The storehouse stood behind the house at the Higuchi house, and about 2 by 3 ken is the common size.
  • The ground between the storehouses and the house is left open as a rear yard.
    The Higuchi house kept open ground there, and our maps label it.
  • How many storehouses does a household have?
    Our plans draw two, as the Yokota house kept two; the number on any one map is a GUESS.
