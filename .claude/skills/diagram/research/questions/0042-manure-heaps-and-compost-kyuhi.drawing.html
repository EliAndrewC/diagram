How our maps place manure heaps (kyuhi)

On a map, a manure heap is a small mound just beyond its farmstead's privy, on about half the farmsteads. Our maps leave out what is recorded only in modern times, but the heap is older than that: Chen Fu's Chinese farm manual of 1149 and the Japanese Nogyo Zensho of 1697 both call for a manure house (Manure heaps and compost (kyuhi)), so the maps keep it. Where it stands, its size and its share are all GUESSES: no page we read says where in the yard the heap stood, and no count of any date sets how many farms kept one.

  • A hamlet keeps its manure either in heaps or in pits.
    Each hamlet rolls one form; the roll is at How our maps furnish a dike-pond hamlet, and where a pit stands, beside the privy or out at a field, at How our maps place privies (benjo).
  • A manure heap is drawn just beyond the privy, on the side away from the house.
    Under the Han dynasty a Chinese latrine was customarily built over the pigsty and piped to a cesspool, so the privy and the muck stood together. That a Japanese farmstead kept them together too is this project's reading, a GUESS. The heap's distance from its privy varies by a few feet from one farm to the next.
  • On a farmstead with no privy, the heap stands behind the house or at its side.
    The place is a GUESS: no page we read says where in the yard the heap stood.
  • Each heap is 8 by 6 ft.
    The size is a GUESS: no page we read measures a heap.
  • How many farmsteads keep a heap?
    Between 40 and 70 in every 100, the share rolled for each hamlet from its seed. It is a GUESS: no count of any date gives it. It is set below the manure house on every farm that the manuals of Chen Fu (1149) and the Nogyo Zensho (1697) call for. The one count is a 1972 survey, which finds 0.24 compost sheds a household on the older farms, those whose main house was built by 1944. That count is from modern times, so it does not set the heap's share. That the open heap was commoner than its shed is a GUESS with no count behind it.

The privy stands on its farmstead's sunny side, as nearly three in four did in one study of farm privies, and the heap beyond it goes the same way - sometimes toward a neighbor.

  • A few privies and heaps stand nearer a neighbor's house than their own.
    Measured over the 13 test maps, 6 of 66 privies and heaps stood nearer a neighbor's drawn house than their own, and 4 of 66 by the distance between centers. The two differ because a privy or heap can be far from a house's center and still stand against its wall. The worst was Kashikawa's heap, 32.0 ft from its own farmhouse's wall and 8.4 ft from a neighbor's. A reader takes such a heap to be the nearer house's; the interactive map says whose it is on a click.

  • None of the three placed the heaps better while keeping them beyond the privy.
    The fan search, which worked for the privy itself, places more heaps but none of them better. The sort over every spot gains by pulling the heap back toward the house, which breaks the rule that it lies beyond the privy. A slightly clearer map is not worth giving up the heap's place beside its privy, which is this project's reading of the record. The sort among the spots beyond the privy cannot break that rule, and it came out the same as the map on every count.
  • The heaps still nearer a neighbor stand where the sunny side faces that neighbor.
    Where a privy stands on the sunny side and the neighbor lies that way too, every spot beyond it lies toward that neighbor, as it would in a real close-set cluster of farms. So a spot clearly nearer the heap's own house is preferred wherever the ground offers one, and is never forced.
