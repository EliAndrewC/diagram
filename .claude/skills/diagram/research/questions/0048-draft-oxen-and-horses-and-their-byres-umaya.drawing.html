How our maps draw and place byres (umaya)

Our maps draw a byre as a small shed of about 16 by 11 ft, in the homestead of the household that keeps the beast: against its farmhouse, or standing on its own near it. In a few settlements a single shed on common ground serves several households instead. Somewhat under half a settlement's households keep a beast. Which beast, an ox, a water buffalo or a horse, is chosen per settlement, and the byre is drawn the same for each: the beast changes what the byre's description says, not what the map draws.

  • Our maps draw a byre at about 16 by 11 ft, close to the frame of a surviving Edo-period stable.
    The Edo stable at Maizuru, registered as a tangible cultural property, has a frame of about 15 by 12 ft.
  • A byre is drawn as a one-room, open-fronted shed.
    It has a plank roof and a dark open front to its stall, so it reads apart from the solid gray storehouse and from the dwellings.
  • A byre is kept small on purpose.
    The building areas registered for the two measured Edo stables, 30 and 34 sq m (~323 and ~366 sq ft), say a stable could be nearly twice that size under its roof. A larger one starts to read as a European barn of many stalls, and there are none on these maps, because the beast of a Japanese farm lived under the farmhouse's roof or in a stable of its own. A byre is a shelter for a beast or two, kept well under the farmhouse our maps draw, about 120 sq m (~1,292 sq ft).
  • A village modeled on Japan keeps horses where it stands for the east and cattle where it stands for the west.
    Japanese farms kept both oxen and horses, so which a settlement keeps is rolled from the map's seed: toward horses for the east, toward cattle for the west, and between the two for the south of Japan. Which part of the setting stands for which region of Japan is this project's decision, not a finding, and how far each roll leans is calibrated liberty.
  • The setting's own history can put a horse in cattle country.
    Where a daimyo hands surplus horses to the farms, a village in cattle country may keep a horse.

Rice farmers in China plowed with other beasts.

  • A paddy hamlet modeled on China's rice country keeps a water buffalo or an ox.
    Both beasts were kept in China's east-central provinces, with more water buffaloes than oxen.

The rule the map follows:

  • A byre is a farmhouse's own stable, whether it owns the beast or has it on loan.
  • Where it stands is a knob, rolled per settlement:
    • the inner stable, drawn against the farmhouse as a part of it, since a stall under the house's own roof cannot be seen from above - a map drawing convention;
    • the outer stable, a small shed of its own in the homestead, about 6 to 12 ft off the farmhouse wall - the distance a GUESS, since no page gives it;
    • rarely, a shed on the common ground among the houses, about one to every four or five households, reached by several of them - a GUESS, found on no page we read. It is drawn the same 16 by 11 ft as a household's byre, so it too holds a beast or two: the beasts of the one or two among its four or five households that keep one, about 20 to 50 in every 100, where the other forms roll 35 to 50. Its size is a GUESS as well; no page we read describes a shared shed, so none says whether one held more beasts and stood larger.
  • The inner stable is the commonest roll, the outer stable next, and the shared shed the rarest - about six, three and one settlement in ten. How much commoner each is, is calibrated liberty, since no page we read counts them.
  • Where a settlement's byres are inner or outer stables, somewhat under half the households keep a beast: the share is rolled per settlement between 35 and 50 in every 100, the top the half one province's farm households kept from the early 1700s and the bottom the fewer that kept one later. The range is a calibration, not a count: how many fewer no page gives, and one village of Saitama district had a stable at 50 of its 76 houses in 1824, about two in three, more than the range allows (The farmstead and what stood on it (yashiki)). This project keeps the province's figure because it is a share of farm households over a whole province, where the village's is one village's houses, and whether its stables held horses is not known.
  • No byre is drawn alone beyond the last house at the village edge.
  • A byre may stand near a well, and never on the wellhead itself.
  • Why may a byre stand near a well?
    This project takes the beasts to have been watered at the household's well, a GUESS on no page we read, and no old rule we found kept the two apart, so a map that held its byres away from its wells would be drawing a modern idea of hygiene, not a village of the time. A byre near a well stands near the well its household draws from.
  • A byre a few dozen feet from a well stands no nearer its water than a farmhouse's own beast did - our GUESS.
    In a farmhouse whose stable and well were both under its roof, the beast and the water would have been the width of the house apart, about 20 to 40 ft, a GUESS: no page we read places the well or gives the house's width. So the shared byre that one of our generated villages, Kashikawa, draws 38 ft from the village wellhead is no nearer its water than that.
