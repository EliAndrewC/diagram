Town walls, and which county seats had one (chengqiang)

In China a county seat was usually walled. By the late Ming more than two thirds of county seats had walls, and by the end of the Qing the great majority, most of them 4 to 10 m (~13-33 ft) high and under 2 km (~6,562 ft) round. A county wall was earth inside, sometimes faced with masonry, with, as at Xi'an, a walk along its top behind a crenellated parapet. On this page's reading, being the seat of government in an age that built walls is what walled a Chinese town.

In Japan the walled town was the exception. Only the lord's castle was fortified, and the town around it stood open; a town that had to defend itself - a self-governing town, a temple town, a warlord's castle town in the Sengoku civil wars - ringed itself with an earth bank and a moat.

  • The Han dynasty ordered every county town walled, in 201 BC.
    An order in the sixth year of Gaozu, the dynasty's founding emperor, told every county and city of the realm to wall itself.
  • The Song dynasty discouraged walls away from the frontier.
    Wary of the provincial warlords who had carved up the late Tang, it did not encourage walled cities in the interior, and many interior seats of prefectures and counties long went without walls or let their old ones fall to ruin.
  • How many county seats were walled by the late Ming?
    More than two thirds, by an estimate. No page we read says which seats made up the unwalled third, or why.
  • By the end of the Qing the great majority of county seats were walled.
    The same holds for the seats of prefectures and departments.
  • A Japanese encyclopedia calls China a society of enclosures.
    It says every county and provincial city, and villages too, had their enclosures.
  • How high was a county wall?
    Most stood only 4 to 10 m (~13-33 ft). Pingyao's, rebuilt on a large scale in 1370, early in the Ming dynasty, stands about 12 m (~39 ft), a little above that band. Zhenhai's wall was about 7.8 m (~26 ft). Shanghai's, thrown up in three months in 1553, late in the Ming, against pirates, was first only about 4.8 m (~16 ft) high, and stood at 7.8 m (~26 ft) after later raisings, one of them by about 1.6 m (~5 ft) in 1598 - our conversions, at the length the Zhenhai page gives the old unit.
  • How thick was a county wall?
    About 3 to 3.5 m (~10-11 ft) on the two measured. Zhenhai's was about 3.2 m (~10 ft) thick at the base and about 2.56 m (~8 ft) on top; Pingyao's averages 3.5 m (~11 ft) wide.
  • How far round did a county wall run?
    Most county seats, the commonest grade of walled city, were ringed by under 2 km (~6,562 ft) of wall. Zhenhai's and Shanghai's each ran nine li round, and Pingyao's, well above the common run, 6.4 km (~20,997 ft).
  • A county wall was earth inside, and sometimes faced with masonry.
    Pingyao's is earth faced on the outside with lime-set masonry and paved on top with gray brick, with a ramp for horses and drains on its inner side. Shanghai's was rammed earth, and only later did Xu Kequ and Liu Yikuang rebuild one long stretch of it in large stone. Chinese city walls in general always had an earthen core, faced in baked brick or, where it was to hand, stone.
  • At Xi'an, a provincial capital, the wall's top is a walk between two parapets.
    The outer edge carries a crenellated parapet and the inner edge a low parapet without crenels, to keep the soldiers walking it from falling.
  • Pingyao's wall carries a tower every 50 m (~164 ft).
    It has 72 projecting platforms (mamian), each with a tower, a corner tower at each of its four corners, over 3,000 crenels, and a tower over each of its gates.
  • No page we read says whether an ordinary county wall carried towers at Pingyao's spacing.
    Pingyao's is a famous and well-kept wall, and may not be typical.
  • The county walls we read had four or six gates.
    Pingyao's and Shanghai's had six, Shanghai's with three water gates besides, and Shou county's four.
  • In rugged country a wall's line followed the ground.
    Most imperial capitals and many important northern cities had rectangular walls, but in rugged country the square gave way to an irregular line set by the terrain. A Chinese study of city shapes finds the planned capitals square, and the cities of hill country irregular, because their walls were drawn to take the surrounding heights inside.
  • Important cities often walled in far more ground than they had built.
    The room was kept for growth, and for timber and farmland in time of war.
  • Did a wall cost more the longer it ran?
    This project reasons so: the same rammed earth, facing and upkeep ran every foot of it, and a wall was kept up for centuries: the walls of Nanjing and Xi'an standing today are Ming walls with later restorations. On the same reasoning a wall was the costliest single work a town built. No page we read prices a wall, by its length or otherwise, or compares it with a town's other works.

Japan's towns took the other course.

  • In a Japanese castle town only the lord's castle was fortified.
    In the medieval and early-modern periods the castle town's residents, ordinary samurai and townsmen alike, lived outside the castle, and a Japanese encyclopedia names that as what sets the Japanese town apart from the walled city of the continent.
  • A wall round a whole Japanese town was rare.
    Town areas bounded by moats or rivers are found, but apart from Kyoto's earth rampart a wall round a whole town was rare.
  • The Japanese towns that enclosed themselves did it with earth banks and moats.
    The self-governing towns of Sakai, Hakata, Hirano and Imai ringed themselves with banks and moats, the temple towns of the Ikko sect were defended by moats and banks, and some warlords' castle towns of the Sengoku civil wars, Odawara among them, were given an outer ring of river, moat and bank. Such an outer ring took in the whole castle town with moats, stone walls or banks, and Odawara's dry moat and bank ran round its whole castle town.
  • Was the town around a jin'ya enclosed?
    The jin'ya itself - in the Edo period the seat of a lord without a castle, or the office from which a local official or a shogunal intendant governed - was in the early years often ringed with earth banks and a moat, mostly without high stone walls, and where a town grew around it, it was called a jin'ya-machi. No page we read says that town was enclosed. We guess it stood open, as a castle town stood open around its lord's castle.
  • On level ground an earth bank was often built of its own ditch's spoil.
    A bank (dorui) on level ground was made together with a ditch, and often filled with the earth dug out of it.
  • How high did a Japanese earth bank stand?
    About 2 m (~7 ft) at the least and about 3 m (~10 ft) at the most, measured from inside.
  • A bank's flat top was wide enough for men and horses.
    A wall or fence set on it left a walk inside and a ledge outside.
  • A bank's faces were held with turf, bamboo grass or bamboo.
    Some had a low course of stone at the foot.
  • Kyoto's great earth rampart, the Odoi, was the outsize case.
    Its bank and its moat were each about 20 m (~66 ft) wide.
  • No page we read gives how wide a small town's enclosing bank was at the base.
    The pages we read on the Japanese earth bank give its height, its top and its facing, but not its base.
