How our maps draw a merchant's townhouse

On a map whose settlement follows the Japanese house forms, a merchant's house is a townhouse on the street, with its rooms in a row behind the shop.

The rule the map follows:

  • A town's townhouses follow one of three plans, set by a knob.
    The plans are the three the provincial towns built: an earth-floored through passage running the house's length along one side, with the shop room, the kitchen and the formal room in a row beside it; an earth floor across the front with the rooms behind it; or the two fused.
  • The knob favors the plan with a through passage down one side the nearer a town stands to the setting's capital.
    That plan was commoner near Kyoto and rarer farther off, and the setting's capital stands in for Kyoto.
  • Every townhouse has a small light garden (tsubo-niwa) open to the sky inside it, about 36 sq ft.
    That is the one-tsubo garden of the Kyoto townhouse.
  • The storehouse, where there is one, stands at the back of the lot, never on the street.
    That was Kyoto's rule.
  • Our maps split the richest merchant houses into two buildings.
    The shop and the dwelling are drawn as two buildings with a garden between and a narrow link, as in the split form common in Kyoto. Which houses take it is a GUESS: the dictionary entry on the split form says nothing of wealth, and the maps give the form to the richest houses because a second building and a garden need the widest lot.
  • A townhouse is one story with a half-story above, and only a large merchant's has a full second story.
    Many townhouses had a low half-story for storage, and in Kyoto, through the early modern period, the full second story belonged to the large merchant houses.
