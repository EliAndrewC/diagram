How our maps draw ornamental gardens, ponds and stone lanterns (teien)

Each compound on our building plans has one ornamental garden, before the great hall, and the private rooms look onto it too. Its water is either a small pond or a dry garden of stones and white gravel standing for water, and a stone lantern or more stands in it as one small gray mark. Where the garden lies in the lot is at How our maps lay out samurai residences and their rooms (buke yashiki).

  • Did the private rooms have a garden of their own?
    Not at Takayama, the office of the shogunate's intendants and the one compound read, where one garden is seen from both the great hall and the intendant's living room, with stepping stones into it from each, and the rooms were rebuilt to an Edo-period plan. So our plans have the private rooms share the one garden, with stepping stones of their own into it. That is accurate for Takayama. Carrying it to other, humbler magistracies is a GUESS, since no office we read had a separate small garden for the private rooms.
  • A garden's water is a pond or a dry garden - a knob.
    Both forms are attested beside samurai rooms: the pond garden, built around real water, and the dry landscape, which shows water without using any. So each map takes one of the two, rolled from its seed, and both are accurate. Each map's note says which it draws.
  • How likely is a small magistracy to have a pond rather than a dry garden?
    The weight between the two forms is a GUESS, and the share each map gives them is our own; no source gives a figure to weigh them by. Which form a samurai house kept varied by place. Among the samurai-house gardens of Chiran, one is a pond garden and six are dry. At Matsushiro the garden south of a samurai house generally had a pond, with an artificial hill beyond it planted with trees and carp kept in the water, and the house of a 150-koku district magistrate there, the Yokota house, keeps its garden pond, though the page does not date it (Samurai residences and their rooms (buke yashiki)). The two offices read that had a pond were far grander than a county post, Takayama and the seat of a domain (han), but a pond was within the reach of an official of a county post's rank in their own house. So the record gives no reason to treat the pond as the grander of the two forms for a small magistracy: either is accurate for one, and how often a map takes each is our own.
  • The pond is drawn small, within sight of the rooms that face the garden.
    Its form and size are a GUESS: no page we read gives the size of a residence garden's pond.
  • Every kind of stone lantern is drawn as one small glyph.
    The lantern came in many kinds, the Kasuga, the snow-viewing (yukimi), the Enshū and the Oribe among them. Drawing them all alike is a map drawing convention.
  • How many lanterns a garden holds, and where they stand, is a GUESS.
    No page we read gives either for a residence garden.
  • Would a lantern stand in the court where guests were received?
    No page we read describes one there. Where our maps draw one, it is a GUESS borrowed from the older shrine and temple use, a single lantern at the center of the front of the shrine or temple, so it stands alone on the line of approach.
