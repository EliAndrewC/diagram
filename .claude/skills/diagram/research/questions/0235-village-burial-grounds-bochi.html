Village burial grounds (bochi)

A village's burial ground often held its dead in common: where a village kept two graves, the ground its dead lay in was often the settlement's shared graveyard. Its graves were small, the simplest of those excavated at one Edo-period temple graveyard mostly under about 5 ft long, and it filled at the pace of a village's deaths, some 18 to 30 of every thousand inhabitants a year in three Edo farming villages.

Where the two-grave custom was kept, the body went to a burial ground away from the houses, and the stone the family visited stood at a temple or a hall in the village. A modern survey of such villages found the burial grounds on average about a quarter of a mile from the houses, as far as the village could carry its dead, never above them on the village's stream. By the Edo period, six stone jizo stood in a row at a graveyard's entrance. Which ground its dead lay in is at Where a village buries its dead: its own ground, the temple yard, the fields or the home plot.

  • How much ground did a grave take?
    About 2 to 8 ft of length, the simplest graves mostly under 5 ft, at one Edo temple graveyard. At the Jisho-in site in Shinjuku, once a temple's grounds, jar burials in wooden chambers run 85 cm (~3 ft) to 1.4 m (~5 ft) along their longer axis, and the jar burials and wooden coffins of another category 65 cm (~2 ft) to 2.3 m (~8 ft), most of them under 1.4 m (~5 ft). The paper finds its wooden-coffin graves like those of village farmers.
  • How closely were graves packed?
    About one to every 4 m² (~43 sq ft), in the one excavation that gives a count. A 40 m² (~431 sq ft) trench at the site of a former temple in Higashi-Komagata, in Tokyo's Sumida ward, held the traces of ten graves, one box coffin and nine barrel coffins. At another temple site, a great many graves were made in one narrow ground. The trench is only part of the ground; its dead lay in coffins.

China measured its charity graveyards (yizhong), the grounds where the unclaimed and the poor were buried, by field area and coffin count.

  • A Qing charity graveyard buried 573 coffins in 4 mu of field.
    After Lake Tai flooded in 1670, leaving upward of ten thousand dead afloat, an endowment bought 4 mu of polder field outside Zhenze's north gate and buried 573 coffins there. At today's mu of 666.7 m² (~7,176 sq ft), which the gazetteer does not state, that is about 4.7 m² (~50 sq ft) a grave, close to the Tokyo trench's 43 sq ft.
  • How large were a county's charity graveyards?
    From 3 fen to 10 mu, in one county: Baoshan's, in a count of the Guangxu era.
  • Was a charity graveyard packed more tightly than a family grave?
    This project thinks so: the Zhenze ground was one for the drowned, while a family grave was sited by feng shui. The comparison is a GUESS.

How fast a village's ground filled depends on how many of its inhabitants died each year.

  • How many died in an Edo village each year?
    About 18 to 30 in a thousand, the higher rates in the famine decades. Three farming villages of the Nihonmatsu domain, followed in their registers from the early 1700s to 1870, died at about 30 in a thousand a year in the decade of the Tenmei famine and 29 in that of the Tenpo famine, and at about 18 in the decades after them.
  • Do the birth rates and life expectancy of Edo villages bear out those death rates?
    Roughly. Birth rates estimated from the same kind of register run from 18.4 to 31.9 in a thousand across seven villages between 1671 and 1871, while the country's surveyed population stayed nearly flat from 1721 to 1846, so deaths ran close to births. One Mino village's registers give a life expectancy at birth of about 37 years for 1773 to 1869, its infant deaths assumed rather than counted, and a population that neither grows nor shrinks loses one over its mean length of life each year - about 27 in a thousand.

How large a whole ground had to be is measured only in modern times.

  • No page we read gives a premodern Japanese burial ground's area against the inhabitants it served.
    A 1934 planner's paper sizes modern city cemeteries, and the survey of two-grave villages measures distances, not areas; the only premodern grounds read with an area and a count are China's charity graveyards.
  • How long before a grave was dug again?
    No page we read gives a term of years for a premodern ground. A 1934 planner held that where cremated remains are interred in a family urn plot, they stay for good, so such a plot is never cleared.
  • How did a modern planner, in 1934, size a city cemetery where the dead were cremated?
    By households, not by burials. A paper in Japan's landscape-architecture journal replaces a North American rule - a plot's area, times the burials a year, times the years before reuse, times a factor for paths and buildings - with one family plot for each registered household, times the same factor, for cities that inter a household's urns together. It advises a plot of 1 tsubo, about 36 sq ft, and a factor of 1.5 to 2. A 1-tsubo plot easily holds the remains of 20 to 30 dead.
  • How much ground did Tokyo's modern cemeteries give each burial?
    About half a tsubo, some 18 sq ft, on average. The 1934 paper's table gives 0.514 tsubo per interment in Tokyo's common cemeteries at the start of 1932.
  • How large were the plots in Tokyo's modern cemeteries?
    About 2 tsubo a user, some 74 sq ft: from 0.5 in one cemetery to 2.61 in another, 2.075 on average. And the site coefficient, the allowance for roads, buildings and open space, averaged 1.78 in the older five.

Where the dead had two graves, a modern survey could measure how far each lay from the houses.

  • In the two-grave custom, the body and the grave stone lay in different grounds.
    The survey defines it as a grave system in which the burial ground and the ground of the stones are separate. The Heibonsha encyclopedia sets the burial grave on riverbeds, in the hills and on beaches, and the visiting grave in the grounds of the temples and halls within the village. Mypedia puts the burial grave away from the houses, unmarked and visited only until the end of the 49 days of mourning, and the visiting grave within a house plot or a temple's grounds.
  • How old is the two-grave custom?
    Perhaps the early Edo period: the Heibonsha encyclopedia thinks so, from its tie to the spread of grave stones.
  • How far from the houses did the two graves lie?
    In 22 two-grave settlements measured today, the visiting graves lay on average about 130 m (~427 ft) from the middle of the houses, 18 of them within 200 m (~656 ft). The burial grounds lay on average about 370 m (~1,214 ft) away, and in some villages about 900 m (~2,953 ft).
  • The survey of two-grave villages reads a burial ground's distance as a compromise.
    The village wanted its dead as far off as it could, and stopped where carrying them grew too hard.
  • The survey of two-grave villages measured them as they stand today.
    Its settlements are in three regions - the Shiwaku islands of the Inland Sea, Shima and Wakasa - and many of them small fishing villages on the coast. It chose villages still in the shape they had when their two graves were made, while granting that the custom's start cannot be clearly dated.
  • Were a village's two graves always far apart?
    Often not. Across Japan the two are often quite close - side by side across a single road, the visiting grave on a small raised terrace above the burial ground, or on one piece of ground. The online encyclopedia's article on the custom gives no date or figure.
  • At Takenami, a Wakasa hamlet, a 1966 folklore record puts the burial ground at the hamlet's edge.
    The volume on the folk customs of Wakasa records it there, and the visiting graves inside the hamlet's two temples.
  • No page we read gives a distance from a village's houses to its burial ground before modern times.
    The one older rule found names none: the eighth-century Yoro code forbade burial near the capital and beside roads. Nor does any page give one for a village that buried in one ground; the two-grave survey measures only two-grave settlements.
  • In 1884 a national rule set new graveyards some 60 ken or more from houses.
    A Home Ministry instruction put a new graveyard about 60 ken, some 360 ft, or more from houses, off national and prefectural roads, railways and great rivers, on high, dry ground that does no harm to drinking water.

Along a village's stream, the same survey found an order of up and down; how near the water a ground lay is another matter.

  • In today's two-grave villages, the burial ground is never above the houses on the stream.
    The survey finds the watercourse giving the village an upper and a lower end.
  • The survey's model of a two-grave village puts the shrine at the upper end and the visiting graves with the temple.
    In the paper's model, small shrines also stand along the roads leading out of the houses and where roads cross the water.
  • Was the burial ground set downstream of the houses to keep their water clean?
    Perhaps. The surveyor suggests, hedging, that where a farming village had room, putting the burial ground downstream may also have been meant to prevent a hygiene problem.
  • Some burial grounds in today's two-grave villages lie right at the water.
    Where the burial ground lies across a stream from the houses, mainly in fishing villages, the water is its boundary, and the burial grounds by the sea stand on unstable ground that tsunami wash away.
  • Did Japan's older burial grounds lie by the water?
    Some did. In 871 the Daijokan kept the lower reaches of Kyoto's Kamo River from being turned to farmland because they were "the burial ground of the common people and a place for grazing", though most commoners of that age were laid out in the open rather than buried, so it shows where the dead were taken more than where graves were dug. The burial grave of the two-grave villages was set on riverbeds, in the hills and on beaches.
  • The only distance from water we found is a modern one.
    One Japanese city today requires a graveyard to stand 20 m (~66 ft) or more from a river or a lake. The earliest Japanese rule on water found, the 1884 instruction, gives none. No source before modern times puts a figure on how far from water a grave must lie.

How a burial ground was laid out is recorded mainly for Japan's cities.

  • The bounds of Toribeno, one of Kyoto's three great burial grounds, were never clearly defined.
    Toribeno in the east, Adashino in the west and Rendaino in the north are called the capital's three great burial grounds, and the extent of Toribeno has no clear definition.
  • Adashino, another of them, was a place of exposure burial, and in the Edo period a cremation ground.
    It was once a place of exposure burial and, in the early modern period, known with Toribeyama, "Toribe hill", as a cremation ground.
  • Osaka gathered its graveyards to the city's rim.
    In the rebuilding of Osaka under Matsudaira Tadaaki, after the war damage of the summer siege, the graveyards scattered through the city were gathered, for military reasons, to its outer rim. Seven graveyards stood around the town from the Edo period to the early Meiji period.
  • One of Osaka's rim graveyards, Umeda's, yielded more than 1,500 skeletons to excavation.
    It also yielded more than 350 ossuaries, besides grave goods and animal bones.
  • Were Japan's commoner burial grounds laid out to a surveyed plan?
    No page we read describes one that was. The nearest is the burial ground of some two-grave villages, divided into sections by household or by age, while others kept no rule at all.

In southern China, feng shui often put a family's graves on the highest hills, far from water and on otherwise worthless land, while the state buried the unclaimed and the destitute in pauper grounds of its own, proposed by Cai Jing in 1104 and kept by the Yuan and the Ming.

  • China's pauper grounds stood on high, open, barren ground, in Zhejiang one to ten li outside the county towns.
    They were generally so sited, and the Song-dynasty and Yuan gazetteers of several Zhejiang counties put them one to ten li outside the county towns, some where they "chose high, barren ground".
  • China's pauper grounds were fenced, their graves numbered, and kept by monks.
    A fence generally ran round them, each grave was numbered by the Thousand Character Classic with the dead's name, home and date, and Buddhist monks managed them.
  • No page we read says how a pauper ground's graves were arranged.
    The pages that give the fence, the numbering and the monks say nothing of rows or of ditches.

The six stone jizo at a burial ground's entrance can be dated by inscription and catalog.

  • Six stone jizo stood at a graveyard's entrance before modern times.
    The six jizo stand in a row at the entrance of a temple or a graveyard, and stone ones are said to appear at temples and graveyards from the end of the Muromachi period. Two sets at a temple gate in Hiratsuka carry inscriptions of 1753 and 1797. A city museum's exhibition of Edo-period gravestones names the six jizo at the entrances of graveyards and temples as a representative stone monument of the graveyard.
  • A set of six now standing in a graveyard at Agematsu is cataloged as work of 1678.
    The set in the Shinden graveyard at Agematsu is cataloged as Edo-period work of 1678, the oldest jizo in the town. The catalog dates the figures, not where they stood.
  • Did six jizo stand at a cremation ground?
    One undated page says so: a Takamatsu district's page on its old cremation ground says six jizo were always set at one, used "formerly". No page dated before modern times puts them at a cremation ground standing on its own.
