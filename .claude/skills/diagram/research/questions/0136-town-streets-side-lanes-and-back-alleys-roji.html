Town streets, side lanes and back alleys (roji)

The Japanese town whose street the record describes in figures is the post town, and there the street was the highway itself: the town was built along both sides of its road in narrow strip lots, and within the town the road ran 2 to 4 ken wide, about 12 to 24 ft. Behind the street fronts, the back tenements were reached by back alleys (roji) only 3 to 6 ft wide. The streets were earth, deep in mud after rain even in Edo, and a post town ran a watercourse down or beside its street, against fire and to water men and horses. At each end the road was bent twice at right angles, so that an enemy could not march straight in.

In China, the record found one popular account of a city's main street about as wide as a post town's, a stone-slab official road through a post station, a walled city's covered drains and a market town's two main streets in a T.

A city's own street plan is another topic, though its figures are drawn on here for comparison: its grid and street widths are at City streets and blocks, its share of the ground in streets at How densely a city is built, and what counts as empty ground, and where its shops and alley housing stand at how a city is zoned. A village's ways are at Village lanes, and the highway between towns at Highways and what lines them (kaido).

  • How wide was the street through a post town?
    It was the highway itself, and a Japanese reference work gives the highway within a post town as 2 to 4 ken, which it calls relatively wide. At a ken of about 6 ft that is about 12 to 24 ft. The highway's own standard, set in 1605, was about 9 m (~30 ft), cut to about 4 to 7 m (~13-23 ft) in the mountains.
  • How wide was an ordinary Chinese city's main street?
    About as wide as a post town's, in the one account found. A popular article puts an ordinary Ming or Qing city's main street at only about 4 to 8 m (~13-26 ft) wide, and its lanes under 3 m (~10 ft); it cites nothing for the figures.
  • How wide was a side street (yokocho)?
    No page we read gives the lane off a town's main street a width. All that is recorded is that it was wider than the back alley.
  • Along the street, an Edo house kept a strip about 3 ft wide by its wall, the inu-bashiri.
    A reference panel on Edo's streets says houses were required to keep one, 3 shaku, about 90 cm (~3 ft).

Behind the street fronts of an Edo commoner district stood rows of back tenements, and the way into them was not a street but the back alley.

  • How wide was a back alley (roji)?
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on the back alley puts the alley between the rows of back tenements at about 3 to 6 shaku, 0.9 to 1.8 m (~3-6 ft).
  • The back alley opened between the merchant houses fronting the main street.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on the back alley places its entrance between one merchant house and the next in the row along the main street.
  • A gate with doors stood at the back alley's mouth, opened at dawn and shut at dusk.
    The gate was called a roji kido or nagaya kido, and it was shut at about 6 p.m.
  • The back alley was a private passage, used by hardly anyone but those who lived on it.
    It was narrower than a side street, and almost no one used it but those connected with the buildings along it.

Underfoot, a town's streets were earth, though an official road in China could be paved where it ran through a post station.

  • Even the shogun's capital went unpaved.
    A writer of about 1630 complained that Edo's streets, after a little rain, were deep in mud and hard to travel, and its inhabitants walked them in high clogs.
  • In China, an official road could be stone-paved through a post station.
    The 1.7 km (~5,577 ft) that survives at Yunnanyi in Yunnan is a road of bluestone slabs, built to about one zhang wide, enough for two horses to pass.
  • No page we read says whether an ordinary Chinese town's own streets were paved.
    The one paved way found is the Yunnanyi official road.
  • In the setting, the Imperial roads, whose stretch through a town is its street, are kept to a deliberately heavy standard, stonework among it.
    Their upkeep pays for specialized labor, stone-cutting among it.

A town street carried water, in one of three forms: an open watercourse, an open ditch at the house fronts, or a drain covered over.

  • A post town laid a watercourse down the middle of its street or along its edge.
    It was there for fire protection and to water men and horses. How wide it ran, no page we read gives.
  • In Edo, an open ditch about 9 in wide ran along the front streets under the eaves.
    It took the rain off the eaves, and before a house it was 7 to 8 sun, 23 to 26 cm (~9-10 in), wide, one stride over.
  • The back tenements drained into the ditch outside, and that ditch into the front street's drain.
    A tenement's kitchen water ran by a trough or a bamboo pipe to the ditch outside, the well's drainage too, and that ditch flowed on into the front street's drain.
  • The back alley carried its own small ditch (dobu), under boards down its middle.
    It was 6 to 7 sun, 18 to 21 cm (~7-8 in), wide.
  • A Chinese walled city could put its drains underground.
    Yichang, in the Tongzhi reign (1862-1874), had five main drains and a number of small ones, every one covered, built of brick or stone with stone lids that carts, horses and pedestrians crossed.

A post town was laid along its one road, but a market town could grow a second main street, and the one planned grid found is a city's.

  • A post town was built along its highway, its lots in narrow strips down both sides.
    The lots were planned, of nearly constant depth and differing frontage.
  • At Oiso, a post town on the Tokaido, fewer than half the houses stood along the highway.
    In 1803, 269 of its 605 houses stood on strip lots, eave to eave, along both sides of it. Where the rest stood, and by what lanes they were reached, the page does not say.
  • A Chinese market town could have two main streets meeting in a T.
    By the early Qing, Zhouzhuang, a market town of Kunshan, had grown around its Fu'an bridge into a busy town of two main streets, its north-south market street and its middle market street, in a T. A county seat's cross or T of streets from gate to gate is at How densely a city is built, and what counts as empty ground.
  • Was a town laid out in a planned grid of blocks?
    The one planned grid found is a city's. The commoner land that the shogunate laid out in Edo from Nihonbashi to Kyobashi was cut into blocks 60 ken, about 120 m (~394 ft), square, with an open lot in the middle and town lots 20 ken deep along the streets. That is a city's grid.
  • No page we read counts the side lanes a town ran off its street.
    The pages we read on post towns, castle towns and two Chinese market towns give no count of the lanes off the main street.

The road did not run straight into a town.

  • At both ends of a post town, the highway was bent into a crank (masugata).
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on the post station defines the masugata as the place at both ends where the highway was bent into a crank. A post town was built to serve in part as a fortress, and its masugata bent the road twice at right angles so that an enemy would find it hard to get in.
  • On the Five Highways, a post town's ends were marked by banks or walls (mitsuke) and gates.
    The mitsuke was built of stone walls or earth banks, and gates stood at the ends near it, the stretch between them counting as the post town.
  • A castle town lined its main road with houses and bent it, to hide its castle and lengthen the way in.
    Past its edge the main road was lined with houses leaving no gaps, so that the castle was hard to see, and bent into a key shape or given dead ends, lengthening the way to the castle.
