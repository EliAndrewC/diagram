How our maps draw the magistrate's manor on a town map

A town map draws its magistrate's compound as walls, a gate and an empty court - a simplification of the compound, not a picture of it shrunk to scale; the compound's buildings, yards and boards are drawn on a map of the manor's own. A town is drawn on a Japanese or a Chinese model. On the Japanese model the manor stands at the town's edge, with its gate facing the town or the road it fronts; on the Chinese model it stands inside the town on the main avenue. Where nothing else decides, the gate faces south.

  • The manor is drawn as a plain walled box, by convention.
    This is a drawing convention, not a plan of any one seat. Real seats were not one plan: jin'ya differed in what they held and in how strongly they were enclosed, so no one plan drawn small would be truer to them.
  • The box need not match the manor's own map.
    Its shape, its proportions and its size on the town map may all differ from the manor's own map, and a difference between the two is the convention working, not an error.
  • What the manor's own map draws outside the walls may be left off the town map.
    Notice boards at the gate, a bounty board, an approach road forking to a cart gate or a parley door need not appear.
  • The box is presumed to hold everything the manor's own map shows, drawn or not.
    Nothing it is presumed to contain is drawn a second time beside it.
  • A county seat draws no separate granary: its tax rice is presumed stored inside the manor.
    That is the historical placement at a Japanese intendant's seat. An intendant's jin'ya was at once their residence, office, court and tax store, and the intendancy at Takayama kept its numbered earth-walled storehouses inside its own grounds. Our maps carry the same rule to a county seat on the Chinese model, as this project's choice.
  • A rice-transit town draws a storehouse row of its own.
    It is the one exception to the granary rule on our maps, our own reading rather than a placement a source gives.

Where the manor stands follows the model the town is drawn on; which way its gate faces follows what it fronts.

  • Where does the manor stand in a town drawn on the Japanese model?
    At the town's edge. Many Japanese seats were built beside a settlement already there, and this project reads that as a seat at the town's edge rather than in its middle.
  • On the Chinese model the manor stands inside the town, on the main avenue.
    The county yamen of Neixiang, in China, stands on East Main Street at the center of its town. What else such a town holds is at How our maps populate and furnish each kind of town.
  • The gate faces what the manor fronts: the town, or the road beside it.
    No one compass direction serves every manor, because the answer depends on where the town lies. Of the three manors our maps draw so far, one faces west to the hamlet below it, another south down its hill to the town, and the third fronts a diagonal road with its gate opening onto the roadbed. No page we read says which way a Japanese seat's gate faced. This rule is our own, fitted to those three maps.
  • A manor fronting a road at an angle is turned to it.
    The whole compound is turned so that its front wall runs parallel to the roadbed and its gate opens onto the road, as on the third of our drawn manors. Like the gate's facing, this is our own choice fitted to that map, not taken from a source.
  • Where nothing else decides it, the gate faces south.
    South is the formal orientation a Chinese local government office took by regulation and the ritual code: the seat to the north facing south, the court in front and the sleeping quarters behind. The maps use it on both models, though the regulation is Chinese, not Japanese.
  • When turning a manor throws a caste's household count out of its set range, the map is redrawn from a new seed.
    Each town map holds the households of each caste within a set range. Turning the compound is cheap to draw and expensive downstream: it changes the ground the manor covers, which reshuffles the seeded blocks of housing around it. The fix used is to try new seeds until the counts land back in range, and each of our denser town maps and our city map carries a hand-picked seed for this reason. Hand-correcting the block counts instead chases its own tail - each correction moves the next block - and the map stops being the output of its own seed.
