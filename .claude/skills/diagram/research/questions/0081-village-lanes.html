Village lanes

A village lane was a track of bare earth, trodden hard by feet and hooves and turned to mud by rain. It was made for people on foot, porters with carrying poles, wheelbarrows and packhorses rather than carts. Neither China's countryside, outside its eastern plains, nor Japan's was laid out for the cart: a load went by barrow, on a person's back or on a horse's.

In a clustered village the lanes ran among the house plots to the doors; out among the paddies the way ran on along the bunds between the fields. No page we read gives a measured width for an ordinary village lane; the one width given for a field road before the Meiji years is 3 shaku, about 3 ft.

  • China's rural ways were built for the wheelbarrow, not the cart.
    Its countryside was served by narrow paths scarcely wide enough for walkers and wheelbarrows, with rough unpaved cart tracks predominating only on the eastern plains. The well-paved paths the same article describes were suited to the traffic that used them: walkers, porters with carrying poles, pushers of wheelbarrows, and bearers carrying passengers in seats slung from poles.
  • How wide a track did that traffic need?
    One barrow or one packhorse wide, in this record's reading - a GUESS. The porter carried the load hung from both ends of a pole on his shoulder, and the packhorse went where the lack of roads kept wheels away; neither page gives a width.

Japan shows the same thing from the other side.

  • Japan had no horse-drawn carriages before 1868, in the Edo period.
    People went on foot or on horseback, and goods moved by human power or on a horse's back.
  • Hand carts were kept to the cities and barred from the highways.
    They were confined to the wide, level streets inside the cities, and on the highways their use was forbidden to the end of the shogunate, to keep the post-horse system alive: more vehicles, it was feared, would mean fewer horses. The Edo hand cart, the daihachiguruma, was limited by law to Edo, Owari (Nagoya) and Sunpu (Shizuoka), and was not allowed on the Tokaido before the Meiji period, which began in 1868.
  • The hand cart spread beyond the three cities allowed it, but never onto the highways.
    An 1815 report already had it in use in Kyoto, Osaka and the castle towns of other provinces, and late in the Edo period it spread beyond the castle towns as well, though the Japanese reference dictionary that says so does not say where to, and the highway ban stood to the end.
  • The hand carts did the road little harm, and Japan never needed carriage roads.
    A 1988 Japanese paper on the history of Japan's roads finds that the slow, light carts a person could push did the road little damage, and that since the cart never became the main way of moving goods, Japan had no need of roads built to stand up to carriages.
  • The same paper puts Japan's never taking up the carriage down to its terrain, climate and horses.
    The causes it gives for Japan's taking up riding but never the carriage are its terrain and climate, the kind of horse, and attitudes toward the horse, among others.
  • Heavy freight went by water.
    Water carries heavy goods in bulk far more efficiently than land, and in Edo Japan almost all goods went by ship, rice included. This setting differs, putting carts and wagons on the roads between its towns (Moving goods: carts, packhorses and river landings (kashi)).

How wide the ways were is known for the highways and for one cart road, but not for the lanes themselves.

  • How wide was a village lane?
    No page we read gives a figure. A search of twenty-three pages found no measured width for an ordinary village lane or farm path in any language. A figure that circulates as "a rural way is one ken (about 6 ft) wide" traces to a page that does not say it, and neither the Japanese article on rural ways nor a prefecture's own list of questions about them gives a width. In neither country was an ordinary village lane a wide, two-lane road: China's rural paths were scarcely wide enough for walkers and wheelbarrows, and Japan, where the cart never became the main way of moving goods, had no need of roads built for carriages.
  • The one width given for a field road before the Meiji years is 3 shaku, about 3 ft.
    It comes from a table of road classes the national road ministry gives from the "hundred articles" it calls "said to be" the testament of Tokugawa Ieyasu, the first Edo shogun. No readable copy of the hundred articles carrying that article was found, and no other page gives an older width for a field road. The farm roads of a paddy replanned in 1869, as the Meiji years began, were 1 ken, about 6 ft, wide - a planned layout, not an old way measured (Bunds between the paddies (aze)).
  • How far can the field road's 3 shaku be trusted?
    Not far: it is a traditional figure of uncertain date, not a measured one. The hundred articles exist in several versions and were plainly added to after Ieyasu's death, though much of them is thought to reflect his principles, and they were at least partly drafted by his successors.
  • Japan's ox-cart stone road was about 9 ft wide.
    The ox-cart stone route was typically 2.7 m (~9 ft) wide - 9 shaku - with a 90 cm (~3 ft) track down its middle for the oxen. It sets a ceiling for a lane rather than a norm.
  • How wide were the trunk highways?
    Wider again, though the readable accounts bracket the width rather than agree on it. One gives the Five Highways about 5.4 to 7.2 m (~18-24 ft), 3 to 4 ken, as the ordinary width, mountain roads left out, and 9 m (~30 ft), 5 ken, close to Edo. Another gives from 4 ken, 7.2 m (~24 ft), up to 7 or 8 ken, 12.6 to 14.4 m (~41-47 ft), in places. In the mountains they narrowed: the standard width of about 9 m (~30 ft) set in 1605 came down to about 4 to 7 m (~13-23 ft) in mountain areas, and at passes such as Hakone, Utsunoya and Suzuka the road was 2 ken, 3.6 m (~12 ft).

What a traveler walked on followed the same split between the lane and the highway.

  • In Japan a village lane was bare trodden earth, and mud in the rain.
    In Japan, until modern times, roads trodden hard by people and horses were the norm, and gravel roads were limited to main roads. A road in its simplest state is a strip worn bare by feet, which rain turns to mud.
  • Japan's highways were dressed with gravel and sand, but never everywhere.
    The Edo road works hardened the highway by spreading gravel and sand, but the dressing was never carried through everywhere, and in rain travelers walked the mud in high clogs. An order of 1612 told those responsible to spread sand and stone where puddles and mud formed. The two government pages that summarize it word its clause on the road's sides differently: letting water run off at the side, or dampening the sides and hardening them.
  • In Japan the villages along a highway kept it up.
    The road magistrate gave the highway's upkeep to the post towns and villages along it, and where traffic was heavy to villages off the road as well, set as the towns and villages within about 20 km (~12 miles) of the highway.
  • Japan's shogunate paved its highways' difficult stretches with stone.
    The shogunate ordered the old highways' difficult stretches paved with stone, and on the old Tokaido some of it survives at Hakone and Kikugawa; a record says the Hakone mountain road was repaired and paved with round stones in 1863.
  • Stone for wheels was laid on the haulage roads into Kyoto.
    On the roads from Fushimi and Otsu, thick granite cart-stones were laid in two wheel tracks, and gravel was spread on the ox path between them - the ox-cart stone route whose width is given above.
  • Did China pave more of its ways than Japan?
    Probably, in this record's reading, a GUESS: China had many narrow stone-slab causeways, above all in the wet rice south, where a paved way keeps porters and barrows out of the mud, while Japan paved little beyond its steep highway passes and the one ox-cart route. No page we read sets the two side by side.
  • No page we read says a village lane climbed a slope by stone steps.
    That one did would be a GUESS.

Inside a clustered village, the lanes ran among the houses themselves.

  • Was every house in a clustered village reached by a lane?
    Not always. No page we read states it as a rule. In Japan, a household whose land had no way of its own to the road crossed a neighbor's land by custom: an edition of local customs published in 1892 reports it from seven provinces, sometimes for a rent or a share of the upkeep, and passage to a neighbor's well from three more. Most speak of plots or land, not houses, and three hold in towns; the one that names a house, in Kaga, is a town's. In Echigo the way ran on across a chain of plots: the farther of two plots shut off from the road crossed both plots in front of it, and paid its rent only to the one next to it. In Enoshima, a village crowded into a ravine, the houses behind its street were reached by the narrowest of alleys. A survey of a traditional Manchu village in northeast China quotes an earlier book on the villages of the northern plain region, with two or three main streets running east and west and supplemental roads running north and south, and measured small blind alleys of 2 to 4 m (~7-13 ft) running off the streets to reach the houses.
  • Did side lanes branch off a main lane to reach the houses behind?
    The Manchu village's blind alleys branch off its streets to reach its houses, though the paper does not say they serve a back row, and in Enoshima the narrowest of alleys led to the houses behind its street. None of the general articles we read describes side lanes serving a back row of houses.
  • Why were the side lanes narrow and irregular?
    Because the houses beside them took them over as space of their own, in this record's reading - a GUESS. The one readable case is Shanghai's lane housing of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, a city form, where every house faces a lane and the lanes become space all the residents use.

Planned villages in medieval England had a second way to reach the backs of their plots.

  • In planned medieval English villages, a back lane ran behind the house plots.
    It ran parallel to the main street at the far end of the plots, framed the village with the street in a rectangle, and often divided the village from its farmland.
  • The surveyed Manchu village in northeast China is not evidence of a back lane.
    The villages of the northern plain region, in the book the paper quotes, line their houses closely along both sides of the street, and the ground behind this village's house lots was opened largely when outlying farmers were gathered into the village around 1968.
  • What do a back lane and side lanes off a main lane each say about how a village grew?
    A back lane implies planning: someone laid out the framework at once, and the plots are regular. Side lanes off a main lane imply growth over time: each household cut its own way to the road, and the result is irregular. This is this record's reading of the two forms, a GUESS, not a finding of any one source.

Not every hamlet was clustered.

  • How were the farmhouses of a dispersed hamlet reached?
    In a dispersed settlement the farmhouses stand scattered among broad fields, each reached from the field paths, in this record's reading - a lane to the hamlet and nothing between the farmhouses.

Where a lane ran, and how it bent, followed the ground and the people walking it.

  • Among the fields, the path ran on the bunds between the plots.
    A bund that also served as a path around the paddies was called an azemichi, or nawate; the bund itself is heaped up from the paddy's mud on the boundary between one paddy and the next, so the path runs on the plot boundary, a narrow path between one field and another.
  • A 1988 wilderness guide says as few as fifteen passages can wear a path.
    As few as fifteen passages over a site can make a distinct trail, which then draws more use; the figure is from Hampton and Cole's Soft Paths (1988).
  • A worn path usually takes the shortest or the easiest route.
    Such a path usually represents the shortest or most easily walked route, though not always a straight one: it may take a shortcut or a less steep course. A switchback within a few paces is neither, so in this record's reasoning no one walks it and it is never worn.
  • Walkers in a London study were taken to prefer the route that turns least.
    Pedestrian movement across four areas of London correlates well with an angular measure of route choice, walkers being taken to prefer the fewest turns or the least change of angle.
  • Did a lane bend at the corners of the house plots?
    In this record's reading, yes - a GUESS: if the lanes are the gaps left between the house plots, a lane runs straight between plot corners and bends at one, and a bend has a reason on the ground, a fence, a bed or a yard. No page we read describes lanes as the gaps between plots.
  • No page we read says how far a lane ran past its last farmhouse.
    Nor does any say whether a lane stopped at the last dooryard or ran on to the fields.
