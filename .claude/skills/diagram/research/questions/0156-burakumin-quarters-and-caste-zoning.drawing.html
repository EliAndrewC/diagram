How our maps zone the castes

Every town and city on our maps keeps its castes apart. The burakumin were the outcastes who handled the dead, the hides and the punishments, and the lords seem generally to have housed these leather workers at the edge of the castle town.

So a town's burakumin quarter stands at the settlement's edge in ground of its own, held off its neighbors by a band of open ground. It holds the setting's own count of households, from a dozen in a county seat to over a hundred in a capital. A walled city keeps at least one of its burakumin neighborhoods inside the wall; the poor lodge in a quarter of their own; and the commoners' blocks are closed by gates at night.

The rule the map follows: at town scale a burakumin quarter stands off every other dwelling by 60 ft of open ground, measured wall to wall, and that gap is held open before anything else is allowed to grow into it. A walled provincial city draws at least one burakumin neighborhood inside its walls.

  • A quarter holds the setting's count: 12 households in a county seat, 30 in a provincial city, 120 in a capital.
    The counts are the setting's own. The history counts one great quarter and whole regions but no small town's, so the setting's 12 stands on the setting alone.
  • Each of a city's quarters draws one religious building of its own, a shrine or a temple hall, rolled per settlement.
    Both are attested, so which one is a knob: a small shrine to the quarter's tutelary god, as many quarters kept the Hakusan god, or a small Jodo Shinshu temple hall, the commonest sect among them.
  • Would a county seat's quarter of twelve households keep a shrine of its own?
    Our maps give it one, a small shrine rather than a temple hall. Many quarters kept a shrine to their tutelary god, but the sources speak of the quarters in general, never of one of twelve households, so that so small a quarter kept even that is a GUESS.
  • The quarter's dwellings are small houses, packed close.
    The packing follows the history: the 232 houses of the outcastes' enclosure at Asakusa in Edo were crowded together. That the houses are small is a GUESS, for want of any page that describes a quarter's houses.
  • The quarter draws its water where its neighbors do.
    This is a GUESS, for want of any page that describes a quarter's well.

Where a town's quarter stands follows the sites the record gives.

  • A town's quarter stands at the settlement's edge, on a road where it leaves the town or on the riverbank below it, rolled per settlement.
    Both forms are attested, so the choice is a knob. The lords seem generally to have housed their leather workers at the edge of the castle town, at a key point of traffic or on a riverbank; Matsumoto gathered its kawata at the castle town's entrances and Takada set its quarter along the Kaga road at the town's edge, while Kyoto's oldest quarter was a village of riverbank outcastes.
  • The quarter shares its side of the town with the tanning yard and the execution ground.
    Their bearings are set at How our maps site tanning yards.
  • Does a town's quarter take the worse ground at its edge?
    On our maps it does, by a degree rolled per settlement rather than a fixed rule: where the edge offers better and worse ground, the quarter takes the low, wet or cut-off ground. It is this project's reading of the poor sites many quarters sit on, not anything a source states of one town.

The gap of open ground is what makes a town's quarter read as segregated rather than merely crowded.

  • Why do our maps keep 60 ft of open ground around a town's quarter?
    Dwellings inside a quarter stand 10 to 30 ft apart on these maps, so the gap must be several times that to read as a gap rather than a wide lane. The figure is a calibration against the drawn maps, not a finding: the historical outcaste hamlet stood apart from the peasants' plots within the village's own territory, at no fixed distance out.
  • The quarter's 60 ft gap marks who lives beside whom; it is no buffer against pollution.
    It is well below the 120 ft a tanning yard keeps from dwellings: it measures neighbors, not how far a pollution keeps off, and is not a buffer against kegare.
  • The quarter's 60 ft gap is measured wall to wall, and held open before the farmland around it is laid.
    Both are needed. A gap measured between house centers counts a house clear while its wall stands inside the gap, and farmland laid around a finished quarter closes in on ground nothing held open. Miss either and farmhouses stand within feet of the huts, and a quarter that runs on into the farms around it is not a segregated quarter at all.
  • A city's quarters keep no gap of open ground around them.
    A city zones its quarters by ward, wall to wall, so its segregation is the ward rather than the gap, and the seams between neighboring quarters on the drawn cities run about 30 ft.

A walled city's quarter departs from the history on purpose.

  • A walled provincial city keeps at least one burakumin neighborhood inside its walls, a deliberate deviation.
    The burakumin handle the dead, the hides and the punishments - and a city shut up under siege needs those hands as much as its granary. The history puts the quarters at the edge, and nothing found ties a quarter's place to a siege: the neighborhood inside the wall, and its reason, are this setting's own.
  • A walled city's burakumin need not all live inside the wall.
    The neighborhood inside is the least our maps draw; rows outside the wall are usual on our maps, as the history puts the quarters at the town's edge.

The other castes are zoned by the same maps.

  • A city's flophouse stands in its poor quarter, on the road in.
    So it was in Osaka's Nagamachi, where the poor quarter and the road out to Sakai were one place.

  • A small provincial city closes its commoner blocks with kido gates, not walled wards.
