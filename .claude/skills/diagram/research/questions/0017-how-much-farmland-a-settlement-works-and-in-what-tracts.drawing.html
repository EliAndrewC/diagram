How our maps size a settlement's farmland

Our maps size a village's paddy by the mouths it feeds: roughly a quarter of an acre of paddy for each inhabitant, so a village of a few hundred works some 70 to 90 acres, with its dry fields drawn beyond the paddy, at the margins. How large a single field is drawn is set by water, terrain and that population, never by whether the settlement is a hamlet, a village or a town. A field that runs off the map may show any slice of it, one enclosed on the map is never drawn under about 8 acres, and a pond is sized to the paddy it waters.

  • A village of 350 draws 69 to 86 acres of paddy.
    At 0.8 to 1.0 tan (~0.20-0.25 acre) of paddy for each inhabitant, 350 inhabitants come to 280 to 350 tan. On our map of Kikuta, the paddy comes to about 79 acres in about 600 plots.
  • How much paddy does a farm household work on our maps?
    About 1 to 2.5 acres, the band the maps are measured against. At the setting's household of about five (The five sizes of settlement), 0.8 to 1.0 tan an inhabitant comes to 4 or 5 tan, about 1 to 1.2 acres, and an eyewitness account of Japanese farming puts all the land one family worked nearer 2.5 acres.
  • How small can a field with its own weir and canals be?
    About 7 to 20 acres, the paddy of a handful of households working together; one enclosed on the map is still never drawn under 8. A comb fan is shared waterworks - a weir, a head race, a fork into supply canals, tapering ditches to the plots and a drain - and in Japan a river's water came down a main canal and lesser canals to each paddy, the smallest ditches run by small groups of farmers. At 1 to 2.5 acres a household, that is the size of a hamlet's paddy.
  • A single household's ground gets no weir of its own.
    A household's 1 to 2.5 acres is watered from the smallest ditches, which small groups of farmers ran, so this project draws no weir and fork for it.
  • A hamlet is drawn as a small tract of paddy with its farmhouses around it.
    It is one field shared by its handful of households, not a scatter of single-household plots.

No kind of settlement has a cap, but what sets how small a field may be drawn is whether it runs off the map or is enclosed on it.

  • A field enclosed on the map is at least 8 acres.
    A field that does not run off the map reads as a complete system, so it is never smaller, and it is that small only on a map that shows why there is no more ground to water: hayfields, grazing, forest or slope, and a thin ring of farms near the town - as the Kiso valley's farmland was held to its narrow flats by the mountains.
  • On an ordinary town map, the enclosed field is at least about 20 acres.
    It is drawn as a modest enclosed field plus fields running off the map, since a town sits amid the countryside it serves (Farmland around towns and cities).
  • A city's fields run off the map.
    The cut edge says the field goes on, so the small acreage on the sheet is honest. A field running off the map is exempt from the 8-acre floor, which bites exactly where a field reads as whole.
  • A town's other farmers work the fields off the map.
    An enclosed field's ring of farmhouses reads as 1 to 2.5 acres a household for the farms around it, and the rest of a town's farmers are taken to work the fields beyond the sheet, which is why every town map shows a field running off its edge (How our maps draw the farmland around a town or a city).
