How our maps draw doors

A door on our building plans is a small dark block set on the wall it opens through, and its width is a drawing convention, not a measurement. On our three hand-drawn magistracy sheets - the plans of the Ochiba, Hayakawa and Ubame magistrates' compounds - the doors of the kitchens, lodgings, barracks and servants' rows are drawn mostly two to three times the width of a real ordinary door, and most of them wider than a farmhouse's one-ken main door, so that they read at the sheet's scale.

  • How wide was a real ordinary door?
    About 3 ft, half a ken at most. This is this project's reading of a source that defines a big door as one wider than half a ken.
  • A farmhouse's main door was about 6 ft wide.
    It was a sliding door one ken wide, about 6 ft, with a wicket set in it.
  • How wide are the doors on the three magistracy sheets?
    From about 3.3 ft to 10 ft. The kitchen doors and the doors of the lodgings, the barracks and the servants' rows are mostly about 8 to 8.7 ft, and Hayakawa's servants' door 5.3 ft. The storehouse doors are about 4.7 ft. The chief retainer's side door is 3.3 ft at Ochiba and Ubame but 8.7 ft at Hayakawa, and Hayakawa's guest-quarters entry 10 ft. The writing pavilion's door is 6 ft and each parley-room door about 7.3 ft.
  • A plan our program generates gives each lodging block a door 6 ft wide.
    It follows the hand-drawn sheets' convention of a door drawn wider than a real ordinary one. It is set flush inside the building's wall, as they set one, and is 1.33 ft deep, the thickness they draw a door.
  • Our storehouses' paired doors are accurate for the end of the Edo period.
    Paired plastered door leaves are attested at the end of the Edo period.
  • How wide was each of a storehouse's paired doors?
    Their width is known only as today's standard of about 3.5 ft each. No drawn storehouse door is set from that figure, so the drawn width stays a convention with no premodern size behind it.
  • A door glyph sits flush to the wall it opens through.
    A door is an opening in a wall, so it is drawn touching the wall it opens through, never floating just inside it, and a storehouse's door is drawn as one block for its pair of leaves.
