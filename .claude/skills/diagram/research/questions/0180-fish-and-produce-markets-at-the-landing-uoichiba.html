Fish and produce markets at the landing (uoichiba)

Edo and Osaka sold their fresh food where the boats brought it in. Edo's fish market grew up at the landing on the Nihonbashi river, because that was the handiest place to land the catch, and an 1842 plan of it, from late in the Edo period, shows a dozen piers and fifty sheds along the bank, and over a hundred shopfronts and stalls. Osaka kept its fish, its rice and its produce at three separate markets.

  • Edo's fish market centered on the landing on the north bank of the Nihonbashi river.
    The ground facing the landing was the core of the market, because it was the handiest place to land goods.
  • A plan of Edo's fish market from late in the Edo period shows piers, sheds, wells and stalls along the landing.
    The plan, of 1842, records twelve piers at the bank's edge along the landing, from the Nihonbashi bridge to the Edobashi bridge. Behind them stood fifty sheds: back sheds along the bank for holding the catch, and front sheds for selling it. The plan also records twenty-five wells on the city's piped water supply, and 126 shopfronts and display stalls pushing out into the street. Many of the stalls were only 3 shaku (about 3 ft) across.
  • Edo's fish sellers laid the catch out on flat boards (ita-bune).
    Each board was about 70 cm (~2 ft) wide and 150 cm (~5 ft) long.
  • Were the narrow stalls of Edo's fish market packed with these boards?
    The 1842 plan does not show the boards. But many of its stalls were only 3 shaku (about 3 ft) across, and from that Chuo ward, the Tokyo district where the market stood, judges that the boards were lined up with no room to spare.
  • Osaka kept its fish, rice and produce at three separate markets.
    A Japanese encyclopedia names the Zakoba as one of Osaka's three great markets, with the Dojima rice market and the Tenma produce market.
  • Osaka's fish market, the Zakoba, stood on a canal bank near the river mouth.
    It stood on the east bank of the upper Hyakkenbori canal, near the river mouth where the city's two great waterways divide, which favored bringing the fish in.
  • Osaka's fish market, the Zakoba, met three times a day.
    It met morning, noon and evening.
  • Osaka's river fish had a market of their own, at the Kyobashi bridgehead.
    The same encyclopedia entry names a river-fish market at the head of the Kyobashi bridge, apart from the Zakoba.
  • Osaka's Tenma produce market stood along the Okawa river.
    The Japanese encyclopedia article on the market places it there.
  • A Japanese dictionary says fish-market landings (uogashi) are found all over the country.
    It defines an uogashi as a landing holding a fish market that wholesales to the trade, though it gives no date.
  • No page we read says how a smaller city's market was laid out.
    Every description we found is of Edo's or Osaka's.
