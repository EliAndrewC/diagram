City moats: how wide and deep, and whether they ring the wall (hori, chenghe)

A walled Chinese city was ringed outside its wall by a moat, so regularly that the written word chengchi names the wall and the moat together. A Song military compendium wanted it dug wide at the top and narrow at the bottom, down to the water, and wide enough that arrows and thrown stones could hardly reach across. A Ming military treatise wanted one at least about 35 ft across, and the moats that survive run from about 11 ft wide at Qufu to about 100 ft at Jingzhou. A few are broad lakes hundreds of feet across.

In Japan a moat (hori) was dug round a castle and round the towns that enclosed themselves, and a river already flowing past could serve as a natural moat.

  • Did a Chinese moat run all the way round its wall?
    Every county wall we read has a moat, and where a page says, it ran all the way round. Shanghai's moat ran round outside its county wall, Kaifeng's surrounds the old city, Guide's - the old prefectural city at Shangqiu - runs round the city, and Xi'an's surrounds its wall and, holding water all year, forms with the wall a complete defensive system. How long Xi'an's moat runs, and how it is fed, no page we read says. At Beijing the moat ran outside the wall, and when the outer city's wall was built in the sixteenth century a moat was dug with it; its water came from springs in the hills to the northwest and drained to the Tonghui River.
  • At Zhenhai, the side the sea guarded had no moat.
    The county town of Zhenhai dug none on its north, where the wall faced the sea.
  • Pingyao stands by two rivers and still has a moat of its own.
    The town lies on the east bank of the Fen River, and its south wall was built along the Zhongdu River, which is why that wall winds; the moat lies just outside the walls. Neither article that says so calls the moat a complete circuit.
  • How far out from the wall was the moat dug?
    The Northern Song's military compendium digs it about thirty paces from the main wall, with a drawbridge over it. Measured today, Qufu's moat stands 7 to 10 m (~23-33 ft) out from the wall, and Guide's 3.5 m (~11 ft).
  • The Northern Song's military compendium fixes no width: the ground decides.
    The width at the surface follows the ground, wide or narrow; the moat is wide at the top and narrow at the bottom, deep enough to reach water, and the digging stops when arrows and thrown stones can hardly reach across.
  • How wide and deep did the Ming treatise Wubei Zhi want a moat?
    At least about 35 ft wide and 15 to 21 ft deep. The treatise, quoted in a modern article, sets a floor of three zhang five chi wide, the wider the better, and one and a half or two zhang deep. The Ming foot (chi) was 0.30 to 0.32 m (~12-13 in), and taking ten of them to the zhang, as a later Republican table does (this page's assumption for the Ming), gives the feet.
  • Pingyao's moat was about 10 ft wide and deep when the town was rebuilt early in the Ming, in 1370.
    That is well under the Ming treatise's floor of about 35 ft; moats built in the period ran both below and above it. When the county town was rebuilt and enlarged in that year its moat was one zhang both deep and wide.
  • How wide were the older and larger cities' moats?
    The figures disagree or cannot be dated. The Western Han capital's moat is 8 m (~26 ft) wide and 3 m (~10 ft) deep in one encyclopedia and 45 m (~148 ft) wide and 4.5 m (~15 ft) deep in another. Zhengding, a prefectural seat in the Ming, is given a moat over ten zhang wide and over two deep, about 100 ft by 20, in a sentence that names no period or survey. No gazetteer's figure for a larger city's moat could be read.
  • How wide are the moats that survive today?
    Among these, the width follows the size of the city. Pingyao's is 4 m (~13 ft) wide and 4 m (~13 ft) deep, and Qufu's about 3.3 m (~11 ft) wide and deep. The great cities' are five to ten times as wide: Kaifeng's 16.66 m (~55 ft) wide and 3.33 m (~11 ft) deep, Xi'an's 18 to 20 m (~59-66 ft) wide, and Jingzhou's 30 m (~98 ft) wide with 4 m (~13 ft) of water. Beijing's is given as 30 to 60 m (~98-197 ft) wide in one sentence of its article, while the same article puts the inner city's moat between about 10 m (~33 ft) at its narrowest and 30 to 50 m (~98-164 ft) at its widest, and the outer city's narrower and shallower. Most are 3 to 4 m (~10-13 ft) deep, whatever the width.
  • How wide and deep is Xi'an's moat?
    The accounts differ. The English Wikipedia article gives 18 m (~59 ft) wide and 6 m (~20 ft) deep, while a survey of surviving walls and the Chinese Wikipedia article give 20 m (~66 ft) wide and 10 m (~33 ft) deep. None says which survey it follows.
  • How wide was a walled county town's moat?
    It varied widely, from Pingyao's 13 ft to Shanghai's, which was 6 zhang wide and 1 zhang 7 chi deep, about 63 ft and 18 ft at a zhang of 3.2 m (~10 ft) (this page's conversion).
  • Shanghai's moat was joined to the town's water gates and to the river's tides.
    It connected with the water gates, for boats, and with the tides of the Huangpu River.
  • One town's moat changed width from side to side.
    Zhenhai's was about 16 m (~52 ft) wide on the east, 14.2 m (~47 ft) on the south and 41.6 m (~136 ft) on the west, and 6.4 m (~21 ft) deep throughout.
  • Some moats are broad lakes rather than ditches.
    Xiangyang's moat reaches 250 m (~820 ft) at its widest, 130 to 250 m (~427-820 ft) wide and only 2 to 3 m (~7-10 ft) deep. Guide's, at Shangqiu, runs from 25 m (~82 ft) wide at its narrowest to 500 m (~1,640 ft) at its widest, 1 to 5 m (~3-16 ft) deep. They are shallower than the dug ditch.
  • We guess that low, wet ground beside a great river made Xiangyang's and Guide's moats so wide.
    This is a GUESS: no page we read says why they are so wide.
  • How old are the broad moats?
    Their widths are present-day measurements, and no page we read dates them. The one page puts Xiangyang's widest 250 m (~820 ft) in the same sentence as the city's extension in the first years of the Ming, without saying which moat it measures, and the page that measures Guide's lake dates the building of the wall it surrounds to 1511, finished in brick in 1558, and gives no date for the lake. No period figure for a moat of this form was found, so on the record it is attested only today.

Japan walled few of its towns, and moated few: a county town under settled rule stood open, with no moat (at Was a county seat walled?).

  • Moated settlements in Japan go back to the Yayoi period.
    A settlement ringed by a ditch is a Japanese form reaching back to the Yayoi period.
  • Temple towns of the Muromachi period dug moats round themselves.
    The temple towns (jinaicho) of the Muromachi period dug moats round themselves for their members' defense.
  • The self-governing town of Sakai was ringed on three sides by a moat about 33 ft wide.
    Medieval Sakai, one of the towns that walled themselves, was enclosed on three sides by a moat about 10 m (~33 ft) wide and 3 m (~10 ft) deep, with gates (kido).
  • A river already flowing past could serve as a moat.
    Where a river or other ground already there is put to use, it is sometimes called a natural moat. At Osaka Castle the Yodo River and its branches, below the plateau to the north, served as a natural moat and also filled the castle's own moats.
