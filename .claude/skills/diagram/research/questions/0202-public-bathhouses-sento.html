Public bathhouses (sento)

A public bathhouse served townspeople who had no bath of their own: in the castle town of Takada and its neighbor Naoetsu-imamachi ordinary townspeople went to one, while the farm households around them each had a bath at home. It had to secure firewood and water, and its fire was a danger to the town. Edo kept about one for every two thousand inhabitants, and one district of villages, now Yashio city, a single one among nineteen villages.

  • Ordinary townspeople of Takada and Naoetsu-imamachi had no bath and went to the bathhouse.
    So it was in the Edo period in the castle town of Takada and in the town of Naoetsu-imamachi.
  • Farm households around Takada each had a bath, and took turns heating one.
    In the farming districts around Takada every house had a bath, but to save firewood and labor the households of a settlement took turns heating one and bathed at one another's.

How many bathhouses a place kept rose with its size.

  • How many bathhouses did a great city like Edo keep?
    About one for every 1,700 to 2,000 inhabitants. Edo in the Bunka era (1804-1818) is said to have had more than 600 bathhouses for some 1.0 to 1.2 million inhabitants.
  • Takada's bathhouses formed a guild and held at 19 for nearly a century.
    They formed a guild of 19 in 1769 and stayed at 19 until the end of the shogunate.
  • Naoetsu-imamachi's bathhouses held at 8 under a guild of their own.
    Its guild was formed after Takada's, and the number stayed at 8 to the end of the shogunate.
  • How many inhabitants did each of Takada's bathhouses serve?
    Roughly 1,300. Takada counted 24,571 inhabitants in the national ranking of 1886, which against the 19 is one bathhouse per 1,300 or so. The figure is rough: that census came nearly twenty years after the count it is set against, and neither source gives the castle town's population in the Edo period itself.
  • One district of nineteen villages kept a single bathhouse, in its largest village.
    In 1829 the nineteen villages of what is now Yashio city, 5,672 inhabitants, had a single bathhouse, in Hachijo, a village of 856, the largest of them and a hub of traffic.

What a bathhouse burned and what it risked shaped how it was run.

  • Men and women bathed together, because separate tubs cost space, firewood and water.
    The bathhouses were mixed from their beginnings into about the middle of the Meiji era: separate tubs for men and women would have needed more space and more firewood and water, and the business would not have paid.
  • Bathhouses were forbidden to open in strong wind, for fear of fire.
    The rule was one of fire prevention.
  • No page we read gives the size of a bathhouse.
    Neither the floor size of a bathhouse of the Edo period, nor that of its furnace room or its woodpile, is given.

Paid bathhouses were not Japan's alone.

  • Bathhouse keepers in Song China had a guild of their own.
    In Hangzhou, the capital of the Southern Song, those who kept bathhouses were called the "fragrant water guild".
