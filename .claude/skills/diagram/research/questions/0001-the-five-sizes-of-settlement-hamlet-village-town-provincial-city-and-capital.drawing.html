How our maps draw and state each size of settlement

Each size of settlement is drawn to its own count of households - from 10 to 20 households in a hamlet to some 12,360 inhabitants' worth in a domain capital - at its own round scale, and the card beside the map states its size in terms a reader can check against the drawing. Where a place is bigger than its sheet - a town ringed by farms, a city fed by the villages around it - the card says what the figure counts and what is left undrawn.

  • A hamlet draws 10 to 20 households, and no headman's house, shrine or tax-free plot.
    These are the setting's hamlet and its institutions.
  • A village draws 40 to 100 households, with a shrine, two or three tax-free plots and a headman's house larger than any other.
    The households, the shrine and the headman's slightly larger household are the setting's village; the setting gives a district one country monk on a plot of tax-free land, and the second and third plots are a guess of ours.
  • No samurai house stands in a hamlet or a village.
    Both are peasant-only in the setting, and a samurai family's country estate stands outside them.
  • A town draws all 82 of its non-farm households and a magistrate's walled manor, and as many of its farming households as the sheet holds.
    Its 24 merchant, 29 laborer, 13 servant, 12 burakumin and 4 samurai households are drawn at those counts. Of its roughly 156 farming households, only a sample fits.
  • A provincial city draws about 600 households and no farmers.
    They are roughly 40% laborer, 25% merchant, 20% servant, 10% samurai and 5% burakumin. Any farms on its sheet belong to the villages around it.
  • The city's mix is held as firmly as its total.
    Each caste is placed within about 30% of its share, because a city that meets its population with the wrong castes reads as the wrong kind of place.
  • A city's servants are placed among its laborers and merchants, not after them.
    When households are packed one caste after another, the last caste in the list gets whatever ground is left, which can be nothing. On our guess that servants lived among the households they served, they are placed among them.
  • A walled estate, samurai or merchant, counts as a household like any other.
    So the samurai estates outside a city's wall and the merchant estates inside it count toward their castes, and the ordinary houses make up only the remainder.
  • A domain capital draws about 12,360 inhabitants' worth of households.
    That is the setting's settled 12,000 or so, with the samurai families who move to the capital. The capital has everything a provincial city has, and where the two differ, the capital's own figures hold.

Each size is drawn at its own scale, so that a sheet fits its place.

  • Each size is drawn at a round number of feet to the pixel.
    One foot per pixel for a hamlet and a town, two for a village, three for a provincial city and a capital, and a third of a foot for the plan of a single building, the finest scale of all. The numbers are round so that a reader can measure a distance off the sheet in their head. Why these scales were chosen, and the real building complexes that bracket the building-plan figure, are at The building-plan scale is 3 px = 1 ft.
  • Every feature is measured in real feet and converted at each map's own scale.
    So a feature thirty feet across is thirty feet across on every size of map. Written in pixels instead, a road, a street, an inn, a stable yard or a storehouse would change its real size from one size of map to the next, and a town's fields would fall four to eight times under their real area.
  • A thin line is drawn at least 4 pixels wide - a map drawing convention.
    A five-foot alley at three feet to the pixel would be 1.7 pixels wide, which cannot be seen. A line narrower than 4 pixels is raised to 4 and no further. Anything truly wider is drawn at its true width.

The card beside a map states the settlement's size, and what the figure counts differs by size.

  • A hamlet's or a village's card states the exact number of farmhouses drawn, with no tilde.
    Every household is drawn, so the count is a statement about the sheet, which a reader could sit and count. The population that follows it is five to a household and carries the tilde (~), the mark of an approximate figure.
  • A town's or a city's card states the exact number of non-farm dwellings drawn, and no farmhouse count.
    Both are surrounded by fields and farmers that are deliberately not all drawn, so a farmhouse count would describe nothing true.
  • A city's population excludes farmers altogether.
    The card counts its figure as the urban dwellings drawn, five to a household; the farms on its sheet belong to village districts and counties counted separately.
  • A town's card states about 1,200 inhabitants, farmers who are not all drawn included - a disclosed departure.
    The setting counts a town's figure in and around it, farmers of its own fields included, so the card gives about 1,200 while the sheet draws every one of the 82 non-farm dwellings and only a sample of the 156 farmhouses. Everywhere else the card describes what is drawn. Here it describes the place the drawing is a sample of, and says so.
  • A town's 1,200 is the town's own, not its county's.
    The town's breakdown adds up on its own, one town map draws exactly its 82 non-farm dwellings, and the setting's table lists towns, villages and hamlets as separate lines that sum to the domain. Reading a town's figure as taking in the farmers of its county's villages would count about 172,000 inhabitants twice across the domain.
  • A card whose drawn houses and declared households differ names both.
    So a reader who counts the houses on the sheet is never left to wonder why the count and the stated households disagree.
  • A village's card names its county, and never a village district.
    A village and the district it heads share one name, so naming the district would say nothing, and the card names the county every village belongs to instead.
  • A hamlet's card names the district it belongs to, and the direction of the district's village.
    A hamlet belongs to a district named for another place, the village at its head, so one name tells the reader both its district and which village its lanes lead to, and the card says which way that village lies.
  • The name a village shares with its district is not flagged on the map.
    It departs from how real places were named, but it is set out in the setting's own core materials, which the players of this campaign already know.
  • The card ranks no kind of settlement as commoner than another.
    No page we read gives a count or share of any form, so the card makes no historical ranking. It states the setting's arithmetic instead: about 1,296 hamlets to 216 villages, and about 40% of a domain's inhabitants in a hamlet.
  • The card says a hamlet farms rice because its map draws rice.
    It does not claim that wet rice was the main crop, which no source we read measures, and does not take the kokudaka for a measure of it.
  • The card states that a hamlet has no headman, and says this is a simplification.
    The rule is the setting's; history had branch hamlets with officials of their own. The card says so in the paragraph that gives its basis, which is what makes the rule a deliberate deviation - one the reader is told about rather than discovers.
  • The card multiplies households by the setting's five, and claims no historical support for it.
    No historical household size could be read.
