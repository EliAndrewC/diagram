Farmhouses (minka)

A farmhouse is the minka of a farming household, a minka being a commoner's house. Its basic form was a rectangle under a hipped or a gabled roof, and most were small: in villages near Utsunomiya in the eighteenth century the commonest house had a floor of about 20 tsubo, some 700 square feet. Five surviving farmhouses of about 1700 at the Nihon Minka-en museum are larger, about 45 to 54 ft long, 24 to 30 ft deep and 1.6 to 2.0 times as long as they are deep.

  • Most farmhouses were small.
    When the shogun went on pilgrimage to Nikko, the villages near Utsunomiya drew up a floor plan of every house, to lodge his retainers. Those eighteenth-century plans show the commonest house at about 20 tsubo, some 66 square meters (~710 sq ft); many were under 10 tsubo (~355 sq ft), some only 4 or 5, and large houses were few.
  • How big was the usual farmhouse at the end of the Edo period?
    In a table of the sixteen households of Kakimochi village in 1885, main houses ran from 6 to 45 tsubo (~215-1,600 sq ft), and seven of the sixteen had one of 18 tsubo (~640 sq ft). The history of Oamishirasato town calls about 18 tsubo the usual size then, and takes the table to show, roughly, the farmhouses of the end of the Edo period, since a house was not rebuilt often. One village's table is weak evidence on its own.
  • Were the farmhouses that survive typical?
    They are larger than the commonest house of the counts. A house that lasted three centuries was most likely a big, well-built one - this project's reading - and three of the five farmhouses of about 1700 at the Nihon Minka-en open-air museum were headmen's. Two ordinary farmhouses measure 14.5 m (~48 ft) long by 8.9 m (~29 ft) deep and 13.6 m (~45 ft) by 8.2 m (~27 ft); three headmen's houses 15.6 m (~51 ft) by 8.9 m (~29 ft), 16.4 m (~54 ft) by 9.1 m (~30 ft) and 14.5 m (~48 ft) by 7.3 m (~24 ft).
  • How much bigger was a well-off farmer's house?
    The surviving houses cover about 106 to 149 square meters (~1,141-1,604 sq ft), 32 to 45 tsubo: one and a half to over two times the commonest house of the Utsunomiya villages (this project's arithmetic). A reconstructed headman-class farm of the late Edo period in Chiba has a house of 10 by 6 ken, about 60 by 36 ft and 193 square meters (~2,075 sq ft), and a hereditary headman's house of 1769 in Aomori is 30 m (~98 ft) long, with every work space under its roof. What else set a headman's house apart is at What makes the headman's house different?

Every farmhouse under one roof that was measured was longer than it was deep.

  • How much longer than deep was a farmhouse?
    The measured houses under one roof run from about 1.6 to 2.0 times as long as they are deep - 1.63, 1.66, 1.75, 1.80 and 1.99 (this project's arithmetic on their figures) - and the Chiba headman-class house 1.7. All of them are large survivors: how long a 20-tsubo house was against its depth, no page we read says.
  • How long and deep was an ordinary 20-tsubo house?
    At the survivors' 1.6 to 2.0 times as long as deep, it would be roughly 35 by 20 ft; that is a GUESS, since no small house was measured.
  • Why was a farmhouse longer than it was deep?
    This project reads it from the plan types: a farmhouse grew by adding bays along its long axis, under the same ridge, and so got longer rather than much deeper, and a square house or one four times as long as deep was rare. The source says only that a plan that looks complicated is almost always the four-room type with rooms added to its raised floor, or a stable set into its doma. No page we read describes the growth or gives a band of proportions.
  • A house built as two blocks could be nearly square in its main block.
    The Ota house, a headman's farmhouse of the bunto type, has a main block 9.6 m (~31 ft) long by 8.3 m (~27 ft) deep, with its doma a second block of 10.0 m (~33 ft) by 8.3 m (~27 ft).

Japan also built L-shaped and two-roofed farmhouses.

  • A Japanese farmhouse was a straight house, an L, or two roofs side by side.
    A farmhouse under one roof is the straight house (sugoya). One with a wing joined on is called a tsunoya in the west and, in the north, a magariya or a chumon-zukuri. A house whose two roofs stand eaves to eaves and are used inside as one is the bunto house.
  • The L and the two-roofed house each had their regions.
    The magariya is the farmhouse of northern Miyagi to southern Iwate, an L with the stable wing thrust forward from the front of the doma under one continuous roof; the chumon-zukuri is the farmhouse of Yamagata, Akita and Fukushima, with its wing projecting from the main house; and the bunto house stands from southern Kumamoto to Kagoshima.
  • The two-roofed house was not only a house of the far south.
    It survives now only along the Pacific coast, but in the eighteenth century many stood near Utsunomiya, far inland, and two of the houses at the Nihon Minka-en museum are two-roofed: the Ota house, a headman's from Ibaraki (above), and a fishing house from Chiba.
  • Did an L-shaped farmhouse mark a household's standing?
    In the Nanbu country of Iwate, a horse-breeding region, it seems to have: the L was common among the upper farmers, such as the village officials, so choosing it over the straight house is thought to have said something about the household's rank.

Chinese farmhouses took comparable plans, one of them built around a court.

  • Was a Chinese farmhouse a single range, an L, or three sides of a court?
    The three-sided sanheyuan - a main range with a wing on each side and a wall across the front - is a basic Han Chinese house, the courtyard house with its front range replaced by a wall. The other forms beside it are the yitiaolong ("one dragon") and the danshenshou ("one arm stretched"), the second glossed by the source as a two-sided court; that these are the single straight range and the L of a main range and one wing is this project's reading of the names.
  • The three-sided court was found all over China.
    A page on the traditional buildings of southern Fujian, on the southeast coast, says so in passing: it was not one province's form.
  • No page we read counts how many of a village's houses took each plan.
    Each source names where a plan stood, in Japan or in China, but none counts the share.

Over the plan stood the roof, in one of three shapes. What the walls under it were made of is at Farmhouse walls.

  • A farmhouse roof was gabled, hipped, or hip-and-gable (irimoya).
    A folk house is classed by its roof as gabled, hipped or hip-and-gable; the irimoya is gabled in its upper part and hipped in its lower.
  • Where each roof shape stood was regional.
    Ordinary houses were roofed irimoya mostly in the hill country from around Kyoto to Kai, Sagami and Musashi. Gabled thatched farmhouses were common around Enzan in Yamanashi (old Kai), and from southern Kawachi into Nara the gabled thatch was held down at each end by a strip of tile.
  • No page we read counts how many of a village's houses took each roof shape.
    Each page names where a shape was common; none counts the share.
  • Five farmhouses of about 1700 at one museum show all three shapes, every one of them thatched.
    They are hipped, gabled and hip-and-gable, and thatched, the ordinary houses and the headmen's alike.
  • Thatch was one roof covering among several.
    A folk house was roofed with thatch of grass or straw, cedar bark or boards held down with stones, or tile of two kinds.
  • Was thatch a mark of poverty?
    Not on the houses we read of: the headmen's surviving houses are thatched too, so this project reads thatch as the ordinary covering of rich and poor alike, and an old farmhouse roofed in metal today may have been thatched.
  • No page we read gives how far a farmhouse's eaves reached past its wall.
    The pages we read say only that a farmhouse's eaves were deep.

The Chinese pages we read describe tiled roofs.

  • In one well-known region of China, Huizhou, a house was white walls under dark tile.
    That is the look of Huizhou architecture.
  • In southern Fujian and Taiwan, the showy upswept "swallowtail" ridge is recorded on temples, mansions and ancestral halls.
    The page we read does not put it on a farmer's house.
  • No page we read says whether a poor Chinese farmer's house was thatched or tiled.
    None of the Chinese pages we could read - on tile roofs, on Huizhou and southern Fujian buildings, and on the three-sided court - says.

Which way a farmhouse faced bore on the yard where its household threshed and dried its grain and on where the sun fell. The houses of a village did not all face exactly the same way.

  • How far did a village's houses stray from one bearing?
    A survey of the houses of 27 Okinawan villages on slopes noted which of the sixteen points of the compass each main house faced. Most were spread over the village's commonest bearing and the point either side of it, the neighboring bearings arising where the roads curve; every other house faced right, left or back from that spread. Of all the houses, 87% faced within the spread, 11% were turned to the right, and only 1% each faced left or back - much as in the study's earlier count of villages on flat ground. That the rest of the spread came from houses set out by eye rather than to the compass is a GUESS.
  • How far could a house turn from its neighbors and still face with them?
    Three points of the compass span some 67 degrees, so a house could stand as much as about 34 degrees from its neighbors' common bearing and still count among them (this project's arithmetic on the survey's figures).
  • In one village on a slope, the houses turned toward the south rather than down the slope.
    That village stands at the foot of hills; on a slope falling to the northeast, 15 of 21 houses faced southeast and 3 south-southeast, turned a quarter away from the fall of the ground toward the south.
  • No survey we read measures a house's bearing to the degree.
    The survey counts by compass point, so how the houses spread inside one point is not measured.
  • Did a village's houses face different ways before Japan began to modernize in 1868?
    The count is of one island region's houses, in a paper of 1985, and no count of house bearings from before 1868 was found, in Japan or in China; no excavated farmstead's bearing was read. Curving roads, the survey's cause for the neighboring bearings, could have shaped a village before 1868 too - this project's reading; the nearest evidence is a village moved to its site in 1736 (below).
  • Maekawa, a planned Okinawan village, had curving streets on its old land-survey maps.
    Planned villages of rows of house plots are said to have spread across southern Okinawa and the islands beyond it under the Ryukyu kingdom from the eighteenth century. One of them, Maekawa, was allowed to move to its present site in 1736 and stands on a gentle slope facing south. On cadastral maps thought to date from the time of the land survey project, which show the village before its house plots multiplied and were subdivided, its streets curved widely and its blocks were bow-shaped.
  • Why did Maekawa's streets curve?
    Most of its blocks were one plot deep north to south and three to seven across, and the authors suppose the streets were laid along the contours of the slope, the plots kept narrow north to south so that no plot rose much from front to back.
  • Were Maekawa's streets laid before 1868?
    The paper does not say which were: the villagers moved in by stages and their numbers grew, and by their own account the blocks were extended first uphill and later downhill, onto steeper ground.
  • Did Maekawa's houses turn with its streets?
    That they did is a GUESS: the paper says nothing of which way the houses faced.

How near a farmhouse stood to its rice, and the bund along the paddy's edge:

  • How close did a farmhouse stand to its paddy?
    Close up against it, as this project reads the old Chinese ideal of a site, with its back to the hill and facing the sun and the water, where the hills encircle and the water embraces. The source's water is a watercourse; taking the paddy as the water a farmhouse faces is this project's reading. No page we read gives the distance in feet.
  • The bund between the paddies was a path as well as a bank for the water.
    Paddy levees were built and kept to hold water in the paddies and to let people pass and carry their tools, and farmers today keep their grass down by mowing or with weedkiller. How wide a Japanese bund was is at Bunds between the paddies (aze).
  • Some Mumun-period Korean paddies were split by bunds about 4 inches high.
    Some Mumun-period Korean paddies on flat ground were divided by bunds about 10 cm (~4 in) high.
  • A modern rice-growing guide keeps a bund under about 20 inches wide and 12 inches high.
    The guide tells a farmer to build bunds no wider than 50 cm (~20 in) and no taller than 30 cm (~12 in) around the field.
  • A bund built to keep fish in a paddy was heavier.
    A manual of fish-farming in rice fields wants such a bund at least 25 cm (~10 in) high and recommends 60 cm (~2 ft), and describes a trial plot at Kipopo, in Zaire, whose bunds were 60 cm (~2 ft) high on a base of 180 cm (~6 ft).
