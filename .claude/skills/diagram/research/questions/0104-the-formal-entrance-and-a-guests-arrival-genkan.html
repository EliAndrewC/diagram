The formal entrance and a guest's arrival (genkan)

A samurai house of standing had a formal entrance, the genkan, kept for the head of the household and honored guests; the rest of the household came and went by an inner entrance. In the Edo period the word meant an entrance with a shikidai, the low board step where guests were bowed in and seen off. A guest of rank came by palanquin, and the palanquin was brought right alongside the step, so that its rider went into the building without setting foot on the ground.

So a guest's arrival was staged: the gate, then open ground a palanquin could cross, then the step. Not every house had such an entrance. The houses of senior retainers did; a middle-rank house could do without one, and bring its guest in through a garden instead.

  • In the Edo period, "genkan" meant an entrance with a shikidai.
    In the shoin style of building, the genkan is the room or building set at a residence's formal entrance. In a warrior's house the projecting board floor where a palanquin was set down had been called the genkan, and the room of reception the shikidai; in the Edo period the whole came to be called the genkan, and the shikidai the lower board step where guests were bowed in and out.
  • The shikidai entrance was for receiving formal guests.
    It was a special entrance room for them.
  • A guest of rank stepped from the palanquin straight into the building.
    The shikidai entrance had a step, and the palanquin was brought right alongside it, so that its rider stepped up into the building without getting down onto the ground.
  • The main genkan was for the head of the household and honored guests.
    The rest of the household and its senior staff used an inner entrance, the uchigenkan.
  • The kitchen door was the way in from outside to the kitchen.
    The kitchen door, the katteguchi, is the doorway leading from outside into the kitchen.
  • The houses of senior retainers had a shikidai.
    They had earthen walls, a gatehouse with rooms in it (a nagaya-mon) and a shikidai.
  • Where an intendant's office and residence shared one compound, did the genkan belong to the office?
    At the Takayama jin'ya, the only jin'ya in the country whose principal parts survive, a visitor enters by the genkan, and the route goes round the rooms of the office and continues on to the residence where the intendants lived. The page describes a visitor's route, not how the office and the residence were laid out against each other.
  • A middle-rank house could have no genkan with a shikidai, and bring its guests in through a walled garden instead.
    The old Koseki house at Shiroishi, the house of a middle-rank retainer, has none. The gate, reached over an earthen bridge, leads along the earthen wall to the kitchen entrance, which the page calls the front entrance. A guest instead passes through a middle gate (nakamon) in the wall and along the roji, the walled garden path, straight to the veranda of the principal zashiki. The earthen wall encloses the roji in an L.
  • Was there open ground between the gate and the entrance?
    One drawing shows it: on a drawing made in 1869, just after the Edo period, of the jin'ya at Joge, the entrance with its step faces the open ground inside the main gate.
  • Was the ground before the entrance ever a garden?
    No page we read puts a garden there; the one drawing that shows that ground, Joge's, shows it open. The garden approach found is the middle-rank house's, where there was no genkan with a shikidai. Nor does any page we read describe a forecourt before the genkan of an intendant's office fenced off from its garden, or a pond set on the line from the gate to the entrance.

In China, a surviving governor-general's office was entered the same way, through its gate into open ground.

  • Through the main gate of the Zhili governor-general's office lies an open front court.
    At that office, a yamen, the open front court inside the main gate is planted with dozens of stout junipers.
