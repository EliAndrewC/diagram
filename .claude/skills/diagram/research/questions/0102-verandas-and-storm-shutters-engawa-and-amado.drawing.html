How our maps draw verandas and storm shutters (engawa and amado)

On our building plans a residence's veranda is drawn as a pale strip along its garden face, 3 to 6 ft wide, and some plans carry it round one or two further faces. A wing whose rooms are no longer lived in may be drawn closed behind its storm shutters in daylight, a GUESS.

  • Which faces carry the veranda is a knob between two forms.
    Both are attested at the scale of a provincial residence: the garden face alone, or, late in the Edo period, a continuous veranda along two or three faces. Each plan takes one. The magistrate's compound our maps generate takes the first, a veranda 4 ft wide along the garden face alone.
  • A veranda round all four faces is not drawn at a provincial residence.
    That ring belongs to the great daimyo mansions.
  • How wide is a drawn veranda?
    3 to 6 ft, half a ken to one ken, the widths of Katsura's verandas, one ken being also the dictionary's width for the inner passage (irikawa) behind the veranda, chosen along that range and free to change from one face to the next.
  • Is the veranda wider on the garden face?
    At Katsura the one-ken veranda on the east of the Ko-shoin's main rooms narrows to half a ken where it turns the corner, and no page we read gives a general width for the outer veranda. Drawing the garden face at the wider end is a GUESS from those widths, not a stated rule.
  • A plan shows a house by day, with its storm shutters stowed.
    By day the shutters stood in their boxes, closing the house at night and against wind and rain. So a veranda is drawn open.
  • A wing shut up behind its shutters in daylight is a GUESS.
    The shutters are real, but no page we read says a household kept the shutters of rooms it was not using shut by day. A plan draws such a wing darker, with the shutters across it, where the map's setting has rooms that no one lives in any more. Whose rooms they were is left to that setting.
