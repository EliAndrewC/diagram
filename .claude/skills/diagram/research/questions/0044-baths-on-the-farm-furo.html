Baths on the farm (furo)

Before 1868, in the Edo period, a farm's bath, where it had one, was a small room of the main house - beside its main door, under its eaves, or out beyond its stable wing - and few farms had one: a farm manual of about 1680, as a modern reader reports it, treats a bath in the house as a luxury for a farm household, and in the villages whose house plans survive only two or three houses in ten had one by the 1820s. No page we read places a bath standing as a building of its own on a farm before 1868.

The bath shed - a hut of its own in the yard - is a thing of the Meiji period and after. A prewar report calls the separate bath of northern Miyagi, in Tohoku in the northeast, a fashion of early Meiji, and housing reformers of the Meiji period sought to set the bath apart from the house as unsanitary. Before the Second World War the share of farms with one ran from none to eight in ten, village by village.

  • A modern reader of a farm manual of about 1680, early in the Edo period, reports that it treats a bath in the house as a luxury.
    The manual, the Hyakusho Denki, was written between 1680 and 1682. As its reader reports it, the manual has the family wash at a fixed place over a pit laid with bamboo slats, so that the water collects to thin the manure.
  • Village registers of the 1700s and 1800s set out the plan of every house, room by room.
    When the shogun went on pilgrimage to Nikko, his retinue lodged in the villages along the road, and those villages drew up registers of the plan of every house so that it could be lodged. Nineteen registers of farm villages from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries survive, and an architectural historian has read them room by room.
  • In the village house-plan registers of 1770 and 1771, the earliest to show a bath, only a very few houses had one, of about half a tsubo.
    A tsubo is about 6 by 6 feet. In three villages a bath room appears under the eaves beside the main door of four houses in one, beside the main door of six in another, and under the eaves beyond the stable of one house in the third - in only a very small part of each village's houses.
  • How common was a bath in a farm village in the 1820s and 1840s, late in the Edo period?
    About two or three houses in ten, in the two villages whose registers show it. In the register of Shimohasuda of 1824, 21 of 76 houses had a bath of 1 to 2 tsubo; at Ukiya in 1842, 15 of 62 houses did. The architectural historian who read the registers sums them up as about 20-30% of all houses.
  • In the village registers of 1824 and 1842, most baths stood out beyond the house's stable wing.
    At Shimohasuda 17 of the 21 were beyond the stable that projects from the earth floor, and at Ukiya 12 of the 15. The Koshigaya city history, reading the 1824 register of Hasuda village, counts 21 houses with a bath beyond the stable wing - the architectural historian's total of baths, though he puts only 17 of them there.
  • Why was the bath put by the stable?
    The architectural historian who read the registers suggests, as a guess, that one reason was that the bath's drain water also served as manure.
  • A few baths were joined to the house's floored rooms, most of them in headmen's houses.
    The floored rooms are the raised, wood-floored living rooms, as against the earth floor. The architectural historian finds a bath sometimes joined to them, and counts such baths in two houses of Kozutsumi in 1771 and two of Ukiya in 1842. One at Kozutsumi was the headman's house, and the two at Ukiya were the houses of the headman and of a peasant with a formal room; the other at Kozutsumi was a bath joined instead to a back room projecting behind the house.
  • Did a bath ever stand as a building of its own before 1868?
    None is found. Every bath the architectural historian reports is at the main house; whether the baths beyond the stable were joined to it he does not say, and his paper covers the main house alone. The pages we read that describe a bath standing apart describe the Meiji period (1868-1912) and after.

After 1868 the record tells of a different arrangement, in which many farms kept the bath in a hut of its own.

  • A town museum says the goemon-buro was widely used in farm villages.
    It says so of farm villages that lived chiefly by their own produce, and does not say when.
  • A prewar report dates the separate bath and privy of northern Miyagi to a fashion of early Meiji.
    A geographer passes on a report compiled before the war by the Japan Society for the Promotion of Science: in northern Miyagi, making the bath and the privy buildings of their own was reportedly a fashion of the early Meiji period.
  • Housing reformers of the Meiji period sought to set the bath apart from the house as unsanitary.
    An architectural historian describes what they aimed at: bath and toilet kept apart from the main house as unsanitary, sometimes joined to it by a corridor, sometimes standing alone outdoors - and, he adds, "farmhouses are like that". Whether "farmhouses are like that" takes in the corridor as well as the bath standing alone, the passage leaves open.
  • A twentieth-century memoir remembers a farm whose bath stood in a hut of its own, while other farm families had theirs indoors.
    The memoir remembers a thatched farmhouse with neither bath nor privy inside, each in a hut of its own, while other farm families had both indoors.
  • How many farms had a bath shed before the Second World War?
    It differed from village to village. Reconstructions of three Tohoku villages for 1935-1944, from town-office records and interviews, put bath sheds at 80% of farms in Tono, 33% in Inawashiro and none in Shiokawa; the author warns that the records may count one building twice, though he says so of the count of outbuildings per farm, not of these shares.
  • How common were bath sheds, and baths inside the house, in one northeastern town in 1972?
    About three bath sheds to every ten households (0.29 a household) over all its houses, and a bath inside the main house in 20 of the 38 houses built by 1944. These are read from tables the paper prints as images; the paper's key (Table 2) gives the bath the column marked yo.
  • In one Miyagi town's farmhouses newly built by 1972, a bath inside the house always stood in the northeast corner.
    In the newly built houses of the town in the 1972 count, the bath inside the main house stood without exception in the northeast corner, where in the old houses the stable inside the house generally stood.
  • A prewar report found the bath and the privy of northern Miyagi usually put in the front yard of the main house.
    A geographer passes the report on; it is the one place for a bath shed that the pages we read give.
  • No page we read measures a bath shed; about 6 by 6 feet is our own GUESS.
    The papers and the memoir that describe a bath shed give it no size.
