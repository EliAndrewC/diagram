The provincial government's seat: the governor's compound and where it stands (yamen)

In this setting a province is governed by its governor through six ministries - Rites, Revenue, Retainers, War, Works and Justice - as every level of government is; that is the setting's own, not a finding of history. Historically, the seat of a provincial or prefectural government was a compound - in China, a yamen - as large as a city block or larger. The surviving Chinese seats cover some 3 to 7 hectares (~7-17 acres), and the one surviving Japanese government office of its kind, the Takayama jin'ya, about 1 hectare (~2.5 acres).

A Chinese yamen usually stood at the center of its city - "governing from the middle" - its gate opening to the south. Behind the gate its courts ran in a fixed order: the gates, then the halls where business was done, then the official's living quarters, with an archive, storehouses, an armory and a jail beside them. At Kanazawa, a Japanese castle town, the offices stood inside the castle.

  • A Chinese yamen usually stood in the middle of its city.
    A book on the yamen of the Qing dynasty says a yamen usually stood at the center of its city, "governing from the middle". The archaeologist Su Bai found that in cities where princes were seated, such as Chengdu, Changsha and Qingzhou, the yamen and the market had stood in the middle. Building a prince's city on that central ground meant demolishing them, and a yamen moved elsewhere in the city needed its streets and its attached offices laid out afresh.
  • The archaeologist Su Bai reads one Chinese city's old plan as a square with a great cross of streets.
    He reads Datong's streets as a square city with one gate to a side, the streets from the four gates meeting in one great cross, and a smaller cross set in each of the four quarters it makes - the plan, he says, of a walled ward of Chang'an and Luoyang under the Sui and Tang dynasties. It is his reading of one city, not a rule that every city took it.
  • Some temples took fixed places in a Chinese city.
    The Confucian temples of Pingyao and of Xie county, in Shanxi, were both built in the southeast corner of their cities in the twelfth century. No page we read places a government office of rites among them.
  • In the Japanese castle town of Kanazawa, the government offices stood inside the castle.
    The government offices stood inside the castle with the ruling Maeda house's own mansion, at the head of a samurai quarter ranked outward from it.
  • Did a seat's offices line the streets around it?
    The pages we read do not go that far. Su Bai's passage treats a yamen's attached offices as belonging with it, laid out afresh with it when it moved; it does not say they fronted the great streets. In the castle towns of the tatemachi type, common in the late sixteenth century, the front entrances of townhouses and samurai residences lined the main street running out from the castle's main gate, but that page does not place the government offices along it.

The figures for a seat's size come from the few that survive, in China and in Japan.

  • How large was a Chinese prefect's or governor's yamen?
    About 3 to 4 hectares (~7-10 acres) on most of the figures, though one page doubles one of them. The prefectural yamen at Nanyang, the only one in China preserved complete, is given as 240 m (~787 ft) by 150 m (~492 ft), 36,000 square meters (~9 acres), and by another page as 300 m (~984 ft) by 240 m (~787 ft), over 72,000 square meters (~18 acres); neither says how it was measured. The Zhili governor-general's yamen at Baoding, the seat of a province, is 134.4 m (~441 ft) by about 224 m (~735 ft), over 30,000 square meters (~7 acres), and the Huozhou department yamen 38,500 square meters (~10 acres).
  • Did a seat's size climb in step with its rank?
    Not at the one lower seat we have a figure for. The county yamen at Neixiang, a rank below all of these, covers more than 47,000 square meters (~12 acres), with 18 courtyards one behind another and over 280 rooms.
  • How large is the Takayama jin'ya, Japan's one surviving government office of its kind?
    About 1 hectare (~2.5 acres), on today's boundary. From the Takayama jin'ya the shogunate governed the whole province of Hida from 1692, through 25 intendants sent in turn from Edo. Its protected site is 11,219 square meters (~2.8 acres) with the plaza in front of it and 9,807 (~2.4 acres) without - the modern legal boundary, not a measured footprint of the Edo period. It is the only jin'ya still standing.
  • How large were the provincial offices of Japan's ancient imperial state?
    Too various to give one size. The provincial office's district (kokuga) was often laid out at several tens of meters to about 100 m (~328 ft) square, one example 300 m (~984 ft) by 200 m (~656 ft), and its size, the Japanese Wikipedia article stresses, differed more than it agreed from province to province and period to period. This record reads them as an older form of a different state, a laid-out district of halls rather than one household's compound.
  • How large, then, was a provincial seat?
    From about 1 hectare (~2.5 acres), the Takayama jin'ya, to 3 to 7 hectares (~7-17 acres), the Chinese prefectural and provincial yamen. The span is this page's reading of the figures above; it leaves out Japan's ancient offices, which run from under a hectare (~2.5 acres) to 6 (~15 acres) and are a different form.

Inside, a Chinese yamen of any rank had the same parts in the same order; a higher seat had more of them.

  • A rule of the Qing dynasty set out the parts of every official's yamen.
    The business halls - a great hall and a second hall - with the main gate and the ceremonial gate outside them, the living quarters, and the clerks' rooms: a senior official had the full set, a junior one a set reduced in order.
  • A yamen was laid out on an axis, in courtyards closed on four sides.
    A study of Song local yamen finds the central axis the backbone of the whole complex. The Huozhou department magistrate's yamen faces south, its surviving buildings 18,700 square meters (~4.6 acres) in floor area, with its gate tower, ceremonial gate, great hall and other buildings in a line on its axis; another surviving yamen has six courtyard groups and 85 rooms, laid out symmetrically.
  • A yamen also kept an archive, storehouses, an armory and a jail.
    It held an archive for its documents and could not do without its granary and storehouses, its armory and its jail, and most had the official's residence attached. A county yamen, too, held a jail and storehouses beside its officials' offices and lodgings.
  • From the street, the governor-general's yamen at Baoding ran in five courts.
    At Baoding a screen wall, flagpoles and the guards' rooms stood outside the gate. Inside, on one axis, came the main gate, the ceremonial gate, the admonition arch, the great hall, the second hall, the inner-residence gate, the governor-general's study and inner office, the family's quarters and a rear storehouse. The inner-residence gate was the line between the front court of business and the residence behind.
  • The great hall at Baoding was 22 m (~72 ft) long and 10 m (~33 ft) deep.
    A report on the Zhili governor-general's yamen gives its size.
  • Six clerks' rooms flanked the path to the great hall.
    They stood between the ceremonial gate and the great hall, the rooms for personnel, revenue and rites on the east and for war, justice and works on the west, generally two bays each. A yamen with heavier business added rooms for dispatch, accounts and stores.
  • A yamen kept its own shrines.
    Among them were temples to the yamen god and the jail god.
  • A larger seat could add side lines of courts.
    The Huai'an prefectural yamen, of over 50 buildings and 600 rooms, ran in three parallel lines: the business halls and the clerks' rooms on the middle one, a military and police office on the west.

The Japanese provincial office kept the same front-to-back sense in a different shape.

  • Japan's ancient provincial office set its halls around a court.
    Its main hall most often stood to the north facing south, with side halls east and west, an open court between them, and a front hall and a rear hall before and behind the main one.
  • The Takayama jin'ya is a gate, an office building and rows of storehouses.
    Its surviving buildings are the front gate and gatehouse, an office building rebuilt in 1816 with its entrance, examination room, office and great hall, and its storehouses.

Which way the seat faced was settled in both traditions; how a visitor approached its gate was not.

  • The seat faced south, in China and in Japan.
    A common Chinese saying held that a yamen's gate opens to the south, its parts strung from south to north; the Zhili governor-general's yamen sits to the north facing south, and the main hall of a Japanese provincial office was usually built toward the north, facing south.
  • Some of Japan's ancient provincial offices stood on a north-south avenue.
    At Shimotsuke the office's hall district stood on a plot one cho square, fenced on four sides with a gate in the middle of each, and an avenue 9 m (~30 ft) wide ran south from the south gate, the other office buildings thought to lie on its west and south. In Suō an avenue named the Suzaku-oji, after the capital's own, ran north and south through the middle of the office's district.
  • How was a Ming or Qing yamen's gate approached?
    Most likely along a street running past it, not up an avenue: a yamen's screen wall stood across the street, facing the gate, and its memorial arch stood outside, by the street. That the street ran past the gate is our reading of that one page.

In this setting, a provincial seat also taxes the trade that comes in through its city's gates.

  • In this setting, a walled city collects tariffs at its gates.
    The Ministry of Revenue, at the provincial and domain level, collects import tariffs at the gates of walled cities: a caravan entering a walled provincial city submits its cargo to inspection and pays a fee on the goods it carries and the merchant's family of origin. This is the setting's own.
  • Japan's barrier stations levied tolls on the roads, often at passes and river banks.
    A barrier station served for defense, for keeping the peace and for levying tolls, and on land routes it stood often at a mountain pass or a river bank.
  • A post station on the road kept porters and horses for the official relay.
    It kept a fixed number of them on hand.
  • Chinese had a word of its own, guanxiang, for the streets just outside a city gate.
    A Chinese dictionary defines it as the streets outside a city gate and the area nearby, the main street outside a gate, and in antiquity the lodges near a city wall. What filled those streets is on no page we read; that markets grew there is this record's reading, at the gate market exists for traffic, not taxes and what stands outside a city gate.
