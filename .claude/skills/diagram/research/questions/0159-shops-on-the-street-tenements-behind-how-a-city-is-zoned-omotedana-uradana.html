Shops on the street, tenements behind: how a city is zoned (omotedana, uradana)

In a Japanese city of the Edo period, before 1868, each block was sorted by wealth from its front to its back. The better merchant houses kept their own shops on the main street, and almost all the other townspeople and craftsmen rented rooms in row houses (nagaya) down the back lanes. The front shops (omotedana) stood on the front of each lot, about five ken (~30 ft) deep along the street, and the back tenements (uradana) on the ground behind them, reached by alleys between the shops.

A back tenement was a one-roof row split down its ridge into two ranks of one-room homes set back to back, each home about 9 by 12 ft. The alley (roji) that led to it was a passage 3 to 6 ft wide, used by hardly anyone but the households along it, gated at the street, with a line of drain boards down its middle and a well and a privy its households shared.

  • The better merchants kept shops on the main street; almost everyone else rented in the back lanes.
    In the dense cities of the Edo period, and in Edo above all, merchant houses of the middle rank and above kept shops of their own on the main street, while almost all the other townspeople and craftsmen lived as renters in the row houses of the back streets.
  • The front shops (omotedana) held the street edge of each lot, and the back tenements (uradana) the ground behind.
    On the front of each lot, along the main street and about five ken deep, either the landowner opened a shop or a fairly wealthy merchant rented the ground and built one; the back part was entered by the alleys between the front shops. A back tenement was a house built on a back street or on the ground behind a merchant house, above all the crude row houses partitioned into homes, rented by petty traders, craftsmen and day laborers.
  • The front-shop households ranked a step above the back-tenement households.
    Most front-shop households rented their ground or their house too, but the name marked a stratum one rank above the common folk of the back tenements, roughly the wholesalers and brokers.
  • An alley into the back tenements began between two shops on the main street.
    The alley's mouth lay between one merchant house and the next, in the row facing the main street, and the way in and out of the back tenements was a wooden gate set between two front shops, and the alley behind it. A column on how Ginza, a district of Edo, was laid out pictures the same thing: shops in a row on the street, and, up a small alley between two of them, the craftsmen's row houses standing on both sides.
  • The alley's gate was opened at dawn and shut at dusk.
    A gate with a door, the alley gate, stood where the alley left the main street; it was opened at dawn and shut at about six in the evening.
  • A back tenement was a ridge-split row: two ranks of homes back to back under one roof.
    The back tenements were split along the alley under their ridge, a wall down the ridge's line dividing the building front from back, so that the two ranks shared a rear wall and opened to opposite sides.
  • Each home in a back tenement was a single room about 9 by 12 ft.
    Each home was nine shaku by two ken, a room of four and a half tatami mats with a mat and a half of earth floor for the stove and the sink. Most of the common inhabitants rented one.
  • Every home in a row house had its own entrance.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on the row house gives each home an entrance of its own.
  • Was there a rule for which way a city house's door faced?
    Not on any page we read. None ties a city house to a compass direction, such as facing the sun, or forbids a door that opens into the back wall of the next row.
  • How many rows of tenements did a shop's frontage carry?
    One or two. Behind a front shop about three ken (~18 ft) wide, the tenements stood along one side of the alley; behind one about five ken (~30 ft) wide, along both sides. The column on Edo's Ginza district gives its shops as some three to five ken wide at the front: about 18 to 30 ft in the Edo ken, or 19 to 32 ft in the longer Kyoto ken the column lays its blocks out in, and it does not say which it means here.
  • Were the tenement rows ever stacked three deep?
    Not on any page we read. The pages describe one row along the alley or a row on each side of it, with no third rank behind them, and a middle rank walled in on both faces would have had no way out to a lane.
  • How wide was a back alley?
    Between one tenement building and the next the alley was about three to six shaku, 0.9 to 1.8 m (~3-6 ft), wide, narrower than a side street.
  • A back alley was used almost only by the people who lived along it.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on the alley calls it a narrow passage that almost nobody but those connected with the buildings beside it used.
  • A line of drain boards ran down the middle of the alley.
    The boards were laid in a line over a small ditch six or seven sun, 18 to 21 cm (~7-8 in), wide.
  • What was underfoot in a back alley, either side of its drain boards?
    No page we read says whether it was beaten earth, gravel or anything else.
  • Where in the alley did the shared well and privy stand?
    The pages differ. The privy and the well were shared; the Japanese Wikipedia article on the townhouse puts them at the alley's far end, while the article on the alley has it widen just inside the back of the merchant house, or partway along, with the shared well or water supply there.
