How our maps draw village lanes

Every lane on a hamlet or village map is a narrow single track of bare, packed earth, with a soft worn edge and no line down its middle - never a wide, two-lane road. At the hamlet's scale of one foot to the pixel, a footpath is drawn 3 ft wide, the cluster's spine and its spur to the fields 5 ft, and the track out to the wider world 6 ft. No page we read measures a lane in a wet-rice village, so the three widths are a map drawing convention, chosen inside the bounds the record gives. Lanes keep to the bunds and dry ground, reach all but a few farmhouses as one network, and bend as little as the plots allow.

  • The widths run from a field road's up to below a cart road's.
    The footpath is drawn at the 3 shaku, about 3 ft, given for a field road in the hundred articles said to be Ieyasu's testament. The main lane and the field spur are drawn a rank wider, as the ways the map routes every household along to its paddy, though the one premodern figure found gives a field road 3 shaku. All three stay under the 9 shaku, about 9 ft, of the stone route laid for oxen.
  • The one measured village lane comes from a very different village.
    A surveyed Manchu village has blind alleys 2 to 4 m (~7-13 ft) wide, but that village is in northeast China, far from the wet-rice hamlets these maps draw, so the maps keep the narrower widths.

The surface does not vary among the forms the maps draw, so it is not a knob.

  • A village lane is drawn as bare earth.
    So is a town's back lane: no paving, no gravel and no steps. No page we read says a village lane was stepped with stone on a slope, so steps are not drawn.
  • An Imperial road is drawn in the same earth tone.
    Its dressing of sand and gravel is earth-colored at a settlement map's scale - a map drawing convention, the real surface being sand and gravel over earth (Village lanes).
  • Stone paving is drawn only where an Imperial road climbs a steep pass.
    That stretch alone is paved on the map. No other way is.

Where a lane may run is set by the fields and the wet ground around a settlement.

  • A lane never crosses row crops.
    It runs on the bund between plots or along the edge of the hem of dry fields, and may touch a plot's boundary, since the bund the path walks on is itself heaped up on the boundary.
  • Where a staircase of dry plots blocks the way, the spur to the fields stops at the hem's edge.
    Past it, farmers walk the narrow paths between the fields. This is a limitation the project accepted: a detour around the foot of the staircase, measured on one map, disturbs the farmsteads' layout on every generated version of it, while the stop at the hem's edge leaves that layout untouched.
  • A lane stops at the flooded paddy.
    From there the way into the fields is on foot along the bunds; how the path to the fields meets the paddy's outer bund is at How our maps draw bunds between the paddies (aze).
  • A track keeps off wet ground.
    None of these maps draws a causeway, so a track does not cross the reeds at the wet foot of the alluvial fan. It leaves downslope past the end of the wet ground, on the flank the cluster already sits on: the wet foot is only as wide as the ground the fan waters (Marshes and wetlands (shitchi)), leaving dry footslope at both of its ends. It turns along the contour only where the fan reaches the edge of the map and leaves no dry ground to pass by.
  • The track out runs off the edge of the map.
    The track that links a settlement to the wider world runs on out of the cluster and off the map. It never stops in open country, which would read as a path to nowhere.
  • The track out carries no label.
    Only an Imperial road is labeled.
  • The spur to the fields and the track out are one through-route.
    They are its two ends. The settlement's own inner lanes may end in the cluster or at the paddy.

Inside a clustered settlement, the lanes form one network reaching every farmhouse but the few reached across a neighbor's land.

  • The farmhouses are placed first, and the lanes are drawn among them after.
    A village lane is a footpath worn by villagers walking between their houses: in this setting the homesteads were built first and the lanes came after, as a worn path forms (Village lanes), so the maps seat every farmhouse before they lay any lane, and each lane follows the houses rather than the houses following it. An Imperial road is the exception, a planned work that houses were then built along, so it may be laid before the settlement that fronts it.
  • A few farmhouses may be reached across a neighbor's land, with no lane of their own.
    In Japan a household whose land had no way of its own to the road crossed a neighbor's land by custom, and in Echigo the way ran on across two plots in a chain (Village lanes). So each clustered settlement rolls a share of its households that may be reached that way, from none up to a quarter, rounded down to whole households. The share is a GUESS: the record shows the custom but not how common it was, and the clustered villages it describes reach their houses by alleys: the crowded ravine village of Enoshima its rear houses, and the surveyed Manchu village, as it stands today, its houses, by its streets and the blind alleys off them. Such a household is seated only where it has no way of its own to the lanes, the custom's own condition, so that much is historically accurate. A way of its own here means a straight path from its dooryard, or one carried round its house's gable; a path that would have to bend round other homesteads is not looked for. That follows the GM's ruling of 2026-10-03: cutting through a neighbor's yard was no great matter, and a household might do it even where a lane ran, if that was faster or more direct. A walk from its dooryard to the neighbor's threshing yard must keep to the two households' land and cross no house, garden bed, shed or fixture of either. Its land must adjoin the neighbor's, no more than 3 ft apart: the 2 ft the cluster's growth leaves between neighbors, and a foot more. That margin is a GUESS, since the custom is land against land, never a walk across open ground. A household may be reached across a neighbor who is itself reached across another, and no farther - a GUESS resting on the Echigo chain, the longest the record reads. The walk is not drawn as a lane: a dooryard and a threshing yard are open, trodden ground, and the walk across them is a custom, not a way - this record's reading, a GUESS. So such a household shows no lane of its own, and the reader sees it standing against its neighbor, whose own way is always drawn. Whether it has a way of its own is judged by its whole holding, not by one way of laying out its beds: if any layout of the homestead would leave it a way of its own, it has one. And it is placed only on the side of its neighbor where the neighbor's threshing yard lies, and not close by a way, since a household near a way has one - a map drawing convention, chosen where the walk to the yard can be had.
  • Every other farmhouse is served by a lane of its own.
    Where a straight path to it would cross its own garden beds or fixtures, the path is routed around them - a map drawing convention.
  • Past its last farmhouse, a lane ends at the dooryard or runs on to something a reader can see.
    That may be a field path, a bund or another way; a lane end that reaches nothing is pulled back to the last house it serves. No page we read says how far a lane ran past its last house, so the rule is a GUESS, and so is how close counts as serving a house.
  • Nothing is built on a lane.
    Farmsteads front a lane, and no corner of a house stands on its tread.
  • Which of two forms a settlement's lanes take is a knob, chosen at random for each map.
    Either side lanes branch off the main lane between the house plots to reach the row behind, or a back lane runs behind the plots, parallel to the main lane. A back lane implies a planned village and side lanes one that grew over time, in the record's reading (Village lanes), and a hamlet in this setting could plausibly be either, so the maps vary it between settlements rather than settle it once.
  • A dispersed hamlet has no lanes between its farmhouses.
    Its farmhouses stand scattered among the fields, so a lane runs to the hamlet and nothing runs between the houses - a value of the settlement-form knob.
  • A clustered settlement's lanes are one network, joined where their treads meet.
    A lane that runs along a garden fence to reach its junction belongs to the network, because the plot fronts the lane.
  • A lane keeps 7 ft clear of a garden fence.
    The gap is a map drawing convention, not a measured figure.

A worn path takes the shortest or easiest way, so a lane is drawn with as few turns as the plots allow, none of them sharp, and never doubling back on itself.

  • Every lane is pulled taut like a string.
    It runs straight from point to point wherever it can keep its usual clearance from fences and buildings. It may come right up to the buildings when the straightened line stays within 6 ft of the old one.
  • A lane never doubles back in a hairpin.
    A turn of more than 140 degrees is a hairpin, and where a lane turns back on itself, a returning leg under 40 ft is cut.
  • A lane does not zigzag.
    Two turns of more than 50 degrees within 40 ft of path, about a dozen paces, are pulled straight.
  • Lane ends that nearly meet are joined.
    Ends within 25 ft of one another are joined at a single point, so three lanes never arrive a few feet apart in a knot.
  • A lane does not run on past a crossing as a stub.
    A tail of under 40 ft beyond a crossing is cut.
  • Two lanes that meet end to end are read as one.
    To whoever walks it, they are one path, so where the one would turn back into the other, the arriving lane meets the other's side as a T instead, and a jog across the meeting point is pulled straight like any other.
  • A lane's end loses its hook.
    A last leg of 12 ft or less turning 90 degrees or more is cut.
  • The limits above are drawing conventions.
    Every turn limit and distance in these bullets is chosen at the scale of a dozen paces, not measured.
