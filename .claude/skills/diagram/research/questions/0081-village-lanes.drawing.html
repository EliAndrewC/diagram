How our maps draw village lanes

Every lane on a hamlet or village map is a narrow single track of bare, packed earth, with a soft worn edge and no line down its middle - never a wide, two-lane road. At the hamlet's scale of one foot to the pixel, a footpath is drawn 3 ft wide, the cluster's spine and its spur to the fields 5 ft, and the track out to the wider world 6 ft. No page we read measures a lane in a wet-rice village, so the three widths are a map drawing convention, chosen inside the bounds the record gives. Lanes keep to the bunds and dry ground, reach all but a few farmhouses as one network, and bend as little as the plots allow.

  • The widths run from a field road's up to below a cart road's.
    The footpath is drawn at the 3 shaku, about 3 ft, given for a field road in the hundred articles said to be Ieyasu's testament. The main lane and the field spur are drawn a rank wider, as the ways the map routes every household along to its paddy, though the one premodern figure found gives a field road 3 shaku. All three stay under the 9 shaku, about 9 ft, of the stone route laid for oxen.
  • The one measured village lane comes from a very different village.
    A surveyed Manchu village has blind alleys 2 to 4 m (~7-13 ft) wide, but that village is in northeast China, far from the wet-rice hamlets these maps draw, so the maps keep the narrower widths.

The surface does not vary among the forms the maps draw, so it is not a knob.

  • A village lane is drawn as bare earth.
    So is a town's back lane: no paving, no gravel and no steps. No page we read says a village lane was stepped with stone on a slope, so steps are not drawn.
  • An Imperial road is drawn in the same earth tone.
    Its dressing of sand and gravel is earth-colored at a settlement map's scale - a map drawing convention, the real surface being sand and gravel over earth (Village lanes).
  • Stone paving is drawn only where an Imperial road climbs a steep pass.
    That stretch alone is paved on the map. No other way is.

Where a lane may run is set by the fields and the wet ground around a settlement.

  • A lane never crosses row crops.
    It runs on the bund between plots or along the edge of the hem of dry fields, and may touch a plot's boundary, since the bund the path walks on is itself heaped up on the boundary.
  • Where a staircase of dry plots blocks the way, the spur to the fields stops at the hem's edge.
    Past it, farmers walk the narrow paths between the fields. This is a limitation the project accepted: a detour around the foot of the staircase, measured on one map, disturbs the farmsteads' layout on every generated version of it, while the stop at the hem's edge leaves that layout untouched.
  • A lane stops at the flooded paddy.
    From there the way into the fields is on foot along the bunds; how the path to the fields meets the paddy's outer bund is at How our maps draw bunds between the paddies (aze).
  • A track keeps off wet ground.
    None of these maps draws a causeway, so a track does not cross the reeds at the wet foot of the alluvial fan. It leaves downslope past the end of the wet ground, on the flank the cluster already sits on: the wet foot is only as wide as the ground the fan waters (Marshes and wetlands (shitchi)), leaving dry footslope at both of its ends. It turns along the contour only where the fan reaches the edge of the map and leaves no dry ground to pass by.
  • The track out runs off the edge of the map.
    The track that links a settlement to the wider world runs on out of the cluster and off the map. It never stops in open country, which would read as a path to nowhere.
  • The track out carries no label.
    Only an Imperial road is labeled.
  • The spur to the fields and the track out are one through-route.
    They are its two ends. The settlement's own inner lanes may end in the cluster or at the paddy.

Inside a clustered settlement, the lanes form one network reaching every farmhouse but the few reached across a neighbor's land; how they are laid - after the houses, in the gaps between them, as side lanes or back lanes - is at How our maps lay the lanes inside a clustered settlement.

A worn path usually takes the shortest or easiest way, so a lane is drawn with as few turns as the plots allow, none of them sharp, and never doubling back on itself.

  • Every lane is pulled taut like a string.
    It runs straight from point to point wherever it can keep its usual clearance from fences and buildings. It may come right up to the buildings when the straightened line stays within 6 ft of the old one.
  • A lane never doubles back in a hairpin.
    A turn of more than 140 degrees is a hairpin, and where a lane turns back on itself, a returning leg under 40 ft is cut. A folded field spur is cut at the fold, an exception to the 40 ft rule.
  • A lane does not zigzag.
    Two turns of more than 50 degrees within 40 ft of path, about a dozen paces, are pulled straight.
  • Lane ends that nearly meet are joined.
    Ends within 25 ft of one another are joined at a single point, so three lanes never arrive a few feet apart in a knot.
  • A lane does not run on past a crossing as a stub.
    A tail of under 40 ft beyond a crossing is cut. A lane laid only to join two others ends where it first meets one, its tail cut however long it is, an exception to the 40 ft rule.
  • Two lanes that meet end to end are read as one.
    To whoever walks it, they are one path, so where the one would turn back into the other, the arriving lane meets the other's side as a T instead, and a jog across the meeting point is pulled straight like any other.
  • A lane's end loses its hook.
    A last leg of 12 ft or less turning 90 degrees or more is cut. A lane run on to a bund turns no more than 60 degrees anywhere, a stricter limit than the 90 degrees above.
  • The limits above are drawing conventions.
    Every turn limit and distance in these bullets is chosen at the scale of a dozen paces, not measured.
