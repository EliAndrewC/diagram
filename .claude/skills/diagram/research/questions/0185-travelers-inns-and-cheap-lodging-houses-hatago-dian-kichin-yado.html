Travelers' inns and cheap lodging houses (hatago, dian, kichin-yado)

A Japanese post station lodged its travelers in grades: the hatago, an inn that gave its guests their meals, and below it the kichin-yado, where travelers cooked for themselves. A post station was, before anything else, a street of inns. The fifty-three stations of the Tokaido held nearly three thousand hatago between them, and most were small houses, about 18 ft wide on the street or less.

The road inns (dian) of north China were built around a broad yard, with a long stable, for the carters' wagons and animals. The cheapest lodging in both countries was a single shared room, let for a coin or a few a night.

  • The Tokaido's post stations differed sharply in how many inns they kept.
    Miya, the ferry port, had 247 and Kuwana on the far shore 120, while Fujisawa had 45.
  • On average a post station held 55 inns on the Tokaido and 27 on the Nakasendo.
    So says a Japanese dictionary's entry on the hatago.
  • How thick on the ground were a post station's inns?
    At Kusatsu in 1843, 586 houses and 2,351 inhabitants kept 72 inns, about one house in eight. The same page puts it elsewhere at one house in five or six, with as many as 132 inns at the busiest.
  • A hatago was built like a lord's lodging, cut down, and came in three sizes.
    It had the form of the lords' lodging (honjin) with the raised audience room, the gate and the formal entrance left out and the whole somewhat reduced, and it was classed as large, middling or small. The lords' lodging is at Lodgings for lords and officials on the road (honjin)
  • At the post station of Mishima, an inn's size class was set by its street frontage.
    An inn of 5 ken or more on the street (about 30 ft) counted as large, one of about 4 ken (about 24 ft) as middling, and one of 3 ken or less (about 18 ft) as small.
  • Small inns were the commonest.
    Of an average Tokaido station's 55 inns, just under 9 were large, just under 18 middling and just over 28 small.
  • How big was one inn?
    One inn at Hiratsuka was a two-story building 5.5 ken (about 33 ft) wide on the street, with a building area of 57.25 tsubo, about 2,040 square feet. If that area is its ground floor, it ran about 62 ft back from the street. A larger inn, and the plan of the building itself, are at Inns (hatago and carters' inns).
  • One Tokaido inn of about 1836 still stands, two stories high, its upper floor the guest rooms.
    The Ohatago Kashibaya, at the old post station of Okabe, is thought to have been rebuilt about 1836; it is of wood, two stories and tile-roofed, and its upper floor was used as guest rooms.
  • Did a Japanese inn keep a stable or a cart yard?
    None of the pages we read gives a hatago a cart yard or a stable of its own, and nothing we read describes a cart yard, a feeding trough or a grooms' lean-to at a Japanese travelers' inn before 1868.
  • An inn that stabled a traveler's own horse was called an umayado, but the dictionary's earliest record of a stable behind one is from 1913, long after the Edo period.
    A Japanese dictionary defines that sense of the word, and its earliest example is a sentence from a novel of 1913: a travelers' inn in front, a large stable at the back. The word's older senses, from the 1670s and 1680s, are a place that kept the post horses and the men who worked there, not an inn's stable.
  • No page we read counts the inns of an ordinary town that was not a post station.
    Every count we found is a post station's.

The north-Chinese road inn is described in two accounts two centuries apart: a Korean traveler's of 1780, and a newspaper's of the wagon inn that lasted into the 1970s.

  • In 1780, under the Qing, a Korean traveling with an embassy found the inns on the road to Shanhaiguan built round broad courtyards.
    Park Ji-won traveled with the embassy sent that year to congratulate the Qianlong Emperor on his seventieth birthday, and wrote that an inn's courtyard was broad, "not less than several hundred paces", because otherwise it could not hold the carts, horses and crowds.
  • Those inns' stables held long troughs of stone or brick, and their yards rows of wooden troughs.
    A stable's stone trough ran two or three bays long, or was built of brick in the same form, and dozens of wooden troughs stood in rows in the courtyard as well.
  • The wagon inn of northeast China, from the end of the Qing into the 1970s, kept the same cart yard and long stable as the inns of 1780.
    A newspaper's account describes it as the house that took in passing carts and horses, set on the main roads and around a city's outskirts, with open ground in its back yard for parking the carts and a long stable with one very long feeding trough and a rail above it for tying the halters.
  • A grooms' lean-to and a well in the yard are recorded only in a newspaper's account of the wagon inn, published in 2026.
    The newspaper, of 2026, adds a lean-to beside the stable where the grooms lived and a well in the yard; the 1780 account names neither.
  • Was a wagon inn before modern times a single story?
    The newspaper's was: the buildings in its yard were uniformly single-story, of mud brick and reed thatch. The 1780 account gives no height, so a single story for an earlier inn is a GUESS, carried back from the later account.
  • A Chinese merchants' inn could also store its guests' goods.
    The Chinese Wikipedia article on rural markets says the didian gave lodging and let traveling merchants store their goods, at once an inn and a warehouse.
  • No page we read gives a Chinese inn's rooms or stories beyond the 1780 courtyard and the wagon inn's single story.
    The Chinese Wikipedia article on the inn carries no sources, and searches found forum answers only.

Below the inn with meals stood the cheapest lodging, in Japan and in China alike.

  • The kichin-yado was the lowest grade of Japanese inn, and stood at the edge of the post station.
    In and before the Edo period it stood on the highways at the edge of a post town, at the spot called the bobana, and lodged travelers for about the cost of the fuel. The dictionaries call it the oldest form of travelers' lodging at the post stations.
  • Its lodgers paid for the firewood to cook food they had brought.
    The "ki" of kichin is firewood: the guests pooled their rice and paid about the cost of the wood to have it cooked.
  • They slept in one large shared room, often on their own bedding.
    It was not uncommon for lodgers to bring their own bedding, and at its worst laborers and the unregistered slept huddled together at about one tatami mat each.
  • A night cost a few mon in the seventeenth century and about two dozen by the 1840s.
    A shogunate ordinance of 1611 set the fee at 3 mon for a person and 6 for a horse, it was 6 mon in 1658, and in Edo in 1843 a lodger paid 24 mon a day for the roof and 16 to 24 more to borrow a futon.
  • By the 1830s an inn with meals cost more than five times as much as a kichin-yado.
    So the kichin-yado became a byword for a cheap inn.
  • Its lodgers were the poor of the road.
    The dictionaries name street traders, porters levied from the villages (sukego), the highway bearers and day laborers, and the poor, traveling entertainers, pilgrims and beggars. Ordinary commoners mostly stayed at the hatago.
  • We guess that peasants from the far edge of a market's reach stayed in town the night before market day.
    It is this project's GUESS: a peasant who lived too far out to walk to market and home in a day would want a cheap bed the night before. No page we read on the periodic markets says so. How often a market met, and how far its customers walked, is at Market days and the market ground (ichi).
  • What did a kichin-yado look like from the road?
    The one period picture we read is a Hiroshige print of the Minakuchi post station, which shows a kichin-yado as a thatched house, its name written large on its paper doors.
  • How big was a cheap lodging house, and how many did it bed on a night?
    No page we read gives one's frontage or a night's count of its lodgers, in any period before modern times.

In a Chinese city the cheapest lodging gathered where the poor or the traffic were.

  • In Beijing a paid night shelter of one great hall slept men, women and children together for a coin.
    Evariste Huc, in the English edition of his account published in 1855, describes the capital's "House of the Hens' Feathers", a shelter "simply composed of one great hall" floored with feathers, where everyone was admitted for one coin a night.
  • Old Beijing's cheapest lodgings, the "chicken-feather inns", clustered in one small district.
    A page on old Beijing's inns places them north of Tianqiao.
  • All the chicken-feather inns' lodgers were poor.
    The same page names them: small traders, workers and craftsmen come to the city, beggars, rag-pickers, day laborers, thieves and vagrants.
  • Inns for travelers stood outside the Chinese city gates that opened onto long-distance routes.
    The ground outside a gate drew markets and businesses for the country people, and inns for travelers and traders were set up outside the particular gates that gave on long-distance routes. What grew outside a gate is at Gate markets outside a town (guanxiang).
  • Were a city's gates shut at night?
    It matters for lodging: a traveler who reached a shut gate after dark had to find a bed outside it. In Tang Chang'an the dusk drum closed the ward gates and the outer wall's gates were barred through the night, and even in the late Ming a city's officials kept so strict a watch on its gates that it was hardly possible to leave a city at night. The article details Tang Chang'an and speaks of the late Ming city only in general; no page we read says a county town's gate shut at dusk.
  • The curfew over the Song capital's markets ended in 1063.
    After it was abolished, the marketplaces of the Song capital Kaifeng were open at every hour, where a strict curfew from dusk had held over the two official markets of Tang Chang'an. So we read lodging at a gate in the later centuries as standing there for the traffic, not as a relic of the curfew.
  • By the Song, the capital's houses no longer stood inside gated wards.
    Kaifeng's houses, rich and common, stood along its streets, not inside the walled compounds and gated wards of the Tang capital.
  • No page we read gives the number of cheap lodging houses in any Japanese or Chinese town or city before modern times.
    The shogunate's survey of the post stations on the five highways set down each station's households and its number of hatago, but no page we read says it counted the cheap houses, and none gives the number of Beijing's chicken-feather inns.
