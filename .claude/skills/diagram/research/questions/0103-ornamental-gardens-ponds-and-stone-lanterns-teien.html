Ornamental gardens, ponds and stone lanterns (teien)

A Japanese ornamental garden (teien) was one of three kinds: the pond garden, built around real water; the dry landscape (karesansui), which shows water without using any; and the tea garden. It was made of a pond and its water, set stones, made hills and planting, with stepping stones, paving, stone lanterns and fences as its furnishings. A garden beside a shoin, the formal reception room, was made to be looked at, seated in the room that faced it or on its veranda.

Where a government office's garden can still be read, it lay before the formal rooms and was looked at from them. Where the garden lay in a residence's lot is at Samurai residences and their rooms (buke yashiki), and its pines at Black pines in Japanese gardens (kuromatsu).

  • At Takayama, the great hall and the intendant's living room look onto one garden.
    The Takayama Jin'ya was the office where the officials and intendants sent by the shogunate governed and lived, and of the 60 or more such offices across the country it is the only one that survives. Its garden is seen from the shoin-style great hall at the heart of the office and from the Arashiyama-no-ma, the intendant's room. That room has a tea room, and stepping stones lead into the garden from it and from the great hall alike.
  • Did the intendant's rooms at Takayama share the garden before 1868?
    As far as the rebuilt rooms show it, yes. Most of the buildings seen there today are reconstructions of the late Showa and Heisei years, the front gate of 1832 and the office range of 1816 excepted. The great hall stands in that range of 1816, and the intendant's residence and the inner rooms were rebuilt in 1996 to almost their Edo-period state from a plan of 1830, with the city's excavation of the residence's site in 1991 as a further reference. The site's pamphlet, as a garden guide reports it, says the garden itself was remade in the 1780s, before either date. So the one garden shared by the hall and the living room is the layout of an office before the Meiji Restoration; none of these sources says the plan draws the garden itself.
  • Did an intendant's private rooms ever have a small garden of their own?
    No page we read describes one at any office.

Both kinds of garden, with water and without, stood beside samurai rooms.

  • Both government offices whose gardens we could read, Takayama and Okutono, had a pond garden.
    At Takayama a pond garden lies further in, beyond the stepping stones. At the Okutono Jin'ya, the seat of a small domain (han) in Mikawa, the garden laid out again after an Edo-period drawing of it is a pond garden on two levels, the lower one seen from the shoin. The jin'ya itself was pulled down once the domains were abolished, so the garden there now is a restoration. Both were seats of government: Takayama was one of the offices of the shogunate's intendants, and Okutono the seat of a domain, if a small one.
  • At Chiran, six of the seven protected samurai-house gardens are dry landscapes.
    Chiran is one of the samurai settlements the Satsuma domain formed around the residence of a district's steward or lord, once it had divided its land into 102 districts. Of the seven gardens protected there, one is a pond garden and the other six are dry landscape.
  • At Matsushiro, a samurai house's garden generally had a pond.
    In that castle town the garden lay south of the house, with a pond, an artificial hill beyond it planted with trees, and carp kept in the water. The house of a 150-koku district magistrate there, the Yokota house, keeps its garden pond, though the page gives no date for the pond.
  • How large was a residence garden's pond?
    No page we read says. The one garden size quoted here is not a residence's: the dry garden beside the shoin of the Daisen-in, a temple, is a level plot of about 100 m2 (~1,076 sq ft), made to be looked at from the room and its veranda.

The stone lantern came to the garden from the shrine and the temple.

  • A stone lantern (ishidōrō) is built of six stacked stone parts.
    From the top, they are a jewel, a cap, the fire-box that holds the light, a middle platform, a post and a base.
  • Stone lanterns came in many named kinds, according to their use.
    The Kasuga, the snow-viewing (yukimi), the Enshū and the Oribe are among them.
  • The stone lantern began as a votive light, one lantern at the center of the front of a shrine or temple.
    It came from China and Korea with Buddhism, as a light offered to shrines and temples, where a single lantern stood at the center of the front.
  • From the Momoyama period the stone lantern was set in tea gardens and ordinary gardens.
    Lanterns came to be set in the tea garden to light gatherings held in the evening, and when the tea garden, once nearly flat, took on made hills, streams and ponds, the stone lantern became one of its important sights. From the Momoyama period it was used in ordinary gardens as well.
  • Was a stone lantern ever set in the court before a residence's formal entrance, where guests were received, as one was before a shrine or temple?
    No page we read describes one outside a garden.
