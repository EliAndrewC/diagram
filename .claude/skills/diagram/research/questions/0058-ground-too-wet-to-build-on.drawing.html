How our maps keep buildings off wet ground

On a hamlet or village map, a drawn marsh is ground nothing is built on: houses, wells, byres, shrines and graves all keep off it, and no farmhouse stands on the wet low ground below a field's drain. Houses and graves keep to dry ground because the record shows them doing so before modern times; a shrine and a well keep off it by this project's choice. On a town or city map, which draws no marsh below its fields, the rule is a strip of about 33 ft either side of a channel on which nothing is built.

  • In China and Japan, graves were put on high, dry ground.
    Graves went to high ground in China by about 239 BC, and a Japanese rule of 1884, early in the Meiji era, sites a new graveyard on high, dry ground.
  • A shrine is kept off the marsh by this project's choice.
    For a shrine's hall no page we read says what the graves' sources say, so keeping it off the marsh, like a graveyard, is this project's decision.
  • No part of a shrine, temple hall, graveyard or mausoleum touches the marsh.
    One whose corner clips the marsh is as wrong as one standing in its middle.
  • A shrine or a graveyard with nowhere dry inside the settlement goes outside it.
    One that finds no dry ground among the houses is drawn on a spur or the high ground beyond them.
  • A pond's reed fringe does not count as marsh.
    It is a water margin wherever the pond lies, not a valley bog, so this project does not extend the rule to it.
  • A well keeps off the marsh because the houses it serves do.
    We found no rule from before modern times that keeps a well off wet ground. A well is drawn among the houses it serves, and the houses keep to dry ground, so the well does too; that is this project's reasoning, not a finding.
  • A well is kept off the ground below the fields' lowest point, not off the whole drawn marsh.
    The drawn marsh reaches about 90 ft up the slope from the foot of the fields, its reeds tucked under the lowest fields (How our maps draw marshes and wetlands (shitchi)), so that strip is farmed and built on, with no reeds showing. Keeping wells off the whole drawn marsh would refuse every well standing there.
  • Wells are kept off wet ground only on hamlet and village maps.
    Towns and cities draw no marsh below their fields.
  • No farmhouse stands on the wet low ground below a field's drain.
    Below the drain that collects a field's water lies reed marsh, low paddy reclaimed from it, or the drainage pond itself. It is not always marsh, but it is the low ground where drainage channels are laid, and we read it as draining poorly, as a river plain's back marsh does, so no farmhouse sits there.
  • On river plains, the old settlements stood on the dry levees.
    The wet back marsh behind them was farmed as paddy, not built on.
  • A field's drain runs downhill, so not all the ground below it is low.
    The maps' ground is a tilted plane, so its height depends only on how far down the slope a point lies; the edge of the wet ground is a contour, not the ditch. The drawn drain is not a contour: it descends along its length to reach its pond, more steeply than a real one would, a map drawing convention (How our maps draw marshes and wetlands (shitchi)). Measured on the drawn maps, a drain's two ends lie 269 ft apart down the slope on the Akagahara map, 340 ft on the Kikuta map and 352 ft on the Hoshigaoka map.
  • Farmhouses are kept only off the ground straight downslope of a drain, between its two ends.
    A farmhouse off to the side, past either end of the drain, stands on a dry flank of the slope and is allowed there.
  • Farmhouses are kept off the ground below a drain only in a dispersed settlement, not in a nucleated cluster.
    Each farm of a dispersed settlement is sited on its own and must stand on dry ground on its own; a nucleated cluster is placed as one block under its own rule. A tight cluster beside a diagonal drain reads as "downslope of the drain" without standing on any wet ground at all: unscoped, the rule would forbid the Ueda map's cluster, which sits on the map's west margin, southwest of a drain running northwest to southeast.
The rule the map follows:
  • Hamlet and village maps: no sacred or funerary ground stands on marsh.
    No shrine, temple hall, graveyard or mausoleum, not even one corner of it. A pond's reed fringe is not marsh for this. One with nowhere dry inside the settlement goes onto a spur or the high ground outside it.
  • Hamlet and village maps: no dwelling stands on the wet ground below a field's drain.
    The ground judged lies downslope of the drain along the map's set downhill direction and within the drain's own span across the slope; a farmstead past the drain's ends, on a dry flank, stands legitimately. The rule applies where farmsteads are strewn one by one, not to a nucleated cluster placed as one block, and needs both a set downhill direction and a drawn drain to mean anything.
  • Town and city maps: nothing is built within about 33 ft of a channel's centerline.
    The width is our own calibration, set to keep a whole farmhouse off the water, not merely its center: a hamlet farmhouse reaches about 26 ft from its center to a corner. On a map drawn at a coarser scale, the strip is as wide as it must be to keep the largest building beside the channel clear. No corner of a building may reach into the strip.
