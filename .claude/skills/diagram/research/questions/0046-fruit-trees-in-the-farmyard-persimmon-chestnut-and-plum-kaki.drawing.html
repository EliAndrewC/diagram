How our maps draw farmyard fruit trees (kaki)

Our maps draw a persimmon beside most farmhouses, in front of the house or behind it, with a crown about 23 ft across. A farmstead may also have one to three other fruit trees near the house - persimmon, chestnut or plum, chosen per village - and some villages plant tea along their field banks. The other small fixtures of the yard are at How our maps draw the farmstead and what stands on it (yashiki).

  • How many farmhouses on our maps have a persimmon?
    80 to 95 in every 100, the share chosen at random per hamlet within that range. The accounts put one in every dooryard, so drawing a few farmhouses without one is a GUESS.
  • Our maps set the persimmon in front of the house or behind it, and behind it wherever the farm dries its grain in a front yard.
    Both places are attested, and the accounts that give a persimmon to every dooryard make the front the commoner, so how often each is drawn is chosen at random per hamlet, with the front the likelier; how much likelier is a GUESS. But a tree in front of the house stands in the drying yard's sun, and our maps keep the persimmon out of every yard's and bed's sun as they keep every other tree (How our maps keep yards and gardens in the sun), so a farm with a drying yard in front keeps its persimmon behind the house. The one survey we read that puts fruit trees in a front yard describes a front yard that was hardly used for drying, the rice being prepared indoors. No persimmon is set on the house's flank, which no source we read records.
  • On a farm with its own grove, the persimmon may stand in the grove behind the house.
    The traditional grove of the Sendai plain was a body of tall evergreens such as cedar and black pine with a few fruit trees planted in it, so where such a farm's yard and kitchen bed take the front, its persimmon stands behind the house at the edge of its own grove, no other farm's, and the grove's trees give way round it.
  • The persimmon stays in the dooryard, or the farm has none.
    Its crown's edge stands within about 30 ft of the house, the reach the fruit trees are given below; a farm whose dooryard has no place for it out of its yard's and beds' sun, and out of its neighbors', keeps no persimmon rather than one set far out in the fields.
  • How wide is the persimmon's crown?
    About 23 ft, a GUESS: a modern horticultural reference gives a full-grown tree's spread as about 7 m (~23 ft). No record we read from before 1868 gives an ordinary dooryard persimmon's crown, and the one Edo-period tree measured, said to be the largest in Japan, spreads about 20 m (~66 ft) today, so 23 ft is a modest tree, well short of that extreme.
  • The persimmon is drawn a yellower green than the groves, with four fruit dots.
    Both are drawing conventions: the color and the dots tell the persimmon apart from the grove's trees at a glance. The dots do not mark a season.

Beside the persimmon, a farmstead may have a few other fruit trees, and a village may plant tea along its field banks.

  • How many fruit trees stand at a farmstead?
    One to three: no source counts them, and the sources speak of them as part of the yard, not an orchard. The count is a GUESS.
  • Where in the yard do the fruit trees stand?
    Within about 30 ft of the house, most often on the side with the storehouse and shed rather than the grove's windward side. The sources place them in the yard; the distance and the side are a GUESS.
  • A village's fruit trees are persimmon, chestnut or plum, chosen per village.
    All three are attested at farmsteads, so each village's kind is chosen at random from the three, weighted toward chestnut in a cold northern district, where chestnut was among the commonest trees, and toward persimmon elsewhere. The weights are a GUESS.
  • Some villages plant tea along their dry-field banks.
    Bund tea is attested, but no source we read says how many villages kept it, so whether a village has it along some of its dry-field banks is chosen at random per village.
  • No loquat is drawn at a farmstead.
    A folk belief warned against planting a loquat at a homestead.
  • Mulberry is drawn only as a field crop, never in the dooryard.
    Mulberry was a crop of the field. A village's mulberry is one of its cash crops on rice land, not a dooryard tree.

The rule the map follows:

  • The persimmon stands in the dooryard in front of the house or behind it, the front the likelier, with its crown about 23 ft across, and never within 50 ft east, west or south of a yard or bed - so behind the house wherever a drying yard lies in front.
  • A farmstead given fruit trees stands one to three single crowns in its yard within about 30 ft of the house, most often on the side with the storehouse and shed rather than the grove's windward side.
  • A village given bund tea draws a single row of low tea bushes along some of its dry-field banks.
