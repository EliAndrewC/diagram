How our maps place household shrines (yashikigami)

Our maps draw a household shrine, a small shrine to the god of the homestead's ground, on only a few homesteads: three to eight in a hundred. Two patterns are recorded: a shrine at every house in some places, and at only certain old families' houses in others. This project draws the rarer pattern, so that a shrine is notable where it stands, and does not draw the every-house pattern. The one count we have found, of the twentieth century, puts the shrine at about one household in a hundred; the share drawn is this project's choice, and no source gives it.

  • A map never has more shrines than three to eight homesteads in a hundred.
    The number drawn is capped at that share, so chance alone cannot make shrines common on a map.
  • Our maps put each shrine in the homestead's northwest, northeast or southwest corner.
    All three are attested: the northwest and the northeast as the commonest, the northeast especially, and the southwest as usual in Tokushima.
  • How often does each corner come up?
    Each shrine's corner is chosen at random: the northwest 45 times in 100, the northeast 35 and the southwest 20. The weights are a GUESS, ordered after the Japanese Wikipedia article, which names the northwest before the northeast; no count we could read gives them.
  • Shrines are drawn at 6 by 6 ft, larger than life, so that they can be seen.
    That is the size of the map's small sheds; the one measured stone shrine is about 40 cm (~16 in) wide, deep and high, and at its true size it could not be made out at the map's scale.
  • Shrines are drawn vermilion, with a ridge line and a torii before the door.
    This marks them apart from the sheds, and is a drawing convention.
