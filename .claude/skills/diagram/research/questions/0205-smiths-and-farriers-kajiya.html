Smiths and farriers (kajiya)

A smith, kajiya, forged and mended the iron things a household and a farm needed. In Japan the village smith was a nokaji, who made farm tools, fishing gear and household tools to order, working small: a husband and wife, a parent and child, or a master and an apprentice. Many villages had no smith and bought their iron in town, while a castle town gathered its smiths onto a street of their own - eight houses in one small town, most of some thirty in a large one.

Shoeing horses was a trade apart. Edo Japan protected its horses' hooves with woven straw sandals and took up the iron horseshoe only after 1868, but China had shod its horses with iron since the Yuan dynasty, some five centuries before. This setting departs from Japan here, deliberately: its horses are shod, so its towns keep farriers.

Whether a village had a smith depended on the lord's rules and on the village.

  • In Iyo, craftsmen were at first not to live in the villages.
    In the villages of Iyo the rule at first was that whatever a village made besides its crops was for its own use only: craftsmen were not to live there, and farm tools and pots were bought in the castle town or a country town.
  • Iyo's villages later licensed carpenters, coopers and smiths, who farmed as well.
    They were issued license tags and paid a levy, worked small, and combined the trade with farming.
  • Traveling tinkers and coopers cut into the village craftsmen's trade.
    They went round from village to village, and pressed on the craftsmen who had settled in them.
  • Many villages recorded no craftsmen at all.
    In the Kyoho era (1716-36) many shogunal villages of one Iyo district recorded none, and those that did had few, of few trades.
  • A village smith worked small, to order, for the farmers round about.
    The nokaji were once found all over Japan, taking orders from the farmers nearby and from the local hardware dealer.
  • How many villages kept a smith?
    No page we read counts them. In nineteen villages of one district, smiths were among the villages' craftsmen, and a survey of 1829 found about a quarter of its roughly 1,000 houses in trade or crafts beside farming. Both forms are attested, then: a village with a smith of its own, and a village with none that bought its iron in town and waited for the traveling tinker.
  • A village could keep several smiths.
    The register of Kawanoe village in 1701 counts 6 smith households among its craftsmen.

What a village smithy looked like is known only from modern times.

  • What did a village smithy look like?
    The one village workshop with a published plan that we found is the model at the Boso no Mura museum, built from a village smith's workshop that stood at Arami in Narita: a gabled, tiled building 3½ ken wide and 3 ken deep, about 21 by 18 ft, whose left half and right front are a beaten-earth floor holding the bellows and the hearth, with a boarded, ceiled room at the back right. But the museum built it from a survey of a workshop it does not date - one still standing to be surveyed in modern times - and one of its fittings, the upright vise, came in only in the Meiji era, after 1868. The house-sized building with its work floor inside is modern practice, remembered and rebuilt, and undated.
  • In the modern smithies one researcher visited, the fittings stood in a row.
    From the smith's left: the bellows, the hearth, the anvil and the water tub, in swordsmiths' and village smiths' workshops alike, as a researcher at the Nara national research institute found them.
  • No village smithy's size or plan from before 1868 was found.
    Not a surviving building, an excavated one or a period picture that gives it.
  • No page we read says where in a village the smithy stood, or how far from its neighbors.
    Nor whether a gap was kept round the forge against fire.

The castle towns did it the other way, gathering their smiths in.

  • Castle towns gathered their craftsmen onto streets by trade.
    A castle town gathered craftsmen from elsewhere, gave them house plots, and set them in craftsmen's streets by trade, to serve the lord's retainers and the townsfolk. Among those streets a castle town could have a smiths' street, kajimachi.
  • Kofu, a large castle town, kept most of its smiths on its smiths' street.
    In the Kofu domain's time, 28 of the castle town's 33 smith houses owing the lord smith's work stood on its Kajimachi, and a register of 1780 still puts 27 of 32 smith houses there, 26 of them working as smiths.
  • Gujo Hachiman, a small castle town, counted 8 smith houses on its craft streets in 1692.
    A house register of 1692 lists 50 houses along its craftsmen's street and smiths' street. Smiths were the largest trade there, with 8 houses, beside 5 doctors, 3 coopers, 2 horse doctors and carpenters, tatami makers, lacquerers, tailors, dyers and sake shops among others.
  • How wide was a town smith's house?
    About 21 ft on the street, on this record's reading. On Gujo Hachiman's streets the houses ran from craftsmen's houses 2 ken wide, about 12 ft, up to large merchant houses. The Sakai gunsmith Inoue's house, recorded on the same site on a map of Sakai of 1689, centers on an early-Edo block 3½ ken wide, about 21 ft, with an earth floor running through it, flanked by blocks 2 and 3 ken wide that are undated.
  • In the Sakai gunsmith Inoue's house, the street-side earth floor was the finishing room.
    Guns were assembled and adjusted there. The forge shown today was built new from a drawing of the Meiji era, after 1868.
  • No page we read says how deep a town smithy ran before 1868, or where its forge stood.
    Whether the hearth stood in the street room or in a workshop behind is not recorded.
  • No page we read counts swordsmiths apart from other smiths.
    The pages count smith households on a street, but do not separate the blade smiths from the tool smiths.
  • In this setting, a county town keeps a master smith.
    The setting's own notes count him among the local notables who send the county magistrate seasonal gifts: "the sake brewer, the master smith, the dye-house owner". Whether other smiths work under him or beside him, the notes do not say.

Shoeing a horse with iron is where Japan and China part.

  • Edo Japan protected its horses' hooves with straw.
    A woven straw sandal, umagutsu, had been used since ancient times, and metal shoes came into systematic use only from the Meiji era, after 1868.
  • Japan knew the iron shoe, and hardly used it.
    The shogun Yoshimune imported Western horses through the Dutch trading house in 1729 and worked to bring in Western shoeing, yet until the end of the Edo period horseshoes were hardly used.
  • China shod its horses with iron from the Yuan dynasty on (1271-1368).
    Rattan and leather shoes were used before.
  • In Qing Beijing, shoeing was a trade of its own.
    Its men made the iron shoes and nailed them on.
  • From the mid-Qing, Beijing's shoeing shops stood near the horse inns and the horse doctors.
    At first the shoers went about with their gear on shoulder poles. From the mid-Qing they kept shops, most with a frame before the door to tie the mules and horses to.
  • A traveler in 1861 found oxen shod with iron in north China.
    Oxen haul this setting's heavy wagons, so how an ox was shod matters here as much as a horse. A traveler up the Yangtze in 1861 found cattle in Sichuan in straw shoes, and horses and cattle in the north shod with iron shoes and nails.

Europe, at the other end of the continent, shod its oxen as well as its horses.

  • In Roman times, Europe already strapped a metal shoe, the hipposandal, to the hoof.
    The Roman Empire's northwestern provinces knew it, fastened with metal clips and leather laces.
  • In the West, a farrier worked from a forge with a yard, a blacksmith from a small shop.
    A farrier's yard held a number of horses at once, and the blacksmith's shop stood often in the center of a village or town.
  • In the West, an ox was shod slung in a frame.
    The frame, in England a trevis, had a belly sling to raise the animal, and in Spain it was a village's shared fixture.
  • No page we read shows an East Asian frame that slung an ox before modern times.
    The only Chinese frame read is the Qing shops' frame to tie the animal to.

This setting shoes its horses, a deliberate deviation from Japan, for two reasons its own notes already carried: iron, and the miles its relay horses run. The Chinese iron shoe is its precedent, not its reason, so the farrier is not a modern form read back into the setting.

  • Was iron too dear in Japan to put on horses' hooves?
    A single smelt in the traditional furnace took about 9.1 tonnes (~10 short tons) of iron sand and 11 tonnes (~12 short tons) of charcoal to leave about 2.3 tonnes (~2.5 short tons) of steel, and the iron went to daily necessities, weapons and tools. We think its cost is why it did not go to hooves, but that is this project's inference: no page says so.
  • This setting's empire has iron to spare.
    It is continental, with real ore bodies and whole iron districts in the setting's canon: Tatarano ("bellows plain"), Kuroiwa, Ubame's furnaces sited by the charcoal, the Reiji hill works, and the iron mines of the Crab Clan, the clan that holds the Kaiu Wall against the Shadowlands. Iron is an ordinary industrial good while good steel stays scarce and dear, which buys horseshoes of plain low-carbon iron without cheapening a good blade.
  • This setting's relay network is about three times the size of Tang China's.
    The setting's notes give about 5,000 waystations on about 50,000 miles of Imperial road, some 10 miles apart, with 500 to 1,000 mounted couriers and mounted patrols against bandits. The Tang dynasty had 1,639 relay stations in all, and under the Han dynasty one was placed every 30 li.
  • Would straw sandals last on a relay horse?
    Straw sandals were cheap, and wore out fast. A relay horse working its stage all year is on the road every day, and straw serves a farmer walking an ox to the next village, not that. That hard roads also wear a hoof faster is a GUESS, not searched.
  • The setting's notes already had shod horses.
    They staff a busy trunk-route waystation with "a smith for shoeing horses and weapon maintenance", and introduce Moto Khuyag as a farrier who offers to reshoe the player characters' horses - a Rokugani-born one, who learned the trade at Kyuden Shinjo.
  • The Mongol Yam's horses were probably mostly unshod, so this setting's shoeing does not rest on them.
    Steppe horses on grass were ridden unshod, we believe. That is a GUESS, not searched, and it would make the Mongol relay post a counterexample.
  • Straw survives in this setting wherever iron is not worth it.
    On the peasants' pack horses, on oxen on soft paddy tracks, and in poor and mountain provinces - because straw is free, not because iron is unknown.
