How our maps draw the bell-and-drum tower (zhonggulou)

Every walled town or city on our maps draws exactly one combined bell-and-drum tower, the tower that struck the night watches and, on this project's reading, signaled the closing of the town gates at dusk. Ours stands at a corner of the main street crossing, a masonry platform a little over 30 ft square carrying a timber pavilion with the drum and the bell. The tower follows the wall, not the population: a town without a wall draws none.

  • How big is the tower our maps draw?
    Between 30 and 45 ft on a side, about one rowhouse's width: about 36 ft in a city and about 30 ft in a town, a GUESS within the band. The band is set by Pingyao's county-seat Market Tower, about 38 ft square. Pingyao's stood out by its height, 18.5 m (~61 ft), not its plan.
  • Our maps draw the tower heavier than the fire towers.
    Its masonry platform is drawn solid, where our fire towers are braced-frame lookouts, light and open. Xingcheng's tower stands on a brick platform; the heavier build of ours is a GUESS from that.
  • Our maps set the tower at the main street crossing, on a corner rather than astride the street.
    In Xingcheng, a Ming garrison city, and in the town of Dai county, the tower stands at the exact center of the town, at the crossing of its main streets. Setting ours on a corner of the crossing is a GUESS.
  • The magistrate's compound (yamen) had its own drum tower, the qiaolou, which our maps do not draw.
    It is a recognized variant, a building of the compound itself. The town's bell-and-drum tower on our maps stands near the compound, not inside it, a GUESS.
  • A garrison city may draw the tower on a platform astride the street, 50-66 ft square.
    That is the form of Xingcheng, whose platform is 21 m (~69 ft) square with its passages running through it. The drawn size is a GUESS, and the form is kept for a garrison city and not given to the ordinary walled towns our maps draw, county seats of about 3,000 inhabitants.
  • A capital may take the full pair, a drum tower and a bell tower.
    The paired towers facing each other are the fuller form, as at Beijing. A capital's time bells are at How many time bells does a capital keep, and where do they hang?
  • A town without a wall draws no tower.
    It has no gates to close at night, so it has no need of the tower's signal.
  • What does an unwalled town keep time by?
    Most of Edo's time bells hung at temples. That most provincial towns likewise relied on a temple bell is this project's extension of Edo's case, a GUESS. Our maps imply the bell within the monastery's precinct and give it no glyph of its own, as our map of Hoshizora, an unwalled town, does.
  • The fire towers do not stand in for the time tower.
    Kaifeng's fire watch was a post of the ward patrol soldiers, and Edo's time bells were a public charge apart from temple bells, though in some Japanese towns the fire tower's alarm bell also sounded the time; keeping the two apart on our maps is a GUESS (see Fire watch towers and firefighting gear (hinomi yagura)), so our fire towers do not cover the timekeeping and the curfew.
  • The tower's caption reads "drum/bell" over "tower".
    The drawn building is the combined tower, both instruments in one pavilion.

One tower is loud enough for any walled town our maps draw.

  • How far does the tower's sound have to carry on our maps?
    On our map of Tango, a walled city, the farthest resident inside the wall stands about 500 m (~1,640 ft) from the tower, and the farthest drawn building in the gate suburbs about 790 m (~2,592 ft). On our map of Nagahara, a walled provincial city, the two are about 435 m (~1,427 ft) and 920 m (~3,018 ft).
  • How far did the bells and drums of history carry?
    Edo's Kokucho time bell was paid for by some four hundred blocks within earshot, a radius of about 1.2-1.4 km (~3,937-4,593 ft) on this project's arithmetic, a GUESS. Beijing's drum and bell towers struck the evening for an inner city walled some 24 km (~78,740 ft) around.
  • How loud would the tower be at the edge of a walled town?
    A large bell or great drum runs about 110-130 dB near the tower. At the farthest building on either map, 500-920 m (~1,640-3,018 ft) off, it arrives about 40 dB above the night's noise floor of about 25-35 dBA in the days before modern traffic, at night, when the night watches matter most, so it is loud, not merely audible, everywhere inside the walls and the suburbs, and its low notes bend over one-story roofs. These figures are a GUESS: no page we read gives them.
  • When would a town need a second tower?
    Only beyond about 2-3 km (~6,562-9,843 ft) from the tower, against daytime town noise, this project's estimate, a GUESS. So even a capital on our maps, some 12,000 inhabitants and about a mile across, needs no second tower to be heard.
  • Edo and China dealt differently with a city too large for one bell.
    Edo multiplied its fee-district bells, while Beijing kept one drum tower and one bell tower and Chinese cities sent night watchmen with clappers and gongs through the streets to announce the night watches. A capital on our maps follows China's pattern and draws one set.
