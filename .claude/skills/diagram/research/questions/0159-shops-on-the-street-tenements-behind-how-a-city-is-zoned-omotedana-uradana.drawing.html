How our maps zone a city: shops on the street, tenements behind (omotedana, uradana)

Our maps zone a city's blocks as the history did: the shops and merchant houses stand on the street, and the laborer and burakumin homes pack the insides of the blocks behind them, reached by alleys. Behind the street the homes stand in back-to-back pairs of rows, never more than two deep, each door opening onto a lane.

  • Our maps put the shops on the street and the laborers' homes inside the blocks behind them.
    The better merchants kept shops on the main street and almost all the rest rented in the back lanes, so our street grid cuts a quarter into blocks whose insides are reached by alleys rather than streets.
  • Our maps give merchants houses of every size and laborers small, uniform ones, in the game setting's own shares.
    Of a provincial city's merchant households about 8% are very rich and live behind compound walls, 12% are rich, in large houses, and the remaining 80% are poor or middling, in small ones. The laborers' homes are uniform, small and packed close, but about one laborer household in eight is a master laborer's, the best-off band of a settlement's laborers, with a larger home on the best back-street lots. These shares are the setting's own, not a historical finding.
  • Our maps give a more mercantile quarter more streets.
    More shops want more street front. This is a calibration against our drawn cities, not a finding.

The rule the map follows - frontage:

  • almost every shop and merchant building stands within about 255 ft of a street, and most laborer and burakumin homes do not: they pack the insides of the blocks, off the street, where the alleys reach them (a calibration against our drawn provincial cities);
  • the exception is the larger master-laborer home, drawn at 6 to 20% of the laborer homes, a generous band around the setting's one in eight, since a map can place only as many of these larger homes as there is back-street frontage for, and placed on back-street frontage with room around it (a calibration);
  • samurai may front their streets, and a merchant building counts as a business and as a home at once;
  • merchant homes are spread out: their median gap to the nearest neighbor is at least 1.3 times the laborers', so a merchant quarter is not cut into shallow strips by too many streets, since the walled compounds need deep block interiors (a calibration).

Which way a house faces, and how deep its rows stack, follow from where its door can open.

  • Our maps open every city door onto ground a person can walk on.
    A door opens onto a street, an alley or a court, and never into the back wall of another house an eave-gap away. The pages we read give every home an entrance of its own and put the alley's mouth between the street-front shops, but none states the rule against a door into a back wall; it is this project's choice.
  • Our maps leave at least 7 ft of clear ground in front of every city door.
    The figure is a GUESS. It is set between the strip under the eaves, about 6 ft or less, which we take to have carried off rainwater rather than people, and a path wide enough to walk along, about 10 ft. No page we read gives either width.
  • Our maps face a farmhouse south, and a city house toward whatever open ground it has.
    A farmhouse faces south wherever it stands, for the sun on its dooryard garden and its threshing ground. That is the setting's own rule, so the farmhouse is exempt from the door rule above. No page we read gives a city house any rule of facing the sun.
  • Our maps stack rows at most two deep, in back-to-back pairs.
    The back tenement was a ridge-divided row, two ranks under one roof sharing a rear wall and opening to opposite sides, and a shop's frontage carried one row along its alley or a row on each side, with no third rank described. A third row cannot occur on our maps, because its middle rank would be walled on both faces with no way out to a lane.

The rule the map follows - doors and rows:

  • every city home's door opens onto at least 7 ft of clear ground, at every map scale (a guess);
  • rows stack at most two deep: in a street-fronting pair the front row faces the street and the row behind it faces the back lane, and the back tenements are entered from the lane, through a gate between two front shops (accurate);
  • a house standing on its own takes whichever side is open;
  • the farmhouse, facing south, is exempt;
  • town and city maps both.

The alleys themselves are drawn wider and simpler than the real ones, so that they read at a map's scale.

  • Our maps draw a back alley 12 ft wide, where the real one was 3 to 6 ft.
    The real alley between one tenement building and the next was about 3 to 6 ft wide. At three feet to the pixel a true alley is one or two pixels wide, too thin to read as a way or carry a caption, so the maps widen it two to four times, a map drawing convention.
  • Our maps run one alley down a block's middle, where a real alley ran in from the street between two shops.
    The real alley's mouth lay between two merchant houses on the main street, rather than threading the block as one spine; a spine is what a map at this scale can draw legibly, a map drawing convention.
  • Our maps draw a line of boards down an alley's middle, over a small ditch, as the real alleys had.
    The boards and the ditch under them are accurate.
  • Our maps draw the ground either side of an alley's boards as plain beaten earth.
    This is a GUESS: no page we read says what it was.

The rule the map follows - alleys:

  • an alley keeps a street's rules: it never runs over a building and never crosses the wall or the moat;
  • it earns its length by serving homes no other lane serves: each home is credited only to the lane nearest it, at about one such home per 90 ft of the alley's own length, which fails both a lane running off into a half-empty corner and a lane laid beside one that already serves the block (a calibration);
  • the rule of thumb is one lane per block, a single spine, never a side branch to ground the spine already reaches and never an alley shadowing a street a few tens of feet away (a convention);
  • an alley running on past its last building to a ward gate serves the gate, not the block, so that last stretch is not counted against it;
  • no large cluster of homes, more than about thirty, sits with no street or alley within about 285 ft: every deep block, including any band between two quarters, is laced with alleys so that each home can be reached (a calibration).

A town is zoned by the same rule of shops in front, but its street is lined one house deep (see how a town is zoned and how deep a town's rows stack).
