Pawnshops (shichiya)

A pawnshop lent money against pledges, goods left in its keeping, and it had to keep those pledges safe. In Japan the storehouse that held them so defined the trade that the medieval lenders were named for it; in China, one account says, the pawnshop was itself a building of several stories for storing pledges. Pawnbroking reached down to villages: in 1829, late in the Edo period, more than half the villages of one district, now Yashio city, had a pawnshop, often as a sideline to another trade.

  • Nineteen villages of what is now Yashio city kept 18 pawnshops among them.
    In 1829 the nineteen villages, some 5,672 inhabitants, had 18 pawnshops spread across 11 of the villages.
  • Hachijo, a crossroads village of 856 inhabitants, had three pawnshops.
    It was the largest of the nineteen villages counted in 1829, and a hub of traffic.
  • Pawnbroking was often a sideline to another trade.
    In Hachijo's list of 1836, a liquor retailer selling soy sauce, sandals and sundries took pledges, and so did the village's oil presser.

What set a pawnshop apart from other shops was the building where it kept its pledges, and Japan and China housed them differently.

  • In medieval Japan pawnbrokers were named for their storehouses.
    The lenders were called at first kura, and in the Muromachi period dosō or dozō, "earthen storehouse", because keeping the pledges called for large, solid storehouses.
  • Were Chinese pawnshops multi-story buildings for storing pledges?
    The Chinese Wikipedia article on the pawn trade says so, in a sentence that gives no period. The pawnshop at Qinxiang, below, had two stories.
  • A pawnshop said to date from the Qing dynasty, in a market town near Wuxi, was a two-story building about 59 ft wide and 66 ft deep.
    At Qinxiang, a rural market town north of Wuxi, a pawnshop said to have been built about the mid-Qing stood on the middle of the old street. It was five bays wide and three courts deep (three courtyards, each with its hall, one behind another), 18 m (~59 ft) wide and 20 m (~66 ft) deep, its horse-head gable wall on the street 8 m (~26 ft) high and its fire walls 5 m (~16 ft) high on the other three sides.
  • A pawnshop in the old Chinese town of Yaowan kept its silver vault and pledge store in its front court.
    The Suqian county gazetteer records two pawnshops in the town in the early Qing. The Baofengcui pawnshop, on the town's West Street, ran back four courts: the front court held the silver vault and the pledge store, the rear court offices and living quarters.
  • No page we read gives the size of a pledge store.
    Nor does any give the size of a Japanese pawnbroker's court, how many storehouses a Japanese pawnbroker kept, or whether a walled court stood behind the shop.
