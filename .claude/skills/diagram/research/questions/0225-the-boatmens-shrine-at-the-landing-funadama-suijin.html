The boatmen's shrine at the landing (funadama, suijin)

Boatmen kept two kinds of god. The boat's own guardian spirit, the funadama, lived aboard, and was sometimes also given a shrine on land. The river boatmen of Katsushika ward kept a water god (suijin) ashore, with a festival of their own, but the one record of it we read dates it no earlier than the twentieth century. The Suitengu faith in a water god is older, but before 1868, when Japan began to modernize, no page we read ties it to boatmen. Nor does any page we read place a shrine of either kind at a landing itself.

  • A boat's guardian spirit, the funadama, is kept aboard.
    Dolls, copper coins, human hair, the five grains, dice and the like are placed at the foot of the boat's post, in a place called mori or tsutsu.
  • Where in the boat is the funadama kept?
    The encyclopedias differ. One says it is in many cases a hole cut in the bow where the mast is stood; another says the middle of the boat, and in the sailing-ship era the foot of the mast.
  • Some places give a boat only a talisman from a shrine or temple, pasted on.
    An encyclopedia says so; the talisman takes the place of the sacred objects set into the boat.
  • The boat's guardian is sometimes also given a shrine on land.
    The Japanese encyclopedia article on the funadama says so, without saying where on land.
  • Fishermen and shipwrights are the funadama's chief worshippers.
    The Japanese encyclopedia article on the funadama says so.

The river boatmen of Katsushika kept a different god ashore: a water god.

  • Nearly every village in Katsushika worshipped a water god.
    The ward's history says so of its villages.
  • At Niijuku in Katsushika, the boatmen led the water god's festival, on their boats.
    The festival was led by the boatmen of the boats carrying night soil and sand, who took their washed boats out onto the Naka river for it, hung with curtains, with music and food aboard.
  • At Koyano the night-soil boatmen kept a water-god shrine called Suitengu.
    The ward history says it was kept by the boatmen of the night-soil boats that plied the Ayase river.
  • The Koyano shrine once stood on the riverbank, and was later moved twice.
    It formerly stood on the bank where the Ayase and the old Ayase rivers part. The cutting of the Arakawa floodway moved it beside the Sumida sluice gate, and it was later moved again, into the precinct of an Inari shrine.
  • On the Koyano festival day the boatmen moored by the bank and spent the day in the precinct.
    The ward history says that on arriving they moored their boats near the bank of the Ayase and spent the day drinking there.
  • Did a boatmen's shrine of either kind stand at a landing itself?
    No page we read places a boat's guardian's shrine on land, or a boatmen's water-god shrine, at a landing, or gives either a size. The ward history puts the Koyano shrine on a riverbank and does not call the place a landing.
  • How old is the boatmen's water-god custom?
    The ward history dates what it records only to the twentieth century. Its earliest time for the festivals is the early Showa era, from the late 1920s, when they were held with spirit in many villages, and it names a votive plaque of 1928. It gives the Koyano Suitengu's first site only as "formerly", and dates nothing before the Showa era.

Before the modern period, the water gods are in the record but the boatmen are not.

  • The Suitengu faith reached Edo in 1818, late in the Edo period, from the lords of the Kurume domain.
    The lords of Kurume revered their Suitengu for its powers against water disasters and for safe birth, and in 1818 enshrined it at their residence in Edo, where its faith spread to the neighbors.
  • Was the Suitengu shrine at Kurume already a guardian of boats before 1868?
    One encyclopedia says the shrine was built around 1648-52 and calls it a guardian of boats, but in the present tense and with no date for that role.
  • When did the water-god shrine on the Sumida river in Edo draw the river trades?
    The shrine was the guardian of the river and drew the faith of those who worked on it, water-transport operators and boat-hire houses among them, but the Japanese encyclopedia article on the shrine gives no date for that faith.
  • Did boatmen keep a water-god shrine before the modern period?
    No page we read places boatmen keeping one before 1868.
