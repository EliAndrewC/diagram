How our maps draw water gates and sluices (shuimen)

Wherever a canal passes through a city wall our maps draw a water gate: an arch on two piers over the canal, with the bars of its grille across the water, and the canal running visibly beneath it. A sluice is drawn as a timber board across its channel between two posts, with the crossbeam and windlass that lifted it, and its name beside it.

  • The bars across a water gate stand for the gates that shut it.
    They stand for the gear that closed the passage: the sluice gate and palisade of Pan Gate, a land and water gate in Suzhou's city wall, and the lattice gates that Kaifeng, the Song dynasty's capital, let down to the water at night. How far the wall is opened for it is at How our maps draw a city's gates (chengmen).

The rule the map follows for a water gate, though not every map draws it so yet:

  • The passage through the wall is as wide as its canal.
    That is a GUESS: no page we read gives a water gate's width.
  • A grille crosses the water at both the outer and the inner face of the wall.
    So Pan Gate was built, with a sluice gate and a palisade at the front and at the back.
  • The channel between the two grilles is lined with stone on both sides.
    Pan Gate had revetments along the channel between its two gates.
  • A canal passes through one opening.
    That is a GUESS: no page we read counts the openings of Pan Gate's water passage.
  • A river crossing the wall is drawn as a row of arched openings.
    So Nanjing's East Water Gate carried the Qinhuai River through the wall, through eleven vaulted openings.
  • How many water gates a city has is a knob, set at random for each map.
    Either every land gate has a water gate beside it, as at Suzhou, and the map lays its canals to reach them, or a water gate stands only where a watercourse crosses the wall, beside a land gate when one is near and alone when not, as at Kaifeng.

A sluice is drawn wherever one channel hands its water to another: where an offtake from a moat or river feeds a field's own canal, or where a field's drain empties into its outfall culvert.

  • A sluice is drawn as its frame above the water.
    The board between its two posts, the crossbeam over them and the windlass drum at the beam's center are the part of a sluice that shows from above. That a sluice had such a frame is our guess from how a lifting gate works, a GUESS.
  • The frame and its drum are drawn larger than life.
    They are drawn at the smallest size the map draws anything, a map drawing convention, as the wells are: a windlass drum about 2 ft across would be under a pixel.
  • A field channel's sluice spans about 16 to 24 ft, and a wider channel's spans bank to bank.
    On the 66 ft feeder channels of Shiro Daika, a domain capital our maps draw, the frame is stretched until its posts stand on the banks. In this record's reading, a GUESS, a real frame spanned from abutment to abutment, its keeper walking the crossbeam.
  • The board lies across the channel it blocks, a few steps down the channel from the junction.
    Set a few steps down from the junction, the board plainly crosses the channel it blocks, not the one that channel branches from.
  • Every sluice is one glyph, with no open or shut form.
    In this record's reading, a GUESS, a moat's gates stood partly raised and were adjusted a few inches at a time, which at 3 ft to the pixel is too small to see. Separate open and shut glyphs would show a state the gates did not have. The state worth showing, a drawdown or a siege, is an event, for a caption on the day it matters.
  • A sluice carries its name.
    At the whole map's zoom a sluice reads as a bare dark bar, so the word does the explaining.
  • Shiro Daika's moat is fed at its east and drains at its southwest.
    Its water enters by the feeder channel on the east, runs round the ring and leaves at the southwest, so it has a gate at each end, its water running through rather than standing.
  • A field's sluice opened and shut with the rice season, but our maps give it no month.
    No page from before 1868 that we read gives the months a rice field's intake opened and shut.
