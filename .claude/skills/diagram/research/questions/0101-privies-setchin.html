Privies (setchin)

In a samurai residence the privy - the kawaya or setchin - was part of the house: a urinal and a privy were built as a pair, attached to its principal buildings, not set out as a hut in the yard. A house might keep two, an upper one for the head of the household and the men and a lower one for the women. In townhouses and farmhouses, a guest parlor had a privy at its rear.

What collected beneath a privy had a price. Night soil was the farmer's fertilizer, bought from town households by the year and carried off by collectors from outside, and a merchant house on a back street kept its back gate for them.

  • A samurai house built its privies into the house.
    In the residences of samurai, court nobles and Buddhist priests, a Japanese dictionary says, a urinal and a privy were built as a pair, attached to the principal buildings. Within the residence, a site on the history of house plans adds, the privy came to be built in a corner of the corridor.
  • The row houses inside a samurai compound shared a small privy building.
    In the row houses and the women's apartments inside a compound, no privy was built for each unit: one urinal and several privies were gathered into a small building of their own nearby.
  • Were a samurai house's privies divided between men and women?
    A writer on the history of the toilet divides the privies of samurai houses into an upper one, for the head of the household and the men of the family, and a lower one that the women, even of the master's family, were trained to use - without saying from when. The site that quotes the book traces the idea of two privies in one house back to the Kamakura period, when the age of the warrior houses began.
  • Did a district magistrate's house of the Edo period, built in 1794, have two privies from the start?
    The Yokota family of Matsushiro held about 150 koku and served as district magistrates, and an inscription on a pillar dates their main house to 1794. The town history counts one bath and two privies in it, but does not say whether both privies were there from the start, and it names rooms added before about 1868.
  • Townhouses and farmhouses with a guest parlor had a privy at the parlor's rear.
    A Japanese dictionary says that where a guest parlor was provided, in townhouses and farmhouses alike, a urinal and a privy stood at its rear.
  • An official hall of the Sayama domain had two upper privies with board verandas.
    The council hall (hyojosho) of the Sayama domain's jin'ya had, with its genkan, its shikidai and a zashiki with a tokonoma, two upper privies, each with a board veranda.
  • Did the Sayama domain's upper-rank samurai houses have the same plan as its council hall?
    The page's author surmises so, because the genkan, the shikidai and the tokonoma mark a samurai house's standing.
  • At the Sayama domain's middle-rank samurai houses, the privy and the bath were meant to be added on.
    The same page on the domain's houses says so, whether the domain supplied an old house, which it repaired, or the money for one.
  • Where did a samurai house put its family's privy?
    No page we read places a family privy near the living rooms of a samurai house before 1868. The nearest forms read are later or humbler. Urban houses after Meiji first set an upper privy at the rear of the parlor and a lower one for the family, jutting out near the living rooms. In the row houses of a daimyo's retainers, in the Edo period, the privy stood at the tip of a board floor projecting past a corridor.
  • Did a master's privy have a sand box beneath it, to inspect his health?
    No page we read says so. A Japanese dictionary names only the sand privy, whose waste was covered with sand and carried away with it.

What went into a privy was sold, not thrown away, and the trade in it decided who came to a house to empty it.

  • Night soil was a good with a price.
    A Japanologist notes that in Japan human excreta were not something one paid to have removed, but a product with a positive economic value.
  • Around Kanazawa, farmers bought a town household's night soil by the year, for rice.
    Farmers 12 to 20 km (~7-12 miles) out held annual contracts with town households: the farmer took all the household's night soil for the year and paid a fixed amount of rice, five to, a measure of about 90 liters (~24 gallons), for a household of seven to ten.
  • In Edo's tenements, the landlord sold the tenants' night soil.
    The landlord held the rights to the tenants' night soil, so the farmers made their contracts with the landlord and paid in cash.
  • Farmers paid in goods for the right to empty the privies of Edo castle and the daimyo estates.
    In the early eighteenth century, well-off farmers close to the warrior households supplied Edo castle and the daimyo estates with firewood, charcoal and seedlings, and in return had the right to clean and collect the wastes of their toilets.
  • The collectors were farmers, who came with carts.
    A local farmer brought a cart and bought up all the night soil of a communal toilet.
  • A merchant house on a back street kept a back gate for the collectors.
    A site on the history of house plans says of the merchant houses on back streets, from Edo times until before the Second World War, that the back wicket gate was there for the night-soil collection. It says nothing of a samurai house's gate.
  • How far did night soil travel?
    Not far: within about 3 to 5 km (~2-3 miles) of most rural towns, and up to 20 km (~12 miles) around Edo.
  • Around Edo, boats carried night soil on the canals.
    A fertilizer boat carried fifty loads at a time, 25 to 50 times what carriage on land could, and the canals made the trade cheaper than the roads.
