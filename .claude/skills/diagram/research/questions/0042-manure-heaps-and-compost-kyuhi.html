Manure heaps and compost (kyuhi)

Stable manure (kyuhi) was the dung of a farm's cattle and horses, trodden into straw and fallen leaves until it rotted, and it went onto the fields as fertilizer. The household's night soil was rotted too, in a jar buried in the ground or a plastered pit, because fresh night soil harms the crop. Where the night-soil store stood is at Farm privies and their night soil (benjo).

The practice is old: farm manuals in Song China and in Edo-period Japan already tell the farmer to keep a house for the manure. No page we read says where in the yard the heap stood, or how many farms had one before Japan began to modernize in 1868.

  • Stable manure was the byre's litter, trodden by the beasts until it rotted.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on stable manure describes it as livestock dung and urine mixed with straw, fallen leaves and the like, and trodden by cattle and horses until it rots.
  • Night soil was rotted in a buried jar or a plastered pit.
    The vessel had to hold its liquid without leaking. The pit looks like a well; the jar was often unglazed earthenware, about 1 to 1.5 m (~3-5 ft) across the mouth, buried in the ground.
  • How long did night soil take to rot?
    One to two weeks in summer and three to four in winter, by the Japanese Wikipedia article on night soil, which also cites the Sendai city history for a year of fermenting.
  • Two Japanese farm manuals of the late 1600s, early in the Edo period, tell the farmer to rot dung before it goes on the fields.
    The Hyakusho Denki, of 1681, says fresh filth on a dry field brings disease and insects to the crop, and that it should be rotted well in the storage jar; the Nogyo Zensho says fresh dung works poorly and ripened dung works well. Both are read as the Japanese Wikipedia article on night soil quotes them, not in their own texts. The Nogyo Zensho was published in 1697.
  • A village record of 1754, in the Edo period, has night soil, bran and ash spread with leaves, straw and turf on the dry fields.
    Ogawa village's record, in what is now Kodaira city, lists them.
  • The history of Kodaira city, where Ogawa village stood, says fallen leaves were made into compost, but gives no date.
    It says that fallen leaves were made into compost as a home-made manure, without tying this to the 1754 record or to any year.

The manuals of China and Japan both called for a building to keep the manure in.

  • A Chinese farm manual of 1149, under the Song dynasty, calls for a manure house beside every farm dwelling.
    Chen Fu's manual, finished in 1149 under the Song dynasty, says the manure house is to be low-eaved, to keep off wind and rain, because manure left out under the sky loses its richness. In it a deep pit is dug and lined with brick, and swept-up earth, ash, chaff, broken straw and fallen leaves are heaped, burned and soaked with liquid manure.
  • The Nogyo Zensho tells the farmer to fit out a manure house and, where there are many beasts, to heap their dung like a small mountain.
    In it night soil, rotting things and the kitchen's and the bath's dirty water are stored until they rot down; the dung of cattle and horses is piled layer on layer, heaped like a small mountain where there are many beasts. It is read here in a modern Japanese rendering by Japan's Environment Ministry, which dates the book 1696, and which does not say where the pile stood.

Where the heap stood, and how many farms had one, is less certain.

  • No page we read says where in the yard the heap stood.
    A 1959 survey of farm privies places the night-soil tank but not the manure heap, and two surveys of farm outbuildings count manure sheds without placing them.
  • How many farms kept a heap or a manure house before 1868?
    No count says. Chen Fu's Chinese manual of 1149 and the Japanese Nogyo Zensho are advice, calling for a manure house on every farm, and neither says how many farms had one. Neither of the two village counts from before 1868 that we read lists a manure shed (see The farmstead and what stood on it (yashiki)).
  • A modern count, of 1972, found about one compost shed to every four of the older farmhouses.
    Sugiura's survey of July 1972, of all 87 households in three hamlets in Miyagi prefecture, in northeastern Japan, counts 0.24 compost sheds a household on the farms whose main house was built in or before 1944, and 0.14 across all the farms. It counts the shed, not the open heap.
