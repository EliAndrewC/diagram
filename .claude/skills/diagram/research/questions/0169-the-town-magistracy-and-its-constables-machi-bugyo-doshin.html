The town magistracy and its constables (machi-bugyo, doshin)

A city in China and in Japan alike kept an office of government for its own streets, below the governor's or the lord's. A Chinese prefectural city commonly held a county yamen as well as the prefect's, and in the Edo period a Japanese town magistrate (machi-bugyo) ran the administration and justice of a city's town quarters. Each of Edo's two magistracies stood on some two acres, where the magistrate lived as well as worked, with 25 officers (yoriki) and 100 constables (doshin); the officers and constables lived apart from it, on plots of their own in one group residence.

In this setting a city is policed by its Ministry of Justice, which oversees street policing, bandit patrols, magistrates, courts and jails.

  • In the late Qing a prefectural city commonly held a county yamen as well as the prefect's.
    A prefecture commonly had one county attached to its seat, and within the one city stood both a prefectural yamen and a county yamen, each with its own institutions, such as its school and Confucian temple.
  • A large prefectural city or provincial capital could hold two or three county yamen.
    The larger prefectural cities and provincial capitals often had two or even three attached counties.
  • In Japan the town magistrate had charge of a city's town quarters, under the shogunate and the domains alike.
    The post was in charge of the administration and justice of the town quarters (machikata), and the domains set it up as well as the shogunate.
  • Edo's two magistracies stood inside two of the castle's gates.
    The North magistracy moved inside the Tokiwabashi gate in 1631, and the South inside the Kajibashi gate in 1698.
  • How big was an Edo magistracy?
    About 2,630 tsubo for the North and about 2,720 for the South - roughly 9,000 square meters (~97,000 sq ft, ~2.2 acres) each. The figures are from a blog that repeats them without naming its source.
  • Each Edo town magistrate lived in the magistracy.
    The town magistrate's official residence was inside the magistracy.
  • Each of Edo's two magistracies had 25 officers and 100 constables.
    Each of the two, North and South, had 25 yoriki and 100 doshin.
  • Edo's North magistracy was office on the east, residence on the west, and court in the south-east.
    The South was built nearly the same. The officers' and constables' rooms were in the north-east, and the court, with its white-sand yard (shirasu), interrogation rooms and holding cell, took the widest quarter, the south-east.
  • Edo's magistracy officers and constables lived apart from the magistracies, in a group residence at Hatchobori.
    Their residences were placed together at Hatchobori, on ground the shogunate granted to the corps as one block.
  • How big was an officer's or a constable's plot at Hatchobori?
    About 300 tsubo, some 991 square meters (~10,670 sq ft), for an officer, and about 100 tsubo, some 331 square meters (~3,560 sq ft), for a constable.
  • How large was the Hatchobori ground?
    About 40,000 tsubo, some 132,000 square meters (~1,423,000 sq ft, ~33 acres), in the Kaei era, around 1850.
  • The Hatchobori ground housed the officers and constables of both of Edo's magistracies.
    It was divided into plots for 50 yoriki and 200 doshin - the 25 officers and 100 constables of each magistracy, twice over.
  • The officers and constables at Hatchobori let part or all of their plots to tenants.
    They rented out part of the residence, or all of it, and took the rent.

The setting's own offices of justice differ from both traditions: it names no town magistrate, so this project reads a provincial city's town magistracy as its Ministry of Justice office. The magistrates it does name are its own.

  • The Emperor keeps one magistrate in each domain, at its capital.
    One Imperial Magistrate is stationed in every domain, at the domain capital. The magistrate's compound is at The Imperial Magistrate's compound in a capital.
  • A few Imperial officers in each provincial city audit the tariffs at its gates.
    A small station of Imperial yoriki in each provincial city audits the tariffs at its gates and reports to the Imperial Magistrate at the domain capital.
  • Clan and Family magistrates hear the disputes between their own houses and families.
    A dispute between two samurai of one house is a local matter, for that house's Ministry of Justice. One between houses of a Family goes to the Family magistrate, and one between families of a Clan to the Clan magistrate.
  • Family and Clan magistrates also oversee their Family's or Clan's tax collection.
    An Ikoma Family magistrate oversees the Ikoma Family's tax collection, and a Lion Clan magistrate the Lion Clan's.
