Clustered and scattered villages (shūson, sanson)

Most farming villages in Japan were clustered: the farmhouses gathered in one limited area and the fields lay around them. A few regions had scattered villages instead, each farmhouse standing apart among its own fields - on the Tonami plain of Toyama, thousands of farmhouses spread across the plain. Some clustered villages were row villages instead, their houses strung in a line along a natural levee, a spring line at a hill's foot, or a road.

The form followed the ground and the water. Where a river crossed a lowland, the old settlements took the natural levee, higher, drier ground used for dry fields, with the paddy on the back lowland behind it, while on the Tonami plain, an alluvial fan, the farmers built their houses in the middle of their own rice fields so that each could manage the water for their own fields.

  • In Japan the clustered village is the general form.
    The farmhouses gather in a limited area, and the arable land is laid out around them.
  • Among clustered villages generally, the block village is the commonest form.
    The block village, the kaison, ranks above the street village strung along a road. The ranking is a general one, not made for Japan alone.
  • How tightly a clustered village gathered differed by region.
    Around the old capital and the Inland Sea, dense villages of farmhouses behind walls, standing side by side, are common; in the Kanto and the Tohoku, loosely clustered villages are common, each house on a wide lot with its own grove, the houses set well apart.
  • How common was each form of village?
    No page we read gives a national count or share of the clustered, block, street or scattered forms, for any period. So the clustered village can be called the usual form and the scattered village a regional one, with no number on either.

Two clustered villages could look nothing alike, because each took its shape from its own site.

  • In a river's lowland, the old villages stood on the natural levee, the high, dry ground beside the paddy.
    Old settlements were sited first on the natural levee, a slight rise that drains well within the wet lowland, with the paddy on the back lowland behind it. Which way a farmhouse turned on its site, its back to the hill and its face to the water, is at How close does a farmhouse stand to the paddy?.
  • A clustered village's shape followed its density, its ground and its roads.
    A clustered village takes various forms by its housing density, its ground and its traffic. Settlement geography names three: houses gathered irregularly in a lump; houses in a line along a natural levee or a spring line at a hill's foot; and houses in a line along a road.
  • A lump-shaped village grew at a hill's foot, on a river terrace or at a crossroads.
    The lump village grows at the foot of a hill or plateau, on a river terrace or other narrow ground where water can be got, or at a highway crossing.

How tightly a clustered village packed its houses is said only in comparison.

  • A clustered village's houses stood closer together than a scattered village's, but by no fixed measure.
    A clustered village is by definition one whose houses stand packed more closely than a scattered village's, but there is no definition of how dense it must be to count as one.
  • A modern survey published in 1956 counted houses within 200 m (~656 ft) of each other as one settlement.
    A survey of the Kanto lowland, as its own counting convention, counted houses within 200 m (~656 ft) of each other as one settlement and farther apart as two, because a household farming about 1 cho, about 1 hectare (~2.5 acres) - the average the survey reports for its two districts - needed about that much room. It is a modern geographer's rule, not one the villagers kept.
  • How much of its ground did a clustered village build on?
    No page we read gives a clustered village's built share of its ground, its houses to the hectare or the size of its house plots, nor, from before Japan began to modernize in 1868, any rule or measure of how far apart its houses stood.

A few regions had scattered villages instead.

  • Scattered villages are found on the Tonami, Sanuki and Izumo plains and the lower Oi river, among others.
    A Japanese encyclopedia gives the Tonami plain, the lower Oi river, the Sanuki plain and the Izumo plain as examples of the irregular scattered village.
  • The Tonami plain's landscape of scattered farms has been over 500 years in the making.
    Its farmhouses, each surrounded by its own woodland, stand on an alluvial fan built mainly by the Sho and Oyabe rivers: over 7,000 of them, across about 220 km² (~85 sq mi).
  • Why did the Tonami farms scatter?
    To manage water: the farmers built their houses in the middle of their own fields to look after the water for those fields, and the houses scattered apart naturally - that no plan lay behind it is this page's reading of the word. On the middle of an alluvial fan the river sinks underground, so the water table is low and the ground hard to use for paddy; that the answer was for each household to control its own water by living on its holding is this page's reading.
  • The domain lord's terms for reclaimed land are thought a major reason the Tonami houses scattered.
    The reclaimed fields belonged to the domain lord, but the peasant who had reclaimed them was allowed to farm them themselves.
  • Each Tonami farm stands in its own grove, the kainyo.
    The grove sheltered the house and its household from the cold seasonal wind and snowstorms of winter and from the summer sun. Kainyo is the local name for the homestead grove found across Japan, at Groves of trees around farmhouses (yashikirin).
  • A Tonami farm's own water was often a channel led into its grounds, not a well.
    On the fan the water table lay deep and wells were hard to dig, so in many areas a small channel was led off the irrigation water into the house's grounds and used for cooking, washing and drinking. How the other areas drew their water no page we read says. A village's shared water is at Washing places at the water's edge and Communal wells (ido).
  • Were the valley farms of the late Heian period scattered too?
    Probably. On two estates of the late Heian period, in Satsuma and Bingo, the paddies lay almost without exception in small valleys, and a narrow valley is thought to have held one house or a few, each beside its own small valley paddy, dotted as small villages or single farmsteads up each branching valley.

A village strung in a row along a line is recorded in Europe and in China as well as in Japan.

  • In a German row village, the Reihendorf, each farm's land lay beside its house.
    It is one or two rows of farmsteads, either side of the village street, and the farmland of each dwelling lies next to it, which saves time and the effort of carrying.
  • In the hills of southwest Zhejiang, in China, villages also stretched along rivers and roads.
    The traditional villages of mountainous southwest Zhejiang are mostly clusters and bands, held in by the ground, and tend to expand in the direction of rivers and roads.
  • The Japanese row villages whose layout is recorded were planned colonies on new fields.
    Japanese row villages ran along roads on new fields and along levees, dikes and spring lines (How was a row village laid out?), but those whose layout is recorded were laid out as colonies on new fields, so for a row that grew by itself the record stands on weaker ground than for the scattered village.
  • At Santome, a planned new-field colony, each farm had a long strip of equal area behind its house.
    At Santome the farmhouses stood along both sides of a road about 10.9 m (~36 ft) wide, each with its field and woodlot running behind it in a long strip; in Kamitome each house had a frontage of about 72.7 m (~239 ft), a depth of about 681.8 m (~2,237 ft) and an area of about 49,500 m² (~12 acres).
  • In a row village the line came before the houses.
    On planned new fields the road was laid first, and on a levee, a dike or a spring line the ground set the line (Along what a row ran, and what came first).

A village's water and the fields around it took their shape from the land, and from how the land had been divided.

  • Irrigation water could be let down through a chain of ponds.
    Water released from a valley pond was stored in the ponds on the flat and from there let onto the fields, and several ponds strung together in a terrace-like series have names of their own, the stacked pond or the parent-and-child pond.
  • A surveyed field was laid out in squares of 1 cho, the jori grid.
    The grid lines crossed at right angles at intervals of 1 cho, about 109 m (~358 ft).
  • Not a few jori grids were tilted off the compass.
    The jori lines usually ran north-south and east-west, but not a few were tilted with the region or the ground.
