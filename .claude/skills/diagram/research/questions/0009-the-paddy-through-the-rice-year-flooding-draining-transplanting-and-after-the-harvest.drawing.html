How our maps show the paddy through the rice year

Our maps show the paddies at one moment of the rice year: high summer, the basins flooded and the rice green. The seasonal things that come and go on the same ground - the midsummer drain (nakaboshi), the winter crop, the straw ricks, the spring rape - are set aside for seasonal maps of their own, which are not yet drawn, though whether a settlement has a winter crop still shapes its dry fields.

  • Rice basins are drawn one green, with the odd plot freshly flooded or ripening early.
    A district of the plains transplanted within seven to ten days, on a calendar set by the crop before the rice (a valley district with water at any hour could take more than twenty), so at any moment its basins stood largely at one stage of growth - our reconstruction. The spread that early and late varieties give is left out, since no page we read gives its size; on some maps about one rice plot in sixteen is drawn still flooded and one in twenty gold, ripening early. The bund network and the footpath dots carry the field's structure instead of a rainbow of stages.
  • A different color on cultivated ground, beyond those few rice plots, means a different kind of ground.
    A blue-green basin is one of the low plots on the drain collector, pooling before the outfall (at How our maps draw wet paddies (shitsuden)). A plot on the upslope margin drawn in its own color, with its own furrow direction, is a dry crop. The other plots a map may recolor beside its rice hold only uses that last all year: a dike-pond, a lotus paddy, a hill tea garden. A different color is never decoration.
  • The paddies are drawn flooded, one moment of a season that runs from flooded to cracked and back.
    That season is old: a Chinese farm manual completed in 544 already drains the paddy to firm the roots and again before the rice ripens.
  • Nothing on the map depends on how deep the water stood.
    The drawn bund is a width, not a height (at How our maps draw bunds between the paddies (aze)), and the flooded basin is a flat color. The depths our research gives for the season's water are modern advice to farmers from agricultural agencies, not history, and the map's pop-up for a paddy says so.
  • Which settlements' paddies would a midsummer map drain?
    Where farmers drained their paddies in midsummer to firm the rice's roots, a map set then would draw cracked mud where this one draws water. A settlement whose water is plentiful could have its paddies drained and cracking; one short of water keeps its paddies flooded, as terraced paddies on unreliable water do not drain today. Such a map is a possible knob, not built.
  • The winter crop is a knob: some settlements' paddies carry one, others lie bare.
    Both forms are attested, so the choice is made at random, settlement by settlement. A double-cropped settlement's dry paddies are green with barley over the winter, while its wet paddies, which never dry, cannot be. A single-cropped settlement's paddies are left as reaped ground. A settlement's drainage and the nearness of a town, whose manure a second crop needed, set the odds, and how the two weigh against each other is a GUESS.
  • Whether a settlement's paddies carry a winter crop changes its dry fields on a summer map.
    A summer map draws neither winter crop, but the choice still shows. A settlement whose paddies lie bare over the winter grows its coarse grain in dry fields instead, and on an alluvial fan whose middle is still wild, those dry fields are widened to hold it (at How our maps decide where a farming hamlet grows its coarse grain).
  • Our maps draw no rape flowers beside the rice.
    Rape is sown in autumn and cut in spring, and comes off as the rice goes in, so the two are never both standing: a map that mixed them at any share of its plots would show two seasons at once. Rape belongs to a seasonal map, where the whole field would turn yellow.
  • No straw ricks are drawn.
    The rick stood on the reaped paddy and its bunds from the autumn, so a summer map has none. A rick on the paddy floor must have been gone before the paddy was flooded - our reasoning, not a source's - and no page we read says whether one on a bund still stood at midsummer, so leaving them off rests on setting the seasonal features aside, not on a finding that they were gone.
  • Where would a winter map put its ricks?
    A seasonal map of the autumn after the harvest would draw ricks on the reaped paddies and their bunds; how far into the winter they stood, no page we read says. On a paddy sown to winter barley, a rick on the floor would stand in the barley's way, so there the bund is the likelier place - a GUESS.
