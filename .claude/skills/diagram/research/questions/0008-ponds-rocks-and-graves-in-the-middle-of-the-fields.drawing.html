How our maps place ponds, rocks and graves in the fields

Our maps put three things other than rice inside a paddy field: a small pond, an outcrop of rock and a family's grave. That flat, flooded paddy on a valley floor hosts such things least, being the most valuable and most intensively worked ground, is this project's reasoning; no source states it. A small open-water pond is the one of the three that belongs in the wet middle of a field, since a plains pond was dug into a hollow or low wet ground. Which a field may carry depends on its kind:

  • Our maps sink each pond wholly inside one low plot.
    It is drawn with a fringe of reeds inside a single low plot, never across a bund, as a low pocket too deep for rice.
  • How often does a field keep a pond?
    A little over half the time, where it has low plots. The rate is a GUESS, disclosed as one: no page we read gives a rate per field or per village, and the dense pond country of Kagawa bounds it from above without setting it. It is chosen so that a pond reads as an occasional feature of a valley floor, not a fixture of every field.
  • The dike-pond ground of a delta has no pond among its plots.
    Our maps draw that ground as fish ponds and the dikes between them, so a pond among its plots would add nothing.
  • Rock outcrops are drawn only on terraces and narrow valley strips.
    They are treated as bedrock the terrace walls wrap around, absent from the alluvial ground of a broad valley, a polder or a delta. That is a GUESS: no page we read speaks of rocks kept in a paddy at all. How many outcrops a field keeps, as the table above gives it, is a guess too.
  • An outcrop stands off-center in its plot.
    Set off-center, it reads as an obstacle the field was cut around.
  • About three maps in ten with a broad valley, terraces or a narrow valley strip draw a grave in the fields.
    No source gives a rate, so the share is this project's choice, and an arbitrary one.
  • A grave stands either as an island inside a plot or in a plot's corner against its bunds.
    How our maps choose between the two is at How our maps choose where a village's and a hamlet's dead lie.
  • No flooded basin runs under a field grave: the paddy is carved round it.
    An island's host plot is split in two through the mound, as if a bund were run out to the island, and each half stops short of the mound. A corner grave stands a short step in from its corner, clear of any ditch or path along the plot's edge, and the plot's bund is carried round it out to the corner (How our maps choose where a village's and a hamlet's dead lie). How the bunds met a grave is on no page we read, so the split host and the bund round the corner grave are a GUESS.
  • Town and city maps draw no ponds, rocks or graves in their fields.
    On a town or city map the fields are a slice of the county's farmland at a finer scale, where these small signs read literally - a grave island as a paupers' bone heap on the paddy, a pond as water spilled over the rice - so their fields stay plain.
  • The crescent pond in front of some villages is not a pond in the fields.
    It stands before the village, and it is at Half-moon ponds in south-China villages (banyuechi).
