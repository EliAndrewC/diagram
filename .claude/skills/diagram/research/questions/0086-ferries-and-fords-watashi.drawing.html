How our maps draw ferries and fords (watashi)

Where a road meets a river too wide to bridge, our maps end it at a ferry landing on each bank, or, on any road but the Imperial road, run it down into a ford. A stream, and any river narrow enough to bridge, is bridged instead, by the rules of How our maps place and draw road bridges (hashi), because on the Tokaido only the great rivers went unbridged and the lesser ones were bridged.

  • Whether a crossing is a ferry or a ford is a knob, chosen at random for each map.
    Both were how the Tokaido crossed the great rivers it left unbridged, so on a village's, a town's or a city's road neither is the rule.
  • Our maps never put a ford on the Imperial road.
    The setting's canon names bridges over major rivers and ferry stations among the Imperial road's engineered works, and this project reads that as the whole of its crossings.
  • On one bank, a ferry's landing has a river office, a notice board and a boatmen's hut.
    Each landing is a cleared stretch of bank where the road reaches the water, with boats drawn up at it. The Tokaido's Rokugo ferry had all three buildings at its landing on the Kawasaki bank, though there the far bank had a notice board too.
  • How many boats does a highway ferry keep?
    Up to about 14, the count kept by the Rokugo ferry, where the Tokaido crossed into Kawasaki.
  • How large are a highway ferry's boats?
    About 30 ft long for a foot boat, and about 43 by 9 ft for a large flat-bottomed boat (hiratabune), the sizes of the boats at the Tokaido's Banyu ferry.
  • A village's ferry keeps two or three boats, a GUESS.
    No page we read says how many boats a village's ferry kept.
