How our maps draw channel widths

Our maps store every channel's width in feet and draw it at that true width, placing each stroke on the ladder of channel widths - the ranked widths of real channels, from a field ditch to a city moat - by the ground it waters. Where a true width would be too fine to see, the stroke is drawn at a floor of 1.5 ft on a hamlet map, so the finest ditches never vanish. What the maps always keep is the order of the widths - moat, river, canal, lateral - and the size of the steps between them. A stroke's width shows its rank in that order, not the water it carries.

  • A field ditch is a hairline at the scale of the fields it waters.
    A ditch of about 0.3 m (~1 ft) beside a block of paddy about 100 m (~328 ft) on a side is about a three-hundredth of its width, and the widths from that ditch to the widest castle moat, about 90 m (~295 ft) at Osaka, span about 300 to 1. One sheet cannot draw both true: a 1 ft ditch on a provincial city's map, at 3 ft to the pixel, would be a third of a pixel.
  • The finest channels are drawn at a floor, and the floor is never a license to enlarge.
    A stroke draws at its true width or at the floor, whichever is larger. The large water is drawn true: a city moat at its real 66 ft, about as wide as Himeji's middle moat.
  • A channel is the same real size on every map.
    Widths are stored in feet and converted when drawn, as every other feature is. The one figure that depends on the map's scale is the floor, 1.5 ft on a hamlet map: true widths show at a hamlet's and a village's scale and vanish above it, and the coarser the map, the more of the ladder rests on the floor.
  • The floor enlarges the finest ditch a little, knowingly.
    A 1.2 ft delivery tail draws at 1.5 ft. A floor of about 2 ft would have served; this project chose 1.5 so that the finest ditches still show a taper rather than arriving already flat at the floor. Enlarging the widths themselves to make a coarse map read better would misstate every scale to fix one.
  • Whatever the scale, the widths keep their order and the steps between them.
    A field ditch at the floor, about 1.5 ft on a hamlet map; a pond feeder at roughly twice that; a village creek at roughly three and a half times it; a stream feeding a moat as wide as the moat itself, because the water that enters has to be carried; and a city moat heaviest of everything on the sheet. The ladder can never collapse into one blue line. This is a map drawing convention.
  • A pond is not ranked among the channel widths.
    A pond is a body of water, drawn as a shape at the scale of the paddies beside it; what sits on the ladder is its outlet. How our maps draw it is at How our maps draw reservoir ponds (tameike), their reeds and their embankments.

The irrigation net of a hamlet's fan of paddies is drawn at true size, and its widths were checked two ways: against a rough hydraulic calculation, and against the ladder.

  • How wide a channel does a hamlet's fan of paddies need?
    Roughly 2 to 5 ft, by how much bank is counted as the channel. The 8.5-hectare (~21-acre) fan of Inashiro, the reference hamlet map, needs roughly 0.013 cubic meters a second on the rough figure for rice, 1.5 liters a second for each hectare. Lacey's regime equation gives that a wetted perimeter near 0.55 m (~2 ft), and Manning's formula roughly a 0.45 m (~1.5 ft) top width on a hand-dug earthen section with 1:1 sides. The working is this project's.
  • Measured widths of real channels decide the drawn widths; the calculation only corroborates them.
    The ladder's rungs are measurements of real channels, while a Manning figure rests on four assumed values - roughness, grade, the section's shape, freeboard - and moves by a factor of two when they are nudged. So each stroke is placed on the ladder by the ground it waters, and a hamlet fan of 8.5 ha (~21 acres) lands its head race between the lateral and a small main canal.

  • Why is the head race 6.0 ft rather than 5.0?
    At 5.0 the three widest channels sat within a foot of each other, so the fork where the head race splits into its two supply canals, the one junction where the order most wants to read, read least. The head race carries both canals, and since a channel's width grows roughly as the square root of the water it carries, a trunk carrying a 4.5 ft and a 4.0 ft branch is the root of the sum of their squares: 6.0 ft. That picks a width within the ladder's band, using the law as a check; it is not the rule that widths add up at a junction, which the maps do not follow (below).
  • Do the drawn widths match channels dug before modern times?
    All but the drain's outfall come close. The head race's 6.0 ft is the width of the central canal of an excavated early paddy at the Hattori site, 1.4-2 m (~5-7 ft), and of the Jugo channel fixed by rule in 1537, just over six shaku. An Edo canal could run far wider, the Minuma canal of 1727 at about six ken, which is why a hamlet's trunk sits at the small end of the main-canal rung. The canals and delivery ditches, 4.5 ft down to 1.2 ft, sit between that canal and the finest rungs.
  • The drain's outfall is wider than the one early drain we found measured.
    That drain, at the same site, is 0.5-1 m (~2-3 ft) wide. The drawn drain passes through that width on its way from a 1.2 ft head to a 5.5 ft outfall; the outfall rests on the hydrology below and on the measurement on our own maps, not on a period figure.
  • The drain is drawn narrower than the head race.
    A collector is sized for draining the paddies and for storm runoff rather than for the irrigation water, so it may carry appreciably more than the supply ever delivered - a reading of the hydrology, not a sourced figure. Its 5.5 ft tail stays under the 6.0 ft head race all the same. Raising it was tried and measured on the reference hamlet: the wider band took the margin the settlement stands on, and the houses came to rest 102 ft from the field they work, against a 60 ft limit.
  • On Inashiro, drawn at a foot to the pixel, the order survives at true size.
    The widths in the table above span 4 to 1, from the 6.0 ft head race down to the 1.5 ft floor, because the floor sits below the finest rung rather than on top of it. The paddies dominate the map and the water reads as a fine net over them.

Because a stroke's width shows its rank, the widths where channels meet follow rules of their own.

  • The widths at a junction are not meant to add up.
    At the head of the fan the brook arrives narrower than the head race it feeds, and the head race forks into two canals whose widths together exceed its own, so drawn capacity seems to grow downstream at every junction. Each stroke is sized by its own rank and taper, with no matching across a junction. Each branch drawn narrower than its trunk is true to life; only their sum exceeding the trunk is convention.
  • Widths that add up at a junction were weighed and declined.
    Matched widths would chain every stroke down from the brook's and thin the far canals to hairlines, and the order trunk, branch, delivery would stop reading with the whole map on screen. So each stroke keeps its own taper and its place in that order. A stilling pool drawn at the intake, where the brook meets the head race, was weighed and declined as well.
  • Why might a head race run wider than the brook that feeds it?
    A weir impounds the water behind it, and the water ponds and slows there, so a head race fed from above a weir runs slower than the brook, and in this project's reading wider. There width shows more than rank alone.
  • A delivery ditch is never drawn wider than the canal feeding it.
    A branch drawn wider than its parent breaks the one thing width-as-rank is for. So a delivery ditch's head is capped at 0.8 of its canal's local width where it leaves it, and a sub-ditch between plots at 0.75 of its delivery ditch's head. The cap bites only low in the net, where the canal has already dwindled; at the first offtake the delivery keeps its full rung. Measured on Inashiro: no delivery is wider than its parent, every head 1.8-2.5 ft against a parent of 2.2-4.5 ft.
  • A delivery ditch is not sized by the water it takes from its canal.
    Sizing it exactly, by the width its canal loses at the offtake, would make widths add up at junctions by another name, and make a delivery's width depend on how steeply its canal happens to narrow there. The cap is the weaker and sufficient guarantee.
  • A ditch between plots is capped against the width its delivery ditch starts at, so it has room to narrow.
    Capped against the local width, about 2.2 ft a third of the way down, a sub-ditch would start at 1.64 ft, leaving 0.14 ft above the 1.5 ft floor to narrow in. Our automatic check that every ditch narrows along its run failed that on 22 of 24 test maps, rightly: a ditch that cannot narrow should not be drawn claiming to.

Drawing the net at true size sets the rules around it, and each was measured.

  • The paddies stand 5 ft back from a supply canal's water.
    The dry strip beside a canal is the stroke's local half-width plus a 5.0 ft berm: about the width of the bank an early Yayoi paddy kept along its canal, 2.6 to 4.9 ft (Where a field meets its ditch), plus room to stand for dredging. The dredging half of the berm is this project's own reason, a GUESS. Measured: a bare berm of 4.89 ft median, unbroken over a 1,592 ft chain.
  • Three corners at the fork keep less than the full berm.
    They keep 2.00, 2.19 and 2.90 ft against the 4.89 ft median. The berm runs along one canal's line and cannot clear a stroke crossing at an angle, and a fork is where three strokes meet. Invisible with the whole map on screen, and accepted.
  • The finest water is drawn exactly as wide as a paddy's bund.
    The floor is 1.5 ft on a hamlet map and so is the bund, so the finest ditch differs from the field edges it runs among only by color. A floor of 1.2 ft was tried and rejected: the floor also sets the room the bunds keep from a supply channel, and at 1.2 the plot seams broke on two of the twenty-four maps drawn for it that hold at 1.5. A small ambiguity in how the map looks does not buy two broken maps.
  • The bund, not the water, could be narrowed.
    The bund is 1.5 ft by its own research: an early Yayoi paddy's small bunds were about 0.7 to 2 ft, and an Edo survey reckoned a bund at 1 ft (Where a field meets its ditch). Taking it to about 1.3 ft would change only how the bund is drawn, not the room kept beside any channel. It has not been tried.
