How our maps draw a polder's dike and its trees

Our maps draw a polder's dike as a band of heaped earth all the way round the polder, up to about 40 ft across and changing in width along its length, as heaped mud patched year after year does, its outer face curving with the water's edge. Two rows of trees run along it, willow on the water side and mulberry on the field side, and the farmhouses keep their own windbreak groves besides.

  • How high and how broad is a village polder's dike?
    A dike's height and breadth were a matter of degree, set by the size of the polder and the water it faced, so this project treats them as a calibrated liberty rather than a choice between forms. What was read gives bounds and one village's breadth: a broad foot several times the height, narrowing to a top a person can walk along; a village's dike lower than a domain's river dike in Echizen, today's Fukui, of about 3.6 m (~12 ft) high on a foot of about 5.5 m (~18 ft); and, in one Lake Tai village of the 1930s, a raised polder margin 10 to 30 m (~33-98 ft) broad that served as the farm's dike (Polder dikes: what they were made of, how big, and what grew on them).
  • The maps take a small polder's dike to stand about 1.5 m (~5 ft) high.
    It is a GUESS, well below the Echizen river dike's 3.6 m (~12 ft) (Polders: fields diked against the fluctuating water (weitian, waju)).
  • The band is drawn 14 to 40 ft across, between the breadths read.
    Its narrower stretches are near the Echizen river dikes' foot of about 18 ft, and its broadest reach the low end of the Lake Tai village's raised margin, 10 to 30 m (~33-98 ft), which served as the farm's dike while it also held the mulberry and the houses (Polder dikes: what they were made of, how big, and what grew on them). That margin is of 1936, and no earlier village dike's breadth was found, so where between the two figures a village's dike stood is a calibrated liberty. The band is kept broader than the 23 ft crop dikes drawn round each fish pond (How our maps draw dike-ponds (sangji yutang)), so that the dike holding the water off the whole polder reads as the greater bank.
  • The band changes in width and is mottled, never a ruled line.
    A dike of heaped mud, patched year after year in this project's reading, as a dike-pond's dikes were raised and repaired with pond mud (Polder dikes: what they were made of, how big, and what grew on them), is drawn thicker in some stretches and pinched in others, and mottled with patches of earth of different ages, as repairs are.
  • The maps curve the dike's outer face with the water's edge and run its inner face along the fields inside.
    That a polder's outer dike followed the water's edge is this project's GUESS. Its inner face follows the edges of the parcels it encloses (Parcels and bunds inside a polder (aze)). Around Lake Tai a rectangular polder is a modern form (Polder dikes: what they were made of, how big, and what grew on them), and this project draws nothing that is only modern.
  • Two rows of trees run along the dike, one on each face of the bank.
    The willow row stands about three quarters of the way out from the field side, toward the water, and the mulberry row about a quarter of the way, on the field side. So the distance between the rows follows the width of the bank, and the maps draw to no figure for it.
  • The maps set the rows on the dike's faces, where a Ming river manual set them on the ground off its foot.
    This is a deliberate deviation: on the bank, the rows can be seen as the dike's own planting (Polder dikes: what they were made of, how big, and what grew on them).
  • The willows are drawn about 8.5 ft apart, looser than the Willow Palisade's planting.
    The Palisade's branches were planted every 5 chi (~5.5 ft). The maps space the willows wider, so their crowns read as a run of touching trees rather than one fused hedge.
  • The mulberry along the dike are drawn about 4.4 ft apart.
    Like the willows, they are spaced so their crowns read as a run of touching bushes rather than one fused hedge, as on the dike-pond's banks (Mulberry and other crops on pond dikes (sangji, guoji)).
  • Where a channel crosses the dike, the band is cut, and the rows stop with it.
    The water runs through a gap in the earthwork, under a sluice gate, not over its top (Dike-ponds: fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes (sangji yutang)).
  • The maps draw both the dike's trees and the farmhouses' groves.
    Neither stands in for the other: in this project's reading the trees on the dike, cut low and bare in winter, do little to shelter a village from the wind, and the homestead groves are a different planting for that job (Polder dikes: what they were made of, how big, and what grew on them).
