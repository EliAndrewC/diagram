How our maps lay out irrigation canals (yosuiro)

Our maps lay a settlement's water network out first and cut the paddies between its lines. The default layout is a comb: supply canals along the high margins of the fields, delivery ditches leaving them at right angles and running down the slope, and one drain gathering the water along the lowest line. Two other layouts are recorded for the ground that calls for them - a fan of canals on an alluvial fan, which the maps can draw, and the ancient jori grid, which they do not draw yet.

  • The comb is the default because the setting's villages are modeled on rice-growing China.
    Decentralized pond systems watered much of China's irrigated land - in the 1950s some 8.3 million of them served 39% of its irrigated area - and the setting's population follows the China of the Song dynasty and the Ming, so a village-scale network fed from its own source is the layout a map draws unless its ground calls for another.
  • The comb follows the Edo reclamation at Minuma.
    Supply along the raised edges and the drain down the low middle is how the Minuma reservoir was reclaimed in 1728, so the comb's supply canals along the high edge and its one collector on the low line are a form from before modern times, and the comb draws them that way.
  • On flat ground, each smaller canal leaves the one that feeds it at right angles.
    Channels meeting at right angles were the rule long before modern times - in the classical Chinese commentaries, where each grade runs crosswise to the next, the Lake Tai polders and Japan's jori grid - so the comb's canals meet at right angles.
  • Our maps give no plot a ditch of its own.
    A supply ditch giving every plot its own inlet, with its own outlet to the drain, is the standard of modern land consolidation, and no source we read shows it earlier, so the maps do not draw one. Below the last ditch drawn, water passes from plot to plot, the older form.
  • A canal is drawn narrowing as its branches leave it.
    The main canals gradually decrease in size as they are tapped by branch canals, so every drawn canal tapers from its head toward its tail. How much it narrows is at How wide canals and ditches are: the ladder of channel widths.
  • The smallest ditch drawn is the edge of the plot it serves.
    The smallest ditches were often counted as part of the paddies they watered, so the last ditch the map draws runs along the boundary of its plot rather than through open ground.
  • A pond drawn at the foot of a field ends its drainage.
    Where a map draws a pond below the fields to gather the water that leaves them, that siting is how the map ends the drain, and it is a GUESS: no page we read describes a drain ending in a pond, and every drain the record has read empties into a river or a natural watercourse. The pond's bank and outlet there are not drawn from a surveyed example either. How a drain lets its water go is at How our maps draw field drains (akusuiro).

The comb's supply reaches the fields as one channel, the head race, but paddy lies on both sides of it.

  • The head race forks into two supply canals, one on each flank.
    A canal waters only the ground below it, and the comb plants paddy on both sides of the division point (bunsuiguchi), so the drawn network shows both branches of the fork, as the two edge canals at Minuma did. On every hamlet map the second canal feeds at least one delivery ditch, so it is drawn partway down its edge, narrowing to a thread.
  • How much of each flank does the drawn supply reach?
    These figures are this project's choice, not a source's: a side of the fields too narrow to matter is left alone, and on a wider one the canal must be drawn far enough to be seen serving it. On every flank with more than about 150 ft of paddy across the slope from the fork, the drawn main or branch supply reaches at least 80 ft or 30% of that flank. Measured, every hamlet's short flank carries about 170-200 ft of drawn supply against about 250-260 ft of paddy.
  • A supply canal ends in one of two ways.
    Where the canal runs past its last offtake, its tail narrows to a thread, about 1.5 ft across, and ends at that width at the edge of the crop (Inashiro, Kashikawa, Mizuguchi); where it ends at its offtake, the canal hands its whole flow to the delivery ditch and stops at the junction (Sawada). What the maps never draw is a canal at its full working width cut off in the middle of an edge.
  • One supply canal is enough where the fields lie on one side of it.
    A strip of paddy along a contour canal, a flight of terraces and a ring of polder need no second canal and have no fork, so the rule for both sides does not touch them; nor does a fan whose far flank is a sliver under the 150 ft line.
  • Inside a settlement's own fields, the fork is drawn as a fork and nothing more.
    How water was shared at a split between villages, and what stood there, is at Sharing water between villages (bunsui), and how our maps draw such a split at How our maps draw a water split between villages (bunsui).

Of those two other layouts, the maps draw one.

  • How would canals be laid out on an alluvial fan?
    Where a valley opens onto an alluvial fan, the maps can draw gently descending canals spreading from the valley mouth over the fan. It is an option, not the default, and its radiating plan is a GUESS, as Irrigation canals and how they are laid out (yosuiro) says.
  • Our maps do not draw the jori grid yet.
    A long-settled plains village in the setting's old heartland, such as the home provinces of the Crane and Phoenix clans, would on this project's reading show semi-regular gridded paddies. The maps draw the irregular patchwork there too, a deviation on such ground (How our maps draw rice paddies and their plots (suiden)).
  • The irregular patchwork is right where fields follow the terrain.
    For villages whose fields follow the lie of the land, like Kikuta, the patchwork the maps draw is accurate: small irregular plots, not a grid (How our maps draw rice paddies and their plots (suiden)).
