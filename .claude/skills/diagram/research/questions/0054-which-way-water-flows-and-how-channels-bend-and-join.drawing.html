How our maps draw which way water flows, and how channels bend and join

Our maps carry no contour lines or any other marking of the ground's height, so the drawing has to show which way the water runs by its layout alone. Every map declares which way its land falls and which way its water leaves, and every watercourse which way it runs. Ditches turn on sweeps with a radius of about two and a half channel widths, a branch leaves its parent leaning downstream, and where channels meet their water is drawn as one body.

  • No map carries contour lines.
    Our maps already hold a great deal of detail, and contour lines would add clutter faster than they add understanding, so the ground's height is drawn nowhere. Leaving the markings off is a map drawing convention.
  • Every map declares its land's fall and its water's bearing, separately.
    With no markings, the terrain exists only in these figures - the land fall, the direction the ground slopes, and the drainage bearing, the direction the water leaves - and in the direction each watercourse carries, so every map must declare them. The two differ by design. A valley floor runs across the fall of the valley sides it lies between: Hoshizora's ground slopes south-southwest at 115 degrees (the maps' angles turn clockwise from east, so 90 is south) while its valley stream runs at 145, and a drain at Hikari no Sato runs 88 degrees off its map's fall. Deriving the flow from the slope would force every stream straight down the fall line, which is wrong for the valley floors and contour drains the maps already contain.
  • A map's water does not all point one way.
    The declarations are there to make a map read like a real place of its kind, not to make it uniform. Real ground rises and falls at small scale, so neighboring fields drain different ways: the comb fans on the map of Tango, a provincial city, span 210 degrees between them, and of the farms round its moat, some drain into it while others are fed by it.
  • A ditch may run across the slope, if it has a reason.
    Much of the water on a map is dug, and a ditch dug along a slope rather than down it is built to catch the water running downhill; that one may be dug to keep that water off something below it is this project's reading. So a watercourse running across the fall is allowed, and its reason is recorded: a collector gathering runoff along the low margin, a ditch protecting something, a canal for boats held level. A course with no descent and no purpose is the mistake.
  • A river at the foot of a mountain range runs down its valley, never level along the range.
    Some published maps of the game this setting is built on draw a river along the foot of a range, parallel to it - handsome, and impossible. Water at the foot of a slope runs along the valley that slope drains into, descending as it goes. Only a dug canal, with a reason to exist, holds a level course.
  • The water's direction may stray from the land's fall by anything short of a right angle.
    A dug contour canal follows the contour line closely, turning off it only enough to keep a working gradient, so a divergence near 90 degrees is what a dug channel is, not a defect. At 90 degrees or more the water would have a net uphill part, so the bound is a strict "under 90". A tighter bound of 60 degrees either way was considered and declined, since it would condemn real contour works. The widest divergences drawn are 60 degrees (a map's bearing against its fall) and 69 (one course against its map's bearing).
  • Where can the limit on the water's direction be wrong, and why is it kept?
    The limit is checked two ways, each comparing one local fact - a map's fall, one course's heading - against a single bearing for the whole map, and where two waters meet that can break with nothing wrong: a settlement whose land falls square into a main river running along it has a fall and a bearing 90 degrees or more apart, and Nagahara is that shape. The limit is kept because what it catches is a reversed direction, a 180-degree error no real ground produces, and no map drawn comes near the bound. The cost is that a future riverbank map may trip it while being correct; loosening or dropping the bound was declined, and such a map would see the bearing model revisited, not the map.
  • The two provincial cities declare no land fall.
    They declare a drainage bearing, as every map does, but no fall. Trying every direction of fall at 10-degree steps found none that fits Tango and exactly one for Nagahara, 350 degrees, the land falling east into the river - and that one is curve-fitting, not terrain, since two of Nagahara's three comb fields declare falls of 100 and 95 degrees, southward. So the two largest maps stay unjudged on slope rather than carry a fictitious fall, and adopting the 350 to pass the check was declined. The cost is that nothing checks their drainage slope.
  • A canal for boats declares that it is held level.
    A cargo canal is cut at the level of the water it joins and held there by a water gate. Canals must be level, joined to water at another level by a gated lock, a device in use since tenth-century China. Giving such a canal a downstream bearing would be a fiction, so the drainage-bearing rule skips it: Nagahara's canal runs at 187 degrees against its map's 90-degree bearing, correctly.

The rule the map follows: Towns and cities, which no program draws yet. Every map declares its land fall and its drainage bearing, and every watercourse on it declares which way its water runs; a moat declares an inlet and an outlet instead. A navigable canal declares that it is held level, and the drainage-bearing rule skips it. No map carries contour lines or any other topographic marking.

Where a channel changes direction, the maps draw the bend an earth ditch would have.

  • Every ditch turns on a sweep, never a sharp corner.
    On this project's reading, an earth channel's corner is worn round by the water, and sharp corners belong to stone-lined work; the ditches our maps draw are all earth, so no watercourse on them has a mitered corner.
  • How tight is a ditch's bend?
    About 2.5 channel widths in radius: the middle of the two to three widths a meandering channel turns on, taken for a dug ditch as a GUESS. So the bend grows with the channel: a wide head race sweeps wide and a thin lateral turns tight, at the same real ratio on every map.
  • Two neighboring bends never swallow the straight between them.
    A bend's radius is capped at 35% of the straight run on either side.
  • Where a branch leaves, the main channel is not rounded.
    The notch cut in the bank for an offtake is a junction, not a bend, and the main channel is not turning there.
  • A turn of under about 8 degrees is left sharp.
    It has no visible elbow, and rounding it would only add detail no reader could see.

Where one channel leaves another, or a moat meets its river, the maps keep to what the period shows, and guess where it shows nothing.

  • A branch leaves its parent leaning downstream.
    The lean is this project's reading of an Edo form: the slanting weir of the period put its intake on the side toward which the water flows, and the maps keep it.
  • A branch is drawn leaving at 35 degrees, a GUESS.
    On the hamlet maps the head race leaves its brook 35 degrees off the brook's downstream heading. No source from before modern times gives an angle; the 30 to 45 degrees of modern engineering is not its warrant, because the maps draw nothing attested only in modern practice.
  • A moat's two ends meet the river square.
    Where a moat's open ends run onto a river, the upstream end is its inlet and the downstream end its outlet. Both meet the river at a right angle, leaning neither way, because every reason found for tilting them - the outlet swept downstream to disturb the river less, the inlet turned against the silt - is modern engineering, and no older angle was found either way. The right angle is itself a GUESS. The river is drawn from its source toward its mouth, so which end is upstream is a fact of the drawing, not a guess.
  • Was the far side of a river-fed moat nearly still water?
    Nearly, and the maps draw it so. The two junctions stand only about 800 ft apart along the river, so the difference in water level driving water round the ring is tiny, and the arc farthest from them is nearly still. Setting the junctions far enough apart to drive a real current was declined: the moats we read of are described by the water they hold, not by a current through them, Shanghai's, which rose and fell with the tides, the one exception. That a river-fed moat was kept full by the river's level rather than flushed, so a still far arc is true to the period, is this project's reading of that silence. The cost: a reader who thinks about the flow will see that the far arc is still water.

Where channels meet, the maps never draw a crossing.

  • Where channels meet, their water is drawn as one body.
    Water on our maps is semi-transparent, so two strokes laid down separately would double up into a darker patch at every junction, and a pond's dark rim would draw a hard line across the mouth of every channel feeding it. So every watercourse on a map is laid down in one pass: every pond's rim first, then every channel bed together as one semi-transparent layer, the pond's own fill last among them, then every highlight on the water's surface. A junction, or a feeder running into a moat, then reads as one confluence.
  • A pond's rim opens where a channel enters it.
    The rim is laid down first, under the channel beds, so each mouth covers it, and the pond's fill covers the small overshoot a feeder's rounded end makes inside the rim.
  • Merging the water is a map drawing convention only.
    It changes how the ink is layered, never where any water lies, and holds on every map at every size. The village lanes are drawn the same way, through one shared block of ground, so that where two lanes meet reads as one tread rather than one lane's shoulder crossing the other's.
  • No channel crosses another.
    The ditches of a rice plain join rather than cross, on this project's reading; where two courses had to pass at different levels the builders passed one beneath the other as an inverted siphon, a structure these maps do not draw.
  • A meeting is a junction when either channel's end lies inside the other's drawn width.
    At every meeting of two drawn channels, the nearer end of each is tested; if neither lies inside the other's width, the stub shows. Where one does, the overrun hides under the ink. The test runs across the other channel, never along the ditch's own line. A branch leaving at a shallow angle overruns the meeting by 4 to 8 ft along its own line and never leaves the main channel's width, so it reads as a clean Y on Honda, Ikegami, Nagahara and Hoshizora alike; the same overrun on a right-angled T pokes out the far side.
  • A ditch's tip is set on the main channel's drawn line.
    A field's lattice of ditches is bent, so a tip set on the straight line between two points of the main channel lands two to five feet off the curve the main is drawn along - just enough for a near-square ditch to poke a tail out the far side and read as a crossroads, not a T. Tips are therefore set on the main channel's own drawn line, which is exact however it bends, to within a fraction of an inch.
