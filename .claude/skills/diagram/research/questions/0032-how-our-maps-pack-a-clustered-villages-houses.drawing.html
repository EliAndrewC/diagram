How our maps pack a clustered village's houses

How tightly a clustered village packs is a calibration against the drawn villages, not a historical figure: no page we read gives a clustered village's built share of its ground, its houses to the hectare or the size of its house plots. The 200 m (~656 ft) gap a 1956 survey used to tell one settlement from the next is a modern counting rule, so the maps do not hold their villages to it.

  • A clustered village is drawn as a compact cluster, not a wide hollow arc.
    Which of the two the map draws depends not on the shape the houses are seeded in but on its width. The program that seats them pulls every homestead in to hug the paddy edge and then packs it along that edge, so a wide scatter of starting points is strung out by that pull while a narrow, deep one packs into a round cluster. On one drawn village, the built share of the ground inside the houses' outline was 0.20 from a wide seed and 0.31 from a narrow one, and the cluster's span about 1,900 ft against about 1,070 ft.
  • How much of a village's ground does the map build on?
    Coverage here means how much of the ground inside the houses' own outline is built - houses, gardens, work yards and homestead groves. The hollow arc measures 0.20; the villages laid out by chance that look right measure 0.28 to 0.31; the tightly hand-placed villages measure about 0.40. A floor of 0.25 sits clear below the healthy range and above the hollow arc: it catches the arc without demanding hand-placed density.
  • A village laid out by chance builds on no more than about 0.31 of its ground.
    Hugging and packing along an edge is looser than a person seating houses one at a time, and that is accepted: a few back-row houses still stand about 700 ft from the crops, which reads as the back of a compact village rather than as a stranded arc. That distance is what the drawn villages showed, not a limit: no house is refused for its distance from the fields.

The rule the map follows (village): a clustered village of a dozen houses or more keeps at least a quarter of the ground inside its own outline built. A hamlet is rightly loose and is not held to it, and neither is a cluster too small for the measure to mean anything. Every clustered hamlet's built share is still measured, and nothing is moved or refused for it.

  • The built-share measure cannot see a village thin when its grove spreads.
    It counts homestead groves as built ground, and the grove fills the smallest rectangle that encloses the houses, so the built ground and the outline move together, and a clump of trees that would fall where a house stands is moved to the next gap instead of being left out. A cluster that spreads therefore grows its grove into the gaps it opened, and its coverage can rise while its houses thin. On the hamlets measured, of two clusters of the same household count, the one whose houses had spread carries 246 clumps of grove against the other's 22, its coverage is 0.339 against 0.294, and its houses, gardens and yards alone cover 0.130 against 0.279, while its outline is more than twice as large.
  • The rule that a quarter of a village's ground be built was set on villages whose groves had not spread.
    So on a village whose grove has spread it can pass a village that has thinned. No village is laid out by chance today, and hamlets are exempt, so nothing now drawn turns on it.
