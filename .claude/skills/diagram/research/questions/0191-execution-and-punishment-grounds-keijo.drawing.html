How our maps site and draw execution and punishment grounds

A settlement that holds a magistrate's court - a county seat, a provincial city or a capital - draws two grounds where sentences were carried out, sited by opposite logics; a settlement without a court draws neither. The punishment ground, where an offender was locked in a cangue for all to see, is a display, so it stands inside the settlement, where people pass. The execution ground, where the condemned were put to death, stands outside: past the built edge on a road out, where travelers had to see it, beyond a boundary stone, on the side of town where the outcaste quarter is. Both are drawn small - the execution ground about 60 ft square at a county seat. Every distance below is measured edge to edge, never center to center.

  • A county seat on our maps executes, and does it outside the town.
    The two traditions the maps draw on differ here. In China a death sentence was reviewed at court each autumn, and this project reads the confirmed sentence as carried out at the county seat where the crime was done, since local deterrence was the point (Execution and punishment grounds (keijo)). Japan put its execution grounds outside the city, on the highways, where everyone coming and going had to see them. Our maps take the jurisdiction from China, so the seat executes, and the siting from Japan, so the ground stands past the built edge. That is what a Chinese county seat run by samurai would do.
  • Does the setting's county budget, which pays for a jail and no executions, fit a county seat that executes?
    A county's budget covers prisoner food, jail upkeep and court materials, while ceremonial execution costs are met at the capital. Read through the Chinese review, the two fit: the county jail holds the condemned while the sentence goes to court for review, as Edo's jail held the convicted until their sentence was carried out.

The execution ground is placed by the road and by the direction its ritual pollution runs, not by the settlement's size or its geometric edge.

  • The execution ground stands on the side of town where the outcaste quarter is.
    The executioners were outcastes, whose quarters often stood at the village's edge or beside the river, and in this setting the burakumin carry out every execution but a samurai's. That this side is also downwind and downstream of the town is this project's reading. The rule is a direction from the town, never a distance: how far out a town's wall happens to stand is an accident of the map.
  • The execution ground stands within about 120 ft of a drawn road, or about 400 ft out from a gate where no road is drawn beyond it.
    A road is a line the ground can hug: Suzugamori stood on the Tokaido. A gate is a point, standing in for the undrawn road that leaves the town through it, and Edo's northern ground, Kozukappara, was miles out along its highway. The ground's road side faces the road, and it stands off the farmland. Both distances are this project's figures.
  • The execution ground stands 400 ft clear of the burial ground - a deliberate deviation.
    The executed were left in the ground at the execution ground itself, while in many castle towns the dead lay in temple graveyards gathered at the town's outer edge. Our maps are a few thousand feet across and cannot hold that, so the separation is compressed, and the order is kept instead: the execution ground is always the further out of the two.
  • Why does the execution ground keep 400 ft from the burial ground?
    It is read from the rendered map, not from history. By analogy with the 120 ft that the cremation ground and the tanning yard keep from dwellings, two polluted grounds would want more, about 150 ft; but at 225 ft an execution ground still reads as part of the burial ground beside it on the rendered map. 400 ft is the distance at which, at the coarsest scale we draw, the two are unmistakably two places.
  • A boundary stone stands on the road out, between the settlement and the execution ground.
    The dosojin stands at a settlement's boundary and is understood to keep out intruders from outside, so it turns "outside the settlement" into a stated ritual boundary: the ground is on the far side of the stone that keeps pollution out.
  • The boundary stone stands past the rampart, or, where there is none, 60 ft clear of the nearest dwelling.
    A stone inside the rampart would bound nothing. Without one, the stone keeps 60 ft from the houses, not the 120 ft the execution ground keeps, because that is a separation for pollution, and a boundary stone pollutes nothing: it marks where clean ground ends, and all it must not do is stand among the houses. The 60 ft is this project's own figure.
  • The stone stands nearer the settlement's edge than the execution ground does.
    Both are measured to the settlement's edge, not to its center: a settlement is not a disc, so a ground tucked in beside a quarter on a different bearing can lie inside the stone's line for most of the town.

The execution ground is drawn small at every size of settlement, scaled down from the one ground whose measurements are recorded.

  • How big is the execution ground at a county seat, a provincial city and a capital?
    About 60 by 60 ft at a county seat, about 100 by 60 ft at a provincial city, and 150 to 250 by 50 to 80 ft at a capital. Suzugamori, which served Edo, measured about 243 by 53 ft; the smaller settlements' grounds are calibrated down from it by how often each executes, not taken from a record. By the GUESS of one to three executions a year for every 100,000 inhabitants, a county sees one perhaps once in five to ten years, a province about once a year, and a domain capital a handful of times a year. This project reads the deterrent as the sight of the posts from the highway, not the ground's size.
  • A county seat's execution ground is drawn weedy and unfenced, with empty post sockets and no hoarding.
    A county executed perhaps once in five to ten years, a GUESS (Execution and punishment grounds (keijo)), so a busy scaffold at county scale would assert something false about how often the Empire kills.
  • A provincial city's execution ground is screened on three sides, its road side open.
    A province sees an execution about once a year, a GUESS like the county's once in five to ten years, so its ground is kept up. The road side stays open because the ground is there to be seen from the highway.

The punishment ground is placed by foot traffic.

  • The punishment ground stands inside the settlement, at the market or the magistracy frontage.
    It stands within about 60 ft of a street, a figure of this project's own, and never in the roadbed, and inside the rampart where there is one. It is a display: the cangue was a public humiliation.
  • The punishment ground holds a cangue frame, a flogging post and a kneeling stone on about 30 by 12 ft of tamped earth.
    The flogging post and the kneeling stone are this project's GUESS: no page read puts either on a public ground, and Edo's jail carried out its floggings on its own premises.
  • Our maps put beatings at the magistracy, not on the punishment ground.
    The runners who served the magistrate's court applied minor punishment, and this project reads the beating as a court act, done inside the yamen before the magistrate's bench - which on our maps is the magistracy compound.
  • The punishment ground has no notice board of its own.
    The settlement already has its notice board (Notice boards (kosatsuba)), and the offender in the cangue is the display.
