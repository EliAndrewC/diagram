Samurai house lots and houses by rank (bukeyashiki)

A samurai did not buy a house lot. The domain granted one by the holder's status and stipend, and at each promotion or transfer the samurai moved to a house that matched the new status. In the domain of Fukui a middle or lower retainer's lot ran from 55 to 400 tsubo, roughly 2,000 to 14,000 sq ft, and a 1,000-koku retainer's was about 1,000 tsubo. The house stood on only part of its lot: a senior retainer's house at Matsue, held by retainers of about 500 to 1,000 koku, is about 67 tsubo, where a 1,000-koku lot at Fukui was about 1,000.

In a provincial castle town every lot was closed off from the street by an earthen wall, a board fence or a hedge, and entered by a gate. The senior houses stood behind earthen walls, with a gate that was itself a building, the long-house gate (nagayamon). The two surviving samurai houses at Kakunodate that we found described have their own wells, the Aoyagi house's standing inside its gate.

Fukui's domain wrote its rule down for its middle and lower retainers, a lot area for each stipend, and its table, a ladder of lot sizes running down the ranks, is the only one we found:

  • At Fukui, retainers of the same stipend had lots of the same size.
    The Fukui archive's page reads its records as keeping the lot areas equal among those of the same stipend.
  • Fukui's table gives no lot for its upper ranks.
    The house elders, the junior elders and the first rank of the middle samurai stand in it with a dash where the lot would be.
  • How large was a 1,000-koku retainer's lot?
    About 1,000 tsubo (about 35,600 sq ft), at Fukui and at Kanazawa. The plan of Fukui's Suganuma family, of 1,000 koku, shows a site of about 1,000 tsubo, 28 by 32.5 ken, about 167 by 194 ft. The Nomura family, upper samurai of 1,200 koku at Kanazawa, had a site of about the same.
  • How large was a yoriki's lot?
    The yoriki was the officer over a town magistrate's constables. In Edo the magistrates' yoriki held lots of about 300 tsubo (about 10,700 sq ft) and their constables about 100 (about 3,600 sq ft). Fukui's table puts its yoriki among the lower samurai, at 96 tsubo, and does not say what they did. Reading the two as one rank at two scales, a domain's and the shogun's capital's, is this project's GUESS.
  • The foot soldiers' plots were about the size of the smallest lots on Fukui's table.
    A foot soldier's plot in the Kaga domain was 165 m² (~1,776 sq ft) or 230 m² (~2,476 sq ft), and one at Hikone 5 by 10 ken, about 30 by 60 ft. Where the foot soldiers lived in a castle town is at A city's garrison: foot soldiers' houses, barracks and the armory

Fukui was not the most generous domain: Yonezawa gave its lower samurai more ground than Fukui's table does. Where in a town the lots stood is at The samurai quarter: where a city's samurai live, and how many (bukechi).

  • Fukui's ladder is one great domain's measure.
    Fukui's fief was 475,280 koku until it was halved in 1686.
  • How much more ground did Yonezawa give its lower samurai?
    About double Fukui's. When the Uesugi house's fief was cut in 1601, its lower samurai were settled on the moors around Yonezawa by about 1609. The domain gave each a lot of 150 tsubo (about 5,300 sq ft), the back half a vegetable field, with a frontage of 6 to 8 ken by status and a depth of about 25 - two measures from the same page, which multiply out to 150 to 200 tsubo. Against the 55 to 96 tsubo of Fukui's lower samurai, that is from one and a half to over three and a half times the ground, and a little over double from the middle of one range to the middle of the other, much of it a kitchen garden.
  • How large were Kakunodate's lots?
    Vast, but not measured. The small town's samurai district still keeps the lot division of the end of the Edo period, on lots the national heritage database calls vast, but no page we read gives the area of any of its lots in the Edo period. The one figure is modern: the estate of the Aoyagi, a middle-ranking family of 45 to 60 koku when they arrived, is just under a hectare (~2.5 acres) today, and whether that is one lot of the Edo period or several joined is not on record.

The house's size followed rank too.

  • A senior retainer's house at Matsue is about 67 tsubo, about 2,400 sq ft.
    It was held in turn by retainers of about 500 to 1,000 koku. What rank a house of that size fits is at How big was a samurai's house, and what rank is a 67-tsubo house?
  • Two senior Fukui retainers' suburban villas were 51 and 35 tsubo, on lots of 1,200 and 1,700 tsubo.
    Some upper retainers were given, besides the main house near the castle, a villa in the suburbs: the Inaba family, of 2,175 koku, had one of 51 tsubo on about 1,200 tsubo, and the Honda family, of 1,975 koku, one of 35 tsubo on about 1,700.
  • A foot soldier's house in the Kaga domain was a single story of 66-82 m² (~710-883 sq ft).
    The Japan Tourism Agency's page on the foot soldiers' houses gives that range.
  • A low-ranking Kakunodate family's main building is about 91 m² (~980 sq ft).
    The Matsumoto house, a rare surviving lower-class samurai house, stands on a lot small by samurai standards.
  • How large was the house of a 100- to 300-koku retainer?
    About 1,200 to 1,800 sq ft, between the senior and the junior houses above. This is a GUESS: no page we read gives the house of a retainer of that rank.

How a lot was closed off from the street followed rank, and differed from town to town.

  • In a provincial castle town every samurai lot was enclosed.
    A house there was closed by an earthen wall, a board fence or a hedge, and entered by a long-house gate or another gate such as the yakuimon or the nukimon.
  • The senior retainers' houses had the earthen wall and the long-house gate.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on samurai residences gives them both to the senior-retainer class.
  • In the Kaga domain the long-house gate was allowed only from the middle rank up.
    The domain, whose castle town was Kanazawa, permitted it to samurai of middle rank and above.
  • Kakunodate's upper-class houses stood behind a black board fence taller than a man, or a hedgerow.
    The city's page on its samurai district describes both.
  • A low-ranking family at Kakunodate had no formal gate.
    The Matsumoto house has two wooden posts and a brushwood fence in place of the gate and the black fence of the upper-class district.
  • In Chiran's samurai district the lanes run between stone walls with clipped hedges behind them.
    The hedges behind the walls are pruned so that those inside can see out.
  • The foot soldiers' plots in the Kaga domain were hedged.
    Each was surrounded by hedges.

The long-house gate of a senior house was more than a gate.

  • The long-house gate held the gatekeeper's and the servants' rooms.
    It became the usual front gate of an upper samurai's house, with a gatekeeper's room and servants' rooms on either side of the passage. Matsue's housed its gatekeeper and menservants, and its rooms' lookout windows face the street. Where the gatekeepers sat is at Where did the gatekeepers sit - in the gate range, or a gatehouse beside it?
  • A large long-house gate in Edo is 21.8 m (~72 ft) by 4.7 m (~15 ft), and two stories.
    The gate in Akasaka, from the 1860s, has a guardroom attached to its face.
  • On a big lot the whole street side could be the servants' long houses.
    The street front of a 1,000-koku Fukui family, the Suganuma, was the wall of the long houses its servants lived in, with the front gate in it. Where a household's servants lived is at Servants in a samurai household: where they sleep and how they were hired (hokonin).

At the gate and inside it, the surviving houses show a garden in front of the house, a storehouse near the gate, and the kitchen within the house. The stable, the bath and the vegetable garden have sections of their own: What was a samurai's stable like?, Did a residence have its own bath? and Where did a residence keep its vegetable garden?.

  • Kakunodate's samurai houses each have a front garden.
    Every dwelling in the samurai districts has one. Matsue's has a plain hill-style garden.
  • A rice storehouse stands inside the gate of the Aoyagi house at Kakunodate.
    Through the gate are a roofed well, the storehouse and the main entrance.
  • A mounting stone and horse-tie stones remain outside the gate of the Ishiguro house at Kakunodate.
    The city's page on its samurai houses records them.
  • In the Kaga domain a foot soldier's plot was split into the house and a garden.
    In the garden the household grew vegetables and fruit trees such as peach, plum and persimmon.
  • Where did the kitchen stand?
    Within the house, to its side. In the Kuchiba house at Hagi the kitchen juts out to the east beside the living room, and Matsue's stove, bath, miso room and privy are rooms of the house. No page we read names a separate kitchen yard; that the kitchen wing and its service ground sat to the side and rear of the house, away from the gate, is a GUESS.

Townsmen shared wells in their alleys (Communal wells and the samurai exception), but the samurai houses that survive at Kakunodate draw their water inside their own fences.

  • Did a samurai house in a provincial town have its own well?
    The two we found described at Kakunodate do: through the Aoyagi house's gate stands a roofed well, and the Iwahashi house has a bucket well. Its family had been senior retainers of the Ashina, and at Kakunodate its house keeps the bearing of a middle-ranking samurai's.
  • How dear was a well?
    A column on wells that speaks of Edo reports a book of 1814, in the Edo period, as saying that digging a well had cost as much as 200 ryo, beyond a samurai household's means, until a boring method brought from Osaka cut the cost to about 3 ryo and wells spread. Not every house had a well: most were shared.
  • Did the foot soldiers have wells of their own?
    Probably not. A well was dear and most were shared, so a private well was the well-established household's; that the foot soldiers drew from a well shared within their block is a GUESS, since no page we read gives their plots one each.
