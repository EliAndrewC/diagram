Wet paddies that never drain (shitsuden)

Japanese farmers told two kinds of rice paddy apart by the ground beneath them. A dry paddy, the kanden, drained well and dried out to a dry field when no water was let in; a wet paddy, the shitsuden, drained so badly that it stayed waterlogged even when no rice was growing, and could carry no second crop such as barley. The wet paddy was the poorer ground: hard to work, cold, and unreliable in its yield.

From the Meiji period the state drained wet paddy into dry as a national undertaking, and by one account most of the country's paddies became dry. A wet paddy is still rice land, cropped every year; the wetland at the foot of the fields, which is not paddy at all, is at Marshes and wetlands (shitchi).

  • Japanese dictionaries define the two kinds of paddy by their drainage.
    One defines the wet paddy as a paddy with poor drainage, in an excessively wet state even when no rice is being grown, and so one on which a second crop such as barley cannot be grown. Another defines the dry paddy as one whose drainage is good, and which dries out to become like a dry field when water is not let in. The difference lies in the ground and holds all year, so a wet paddy is a kind of plot, not a moment in one plot's season.
  • A wet paddy was hard to work and bore no winter crop.
    An encyclopedia calls it a poorly drained paddy on which excess moisture and standing water make farm work hard, and on which no winter crop such as wheat can be grown.
  • A wet paddy's soil was colder in summer than a dry paddy's, and short of oxygen.
    An encyclopedia says the soil of a wet paddy is cooler in summer than a dry paddy's, and short of oxygen.
  • A wet paddy's yield was unstable, and its productivity generally low.
    An encyclopedia puts the unstable yield down to lodging (the standing crop falling flat before the harvest), disease and delayed growth, and says plainly that the productivity of wet paddy is generally low.
  • How much wet paddy was there before Japan modernized?
    Enough that draining it was a national undertaking. From the Meiji period on, the state converted wet paddy to dry, and more than two-thirds of the country's paddies are said to have become dry paddy. The passage gives the result, not the share that was wet before the work began; that a program of that size measures how much wet paddy there was is our reading of it.
  • Valley-bottom paddies (yatsuda) were generally wet.
    The yatsuda are the paddies of the low ground in valleys cut into a plateau. They are generally wetland and were opened up long ago, though many, lacking a good source of water, came to depend on rain alone.
  • A paddy of deep mud had its own name, fukada.
    The dictionaries define it as a paddy with deep mud, a swamp paddy, the opposite of the shallow paddy (asada).
  • Where in a field did the wettest plots lie?
    At its foot, on our reasoning; no source says so. In a traditional terraced paddy, water flows from one plot to the next and no line can be drawn between irrigating and draining, and part of the water that seeps away or runs off can be used again within the system. So the plots at the bottom take what the plots above shed, and gravity makes them the wettest ground - an inference from that mechanism, not a finding anyone wrote down.
