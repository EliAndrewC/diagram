How our maps set a city on its river

Most of the provincial cities our maps draw sit on a river. The exception is our map of Tango, whose river lies beyond the edge of the map. A river city's map shows its main river crossing from edge to edge, in a heavier line than anything else on it, the dug moat included, because on that side the river is the city's moat. The moat covers the landward faces, and a canal brings boats in through a water gate.

  • The moat is an open arc, not a closed ring.
    It covers the three landward faces, and both of its ends run onto the river, which closes the ring of water itself, so every point of the wall stands behind either the moat or the river.
  • A navigable canal leads from the river through a grated water gate in the city wall.
    It is drawn lighter than the moat and heavier than a field ditch. Its water gate is grated, as the Pan Gate's at Suzhou was.
  • The city wall is left open where the canal passes through it.
    No tower stands on the wall beside the water gate.
  • Inside the wall the canal ends in a dock basin, a GUESS of ours.
    No page we read describes one. The canal runs into the basin with no visible break, as a street joins a road, and no building stands in the canal's water.
  • Jetties are fingers from the near bank, never bars across the river.
    Each planked jetty is rooted at the bank, with its tip in the near half of the water.
  • The wharf stands outside the gate that faces the river.
    Its jetties, stores and market stand there, as Shanghai's wharf district lay between a gate of the county seat and the river. It is the river city's form of a gate market.
  • Our maps run no Imperial road through a river city.
    The highway that serves the city passes beyond the edge of the map, so the map draws instead a network of local roads that leave it in at least two directions.
  • A river city's burial ground lies across the river.
    A burial ground keeps a set distance from open water. With the moat along the city's landward faces and the river along the last, no dry ground beside the city is far enough from both, so the burial ground sits on the far bank, downstream of the city and clear of the road from the bridge.

These rules our maps give every city hold for a river city too.

  • A gate's two posts stand square to the wall where it is.
    They stand to either side of the opening, square to the wall's local line, so the posts of a gate in an east or west wall stand north and south of it, not upright on the page.
  • A burial ground sits beside the farmland, never on a paddy or its ditches.
    In southern China graves were often kept far from water and off good farmland; the set-back from a flooded field is part of how far from water a burial ground lies.
  • A city holds a temple to each of its clan's two patron Fortunes.
    That is the setting's own rule. These two are the temples our maps label.
  • A city with a samurai quarter may also hold a temple to the warrior Fortune.
    It is the one addition our maps make to the two patron temples.
  • Every other shrine in the city is a small wayside shrine.
    That holds however fitting a larger dedication would be.
