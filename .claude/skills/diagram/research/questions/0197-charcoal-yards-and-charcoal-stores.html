Charcoal yards and charcoal stores

A charcoal yard was a town's stock of fuel charcoal, brought in from the kilns in straw bales and sold on; a charcoal store, in this project's GUESS, was the same stock kept behind the thick plastered walls of a storehouse (kura), though no page we read says charcoal was kept in one. In China charcoal was an industrial fuel moved at state scale: the Song-dynasty state supplied it to iron-smelting households, and north China's ironworks burned so much of it that they deforested large parts of the north. Edo-period Japan's bulk goods passed through the ton'ya, the wholesaler who was also a warehouse-keeper, and in this project's reading charcoal was such a wholesaler's stock.

Charcoal is a fire risk: fresh charcoal can heat itself until it catches, and in a town of timber houses heated by charcoal, Edo's great fires came in the dry, windy months of winter and early spring. Before modern times it was cooled at the kiln, not at the depot. Its straw bale had no standard size, so in this project's reading it was sold by weight, and China's market scale was the steelyard, which could be built to weigh heavy loads.

  • At Hancheng, the state under the Song dynasty supplied charcoal to some of its iron-smelting households.
    The magistrate Bao Qingtian (999-1062) wrote of 700 iron-smelting households at Hancheng, on the Yellow River, overseen by government regulators; 200 of them had the most state support, such as charcoal supplies and skilled craftsmen.
  • How much iron did China smelt under the Song dynasty?
    About 125,000 metric tons (~138,000 US tons) a year by 1078, on the historian Robert Hartwell's estimate, per capita output having risen about sixfold since 806; the historian Donald Wagner questions how Hartwell reached the figure. The next finding shows how much of that smelting ran on charcoal.
  • Charcoal smelting deforested north China until coke replaced it.
    The waterwheel-driven bellows of the smelters burned so much charcoal that large parts of north China were deforested, and by the end of the 11th century coke from bituminous coal was found to replace it.
  • How many charcoal workers did a large Ming iron enterprise employ?
    The source does not count them apart. A late-Ming account of Guangdong gives an ironworks more than 200 furnace tenders, 300 miners, and 200 water-carriers and charcoal producers together, moved by 200 oxen and 50 river vessels, so the fuel workers are counted with the water-carriers. The historian who reports the passage reads it as a large firm running numerous ironworks over a wide area, not the single works its source calls it.

In Japan charcoal was a wholesaler's stock, and the record shows how it was cooled and packed before it reached a town.

  • Japan's wholesalers, the ton'ya, were also its warehouse managers.
    The ton'ya were trade brokers - wholesalers, warehouse managers and shipment managers - and came to serve a crucial role in the economy of the Edo period (1603-1867).
  • Fresh charcoal can set itself alight.
    Freshly made charcoal absorbs oxygen from the air fast enough to heat a stockpile until it burns, and tightly packed fines, and charcoal high in volatiles, catch more readily than lump charcoal.
  • Today's handling guidance cools fresh charcoal in the open for at least a day.
    It is to stand freshly discharged charcoal in the open, apart from charcoal already cooled and conditioned, for at least 24 hours. The rule is modern: no period source we read puts a cooling ground at a charcoal depot.
  • Before modern times, charcoal was cooled at the kiln.
    Black charcoal is put out inside the kiln by closing its mouth and smoke outlet until no air enters; white charcoal is drawn out red-hot and smothered outside the kiln under a damp powder of ash and river sand. Black charcoal made by putting the fire out in the kiln, and taking the charcoal out once the kiln had cooled, came in from the late Muromachi into the Edo period, though the naming of white and black charcoal as two kinds is said to be modern. The white-charcoal method is said to have taken its settled form in the Heian period.
  • Was charcoal baled at the kiln?
    Probably, a GUESS: cooled at the kiln, it would be packed there for the journey, and a local history of the Tama district counts the charcoal carried downriver to Edo already in bales, 25 to 30 to a raft. But no page we read says where or when in the Edo period charcoal was baled.
  • A straw bale (tawara) had no standard size.
    Not even for rice: from the Sengoku to the Edo period a bale of rice held anything from 2 to 5 to by time and place (a to being a tenth of a koku) - 3 to 5 sho by the shogunate's measure, 5 to in Kaga domain (han) - and the bale itself came in many sizes. A bale's size is taken up at Straw bales of rice and charcoal (tawara)
  • How heavy was a bale of charcoal?
    It depended on the grade, in the one district we found. In the Iburi district of Hokkaido the best charcoal was packed at 4 kan (about 15 kg, ~33 lb) to the bale, the next grade at 10 kan (37.5 kg, ~83 lb) and a lower grade at 8 kan (30 kg, ~66 lb), rolled in straw matting. The article gives no date for it.

Whether it was weighed, and on what, rests on the scales of the time, in China and in Japan.

  • Was charcoal weighed when it was sold?
    In this project's reading, yes: a stock packed to no standard bale cannot be traded by counting its bales, so it must be weighed at the point of sale. Where bales were packed to a set weight by grade, as in Iburi, a count of graded bales would stand for a weight, but that is one undated district. No page we read says a charcoal dealer weighed the bales at sale.
  • China's traditional market scale was the steelyard.
    The steelyard (gancheng), also called the hanging scale, was used above all in vegetable markets. It is a wooden or bamboo beam with a large hook at one end and a weight hung at the other: the load hangs on the hook, and the weight is slid along the beam until the two balance.
  • Some Chinese steelyards were built for heavy loads.
    A wooden steelyard more than 200 years old, collected from Shaoxing, is 2.2 m (~7 ft) long and weighs up to 175 kg (~386 lb) against a stone weight.
  • One account has the steelyard weighing everyday goods, and the two-pan balance valuables.
    An Osaka prefectural history of measurement says the balance was used for valuables and gems, and the steelyard for food and daily goods, naming no country or period. Its account of the steelyard's invention in the ancient world says the balance is precise, but needs many weights, which make it heavy and awkward to carry.
  • The same account calls the steelyard easy to cheat with.
    It says the steelyard was, by its structure, easy to cheat with on weight.
  • Japan used steelyards from the Edo period, some for loads of 16 kan.
    Steelyards were in use from the Edo period to the mid-20th century, in sizes that included one for 5 kan and one for 16 kan (60 kg, ~132 lb) - four of the best Iburi charcoal bales.
  • A museum dates the platform scale that weighed rice bales from after 1868, in the Meiji period.
    It was in use from the Meiji period to the mid-20th century.

Kept in a town of timber houses, the fuel was also a fire risk.

  • Edo's great fires came in the dry months of winter and early spring.
    Counted by month, March saw the most great fires, then February, April and January, together 70% of them, in dry weather under the northwest and north winds, among wooden townhouses heated by charcoal-burning fireplaces.
  • Was a fire gap kept round a charcoal store?
    No premodern figure for one was found.
  • After its great fire of 1641, Edo gathered its timber dealers in one place.
    The only rule found on a stock of fuel was on timber, not charcoal. After the great Edo fire of 1641, in the Kan'ei era, whose spread was blamed on the high stacks of timber dealers scattered through the town, the dealers were gathered onto Eitaijima.
  • The plastered storehouse was built to keep its goods from fire.
    Storehouses (kura) were named for what they held - rice, unhulled rice, miso, gunpowder, money, sutras - and were for protecting it against fire, damp and theft. The plastered storehouse is one of Japanese architecture's fire-resistant constructions. Most were tile-roofed, and their thick walls made them ideal for storing commodities, household belongings, tools and valuables.
  • A storehouse's walls were often a foot thick, and its doors could be shut against fire.
    Their walls were often 300 mm (~12 in) thick or more, and their outer doors might be faced with earth and plaster. One kind of door, the Osaka-do, was a sliding door shut in a fire.
  • From the Kyoho era (1716-36) on, Edo spread the storehouse's plastered construction to townhouses by order, against fire.
    In Edo the same construction, used for townhouses, was at first restrained as a luxury, then spread by order for fire prevention from the Kyoho era on.
  • Was charcoal kept in a plastered storehouse?
    No page we read says so, or gives the spacing kept between a storehouse and its neighbors. That it was is a GUESS resting on the storehouse's recorded purpose: it kept gunpowder behind its wall.
