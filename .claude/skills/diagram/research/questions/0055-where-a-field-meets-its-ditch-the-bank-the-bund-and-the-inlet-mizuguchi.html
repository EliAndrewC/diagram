Where a field meets its ditch: the bank, the bund and the inlet (mizuguchi)

A rice paddy is a basin of level, flooded ground, held in on every side by a low bank of earth, the bund, heaped from the paddy's own mud so the water does not leak away. Where a ditch runs beside a paddy, the bund on that side is the ditch's bank. Water comes in through a small opening in the bund, the inlet, and leaves through another, the outlet, into a drain or the next paddy down; paddies were built that way in Japan from the Late Jomon on. The water could also be passed from an upper paddy to the one below it: plot-to-plot irrigation (tagoshi).

An Edo land survey reckoned the bund between two fields at about a foot, and the bund along a canal wider, about 2.6 to 4.9 ft at the one early site where the record found it measured.

  • Water could pass from paddy to paddy, with no ditch between them.
    Japanese paddy can be watered plot to plot: water enters the topmost paddy of a chain down the slope and is supplied from each paddy to the one below it, so the paddies below it in the chain need no ditch of their own - this project's reading of the term. A modern paddy draws from a supply canal and lets its water out into a drain, while the old way is still kept at Tashibu-no-sho Osaki, in Oita.
  • When did every paddy get a ditch of its own?
    Around 1900, as Japan began consolidating its paddy land. A 1948 essay on the history of Japanese irrigation says that only with the amendment of the land consolidation law in 1910 were paddy plots and irrigation channels properly arranged, and that before it most irrigation had been flow-through irrigation (kakenagashi) - though it gives this as a consequence and cites no evidence for it. In the model layout for consolidated paddy tried at Konosu from 1902, each plot of about 54 m (~177 ft) by 18 m (~59 ft) touched its irrigation and drainage channel and a road.
  • On one Japanese alluvial fan in the 1900s, the smallest ditches were counted as part of the paddies they watered.
    Tabayashi records, for the ditches of the Tedori alluvial fan as rebuilt in the early 1900s and seen in 1976, that the smallest ditches bringing water to the plots' inlets are run by small groups of farmers and are often considered parts of the paddies they serve.
  • Openings in the bunds are found in Japan's early paddies.
    The excavated paddies of the Hattori site in Shiga, from the start of the Yayoi period, already had water openings set in their bunds and channels. A general history of Japan's water works gives paddies with intakes and outlets fitted with weirs from the Late Jomon on.
  • The inlet has a name, the mizuguchi, and an old rite of its own.
    A rite held at a paddy's inlet, the mizuguchi, is in a poem the dictionary dates, with a question mark, to about 1134.
  • How wide was a paddy's inlet?
    Under 50 cm (~20 in): modern design practice makes it at most that wide, and that is the only source of a size. No page from before 1868 we could read gives one.
  • Modern practice gives each paddy one inlet, its sill near the field's own level.
    It asks for one inlet per plot, two or more only where they would stand 50 m (~164 ft) or more apart, the sill within 0-10 cm (~0-4 in) of the field surface so the inflow does not scour the bed, and water passing it at no more than about 0.4 m (~1.3 ft) a second.
  • Modern practice makes a paddy's outlet, the mizujiri, an overflow set just below the field.
    It sets the outlet's sill 5-10 cm (~2-4 in) below the field surface and makes it an overflow, so it holds the depth of water on the paddy and stores water there in heavy rain. No page we read gives its width.

The ditches that bring the water down to the plots were graded by size, from the canal to the last ditch before the paddies, in China's oldest texts and in the modern standards of both China and Japan.

  • The Rites of Zhou name five field channels, each twice as wide as the one below.
    The smallest, the quan, is the cut a pair of spades makes, 1 chi wide and deep - about a foot or less, by this page's reckoning of the Zhou-dynasty chi; the largest leads directly into the rivers.
  • The Rites of Zhou put a channel between every farmer's allotment.
    The same text lays out the countryside with the sui channel between single farmers' allotments, and a larger channel for every ten, hundred and thousand allotments. It is an ideal plan, not a survey - this project's reading.
  • Modern Chinese and Japanese standards grade canals by size, down to a smallest ditch.
    A modern Shanghai standard names five grades - main, branch, lateral, farm and field. The first three are the canal system and the last two the distribution inside the fields, and the smallest is laid out to the size of the paddy and may be dug by the farmers themselves. Japan's design vocabulary has three - trunk, branch and terminal canal, and one canal's own description names its trunk and branch canals the same way. The widths of each grade are at How wide canals and ditches are: the ladder of channel widths.

The bund on a ditch's side, and the bank along a larger canal, have a record of their own: in Japan's excavated paddies, in its Edo-period records and in China's oldest texts.

  • A paddy's bund beside an open ditch has a name of its own.
    Japanese distinguishes the bund between a paddy and the open channel beside it as a ditch bund (kohan). The channel is often public land, and the boundary is sometimes set at the paddy-side foot of that bund or at the water line.
  • Did a paddy's lowest bund stand on the bank of its drain?
    The bund is what holds a paddy's water in and the drain is what carries it away, so a bund across the drain would dam it. Where the ditch beside a paddy is the field's drain, then, the paddy's lowest bund stands on the drain's bank and runs with it, and the bunds between plots that run down the slope meet it end-on - this project's reading of the ditch-side bund, which the source does not state.
  • Which paddies had a drain beside them?
    On a field watered from paddy to paddy, the drain would run along the field's lower edge rather than past every plot, which only the consolidated paddy of about 1900 was laid out to do, so only the row of paddies on that edge had one beside it. This is this project's reading of the two sources, which neither states outright.
  • At one of the earliest paddies dug up in Japan, large bunds ran along the canal.
    At the Hattori site, paddies of the first years of the Yayoi period, the central canal is enclosed by large bunds, which ran along it and seem also to have served as paths for the farm work. At the Sena site in Shizuoka, from the Late Yayoi into the Kofun period, the channel banks and the large bunds were both reinforced with rows of stakes and sheet boards.
  • The Rites of Zhou put a path along every field channel.
    It puts a path on the bank of every grade of field channel, from the smallest up, and a Tang-dynasty sub-commentary says the path on the smallest takes no cart, but oxen, horses and people on foot.
  • An Edo aqueduct kept a protected strip along each side.
    A notice board of 1739 forbade cutting the trees, the undergrowth or the grass within 3 ken (about 18 ft) on each side of the Tamagawa aqueduct. The aqueduct's main purpose was the city of Edo's water, and the strip was one kept from cutting rather than a bare bank top - this project's note, not the quoted notice's.
  • How wide was the bank along a village's own supply canal?
    No record from before modern times we could find gives it, nor the width of a bund along a canal in the Edo period. The nearest figure is the Hattori site's large bund along its canal, 80-150 cm (~2.6-4.9 ft) wide with a flat top and wider than its small bunds, and it has plain limits: one site, far older than the Edo period, whose use as a path is the excavators' "seem".
  • How wide was a bund between paddies?
    About a foot. An Edo land survey reckoned a bund at 1 shaku, about 30 cm (~1 ft), and took it out of the field's surveyed area; the dictionary's example, a shogunate order of 1726, reckons that deduction with the adjoining land at 1 shaku 5 sun for each holding (a sun is a tenth of a shaku), about 45 cm (~18 in). The Hattori site's small bunds were 20-60 cm (~8 in to 2 ft) wide and not for walking on. A modern standard's smallest bund, its type I, is 40 cm (~16 in) across its crest and 30 cm (~1 ft) above the higher paddy, with sides sloping 1:1, one unit out for each unit up; its larger types are 60 cm (~2 ft) across.
