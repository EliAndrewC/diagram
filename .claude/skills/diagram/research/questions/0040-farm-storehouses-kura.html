Farm storehouses (kura)

A kura was a farm's fireproof storehouse: a small block, commonly about 15 by 18 ft and two stories high, walled in thick earth under a tile roof. It was built against fire and theft, and it came to be a sign of wealth. A farm built one when money had accumulated, so most farms had none: before 1868, in the Edo period, one village of sixteen households had two, both in the households of its leading families.

  • Who had a storehouse?
    The well-off, on this project's reading. A storehouse's walls took coat after coat of earth on a bamboo lattice, costly work, so it was the well-off farmers and landlords who had one; that is this project's reading of the construction the pages describe.
  • How many farms had a storehouse before 1868?
    In Kakimochi village, 2 of the 16 households had one. Both were among the households the town historian places in the powerful stratum, the second with a "probably". The count is of 1885, and the historian reads it back to the last years of the shogunate, since houses were not often rebuilt. It is one village's count. The farmstead it describes is at The farmstead and what stood on it (yashiki).
  • Did the largest houses have the storehouses?
    Mostly. The Kakimochi historian ties the outbuildings to the size of the house: the more of them a household had, the larger its main house, and the powerful farmers who served as village officials owned such houses. The village's table bears that out for the storehouse, though not to the letter. Its two storehouses stood with main houses of 40 and 32 tsubo (about 1,400 and 1,100 sq ft), the second and third largest of the sixteen; the largest, 45 tsubo, had none; and seven of the sixteen main houses were 18 tsubo. Why those two households had a storehouse and the largest house had none, no record we read says.
  • When did farms in one district west of Edo come to own storehouses?
    Probably only from the last years of the shogunate. An architect who surveyed the storehouses of a new-field village there reports that this is what is said locally, so the storehouse's history there is short.
  • How common were storehouses in the twentieth century?
    A survey of farmhouses in Miyagi, in northeastern Japan, in 1972 counts 0.24 storehouses per household among those whose main house was built in or before 1944, and 0.13 over all the households surveyed.
  • Was a shed commoner than a storehouse?
    Yes, though a shed was not on every plot either. The Miyagi survey of 1972 counts 0.58 sheds (naya) per household in the older farmhouses, against 0.24 storehouses. Many households worked and dried their crop inside the farmhouse's own earth-floored room, the doma, and the poorest had little to store; that is this project's reading. The shed is at Farm sheds and barns (naya).
  • How big was a farm storehouse?
    About 15 by 18 ft, in two stories, in one district west of Edo. An architect who surveyed the storehouses of a new-field village there found that to be the commonest, standard size: 2.5 by 3 ken. Kakimochi's two were 2 by 3 ken, about 12 by 18 ft.
  • A grain storehouse built on Sado island in 1890, after the Edo period, has two stories over about 366 sq ft.
    The national register gives it 34 sq m (~366 sq ft) in two stories, a little larger than the commonest storehouse west of Edo, and calls it an ancillary building a farmhouse could not do without.
  • A storehouse's walls were thick earth.
    Earth was laid on a lattice of bamboo and palm rope, coat after coat, to 20 to 30 cm (~8-12 in) thick, and finished in lime plaster.
  • Were storehouses roofed in tile?
    Both that we found described are. The reconstructed headman-class farm in Chiba, east of Tokyo, has a two-story storehouse, entered at its gable end and walled in earth roof and all, with a tiled roof set over the earthen one. The Sado storehouse is tiled too. Two examples are all this rests on.
  • Where on the plot did a storehouse stand?
    In front of the house or behind it, on any side, and seldom against it, in a district west of Edo that an architect surveyed. A site was set aside for it, but circumstances changed before it was built, so storehouses ended up in front of the house or behind; where a seasonal wind blew, few were built against the house on its downwind side; and they stood east, west, north or south. That a storehouse as a rule stood free of the house is this project's reading: the source counts only the downwind side, and of how rare an attached storehouse was it says no more than "few".
