Communal wells (ido)

Most wells in premodern Japan were shared, because a well cost a great deal to dig. A whole village, or a group of households within one, drew from a common well, and in the back-alley tenements of the cities the tenants shared one. The shared well was also where the neighbors met: the women of a tenement row gathered at it to draw water and do the washing, and to talk. A samurai household, by contrast, could have a well of its own inside its fence.

A well was a shaft dug by hand about 1 m (~3 ft) across, wide enough for a digger to work in, with a frame round its mouth, the one we found measured, in a modern book, about 4 ft square. The bucket came up on a counterweighted pole, the sweep, where the water stood shallow, and on a rope over a pulley where it lay deep; some wells had a roof on posts over them.

  • Most wells were shared, because they were costly to dig.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on wells says a well might be owned by one household or in common, and that common wells were the majority, since digging cost a great deal and in many places the ground made water hard to reach. A Japanese encyclopedia counts many examples of a well used in common by a village or by a group within one.
  • How many houses did one village well serve?
    In Karihama, a village on the Ehime coast, the wells were dug at about one to every five to ten houses, some 150 in all. Karihama was short of water and drew its water from dug wells and springs until piped water came in 1952. It is the one village count we read.
  • At Karihama, a dozen of the village's roughly 150 wells were used in common.
    The survey found eleven shared wells in one part of the village and one in the other; it does not say who drew from the rest.
  • Did a village on good water need fewer wells?
    Villages were often sited where water was easy to draw, along the spring line at the foot of an alluvial fan and on natural levees. That such a village needed fewer wells than water-short Karihama, or none, is this project's reading, a GUESS: no page we read counts a valley village's wells.
  • Where could a household have a well of its own?
    Where the water lay close under the ground, in this project's reading. The Japanese Wikipedia article on wells describes shallow wells made where the water table is near the surface, as on the loam uplands of the Kanto, and says that where the water lay far down, a mortar-shaped hollow was dug first and the well sunk at its bottom. It counts no wells to a house.

For China, the one count of wells we read is the capital's, Beijing's, at the end of the Qing.

  • How many persons could one well serve?
    Several hundred. At the end of the Qing, Beijing had 1,228 wells for an official population of about 800,000, so each well served at least 652 persons - a modern writer's division of two Qing counts, which took in registered residents only. A modern disaster-relief standard plans on about 400 persons to an open well; it is a present-day figure, given for comparison.
  • A south-China rice village of some seventy households had about two wells, by this project's guess.
    One to three in all is this project's reading, a GUESS: no page we read counts a Chinese village's wells.

In the cities the shared well stood in the alleys among the tenements.

  • The shared well of an Edo tenement was often an aqueduct intake, not a shaft.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on the nagaya says its communal well did not draw groundwater but was an intake for piped water from the Kanda aqueduct, and the article on the Kanda aqueduct says aqueduct wells were set in the tenements and the tenants used them in common.
  • Ten to twenty tenement households to one well is this project's guess.
    The pages we read place the shared well in the alley, and none gives a ratio; the figure is a GUESS.
  • Did a samurai household have a well of its own?
    At Kakunodate, yes: through the Aoyagi house's gate stands a roofed well, and the Iwahashi house has a bucket well. More is at Does a samurai's house have its own well?
  • In Edo the aqueduct served the samurai districts first.
    The Kanda aqueduct gave the samurai districts priority and sent what remained to the commoner districts, so in the capital the difference was one of priority as much as of a private well.

A shared well is recorded in two places: by the roadside, and among the houses that drew from it. A village well by the lane is this project's GUESS; the roadside well is attested only in general.

  • A shared well by the roadside had a name of its own, the wayside well (tsuji-ido).
    A Japanese encyclopedia gives the name; it speaks of roadside wells in general, and does not say that villages had them.
  • Did a village's shared wells stand among the houses that shared them?
    This project reads it so, from Karihama: each of its wells served five to ten houses, and round many of the common ones the ground was paved with stones, making a space at the wellside - where, in this project's reading, the neighbors met. The survey counts the houses to a well, but does not map where the wells stood.
  • At Harie, on Lake Biwa, spring-fed wells stood both inside the houses and outside them.
    The kabata of Harie served for drinking, cooking and washing; some of those outside were open to others than the household, and they were cherished as places to gather and talk.
  • No page we read sets a village's wells out on open common ground, away from its houses.
    The pages we read place a shared well by the roadside or among the houses that drew from it.

From above, a well was a frame round the shaft's mouth - the curb - with a lifting gear over it and sometimes a roof.

  • The curb was a frame of wood or stone round the shaft's mouth.
    Older wells had a hoop of bent wood or a square frame of timbers, and a registered well of about 1900 has cut granite laid in the crossed-timber pattern of the igeta.
  • How big was a curb?
    About 4 ft square, from the one we found measured: a bucket well photographed for a modern book of old implements is 118 cm (~46 in) square, and the book does not date it. No page we read measures a premodern curb, and an excavated well never keeps the curb that stood above ground, but the shaft survives: a stone-lined well of the Muromachi period in Yamaguchi is about 1 m (~3 ft) across inside.
  • A Chinese well's curb could have two, three or four openings.
    The openings, on one shaft, kept the buckets from knocking together.
  • The platform round a Chinese well usually had separate ditches for clean water and for waste.
    They kept the well water from being fouled.
  • The bucket came up on a sweep or on a rope over a pulley.
    A Japanese encyclopedia names the two: a balance-beam lever, and a pulley with the bucket at the rope's end. A museum guide calls the rope and pulley the ordinary gear, and the sweep, the hanetsurube, a pole worked like a seesaw with a weight at its far end. From above, a sweep is a long pole crossing the well from a post beside it; a pulley hangs from a roof or from a frame over the well.
  • Which gear a well had followed the depth of its water.
    Around Hiratsuka the shallowest wells were worked with a bare bamboo pole, a somewhat deeper one with a sweep - a pole crossed over a post with a bag of stones at its butt - and the pulley well was less common, because the wells there were shallow. A Japanese encyclopedia puts the pulley at deep wells and the sweep where the water stands higher, and a Chinese farming treatise of 1313 makes the same choice between the sweep and the windlass.
  • No page we read gives the length of a sweep's pole.
    The encyclopedia and museum pages that describe the sweep give no measure of it.
  • A well house was a roof on posts beside the well, and nothing more.
    So two Japanese dictionaries define it.
  • A well of about 1900 at Tokugawa-en in Nagoya shows the well house in its finished form.
    The heritage register describes it, a Meiji-period well now a registered cultural property: a cut-granite curb, dressed posts beside it, a gabled roof and an iron pulley hung from it, the whole on about 1.6 square meters (~17 sq ft).
  • No page we read counts the roofs over ordinary wells.
    The dictionaries define the well house and the palace pavilions are recorded, but no page says how many village or town wells had a roof.

Most of the pages above describe wells as museums and folklorists recorded them in modern times; older writings and finds date each form.

  • The sweep and the windlass frame were in use in China by the early fourteenth century.
    Wang Zhen's farming treatise of that year names the sweep that rocks and the windlass that turns, and its windlass stands on a frame set up over the well carrying an axle. The Tiangong Kaiwu of 1637 still names the pair.
  • The pulley on a well frame goes back to the Han dynasty.
    Historians of technology read a waist-drum-shaped pulley on a well frame in a tomb mural from Liaoyang that their paper dates to the Western Han, and many more pulleys on Han-dynasty reliefs and pottery models of wells.
  • In Japan the pulley well is attested by the mid-eighteenth century, in the Edo period.
    A comic verse of 1753 says every well in Kyoto was one.
  • Every well in the Forbidden City had a pavilion over it.
    In the Ming and Qing some of the palace's pavilions had pulleys - one carries an iron pulley on two beams under its roof, very likely of the Qing - though most palace wells were drawn by rope and bucket by hand. No single pavilion is dated. A palace is the richest case.

Not every well served a household: in China the windlass over a well watered farms as well as homes, though no page we read says where a well dug to water a field stood. How villages lifted water onto their fields is at Did a village lift water onto its fields - and with what?

  • China's wells were worked with a sweep (jiegao) or a windlass (lulu), and the windlass watered the fields too.
    The north-China plain and the loess country west of it get 400 to 800 mm (~16-31 in) of rain a year concentrated in the summer. The jiegao, China's sweep, was an upright post and a hinged beam, with, in this project's reading, a counterweight; the lulu was a windlass on a wooden stand with a wheel, an axle, a hand crank and ropes. Both lifted water from wells or near rivers, and the lulu served in everyday life and for farm irrigation.
  • Under the Ming and Qing the windlass became north China's usual way to lift groundwater.
    It was improved rather than replaced: horses in place of men, several containers at once, and deeper wells. The paper gives no depth; tens of meters (~30-300 ft) is a GUESS.
  • At Hata village in Japan, the villages downstream stopped every plan to bring canal water to the dry fields.
    The district held wet-rice paddies and, on the upper terraces by the river, dry fields; late in the Edo period new canals for Hata's fields were planned many times, and never built, because the irrigators downstream opposed any new intake above them.
  • On the Noubi plain, the villages behind a ring levee capped the number of their wells.
    The system, the kabu-ido, began in the Fukuzuka ring levee, whose sixteen villages agreed a cap in 1812; it lasted until the 1930s. The wells were artesian, dug by the upper villages for irrigation and household use, and the quarrel was not scarcity but their drainage, which flooded the paddy of the lower villages.
  • The kabu-ido was a permit system the villages ran themselves.
    In the Takasu ring levee, where it is best documented, it was a survey of the wells standing, a total agreed between the upper and the lower villages, a fee for the right to dig, and patrolmen the lower villages provided for each other, while the local government declined to step in.
