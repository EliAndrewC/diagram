How our maps draw the farmstead and what stands on it (yashiki)

Our maps draw on a farmstead what two village records of the 1800s found there, in about the numbers they found it: a farmhouse, a privy, on average about one and a half outbuildings more, and the small fixtures of the yard. The fixtures are drawn at their real size; where no source measures one, its size is a GUESS. What runs along the plot's edge is chosen per settlement from the forms the record shows.

  • The counts follow two villages' records from the 1800s, not a modern survey made in 1972.
    A survey of three Miyagi hamlets made in 1972 found 4.4 outbuildings a household. In 1885 the village of Kakimochi counted about 1.4, besides the privy and the retirement house, a count its historian reads back to the farmhouses of the last years before 1868 (see The farmstead and what stood on it). This project draws only what is attested before modern times, so the maps take the premodern counts, and the 1972 figures show only which kinds of outbuilding a farmstead had. Keeping the 1972 figures, scaled down for a warmer lowland than snowy Miyagi, was not taken, because the survey is modern.

The rule the map follows:

  • Besides the privy, a farmstead carries on average about one and a half outbuildings. A few carry none, a headman's carries several, and the storehouse and the sheds go to the larger houses first.
  • A storehouse stands on about one farm in eight (see How our maps draw farm storehouses (kura)), a firewood shed on about four in ten (see How our maps draw firewood sheds (kigoya)), a storage shed on about one in three, and a byre on somewhat under half (see How our maps draw and place byres (umaya)).
  • A firewood shed is 12 feet deep and 24 feet long, the common size in the village of Kakimochi's count of 1885; the storehouse's size is at How our maps draw farm storehouses (kura).
  • A storage shed stands free of the house on a side of the yard rolled per farm. It is about 18 to 27 feet long and 1.5 to 1.8 times as long as it is deep, the size of the two sheds measured from the end of the Edo period, not of the larger barns built after it (see Farm sheds and barns (naya)); that those two stand for an ordinary farm's shed is a GUESS. Its roof is gabled on the Osaka shed and hip-roofed on the Tokyo one, two attested forms, so the roof is a knob rolled per farm.
  • These shares are a calibration against the two village records, of Hasuda in 1824 and Kakimochi in 1885, not a figure any one source gives.
  • The maps draw seven of the yard's small fixtures.
    The privy, the wood shed that holds the firewood (no open stack is drawn; see How our maps draw firewood sheds (kigoya)), the manure heap, the bath room and the persimmon, each large enough to see at the map's scale; the household shrine, rare but notable where it stands; and the chicken coop, a small square roost on the ground, which follows imperial China's practice by this project's choice. Each has its own section on the research page, with the historical shares, and the map gives it a share and a seat on the plot from those.
  • How many fixtures does a map draw?
    Each kind's share of farms is rolled per hamlet, from the map's seed, within a range set from the historical shares in its own section. On the sample hamlet the maps are tested on, of 15 houses, 13 have a privy, 6 a wood shed, 13 a coop, 8 a manure heap, 12 a persimmon, 4 a bath room and 1 a household shrine.
  • The straw rick is not drawn.
    Like every seasonal thing, it waits for maps of a particular season; what it was, where it stood and for how long are at The paddy through the rice year.

Three things a farmstead might be expected to hold are drawn elsewhere: the well and the boundary stone off the farmstead, and the stable wing with the house's plan.

  • The maps' wells are shared ones.
    Jointly owned wells were numerous, because digging one was costly, so a village's wells are drawn as the households' common wells, not one to a farm.
  • A village's boundary-god stone (dosojin) stands by the road at its entrance, not on a farmstead.
    A dosojin stood chiefly at a village's entrances and exits, and in Matsumoto most stand at a settlement's entrance, so a map's entrance stone is drawn beside the road where it comes into the village.
  • A farm's byre is a stable wing of the house (magariya) only where the house itself is L-shaped.
    The stable wing is recorded over almost all of the old Nanbu domain in what is now Iwate, most often in its horse-breeding districts, and one village near Edo in 1824 had most of its stables as a wing too (see The farmstead and what stood on it). The wing belongs to the L-shaped plan, one of the farmhouse's regional plan forms at How our maps draw farmhouses (minka). Otherwise the maps place the byre as the inner stable, a stall inside the house drawn against it, as a shed of its own in the yard, or rarely as a shed on shared ground, the forms at How our maps draw and place byres (umaya).
  • What runs along a farm plot's edge is one of four recorded forms, or rarely none, chosen per settlement.
    It is a knob, rolled per settlement from the map's seed:
    • a hedge, or a woven bamboo fence, along the plot line;
    • a grove, which is also the windbreak, on the sides its settlement rolls - two, three or all four (see How our maps draw groves of trees around farmhouses (yashikirin));
    • the plot closed in by its own house, barn and sheds set round the yard, the form more common going west;
    • where a map stands for a flood plain or a storm coast, an earth bank under trees or a stone wall.
  • A hedge is a thin green line, and a wall a thin gray one.
    The hedge is broken for the way in. How thick the lines are is a map drawing convention, since no size was found.
  • A few plots are left open, with nothing along the edge.
    It rests on no page we read, and is a GUESS.
