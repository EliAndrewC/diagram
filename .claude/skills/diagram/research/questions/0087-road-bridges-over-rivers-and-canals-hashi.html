Road bridges over rivers and canals (hashi)

Before the modern era most of Japan's river bridges were timber: logs laid side by side under a deck of trodden earth, or posts and girders, with a planked deck kept for an important few and stone rarer still. The castle towns built theirs of timber posts and girders because of the character of their rivers and the conditions of the alluvial plains. In China, Zhouzhuang, a water town of the Lower Yangzi, keeps its old stone arch bridges. A canal city might have hundreds of bridges.

The biggest we found a size for is Nihonbashi, the chief bridge of the shogun's capital Edo: over 200 ft long and some 25 ft wide. A bridge does not stop at the water's edge: its ends rest on an abutment on each bank, which also holds back the earth of the road. How the earth-decked bridge differs from the footplank over a farm ditch is at Plank bridges over farm ditches (itabashi).

  • Most Japanese river bridges, up to the Edo period, were earth-decked.
    An earth-decked bridge, the dobashi, was made of logs laid side by side with no gaps; the uneven surface was covered with earth and trodden firm until it was flat. Until the Edo period (1603-1868) the overwhelming majority of bridges over Japan's rivers were built this way, quite long ones among them; bridges decked with planks were limited to an important few, and stone bridges were very few indeed.
  • The castle towns built timber post-and-girder bridges.
    A Japanese government magazine's account of traditional bridges says that before the early modern period almost all Japanese bridges, except very short stone ones, were timber, most of them timber girder bridges, and that when the main castle towns built their bridges they were nearly all timber posts and girders.
  • Stone bridges were an Edo-period exception.
    The same account names stone arch bridges built in parts of Kyushu, stone cantilever bridges in parts of Yamaguchi, and a very few stone arches on Honshu, and calls them exceptions.
  • Zhouzhuang, a Lower Yangzi water town, keeps its old stone arch bridges.
    The town, under Suzhou, ringed by lakes, crossed by waterways and called a typical water town of the Lower Yangzi, keeps them among its surviving Ming and Qing streets.
  • How many bridges did a canal city have?
    The 1229 stone plan of Suzhou, the Pingjiang tu, marks 359 bridges. In Osaka the Higashi-Yokobori, the city's oldest canal, dug in the late sixteenth century and about 3 km (~9,843 ft) long, is crossed by 14 bridges on today's list, which does not say which of them stood in the Edo period.
  • Nihonbashi, Edo's chief bridge, was about 69 m (~226 ft) long and 8 m (~26 ft) wide.
    Miura Joshin's Kenmonshu gives the deck of Nihonbashi, the bridge in Edo from which the five highways were measured, as 37 ken 4 shaku 5 sun long, about 69 m (~226 ft), and 4 ken 2 shaku 5 sun wide, about 8 m (~26 ft).
  • How long and how wide was a town's own bridge, and how many did a town keep?
    No page we read gives a town bridge's length or width, or counts the bridges a town kept over its river and canals.

Modern manuals for small timber bridges say what an abutment does. Older bridges in Japan and China show that setting a bridge back into its banks is not a modern idea.

  • A modern abutment carries a bridge's end and holds back the road's embankment.
    A US manual on timber bridges says an abutment supports the bridge's ends and holds the roadway embankment in place.
  • A modern small bridge's abutment is guarded from scour by stone on the bank, not a deep footing.
    A US guide to small single-span bridges on timber stringers says the abutment's footing must be protected from the scour of the current: a small bridge stands on a shallow strip footing, too shallow to sink below the scour, so the bank is armored with stone instead.
  • A modern timber beam rests on only 10 to 24 inches of its abutment.
    A US manual on timber bridges, in its worked designs, bears its beams on 10 to 24 inches, one of them a beam 95-1/2 ft long out-to-out, so even a beam that long bears on no more than two feet of its bank, and that seat cannot be what carries a deck feet past the water.
  • Edo-period Japan built bridges carried out from their banks with no pier, the cantilever bridge.
    In the 刎橋 (hanebashi, "cantilever bridge"), holes were cut in the bank rock and timbers set into them at a slant to project over the water, each layer reaching a little further than the one beneath it. The Aimoto Bridge over the Kurobe River, which a heritage page dates to 1663, stood where floods carried off any pier within a year, and was made of great timbers thrust out from both banks. The Saruhashi, 30.9 m (~101 ft) long and 3.3 m (~11 ft) wide, has been a bridge of that form since at least 1756.
  • Old Chinese books named the walls of a bridge's abutment.
    The front wall of a stone arch's abutment, the one that bears the load, was anciently the 金刚墙 (jingang qiang, "diamond wall"), and the splayed walls either side of it the 顺水金刚墙 (shunshui jingang qiang, "with-the-current diamond wall").
  • How far past the water did a premodern bridge's deck run?
    No page we read, premodern or modern, gives a distance from the water's edge to a small bridge's abutment.

A walled Chinese city let its canals in through water gates in the wall, and a bridge could stand close by one.

  • At Suzhou's Pan Gate a bridge stands near the gate, outside the wall.
    Pan Gate pairs a road gate with a water gate for boats. The Wumen Bridge, first built in the Northern Song and rebuilt in stone in 1872, carries the main way in and out of that gate over the Grand Canal, near the gate tower. It stands outside the wall, where the water gate leads out from the city's inner canals.
  • Was a Chinese city's canal bridged just inside its water gate, as Suzhou's Grand Canal is just outside one?
    No page we read says so; the roads of the Suzhou plan are described as running beside the canals.

Where a road met a river too wide to bridge, it was ferried or forded instead: Ferries and fords (watashi).
