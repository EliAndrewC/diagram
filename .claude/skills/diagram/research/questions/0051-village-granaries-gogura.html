Village granaries (gogura)

Villages of the Edo period kept storehouses, the gogura, for their tax rice and for grain laid up against a bad harvest. After the harvest the tax rice went into them before it was shipped on to the lord's stores, and from the middle of the period they held reserve grain as well. A storehouse stood among the village's houses, mostly on dry-field ground.

  • Not every village had a storehouse of its own.
    Several villages might share one, or the headman's own storehouse served instead.
  • When did the shogunate order villages to build storehouses of their own?
    In 1789, says a dictionary of Japanese history. At first village officials' private storehouses were often used, until grain was found going out of them improperly, and the shogunate then ordered storehouses built at the village's expense. A Yabu city page dates the order to 1790.
  • The village officials kept the storehouse and entered its deposits and releases in books.
    A dictionary of Japanese history says the storehouse was entrusted to them and its grain booked in and out.
  • How many storehouses did a village keep?
    The dictionary speaks of each village's gogura, but the unit varied. At Hataya in Katashina (Gunma) one was built for each hamlet section on the intendant's order. In the Nikko shrine estate villagers built 8 together. The island of Sado once had 96.
  • How big was a village storehouse?
    The measured village storehouses at Storehouses for the tax rice run from about 21 by 21 ft to about 37 by 20 ft; that section also takes up whether the floor stood on posts. The surviving one at Yamaji, in Yabu city, is smaller than any of those: a two-story tiled building 340 cm (~11 ft) across the front, 290 cm (~10 ft) deep and 500 cm (~16 ft) high to the roof. The one built for each hamlet section at Hataya held 10 koku.
  • A manual for rural officials of 1794 sets the storehouse among the village's houses.
    It is mostly built on dry-field ground, and on a house plot where a vacant one is to be had.
  • The surviving storehouse at Yamaji, in Yabu city, turns its door to the old road.
    Yabu city's page on it says a single storehouse stands there, its door to the old prefectural road.
  • The Kurobane domain built famine stores in its villages in 1769, and they too were called gogura.
    The city of Nasushiobara's page on one of them says they were graded upper, middle or lower by the village's population, held barnyard and foxtail millet, and were run mainly by the village officials. On the ground each was one more village storehouse, not a second kind of building.
  • How much did each household lay up in the Kurobane domain's village stores?
    About 2 sho of barnyard millet a year, as far as the city's page can say ("it seems").
  • Japan also had relief granaries on a Chinese model (shasō), which farmers filled at harvest.
    Farmers put in grain in proportion to their holdings. It was stored district by district against famine and lent out to farmers in ordinary years. The first, it is said, was set up in the Aizu domain in 1655.

China, too, had grain stores that belonged to the village, apart from the state's granary in the county seat.

  • Under the Sui dynasty a charity granary was set up in each village community.
    Its grain was levied by law according to wealth.
  • The later village relief granary (shecang) was mostly run by the villagers themselves.
    It stood mostly in the rural community, and its grain was given by well-off or ordinary households, stored in ordinary times and given out in disaster.
  • The ever-normal granary (changpingcang), the state's store, stood mostly in the county seat.
    It steadied the grain price and served for relief, and the county magistrate ran it directly.
