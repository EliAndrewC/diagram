The dike-pond hamlet: its houses, boats and manure jars

A dike-pond hamlet lived by silk and fish rather than by rice: its households grew mulberry on the banks of their fish ponds, fed the leaves to silkworms, and sold the silk and the fish (Dike-ponds: fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes (sangji yutang)). What stood on its ground differed from a rice hamlet's. In the one silk village studied closely, on Lake Tai, the silkworms were reared inside the houses, nearly every household kept a boat and the houses stood along the water, the mulberry grew on raised ground beside the houses, and the household's manure was kept in earthenware jars half buried in the ground rather than in a heap.

Most of what is known of such a hamlet's farmsteads comes from one book, Fei Hsiao-tung's Peasant Life in China (1939), on the village of Kaihsienkung on the southeast bank of Lake Tai, studied in July and August 1936. Kaihsienkung grew rice as well as silk, on a net of streams, so it shows the silk side of the hamlet directly. Fei's study is of modern times, so where an older account of one of its forms was found, its bullet gives it. The pigs, the fish fry and the other dike crops rest on accounts of the Pearl River delta, told in their own sections. No source we read describes the farmsteads of a hamlet wholly given over to ponds, so what follows is assembled from the two regions, and each bullet says which it comes from.

  • Around Lake Tai, silkworms were reared inside the houses.
    At Kaihsienkung, "Silkworms are raised in rectangular and shallow containers about [1.5] by [1] metre in size. They are put on the shelves of a stand. Each stand has eight containers. Each house has enough room for five stands." In the busiest weeks "all the rooms except the kitchen may be used for sheltering silkworms. All the members of the household will crowd in one bed-room."
  • The front room was the household's workroom.
    At Kaihsienkung the front room "is used for working, such as raising silkworms, manufacturing silk, threshing rice".
  • Did the silk-reeling machine stand in the house?
    Fei describes the old machine, "a furnace to boil water, a wheel ... and a rotating axle connected to a plate for treading", but not where it stood. The front room was where silk was made, so this project reads the machine as standing there.
  • Rice was threshed in the open space before the house, or in the front room.
    At Kaihsienkung the rice was carried in bundles to the open space in front of the house, and "Threshing is done in the open space or in the front room".
  • Was there ever a separate silkworm house?
    No page we read shows one before modern times.

The village lived on its water, and its boats carried what it sold.

  • Around Lake Tai, nearly every household had a boat.
    Fei writes: "Nearly every household possesses one or more boats except those who are not engaged in agricultural or fishing work."
  • Around Lake Tai, the houses stood along the streams, because the boat was how the villagers traveled.
    Fei writes: "The importance of the boat in communication means that the houses must be near the water and consequently determines the plan of the village. Villages grow up along the streams."
  • Each Lake Tai village had an agent boat that bought and sold for it in the town market.
    Fei writes: "Every village has its own agent boat which serves as the buying and selling agent of the villagers in the town market."
  • Did the Pearl River delta's silk also go out by boat?
    A proverb of Minle, in Xiqiao in Nanhai county, says 「a boat of silk goes out, a boat of silver comes back」 (translated; original: 「一船蚕丝出，一船白银归」).
  • Did the delta's silk farmers buy their grain in?
    In the Qing, farmers of Nanhai and Shunde 「abandoned rice to plant mulberry」 (translated; original: 「废稻树桑」). The page does not say where their grain then came from; this project reads that they bought it in, by boat.

The mulberry and the houses shared the raised ground at the edge of each plot.

  • Around Lake Tai, mulberry grew on the raised margin of each plot, with the houses beside it.
    Fei's yu is "the unit of land surrounded by water", and "Along the margin of each yu, ten to thirty metres of land is left for plantation of mulberry trees and a wider space for house building. This land on the margin is higher. It also serves as a dyke for the farm."
  • Around Lake Tai, vegetables grew under the mulberry and in small gardens by the houses.
    Fei writes that the villagers had "only very small gardens adjacent to the houses and limited space under the mulberry trees for growing vegetables."
  • Sheep huts were common by the Lake Tai houses in the 1930s, after sheep raising had grown important there.
    Fei saw "little huts for the sheep and sometimes also for storage" beside the houses, the hut "a common appendage of a house", but sheep raising had become an important domestic industry only "in the past ten years".

Where a rice hamlet heaped its muck in the yard (Manure heaps and compost (kyuhi)), the silk village kept its night soil in jars. Where a Japanese farm kept its night soil is at Farm privies and their night soil (benjo).

  • Fei's village kept its night soil in earthenware jars half buried behind the houses, and all along the road.
    "Human manure, being the most important fertilizer in the farm, is preserved in the pits made of earthenware, half buried in the ground at the back of the building. Along the southern bank of the Stream A, the public road is lined up with these manure pits."
  • A British visitor saw manure jars sunk by paths and roads in eighteenth-century China.
    The account of Britain's embassy to the Qing court, printed in 1797, says of Chinese farmers' care for manure: "In various parts of a farm, and near paths and roads large earthen vessels are buried to the edge in the ground for the accommodation of the labourer or passenger who may have occasion to use them." So the jar, the ground it is sunk in, and its place by a path or a road are all older than modern times. The passage stands in the chapter on the embassy's journey toward Canton, but it speaks of Chinese practice in general and names no province, so it ties the form to neither Lake Tai nor the Pearl River delta. Its jars are buried to the rim, where Fei's were half buried.
  • A Chinese farming book of 1313 already puts manure pits at the head of the field.
    A modern Chinese encyclopedia of farming history quotes Wang Zhen's farming book of 1313: 「Southern households that farm fields commonly set a brick enclosure at the head of the field and use [the manure] after it has ripened in the pit; their fields are very fine」 (translated; original: 「南方治田之家，常于田头置砖槛，窖熟而用之，其田甚美」). Wang Zhen's is a manure store out at the field rather than by the house, and lined with brick, not made of earthenware.
  • A Ming-dynasty farm manual wanted its manure pits deep and large.
    The same encyclopedia says the Shen family farm manual wants its manure pits 「deep and large」 (translated; original: 「潭要深大」). Its pit takes manure brought home, and the page does not say what lined it.
  • Did farmers also seal night soil in buried jars to ripen?
    The same encyclopedia's "golden juice" is got by 「sealing human excrement and urine in jars, pots and other vessels and burying them in the ground for a long time」 (translated; original: 「将人粪尿放在缸罐等容器内密封后长期埋于地下」). It gives the practice no date, and a sealed jar left to ripen is not the open jar a passer-by used, so it bears on the roadside jar only weakly.

Japan kept its night soil in jars too.

  • In Japan a storage jar for night soil is on record by 1681, in the Edo period.
    The Hyakusho Denki, as the Japanese Wikipedia quotes it, warns against putting fresh night soil on the fields and says it should be "rotted well inside the storage jar (tametsubo)". The passage does not say whether the jar was buried, or where it stood.
  • How big was a manure jar?
    None of these passages gives its size, and no other page we read does.

Four more things stood on the delta's dike-pond hamlets; each has its own section.

  • Pigs were kept on the pond banks, and the ducks were herded in the rice fields.
    The pig was the dike-pond village's animal, and the ducks were not penned at the ponds: Pigs and ducks at a dike-pond: the sty on the pond dike
  • Fish fry were a trade of their own, raised in nursery ponds.
    Most villages bought their fry: Fish fry and nursery ponds (yumiao).
  • Fruit and tea grew on pond dikes as well as mulberry.
    The fruit dike is the oldest: Mulberry and other crops on pond dikes (sangji, guoji).
  • Sluices in the polder's dike let the water in and out.
    They are at Dike-ponds: fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes (sangji yutang).
