Country estates of the gentry and samurai around a city

A landholding family's country house stood by its own fields or its home village, scattered across the countryside, not in a ring just outside a city's wall. In China by the Qing dynasty, much of the gentry lived in the towns as absentee landlords and worked their land through agents, and that land lay in scattered plots, not one block. Where a lineage kept its home village, that village could lie a few miles out: around the city of Ningbo, eleven gentry lineages had theirs within about six miles of the wall. In Japan in the Edo period, the samurai were moved off the land to live gathered in the castle town.

A country seat that did stand alone in the countryside was fortified: a moated compound for the medieval Japanese lord, a walled village for a southern Chinese clan. The ground hugging a city's gates was not a genteel quarter but a commoners' suburb of markets and lodging houses.

  • By the Qing dynasty, much of China's gentry lived in town as absentee landlords.
    A study of Chinese local elites finds them living the leisured life of absentee landlords in the administrative centers or in the small towns along the canals. Why they moved in, the source does not say: that the examinations, office and the social life of the seat drew them is this record's GUESS.
  • In the Lower Yangzi, landlords who moved to town worked their land through agents.
    After the landlords of the Lower Yangzi moved into the towns and cities, they acted in the villages through agents and rent-collection offices, and less of a truly rural elite remained.
  • In China, an elite family's land usually lay in scattered plots.
    In most times and places land was gathered only slowly, in small pieces and scattered plots, so a family's holding was not one block around one house.
  • In the Lower Yangzi, the large estate under one resident landlord gave way in the seventeenth century.
    At the Ming-Qing transition, landholding there moved away from large estates managed by a single resident landlord toward small, fragmented holdings farmed by fairly independent peasant households. The resident landlord's estate was the older form, though the passage does not say when it began.
  • In southern China, a lineage could have a village of its own.
    Clan members there could form a village of their own, an ancestral village. That a lineage's big house therefore stood by its own village and fields, scattered across the district rather than gathered at the city, is this record's reasoning.
  • In southern China, some clans lived in walled villages, fortified as a whole.
    A walled village is a large communal settlement of extended families or a clan sharing one surname, surrounded by thick defensive walls; such villages still stand in southern China and Hong Kong. It is a whole clan's village, not one rich family's compound.
  • How far from a city did the gentry's home villages lie?
    The one count we found is from Ningbo, from 1368 to 1911. Of 42 gentry lineages whose addresses are known, 24 were in the city or its immediate suburbs, and another eleven had their home villages west and southwest of the city within ten kilometers (about six miles) of its wall; one of them lay some six kilometers (about four miles) out.
  • How far beyond six miles the estates reached is this record's guess.
    No page we read puts gentry estates farther out than the Ningbo villages or sets a floor on how near they came. That the estates themselves, as against the home villages, reached out to some fifteen miles, beginning about two miles from the wall, is this record's GUESS.
  • The streets just outside a city gate held lodging houses and markets.
    A Chinese dictionary defines the guanxiang as the streets outside a city gate and the area around them, and in antiquity the lodging houses near the walls. At Beijing, the horse market moved to the guanxiang outside the Desheng Gate, with 48 horse inns at its height: commoners' trade, not gentlemen's houses. The suburb itself is at The ground outside a city gate.

Japan's warriors went the other way in time: a medieval lord lived on his own land, and the Edo period gathered the samurai into the castle towns.

  • A medieval Japanese lord's seat stood moated among its own paddy.
    The resident local lord's main house was a residence plot with the paddy and dry fields at its gate, holding a storehouse for seed and tools, huts for retainers and servants, a stable, riding and archery grounds and earthen ramparts; its moat both defended it and watered the gate paddy. A moat and a bank of piled earth were usually run round such a house, which gave it its names. The gate paddy itself was usually long-established rice land, managed directly by the lord and worked by his dependents.
  • In the Edo period, samurai were moved off the land into the castle town.
    The separation of warriors from farmers moved the warrior class from living on its land to living gathered in the castle town. Even retainers who held villages as their fief were bound to live in the castle town; Sendai's retainers, who moved between the castle town and their fiefs, are the exception the article names. That most samurai lived in town is this record's reading of a shift the article does not count.
  • Not every domain moved its samurai into town.
    No policy ever moved the whole warrior class into the towns, and in domains that kept a half-farming system, under which retainers went on farming their own land as well as serving their lord, or where food ran short, many retainers lived in the farming country or the townsmen's quarters to the end of the Edo period.
  • Inside a castle town, the upper samurai lived close to the castle.
    Upper samurai built hedged or fenced residences inside the middle moat, near the castle, and the lower ranks, the ashigaru, lived in row-houses around its outer edge.
  • Some Japanese lords kept a retreat just outside their town.
    Lords kept retreats on the outskirts, told at A lord's retreat just outside town (shimoyashiki). That such a retreat stood within a mile or a few of the town, and apart from the next, is this record's GUESS.

In both countries, a seat that stood alone in the countryside was fortified.

  • Why did the country seats stand apart instead of in a row outside the wall?
    A seat that stood alone beyond a city's protection had to defend itself, and a row of rich, undefended compounds just outside a wall would draw bandits to the very ground the wall was built to guard, so the seats kept apart, each defending itself. Both points are this record's reasoning: the pages that describe the moated Japanese seat and the walled Chinese village put them in the countryside, but neither says they were fortified because they stood beyond a city's protection: the walled-village article says only that the walls protected the residents from wild animals and enemies.
