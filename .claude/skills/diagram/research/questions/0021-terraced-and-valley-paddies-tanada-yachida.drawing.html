How our maps draw terraced and valley paddies (tanada, yachida)

A map draws rice on a slope in one of two forms, and only where its ground calls for one: terraces on hill ground, and a long valley paddy in a confined valley. Terraces are drawn wherever hill ground leaves no valley floor, because this project reads the terrace as the usual answer to such ground, not a rarity. A map with neither draws the ordinary valley fan. In both forms the field is divided into small cells, so it reads as a row or a chain of small paddies, as the history has it, rather than as one long sheet. No scripted map draws either form yet; only hand-drawn maps use them.

The rule the map follows, on hill ground:

  • Rice is drawn as stacked terrace steps along the contour.
    They step down the slope from the catchment above.
  • Water comes down one flank and spills from step to step.
    A supply channel runs down one flank and cascades from each step to the next, into a drain at the foot; the route is a drawing convention, so the map shows where the water goes.
  • A map that draws terraces carries at least eight retaining walls across the slope.
    The count is a drawing convention; no page we read gives a number of steps.
  • Every step is divided along the contour into small cells.
    Each cell is one paddy, sized by the slope as set out below.

The rule the map follows, in a confined valley:

  • Rice is drawn as one long narrow field meandering down the valley floor.
    A valley paddy is long and narrow, following the valley floor.
  • The valley's brook runs down the middle of the field.
    No page we read says where the stream ran through a valley paddy; the middle is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • Banks across the field step it down the valley.
    Each bank holds one level stretch of the field above the next, a drawing convention so the field holds water as the valley falls.
  • Each stretch is split across its width into small cells.
    A cell is the same size as a terrace paddy on a slope as steep as the valley floor.

The paddy's size and the wall's height follow the slope the map draws; the wall's form does not.

  • How big is a terrace paddy on our maps?
    It is tied to the slope, a calibrated liberty: on a steep slope the benches are narrow and the paddies small, down to about twenty square meters (about two hundred square feet), as at the Shiroyone Senmaida, on ground falling about 50 m (~164 ft) to the sea; on a gentle one the benches are deep and a paddy reaches a few hundred square meters (a few thousand square feet), as at the Obasute terraces, on a slope of one in seven.
  • The wall stands higher where the ground is steeper.
    That is the direction the history gives, so the wall's height moves opposite to the paddy's size.
  • The wall's form is a knob, chosen at random for each settlement.
    A stone wall built to stand on the steep slope, as western Japan built, or a sloping earth bank that takes up more of the slope, as the east built. They are two recorded forms, not two degrees of one, so each settlement gets one or the other rather than something in between.
