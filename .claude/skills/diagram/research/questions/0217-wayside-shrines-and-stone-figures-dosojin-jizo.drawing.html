How our maps place wayside shrines and stone figures (dosojin, jizo)

Our maps set the stone figures of the deity of the way (dosojin) and of jizo where the history puts them, at the places a road enters a settlement's houses, and give a town a small street shrine for each of its blocks. The halls some regions built beside their roads are drawn in some villages and not others.

  • A group of one to three stones stands beside the way where each road or lane enters a hamlet or a village.
    Most dosojin stand at a settlement's entrance, and at one entrance two stand in a row with other stone monuments, so a map draws a group rather than a single stone. The count of one to three is a GUESS: the one entrance counted has two.
  • A crossroads, the foot of a bridge or a village well may have a stone or a small shrine of its own.
    The deity of the way was enshrined at crossroads and bridges' feet as well as at a village's entrance, and a shrine could stand beside a well. Which of them do is a GUESS, left to each settlement's seed.
  • Each stone is drawn about 4 ft across, a map drawing convention.
    That is the smallest mark that shows at the map's scale. Real stones ran from palm-sized to nearly 2 m (~7 ft) tall.
  • Does a village keep wayside halls as well?
    Each village's seed decides, at even odds - a knob, because the halls were common in the province of Bingo and rare in others. That half of villages keep them is a GUESS.
  • A village that keeps wayside halls has one for every 20 to 30 of its households.
    That was the rate at which the settlements of the Fukuyama domain built them.
  • Each hall is an open hall about 6 to 18 ft square.
    Most standing today have a floor of about two mats, and an account of 1862 gives such halls 2 to 3 ken on a side.
  • A hall stands beside the road between settlements, at a pass or in a busy place, never far from the houses.
    That is where the Fukuyama halls stood.
  • Does a town keep a small street shrine, a jizo's or an Inari's, for each of its blocks?
    Our maps give every block one. Towns in the Kinki region kept a jizo for each block, and Inari shrines were met everywhere in Edo. That the count holds outside the Kinki is a GUESS: from the Kanto eastward the dosojin is said to have done the street jizo's work.
