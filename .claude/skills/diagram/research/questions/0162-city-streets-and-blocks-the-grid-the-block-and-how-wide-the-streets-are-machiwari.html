City streets and blocks: the grid, the block and how wide the streets are (machiwari)

A planned Japanese city was laid out as a grid of streets around blocks of a known size. The blocks of Heian-kyo, the old capital at Kyoto, were about 120 m (~394 ft) square, and the commoner quarter of Edo, laid out many centuries later, kept a square of about the same size, with its house lots along the streets and open ground at the middle. Most castle towns cut their blocks as rectangles instead. A castle town also laid its streets out for defense: houses lined the trunk road without a gap so that the castle was hard to see, and the roads bent to lengthen the way to it.

The streets between the blocks were narrow beside the grand avenues of the ancient capitals. Heian-kyo's lanes were about 12 m (~39 ft) wide and its central avenue over 80 m (~262 ft), while a highway of the Edo period ran some 9 to 15 m (~30-49 ft).

  • Heian-kyo's blocks were 40 jo, about 120 m (~394 ft), on a side, not counting the roads.
    The avenues and lanes of the old capital cut it into square blocks, each called a machi, fixed at 40 jo a side exclusive of the roads, with lanes about 12 m (~39 ft) wide between them.
  • Fujiwara-kyo and Heijo-kyo, the capitals before Heian-kyo, cut wards of different sizes into blocks of one size.
    Fujiwara-kyo's wards (bo), as inferred from its law code, were 90 jo, about 270 m (~886 ft), on a side, and Heijo-kyo's twice that, 180 jo. Both were cut by lanes into blocks 45 jo from road center to road center, so the block stayed the same while the ward grew, and the ground inside the lanes was less than 45 jo.
  • At Heian-kyo a lesser official's house lot was, in principle, a thirty-second of a block.
    It was 5 by 10 jo, about 15 m (~49 ft) by 30 m (~98 ft), some 136 tsubo, and some households lived on less.
  • In practice, Heian-kyo's blocks were divided many ways.
    They ranged from a noble's mansion covering several blocks to commoners' lots cut so as to face only the north-south streets.

Edo's commoner quarter, laid out from 1603, took up the square again, and its blocks are the best described.

  • Edo's commoner quarter kept the square block, about 120 m (~394 ft) a side, with open ground at its center.
    Its plan took as its unit a square 60 ken on a side in the Kyoto measure, with lots 20 ken deep along each of its streets and an open ground, the kaishochi, 20 ken square, left at the middle. Twenty ken, a third of the side, is about 40 m (~131 ft) - this page's arithmetic. What the open ground was used for is not clear.
  • In Edo a ward was a street, not a block.
    A ward, a machi in the other sense of the word, was the two sides of one street facing each other across it. Who ran a ward is at City wards and the gates that shut them at night (machi, kido).
  • On the blocks of Edo's first commoner quarter, shops fronted the street and tenement rows stood down alleys behind them.
    Shops stood in a row on the street side of a lot, and down the alley behind them stood the nagaya, the craftsmen's tenement rows, on both sides. The page describes the quarter laid out from Nihonbashi to Kyobashi, not every part of Edo.
  • How many house lots did an Edo block hold?
    Some fifty along its streets. A lot's frontage marked its holder's standing - in Kyoto from the middle of the Edo period, as in Edo: 10 ken or more for a large house, 4 or 5 for an ordinary one, and 1.5 to 2 for a small one, whose holder might instead rent. On a 60 ken side, ordinary frontages give about twelve to fifteen lots a side, so a block held some fifty street-front lots, fewer where a large house took several frontages, with the tenement rows behind them uncounted. The count is this page's arithmetic, not a figure from a source.
  • In Edo, a quarter's bustle was on its main streets, not its alleys.
    The alleys of Edo's commoner quarter were no place of bustle; the main streets alone carried it. What a back alley was like underfoot is at Shops on the street, tenements behind: how a city is zoned (omotedana, uradana).

Most castle towns did not follow Edo's square, even square blocks were later cut through, and a castle town laid out its main road for defense.

  • Most castle towns used a rectangular block with no open ground.
    Square blocks were limited, outside Edo, to a few towns such as Sunpu (today's Shizuoka) and Nagoya; the great majority of castle towns laid out rectangular blocks that kept no open ground.
  • In Kyoto and in Edo, square blocks were later cut through by new lanes.
    In 1591 Toyotomi Hideyoshi ran a north-south lane through the middle of the blocks around Kyoto's center, remaking its squares as rectangles, and after the Meireki fire of 1657 two new lanes were cut through the Ginza blocks of Edo.
  • A castle town lined its trunk road with houses to screen the castle, and bent its roads to lengthen the way to it.
    Houses stood along both sides of the trunk road with no gaps between them, making the castle hard to see, and the roads were bent into right-angle dog-legs and stopped in dead ends to lengthen the way to the castle.

Chinese cities are a different case.

  • No page we read gives the size of a block in a Chinese prefectural city or county seat.
    The capital's walled wards are on record (at City wards and the gates that shut them at night (machi, kido)), but not the block of a provincial city, nor how many lots it held. How a county seat's trunk streets ran is at How densely a city is built, and what counts as empty ground.

How wide the streets were varied far more than the blocks, from the processional avenues of the ancient capitals to the highways of the Edo period. How wide a town's own street and its lanes were is at Town streets, side lanes and back alleys (roji).

  • How wide were the central avenues of the ancient capitals?
    Heijo-kyo's Suzaku avenue was 73 m (~240 ft) wide, and Heian-kyo's 82 m (~269 ft) by the Engishiki, or 84.0 m (~276 ft) by a later estimate, 70.2 m (~230 ft) of it roadway inside the side ditches. The Suzaku avenue of Tang Chang'an was 147 m (~482 ft) wide.
  • Early in the Edo period the Tokaido's width was set at 5 ken, about 9.1 m (~30 ft).
    In the second month of 1604 Okubo Nagayasu and others were ordered to make the highway 5 ken wide, with hackberry trees planted along it and a marker at every ri.
  • How wide did an Edo-period highway run?
    Roughly 7 to 8 ken, 13 to 15 m (~43-49 ft), by a page on Ginza's history, which gives Ginza-dori, the Tokaido's run through Edo, as about that width too. That is wider than the 5 ken ordered in 1604. No page we read says where either width applied; we take the 5 ken to be the road's standard between towns and the 7 to 8 ken its width where it became a city's main street, a GUESS.
