Firewood stacks and sheds (kigoya)

Firewood was a farm's fuel, and before Japan began to modernize in 1868 many farms kept it in a shed of its own, a wood shed (kigoya), standing apart from the house. In one village at the end of the Edo period, more than a third of the households had one, most often about 24 by 12 ft.

Twentieth-century records from the cold north add a woodshed for the winter's fuel and a stack of firewood along the farm's windbreak grove, and a present-day page stacks firewood in the open under the eaves. No page we read dates either stack before 1868.

  • A wood shed was a building for keeping fuel.
    The reconstructed farmstead at the Boso-no-Mura open-air museum lists one, for the charcoal, firewood and the like used as fuel; the page does not say where on the farmstead its firewood was stacked.
  • In 1824, late in the Edo period, many of Hasuda village's houses had a firewood shed or a storage shed of their own.
    The village's house-by-house record of 1824 shows them standing apart from the main house, on its plot.
  • A village headman's homestead plan of 1849, in the Edo period, has a firewood barn among its buildings.
    It is the plan of the Ohara house of Seinagoya village, in the town history of Oamishirasato.
  • How many farms had a wood shed?
    In Kakimochi village, 6 households of 16, with 7 sheds among them. The count is of 1885, and the town's historian reads its houses as roughly the village of the last years of the shogunate.
  • Did the larger farms have the sheds?
    The town's historian says it of the outbuildings together, not of the wood shed alone: the more of them a house had, the larger its main house, and the houses with the most were those of the leading farmers who served as village officials.
  • How big was a wood shed?
    The seven wood sheds counted in Kakimochi village in 1885 ran from 1.5 by 1.5 ken (about 9 by 9 ft) to 5 by 2 ken (about 30 by 12 ft), and three of the seven were 4 by 2 ken (about 24 by 12 ft).
  • Where on the farmstead did the wood shed stand?
    The pages we read say only that it stood apart from the main house, on the house's plot, as at Hasuda in 1824, and the Seinagoya headman's plan of 1849 has its firewood barn scattered over the plot among the other buildings. None says where on the plot.
  • A modern count, of 1972, found about three firewood sheds to every four of the older farmhouses.
    Sugiura's survey of three hamlets in Miyagi prefecture, in northeastern Japan, counts 0.76 firewood sheds a household on the farms whose main house was built in or before 1944 - higher than Kakimochi's.

In the cold north, twentieth-century records show the wood shed again, and a second way of keeping the winter's firewood: stacked along the farm's windbreak grove.

  • On the Shonai plain of the Japan Sea coast, a twentieth-century survey names a woodshed for the winter's fuel as a mark of the cold country's farmsteads.
    A survey of farmstead layouts, published in 1962, names it, with a fine outdoor privy, as characteristic of the plain. Its example farmstead was surveyed in 1948.
  • On the Isawa plain, firewood was stacked under each farm's windbreak grove as a wall (kizuma).
    Each homestead's grove stood on its northwest side against the winter wind, and the firewood was stacked beneath it, some stacks roofed with straw or tin so that from afar they looked like a wall. The stack filled the gaps under the grove's lower branches against wind and snow. The grove itself is at Groves of trees around farmhouses (yashikirin).
  • At Yahaba, in the north, a twentieth-century study found some firewood simply stacked with a roof set over it.
    Sugiura's 1977 study of farm outbuildings in the northeast did not count these as outbuildings, and reads them as a small form of the Isawa plain's stack along the windbreak.
  • Was the stack along the windbreak older than modern times?
    No page we read places it before modern times: it is described only on pages of today and of the twentieth century, as the farms' way in the past, with no date.

Elsewhere, present-day pages show a third way, firewood stacked in the open under the eaves.

  • Was firewood stacked in the open under the eaves before 1868?
    The open stacks we found are all on present-day pages. A modern page on keeping firewood calls for a rack, stacks it to about 1.5 m (~5 ft), and prefers a spot under an eave. No page we read places an open stack, or its height, before 1868.
