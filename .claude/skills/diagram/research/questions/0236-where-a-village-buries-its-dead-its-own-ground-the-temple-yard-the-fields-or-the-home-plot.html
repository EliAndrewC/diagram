Where a village buries its dead: its own ground, the temple yard, the fields or the home plot

At the end of the Edo period, before 1868, a Japanese village's dead did not all lie in one kind of ground. A temple's graveyard, a graveyard the settlement held in common, a lineage's ground and a single person's grave all stood across the country, each left to local custom. Where the dead had two graves, the body lay, most often in common ground, on a riverbank, in the hills or by the shore, while the grave the family visited stood at a temple or a hall in the village. And many households each held a graveyard of their own, some of them within their own house land.

Chinese graves often lay out in the working fields instead, taking 2.6 per cent of the farm land in the five level-land localities of one 1920s survey.

  • Japan had graveyards of several forms at the end of the Edo period.
    Graveyards of an individual, a lineage, a settlement and a temple stood across the country into early Meiji, with little idea of private ownership in them, each left to local custom.
  • Temple graveyards spread in the Edo period.
    Supported by the temple-certification system, many temples were founded, the graveyards attached to them spread, and the dead came more often to be buried at a temple.
  • One excavated temple graveyard was so crowded that burials overlapped.
    At a temple ground in Sagamihara, reburied when its temple moved, the ground was small and burials were made in the same place, so many overlapped.
  • A household was registered with the temple that buried it.
    Under the Tokugawa parish system the registered unit was the household, not the person, and what the temple gave it was the funerals and the continuing memorial rites for all its members. The system grew out of the government's campaigns against Christianity of 1613 and 1614.
  • Did a family's grave stand at a temple outside its own village?
    Making one's grave at one's parish temple was half an obligation. Most villages' households were registered with temples outside the village (Village temples (tera)), so where that obligation was kept, a family's grave stood at a temple away from its own settlement. Where the dead had two graves, the grave at a temple was the visiting grave, and it stood at a temple or a hall within the village.
  • A village could hold a graveyard in common.
    Japanese has a name, 村落墓地 (sonraku bochi, "village graveyard"), for a graveyard that the inhabitants of a settlement own in common and manage themselves. New ones are not permitted today.
  • Where the dead had two graves, the body's grave was usually on common ground.
    The two-grave custom separates the ground where the body is buried from the ground where its grave is visited. The burial grave lay on a riverbank, in the hills or by the shore, most often on common land, filled in the order of the deaths and marked with no more than a stone. Many lay in mountain forest far from the houses. That ground was often a graveyard each settlement held in common, sometimes divided into plots by household or by age and sometimes under no rule at all.
  • The grave the family visited stood at a temple or a hall in the village.
    The visiting grave was a stone pagoda holding neither body nor bone, raised for visits at Bon and for the memorial of the ancestors.
  • In one Wakasa hamlet the burial ground stood at the hamlet's edge.
    A 1966 Fukui record of the funeral custom puts the sanmai, the burial ground or the hall at one, at the edge of the hamlet of Takenami, while the visiting graves stood in two temples inside the hamlet.
  • In two-grave villages the shrine stood at the upper end, and the body's grave never above the houses.
    A survey of 22 two-grave settlements puts the shrine at the upper end of the village, the end its watercourse makes the upper one, and the burial grave below the settlement but never above it.
  • A shrine's priests were bound to keep away from death, which Shinto counts a defilement.
    Death and everything to do with it is a primary source of defilement, kegare, which a shrine's priests are bound to avoid.
  • How common was the two-grave custom?
    Prefectural folklore surveys counted the settlements keeping it prefecture by prefecture: 60 in Shiga, 59 in Nara, 45 in Mie, 42 in Kyoto, 39 each in Hyogo and Fukui, 22 in Shizuoka, and few elsewhere. A dictionary of Japanese history centers it on the Kansai region and finds it hardly at all in Kyushu or Tohoku. The counts are of the two-grave custom rather than of village graveyards in general, and of settlements confirmed in surveys of 1973 to 1983, so they are a floor rather than a rate.
  • One person's remains could be divided among several graves.
    Divided remains - one person's cremated remains shared among two or more graves or ossuaries - is attested, at no stated period or place.
  • Did every village, or every hamlet, keep a burial ground of its own before 1868?
    No page dated before modern times says so. Every page that dates a settlement's own graveyard before 1868 names it beside other forms. That each village and each hamlet kept one rests on folklore records and surveys of the twentieth century, such as the prefectural surveys of 1973 to 1983.
  • No page we read says how common each form of graveyard was, or where.
    None says how common each form was village by village, or which regions favored which, and none attaches a count of households to a village's graveyard.
  • No page we read counts the burial grounds of a village of several hamlets.
    Nor does any say how often a hamlet's dead went to the temple's ground rather than to a ground of their own.
  • On Kikaijima, graveyards of each settlement formed after 1868.
    When the Meiji government banned exposure burial, where to make the burial place varied from settlement to settlement, and graveyards of each settlement began to form outside the old cliff caves. The island's settlements are assumed to have differed widely in custom.
  • A Meiji law of 1884 opened a village's graveyard to all its inhabitants.
    Under the state's first systematic graveyard law, a Home Ministry instruction of November 1884, anyone registered in a town or village, or dying there, could be buried in its graveyard whatever their sect, unless an older special custom stood.

A household could also keep a grave of its own, on its own land, apart from any shared ground.

  • Many Japanese households once kept a grave on their own land.
    Formerly many households each held a graveyard, or a branch house set its grave beside the main house's so that the kin shared one, and some set the grave within their own house land - the yashiki-baka, the house-plot grave. Until the Second World War many graves were still built on a corner of a household's own land, or kept by a neighborhood group.
  • Where were house-plot graves common?
    Yanagita Kunio, as a local-history blog reports him, found houses with a graveyard within or beside the plot common in the Kanto, in the Ou region of the northeast and in the mountain villages of southern Kyushu, among other places. Today such graves are few across the country, and are said to be especially few in the Kansai and many in the Kanto.
  • Where on its plot did a household set its grave?
    One family's history, set down by a descendant in the former Hatara district, puts its graveyard before the Genroku era (1688-1704) at the southeast corner of the house plot, and another at the northwest corner, ground now counted as woodland. The writer believes the dead there were buried without gravestones in the woods behind the plot's shrine to its guardian god, part of the grove round the house. In that district, the writer says, many houses have a graveyard adjoining the plot or close by it, though not within the grounds, while in one area of Saitama's Kodama district many are said to have one beside the plot. In the hills, where flat land is scarce, the graveyard is said often to stand part-way up the slope behind the house.
  • A household's grave could also stand in its own field.
    A stonemason who visited Fukaya, in the north of Saitama, saw each household's grave built on part of its own land: in the middle of a field along the road, or in a house's front garden, among other places. In Ibaraki such graves are still often seen within the house grounds, in the garden, before the entrance, or in a corner of a field.
  • Where did a household's grave go when it left the house plot?
    A local historian of the former Hatara district surmises that its graves were moved out of the house plots, and says that no house moved its grave far: many used part of their own fields or woods within the hamlet. In some areas of western Tokyo the burial graves on the home's own land were later moved to a communal graveyard.
  • From the Meiji period on, household graves were often gathered into shared grounds.
    Because households setting up graveyards here and there as they pleased, all on tax-exempt land, ran against the common interest, graveyards were often consolidated from Meiji on. By Takatori Masao's account, as a local-history blog on the former Hatara district reports him, the authorities advised gathering each district's graves into one communal ground, and many districts did so while others did not.
  • Some Japanese burial graves stood beside the bunds of a family's own fields.
    In the Tenryu river basin and in the Otawa district of Narusawa village in Yamanashi, the burial graves are reported to have once stood on private land such as beside the bunds, and to have moved to common land by order of the Meiji government. In 1872 a Finance Ministry notice forbade burying the dead at the bund edge of one's own fields, having heard that people were doing so. The notice does not say how common the practice was.
  • Did a Japanese grave ever stand out in the middle of a paddy?
    No Japanese page we read puts one there: every placement read is at the bund edge, in a field's corner or on the house plot.
  • No page we read says what share of households buried on their own ground.
    Nor does any say what share of hamlets did rather than use a shared ground, or how that differed between hamlet and village.

In China the dead lay in single graves out in the fields, in a lineage's small ground, or, for the poor, in a charity graveyard.

  • Where no hill land could take them, Chinese graves stood anywhere in a field.
    A farm survey of the 1920s found that in most regions with no hill land to take them, graves stood in every conceivable position in a field, whatever the trouble to cultivation, and it shows a well-to-do family's graveyard in north China filling almost a whole field.
  • In south China, graves also stood on the hillsides.
    Hillside burial is attested in southern China as well (Ponds, rocks and graves in the fields), so the field grave is one of two southern forms.
  • How much of the farm land did graves take?
    In the 1920s farm survey's five localities of level land only, graves took 2.6 per cent of the farm land, and at most 9.1 per cent, on the fertile land of Wuchin county in Kiangsu.
  • Around Shanghai, many villagers buried their dead one by one in the open fields.
    Villagers usually had a small burial ground, often a lineage's, but many buried their dead in single graves in the middle of open fields, at a spot a Taoist priest or a geomancer chose. A column of soldiers that marched into Shanghai from Wusong in 1842 reported graves in every field, as mounds of earth.
  • How common were graves in the fields?
    No source gives a rate: the Shanghai account says outright that there is no way now to know how many such graves there were or where they stood. What it does give - "many" villagers, graves "in every field" on one march - argues that where the custom held they were common, not rare.
  • No page we read says whether the field graves around Shanghai stood in flooded paddy.
    The account of Shanghai's graves does not say what the fields grew, so it shows graves inside working fields in the rice country of the Yangzi delta without saying that any one stood in a paddy.
  • Graves were still scattered across the farmland of Henan in 2012.
    In the spring of 2012 the province's party secretary saw many graves scattered across farmland, and the province later announced it had leveled more than two million graves to make fields. It is a modern report.
  • In Jiangnan, rich households' graves took good farmland.
    In the Jiangnan region most of the many surviving old graves occupy good farmland and prime scenic ground, and a study of Ming and Qing Jiangnan calls the seizure of good land and feng shui ground by rich households astonishing.
  • Jiangnan's shared burial grounds were chiefly charity graveyards for the poor.
    The communal grounds a study of Ming and Qing Jiangnan describes are chiefly charity graveyards: the Ming code required every prefecture and county to bury the destitute, and the charity graveyards of benevolent societies stood mostly in the towns.
  • In China, brothers dividing an estate are often said to have left the grave land undivided.
    In traditional China what brothers left undivided is often said to have been only grave land and land for the rites. One Huizhou lineage kept its founding ancestor's grave-hill with only its ridge in the ancestral hall's name, its slopes worked in common and shared among the lineage's members.
