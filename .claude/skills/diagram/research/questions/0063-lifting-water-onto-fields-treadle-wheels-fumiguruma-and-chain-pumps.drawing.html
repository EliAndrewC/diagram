How our maps draw treadle wheels (fumiguruma) and chain pumps

Treadle wheels, chain pumps and norias lifted water from a stream or ditch up onto fields that stood above it. Our maps water every field by gravity, from a ditch that leaves a stream above it, so the water runs down to the paddies on its own and no lifting device is drawn. Only where a settlement's fields stand above the water they draw from is one drawn, and then only sometimes: the Japanese treadle wheel was carried to whichever paddy needed it, while the Chinese chain pump and noria were fixed in place.

  • A treadle wheel is not drawn, because it was a tool, not a fixture.
    The treadle wheel was not fixed in place: it was carried to whichever paddy needed it. A map does not draw a tool.
  • In China, the chain pump and the noria were fixtures, and can be drawn.
    Both stand only where a settlement's fields lie above their water: a chain pump station at still water (the Ming text names lakes and ponds; setting it on a polder's bank or at a low terrace's edge is our placement), and, on a river bank, a noria in the stream with its trough to the fields.
  • Where were fields likeliest to need their water lifted?
    In the reclaimed lowlands downstream, the polders, where new channels were hard to dig: that is where the treadle wheel spread in Japan. Even there it was carried, not fixed in place, so a Japanese polder still shows no lifting device.
  • Where fields stand above their water, the form is a knob.
    The two traditions differ, so the form is chosen at random for each settlement: the Japanese form, the carried wheel, which leaves nothing to draw, or the Chinese form, a fixed pump or a noria. The share each form gets is a GUESS, not yet set.
  • A settlement whose fields stand above their water gets one lifting device per feeding ditch.
    It stands where the ditch meets the raised fields. This is a GUESS: no page we read counts a village's devices.
  • How long is a drawn chain pump?
    About 10 to 21 ft up the bank: the Tiangong kaiwu, a Ming encyclopedia of technology, gives a pump one to two zhang long, and the conversion takes the zhang at about 3.2 m (~10 ft), our GUESS, since no page we cite gives its length.

The rule the map follows: no lifting device is drawn at a gravity-fed field. Where a settlement's fields stand above the water they draw from, the map chooses the form at random, from its seed: nothing drawn (the carried wheel), or one fixed lifting device per feeding ditch where the ditch meets the raised fields - a chain pump, about 10 to 21 ft long up the bank, or, beside a river with current, a noria. The share between the forms, and one device per ditch, are guesses.
