How our maps draw a magistrate's compound (jin'ya and yamen)

Our maps draw a magistrate's compound as a building plan: an outer court with the office hall, an inner court with the magistrate's residence behind it, and a gate between them, all inside a wall. The plan follows the Japanese intendant's office where the Japanese and Chinese records differ, and takes from the Chinese county yamen what the Japanese record leaves out. It is drawn at 3 px to the foot, and its layout is checked by a program.

  • Where the two traditions differ, our maps follow the Japanese form.
    The setting's administrative culture is modeled on Japanese samurai society, so where a Chinese yamen and a Japanese intendant's office differ, the plan takes the office's form, scaled to the setting's population:
    • A small jail inside the walls. A yamen had a jail inside its walls too, but it was a walled prison courtyard of more than twenty rooms. No page we read gives the size of an intendant's jail.
    • A small staff, as an intendant's was, not the clerks by the hundred of a large Chinese county.
    • No fixed central axis. The yamen had one by regulation. No page we read on the jin'ya records one.
  • Only inside the compound do our maps always take the Japanese form.
    Whether the houses and shops outside it take the Japanese forms or the Chinese courtyard forms is a knob set at random for each settlement (How our maps choose between the courtyard house and the Japanese house).
  • Our maps borrow a granary and a records archive from the yamen.
    A yamen of the Qing dynasty kept both, by a book on its yamen (The provincial government's seat). The pages we read on the Neixiang county yamen name neither.
  • The hearing court follows the Japanese record, not the yamen's.
    The parties kneel on straw mats before the magistrate's hall, arranged as at the Edo town magistracy (How our maps draw the hearing court).
  • Every compound our maps draw has two courts, the office in front and the residence behind.
    That is the yamen's order, and in Japan too the magistrate's household lived inside the compound. Drawing the residence behind the office in a Japanese compound is a deliberate simplification: at the intendant's office at Takayama the residence stood beside the office, on the lot to the west.
  • A gate between the two courts divides the office from the home.
    The yamen's last gate was the inner residence gate. Our maps treat it as guarded, a GUESS: no page we read says it was.
  • A poorly funded office is drawn poor.
    Some intendants' offices ran short of money, so the plan has a knob for how well funded a posting is, and at its poor end a compound shows patched walls, weathered timber and a hall too small for its ceremonies. That picture is a GUESS: no page we read describes how a short-funded office looked. The range from a well-funded office to a poor one is qualitative and rests on no figure.

The plan's scale was set against the sizes of real compounds.

  • Why 3 px to the foot?
    At 3 px to the foot, our drawn compounds fall between the smallest and the largest real sites. The smallest is the branch post at Joge, about 70 m (~230 ft) by 27 m (~89 ft), some 1,890 square meters (~20,300 sq ft). The largest is the site of the intendant's office at Takayama, 11,219 square meters (~120,800 sq ft), or about 9,800 square meters (~105,600 sq ft) without the plaza in front of it. A county seat of fifteen samurai belongs between them, and both of our drawn compounds land there: Hayakawa at 247 by 200 ft and Ochiba at 267 by 200 ft.
  • At that scale the office hall runs 113 ft long.
    The office hall is 38 ft deep, and the hearing court before it is 80 by 32 ft. A cell is 12 by 10 ft (How our maps draw a holding cell), and the walls are 3 ft thick.
  • A smaller or larger scale would not fit.
    Drawn at 2 px to the foot, the same plan would stand for a compound of about 370 by 300 ft, nearly the whole site of the intendant's office at Takayama and far larger than the county seat the plan stands for. Drawn at 4 px to the foot, it would stand for about 185 by 150 ft, and every building in it would come out a quarter under its size, the office hall 85 ft long instead of 113.
  • A compound plan is drawn at three times the detail of a hamlet or town map.
    A hamlet or town map is drawn at 1 px to the foot, a village map at 1 px to 2 ft and a city map at 1 px to 3 ft, so a compound plan is the most detailed map we draw.

The layout of a building plan is checked by a program, because it is a matter of geometry, not judgment.

  • Labels and small glyphs such as tubs and wells are drawn last.
    That way nothing drawn after them can paint over them.
  • A label that names a group of small glyphs sits beside one of them.
    The fire-water tubs and the well are named where they stand.
  • The notice board stands within about 15 ft of a gate in the wall.
    It is read where people pass.
  • A label never sits on a dark block or on the wall.
    Dark ink on dark ground cannot be read, so the check moves the label onto clear ground.
  • No two labels overlap.
    Overlapping labels smear together, so one is moved apart.
  • A door glyph sits flush to the wall it opens through.
    It never floats inside the building (How our maps draw doors).
