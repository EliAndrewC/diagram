How our maps draw lodgings for lords and officials on the road (honjin)

No map yet draws a lord's lodging. Where one does, these are its rules.

  • A lodging for lords and officials is drawn as a gated house of one story.
    A honjin was single-story in principle, with an imposing gate and a wide entrance, and that the gate and the entrance set it apart from the inns on the same street is this project's reading: the page does not compare it with them.
  • Is a lord's lodging the grandest house in its street?
    That it outranks every inn and shop beside it is this project's reading of its gate, its entrance and its raised audience room, a GUESS: no page we read compares it with its neighbors. Our maps draw it as the grandest house in its street.
  • How large a site does a lord's lodging have?
    Our maps make it no larger than the site of Kusatsu's honjin, about 50,800 sq ft, among the largest that survive.
  • A second-rank lodging (waki-honjin) is drawn smaller than a lord's lodging.
    A waki-honjin was smaller, but how much smaller no page we read says.
  • A lord's lodging may stand anywhere in its street.
    No page we read says where in the street a honjin stood, so our maps prefer no place in it over another.
  • Does a waystation that hosts lords and officials keep a lodging court of its own?
    Busy trunk-road waystations occasionally host ceremonial Imperial travelers, and the Chinese station at Jimingyi kept an official lodging court three courtyards deep, so a waystation on our maps may have one.
