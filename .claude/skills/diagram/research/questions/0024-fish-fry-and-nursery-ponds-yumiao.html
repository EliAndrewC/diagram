Fish fry and nursery ponds (yumiao)

The carp that stocked a dike-pond were not bred there. Their young, the fry, 魚苗 (yumiao, "fish fry"), which Qu Dajun, writing of the delta, calls 魚花 (yuhua, "fish fry"), were netted wild in the rivers and sold. In the Pearl River delta one township, Jiujiang in Nanhai, held the West River catch and supplied the other pond districts: its men traded fry while its women fed the silkworms. Most villages raised grown fish from bought fry and kept no nursery ponds; Jiujiang's own ponds were mostly nursery water.

Away from the delta, a late-Ming farming compendium has the fry started in a pond about ten feet square beside the big pond, and moved to larger ponds as they grew.

  • The men of Jiujiang netted the fry in the West River.
    Qu Dajun, whose account of Guangdong was completed in 1678, says most of the people of Jiujiang village in Nanhai made their living by netting fish fry and were called fry households, and that they held the several dozen fry landings on the river and paid a levy on each. A modern article attributes to him the words "the men trade fish fry, the women feed the silkworms".
  • The West River fry landings were granted to Jiujiang in the Hongzhi reign.
    A governor-general memorialized that the river landings on both banks of the West River be taken up by the people of Jiujiang. A modern history of the delta's farming says the same from the other end: from the mid-Ming on, Jiujiang held the right to catch fry on the West River and supplied fry to the pond districts.
  • Qu Dajun says fry ponds were found only in Jiujiang.
    Elsewhere, he writes, the ponds mostly raised grown fish, and where they tried to raise fry the fry did not thrive.
  • Qu Dajun puts seven parts in ten of Jiujiang's pond water to raising fry.
    Qu Dajun describes its land as a chessboard, the black lines the dikes and the squares the ponds, and says that of its pond water seven-tenths raised fry and three-tenths grown fish.
  • In Jiujiang, the color of a pond's water told whether it held fry or grown fish.
    In Jiujiang the fry water was turbid and the grown-fish water clear.
  • How many nursery ponds did the other delta villages keep?
    Beyond Qu Dajun's word that fry ponds were found only in Jiujiang, no page we read counts the nursery ponds of any village but Jiujiang, or gives nursery ponds as a share of a village's ponds before modern times.

Away from the delta, fish farmers bought their fry too, and reared them on through a graded set of ponds. The Nongzheng quanshu, a late-Ming farming compendium printed in 1639, describes the set twice: once in an older record it quotes, and once in its compiler's own method from Jiangxi.

  • The Nongzheng quanshu has a fish farm dig its ponds in twos.
    Two ponds store water, let the large fish be taken at a sale and the small kept, and give the fish somewhere to go if one pond's water fouls.
  • An older record quoted in the Nongzheng quanshu moves the young fish from a small pond to larger ones as they grow.
    Taken from the river in mid-spring, the fry are kept in a small pond, moved on to a second pond as they grow, and moved at about a foot long to a wide pond where they are fed grass.
  • The Jiangxi method's nursery pond was about ten feet square and eight feet deep.
    It was a small pond set beside the big one. The compiler's method digs a small pond one zhang square and eight chi deep and stocks it with bought fry about an inch long, six hundred silver carp and two hundred grass carp to the pond. At the Ming foot of about 30 to 32 cm (~12-13 in), a zhang of ten feet comes to about 3.0 to 3.2 m (~10 ft), by this project's arithmetic.
  • In the Jiangxi method, the fry were carried to a middle pond after the fifth day of the fifth month, in early summer.
    The silver and grass carp are carried in a bucket to the middle pond.
  • The Jiangxi method's middle pond was about 20 to 30 ft square.
    The compendium makes it two or three zhang square, holding seven or eight hundred fish.
  • How big was the Jiangxi method's big pond?
    Some 30 to 57 m (~99-188 ft) a side, by this project's arithmetic. The compendium sizes it to the number of fish, and says one twenty or thirty paces square or more can hold fish of three or four jin and more. The Ming pace was five or six feet, about 1.5 to 1.9 m (~5-6 ft).
  • In the Jiangxi method, the small pond stood beside the big one, to lodge the fish in winter.
    The compendium says the small pond is always set beside the big pond so that in winter the fish can be lodged in it, and that in winter a pond is drained and its fish lodged in another pond or in large tubs.
  • In the thirteenth century, dealers carried fry from Jiangzhou to other provinces.
    Zhou Mi, who wrote after the fall of the Song dynasty, has the fry of Jiangzhou bought by dealers and carried home to Jianchang, Fujian, Qu and Wu.
  • Zhou Mi has the fry reared in a cloth pen in open water.
    The dealers let the fry go into a large cloth pen hung from bamboo in wide water, rear them there a month or half a month, and sell them.
  • No page we read gives the size of Jiujiang's own fry ponds.
    Qu Dajun gives their share of Jiujiang's pond water, not their size; no other page we read gives it either. The Jiangxi ponds and Zhou Mi's pen are other provinces' ways.

A modern fish farm rears its own fry and fingerlings in ponds kept for them, at sizes and shares a modern manual of Chinese integrated fish farming prescribes. Neither is an observation of a farm before modern times.

  • The modern manual sizes a fry pond at one to three mu.
    About a sixth to half an acre, by this project's arithmetic, and a fingerling pond at two to five mu, about a third of an acre to four-fifths of one. A pond that size is a pond in its own right, not a small pond set beside a big one.
  • The modern manual gives nursery ponds a quarter to three tenths of a farm's pond area.
    That is the share for a farm whose main stock is grass-eating or grain-fed fish, which needs large fingerlings. It is not what Qu Dajun saw, where the fry came in by trade and a village's ponds were either nearly all nursery water or none.
