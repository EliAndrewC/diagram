The main gate and its gatekeepers (nagaya-mon)

Both jin'ya we read of, a domain's seat and a magistrate's branch office, were entered through a main gate. In Japan the pages we read show two forms of that gate. The nagaya-mon was a gate opened through the middle of a long range of rooms, its form fixed by the standing of the house it served, with an opening, in the one registered example we read, of about 12 ft. The yakuimon was a gate of one bay, about 6 to 9 ft across in the three registered examples we read. A Chinese county office, the yamen, built its main gate instead as a roofed building three bays wide.

The gatekeepers who kept the gate sat in one of two places: in a room of the gate range itself, or in a gatehouse, a building of its own.

  • The nagaya-mon arose as the gate of castles, jin'ya and samurai residences, and the house's rank set its form.
    The Japanese encyclopedia article on the gate form says it began as the gate of the castles, jin'ya and samurai residences of the daimyo, and that its design and structure were fixed in detail by the house's standing and stipend.
  • A nagaya-mon registered as a cultural property opens in its central two ken, about 12 ft.
    The gate range is seven and a half ken long and two deep, with rooms on both sides of the opening and double-leaf board doors hung between its main posts; two ken is about 12 ft. The page does not say whose house the gate served.
  • The gate range of the jin'ya at Kaibara survives, about 81 by 12 ft.
    The Kashiwara domain's seat at Kaibara keeps the front gate of its jin'ya from the year it was built, 1714. The range is 13.5 ken long and 2 ken deep, with rooms on both sides of a gate with a side door. No page we read gives the width of its doorway.
  • How wide was a one-bay gate (yakuimon)?
    About 6 to 9 ft across. Three examples registered as cultural properties measure 2.6 m (~9 ft), 2.5 m (~8 ft) and 1.8 m (~6 ft) across their frontage. None of these pages says whose house the gate served.
  • Even the smallest magistrate's office we read of had a main gate and a gatekeepers' room.
    A drawing of 1869, the year after the shogunate fell, of its former branch office at Joge, a post of three officials (at Magistrates' compounds (jin'ya and yamen)), labels a main gate, a small gate, a gatekeepers' room and stone steps together at its east wall. The pamphlet that reproduces it gives the gate no width.

The record shows gatekeepers in both places.

  • Some gatekeepers sat in a guardroom built into the gate range.
    At Kaibara one side of the gate holds the guardroom, a room of eight tatami mats, and an earth floor; the other holds three more such rooms, a horse-viewing room and a gun store among them.
  • At Matsue the gate range was also where the gatekeepers and servants lived.
    The nagaya-mon of a samurai residence there served as the dwelling of its gatekeepers and of the household's servants.
  • At the Takayama jin'ya the gatekeepers' house is a building of its own.
    At the Takayama jin'ya the gate and the gatekeepers' house were both built in 1832 and are listed separately, each a gabled building of one story; both survive.
  • Guardrooms stood at Edo's great gates: the castle's, and both sides of the Akamon.
    Guardrooms were set at the gates of Edo castle, and the Akamon, the red gate now at the University of Tokyo, has one on each side of it.
  • How large was a gatehouse of its own?
    About 18 by 12 ft, in the one example measured: the guardroom of the Kita-in temple at Kawagoe, a small gabled building 3 by 2 ken, of the mid to late Edo period. It is a temple gate's guardroom, not a samurai compound's. No page we read gives the size of the Takayama gatehouse, nor of any other gatehouse of its own.
  • Did a gatehouse of its own stand beside the gate or behind it?
    The one we can place has stood in both spots: the Kita-in guardroom now stands against the side of the temple's gate, but a plan of 1841 draws it inside, behind the gate, and the temple believes it was moved later, without saying when.
  • How deep was the building the gatekeepers sat in?
    About 2 ken (~12 ft), in the one example of each kind measured: the Kaibara gate range is 2 ken deep, and so is the Kita-in guardroom.

In China a county office, the yamen, gated its compound another way.

  • A yamen's main gate was a roofed building, by law three bays wide.
    It was not a simple opening in the wall but a building with a roof, and by law and ritual, however large the county, it could be only three bays wide, with two black-lacquered leaves in each bay, six in all.
  • The Zhili governor-general's surviving office has a three-bay gate, raised on a stone platform.
    Its three-bay black gate stands on a stone platform a meter (~3 ft) high, with an open front court beyond it.
  • No page we read gives a yamen gate's width.
    The pages found count its bays, not its meters.
