How our maps draw smiths and farriers (kajiya)

A village either has one smithy or none. A county town draws one, for its master smith, on its shop rows, and a provincial city draws its smiths together on a street of their own. A farrier's shed and apron, where a horse is shod, stand only where horses gather: the caravan yard at a city gate and the relay town on an Imperial road.

  • About one village in two draws a smithy, a knob decided for each village from the map's seed.
    The rest draw none. The share is a GUESS: no page we read counts the villages with a smith.
  • A hamlet draws no smithy.
    A GUESS: the trade needs more customers than a handful of households.
  • A village smithy is drawn as one of the village's ordinary houses.
    It stands among them, fronting a lane, clear of its neighbors by the ordinary gap between houses, and 18-20 ft across. Its size and its gap are GUESSES, since no page we read gives a gap round a village forge.
  • The one measured smithy we have sets no size on our maps.
    The smithy at the Boso no Mura open-air museum, about 21 by 18 ft, was built from a survey of a workshop the museum does not date, one still standing to be surveyed in our own times, and this project draws nothing attested only in modern times. Neither a village smithy's size nor a town smithy's depth is taken from it.
  • A county town draws one smithy, for its master smith, on its shop rows.
    The setting's notes count a master smith among a county town's notables; his smithy takes a shop-house's plot among the shops.
  • How big is a county town's smithy?
    About 21 ft wide on the street: the frontage of the early-Edo core block of the Inoue family's gunsmith house in Sakai, near Osaka; its undated side blocks are not counted. It runs as deep as the shop-houses beside it, a GUESS, since no page we read gives a town smithy's depth before 1868.
  • A provincial city draws its smiths together, along one street of its commoners' quarter.
    That is what the castle towns did, setting their craftsmen in streets by trade.
  • How many smiths line a city's smiths' street?
    Between about 8 and about 28, rising with the city's size: 8 for a small castle town like Gujo Hachiman, and 26 to 28 for a large castle town like Kofu. Our maps scale the number between the two towns counted by the city's size; how a given city's size maps onto that range is a GUESS.
  • Swordsmiths are not drawn apart from other smiths.
    No page we read separates them on the ground.

The farrier is drawn for this setting's shod horses, a deliberate deviation from Japan, where until the end of the Edo period horses went mainly in straw sandals.

  • A town smith who also shoes is drawn as an ordinary shop.
    Shoeing changes his work, not his premises, so no new establishment joins the shop rows. In the West a farrier worked from a forge with a yard and a blacksmith from a small shop; a forge with a stable yard would take more ground than a city's shop rows can spare, so this project does not draw one.
  • A village smith who tends hooves builds no shoeing shed.
    He trims hooves and ties on straw sandals, which this setting's peasants still weave for their pack horses, from his own small workshop.
  • A farrier's shed and apron are drawn only where horses gather.
    Qing Beijing's shoeing shops stood mostly near the horse inns and the horse doctors, most with a frame before the door to tie the animals to.
  • A provincial city draws exactly one farrier, at a gate's caravan yard.
    Not one per gate. This setting shoes only the horses whose mileage pays for iron - couriers and patrols, garrison and samurai mounts, and caravan teams on the trunk roads - so farriery is not a mass trade, and the count does not rise with the city's population.
  • A town draws a farrier only if it stands on an Imperial road.
    With the waystations about 10 miles apart, plenty of towns are not on the network, so the rule follows the road, not the town's size.
  • A village or a hamlet never draws a farrier.
    Too few horses pass through to keep one.
  • A county magistracy draws no forge of its own.
    It keeps only 2-4 horses, per the buildings research, so the smith walks over from town.
  • The farrier's shed is not built against the stables.
    An open forge against a range of hay and timber stalls is a fire the yard would not survive, so the shed keeps the fire gap between wooden service buildings that the buildings research gives, about 6-8 ft, measured from the outer edge of shed and apron together - deliberately cautious, since the shed stands at the apron's far end. No page we read speaks of setting a forge apart from a stable range.
  • The farrier's shed is 18-20 ft across, and its apron about 8 ft deep, a horse's length.
    Both are GUESSES: the shed takes a village smithy's size, and the apron a horse's length with room to lead one in and turn it, drawn at that size and not enlarged to read better. No page we read gives a farrier's shed plan or an apron's depth.
  • The apron carries an ox-shoeing frame, drawn in its Western form.
    Oxen haul this setting's heavy wagons, and oxen were shod in north China. The frame is the Western one that slung an ox to shoe it, about 7 by 4 ft, a size of our own, drawn at its real size. The Western form is drawn because no page we read shows an East Asian frame that slung an ox before modern times.
  • An ox is shod with two plates to each hoof.
    A GUESS from the hoof's form: an ox's hoof is cloven, split in two.
