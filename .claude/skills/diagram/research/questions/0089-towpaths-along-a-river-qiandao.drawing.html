How our maps draw a towpath along a river (qiandao)

A towpath was a narrow path on a river's bank; on this project's reading, haulers walked it to pull boats upstream. A river capital's map draws one on the wharf's own bank of the river only, not on both, and draws no road beside the river.

  • The map draws no trunk road beside the river.
    Water carried bulk goods far more cheaply than land, so on this project's reading a road shadowing the river would be redundant. A capital's overland roads leave it in the directions its river does not serve.
  • The towpath is drawn as one thin line on the bank.
    It has no roadbed and no dashed center line, only a single fine line, a map drawing convention for a path beaten by the haulers' feet, which the map takes as about 8 ft wide.
  • Buildings stand back from the towpath.
    The map keeps a narrow strip beside the towpath clear, so no building stands on the bank the haulers walked.
  • The map runs the towpath only as far as the wharf.
    A towpath served the boats, and on this project's reading it went only as far as they did, so the map draws it along the bank up to the wharf it serves and no further.
  • The quay inside the wall is the wharf's frontage, not a road.
    A river landing was a place where cargo was loaded and unloaded, and on this project's reading a place, not a route, so the map draws the quay with the wharf district. The wharf itself is at How our maps draw wharves and landings (kashi, gangi).
  • A towpath out in the water is not drawn yet.
    At Shaoxing, a canal city in eastern China, part of the towpath runs in the watercourse itself, parallel to the bank, on stone slabs laid across stone piers. The bank path is the common form and the map's default; the causeway in the water is held for a capital whose bank is too steep or too built up to carry a path.
