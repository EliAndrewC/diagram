Rice planted in rows (seijoue)

Before the Meiji period, rice in Japan was transplanted by hand into flooded mud in no ruled order. A dry field's crop grew in rows at no cost, since the seed went into the furrow (Dry fields and their crops); a paddy had no furrow to follow, and evenly spaced rows came to most of it only once row planting was encouraged nationwide in 1897-1906.

  • Before Meiji, rice was transplanted in "disorderly planting", not evenly spaced.
    The old way was ranzatsu-ue, "disorderly planting". Row planting was not seen before Meiji except in parts of Shizuoka and of Katori district in Chiba.
  • Row planting spread across Japan once it was encouraged nationwide in 1897-1906.
    It evened the spacing, let sun and air reach the rice evenly, and made weeding quicker. It spread once it was encouraged nationwide.
  • Rows in a paddy were marked with a planting rope or a rolling frame.
    A rope was stretched across the paddy and the planters stepped backward along it, or a hexagonal frame was rolled forward to print cross-shaped marks in the mud.
  • Row planting spread with the rotary weeder, as one package.
    As the hand-pushed weeder, a frame of turning shafts with short claws run between the rows, came into wide use in mid-Meiji, row planting with planting ropes and rulers became general.
  • Why did ruled rows come to the paddy so late?
    This record's reading is that they bought little until the weeder came. On dry land rows pay because a tool travels between them to weed; before the rotary weeder, a paddy was weeded by hand or with a hand claw whether it had rows or not. Ruling rows meant a rope or a frame and every planter keeping to it, in a short season: on the plains of central and northern Wakayama, a 1977 survey of the practice before 1955 found the transplanting took seven to ten days.
  • How far apart did the hills of rice stand?
    Today the hills, the clumps of seedlings set in together, stand about 30 cm (~1 ft) apart each way for well-grown seedlings, closed up to 20-25 cm (~8-10 in) for short ones. No page we read gives a spacing before Meiji.
