Rice paddies and their plots (suiden)

A rice paddy is a level basin of ground walled by a low bank, the bund, heaped from the paddy's own mud to keep the water in, so that the rice stands in water at an even depth. Before modern times Japan's paddies were small, irregular plots fitted to the lie of the land and its water as it was opened piece by piece, and each farm's paddy lay scattered over many of them. The paddy parcels of two land surveys of the 1600s run from about a tenth of an acre to under half an acre, and a single terraced paddy could be far smaller.

The tidy grid of equal rectangles that much of Japan's rice land shows today is modern: land consolidation, begun in the 1870s and made law in 1899, in the Meiji era, first laid it out. An older grid is the ancient jori layout of the western plains. Rice farmers also kept up a paddy's nitrogen: in China a water fern was grown on the flooded paddy for over a thousand years, and in Japan a legume was sown in it over the winter.

The plots themselves were shaped by the way the land was opened.

  • Before modern times, most paddies were irregular plots of every size.
    Lords of manors, local families and leading farmers opened land in small projects - ponds built in ravines to water narrow strips of paddy, terraces on hill and mountain slopes - and before the consolidation law of 1899 farmers in many parts of the country sought to straighten fields that were widely scattered and irregularly shaped. In the domain era, when most paddies were opened, plots as orderly as the jori grid's were few, and irregular plots of every size were the great majority. Consolidation later reorganized these small, irregular plots handed down from the Edo period.
  • Before consolidation, a farm's paddy lay scattered over many plots.
    A household's fields were widely scattered, and consolidation exchanged them and gathered each owner's together.
  • The patchwork's bunds probably met in T-junctions rather than crossings.
    Plots opened one after another, each fitted to its own ground, seldom line up across a bund; but this is a GUESS, which no page we read states.

The tidy grid came with the modernizing Meiji state, and grew larger in the twentieth century.

  • The rectangular grid of paddies was laid out by Meiji-era land consolidation, begun in 1872 and made law in 1899.
    The Shizuoka method, first carried out in 1872, aligned roads and channels exactly north-south and east-west and squared the parcels for planting in rows. The Arable Land Consolidation Act was passed in 1899 and came into force in 1900.
  • Under the Ishikawa method, every paddy was made a rectangle touching a road and a channel.
    Every paddy parcel was made a rectangle touching a branch road and a supply and drainage channel.
  • From 1902 the standard plot was one tan, 30 by 10 ken, about a quarter acre, touching a canal and a road.
    The Konosu method, begun in 1902, made the standard plot one tan, 30 by 10 ken (about 54 m (~177 ft) by 18 m (~59 ft)), every plot touching a canal and a road.
  • Land consolidation in the early 1900s also straightened the ditches.
    The secondary canals and ditches of the Tedori River's alluvial fan were rebuilt as straight channels in a land reorganization of the early 1900s, under laws for the adjustment of farm lands in force since 1900.
  • From 1963, consolidation grew the grid to blocks of 30 ares (~0.74 acre).
    The farm consolidation projects begun in 1963 re-laid village land in rectangular blocks of 30 ares, each with a road, an irrigation channel and a drainage channel beside it.
  • The western plains kept an older grid, the ancient jori layout.
    The alluvial plains of the Kinki, Setouchi and northern Kyushu districts were laid out on the jori grid of paths, ditches and fields, whose paddies and canals went on working through the medieval period. Regular plots of that kind were widespread in western Japan up to the Meiji era, so consolidation there was slight.

New land opened in the Edo period was laid out on a plan, but the record shows it in strips, not in a grid of paddies.

  • An Edo new-field village was planned, and on the Kanto plain laid out in strips of equal area.
    Such a village was laid out with its roads, fields and house plots on a plan, so its form came out regular. In the street villages common on the Kanto plain the houses line both sides of the road, and behind each lies its land in a strip of equal area. On the Musashino plateau those strips were dry field and woodland, long and narrow behind a row of houses and allotted nearly equally at the start in many villages; in the south of the plateau they run the way of the canals dug along the roads. Neither source says whether such strips elsewhere were paddy.
  • In Edo new fields won from the sea or a lake, the farmhouses stood in a row on the dike or old shore, each holding near its house.
    The holdings lie near the houses and fairly compact; no strips are named.
  • Were an Edo reclamation's paddies laid out as a grid?
    No page we read says so. On the Ariake coast each stage of reclamation is a block of paddy inside its own dike, the stages added seaward like fish scales, but nothing says how the paddies within a stage were divided. At Kojo village on Kojima Bay, reclaimed at the very end of the Edo period, the leading farmers consolidated its subdivided fields into blocks, the ground of its prewar mechanized farming, though the page gives the readjustment no date.

How big a plot was depends on what is counted: the parcel a land survey entered, or the single diked basin inside it.

  • A land survey entered each parcel on a line of its own, whatever its size.
    A survey register set down each lot of paddy, dry field or house land one place at a time, regardless of its size. It gave each parcel's sides, found by converting the parcel to a rectangle, and its area.
  • A register's area was the surveyors' figure, which could differ from the ground's.
    A slack rope or a shortened rod could push it away from the real area.
  • Was a register's paddy parcel one diked basin, or several?
    No page we read says.
  • How big was a paddy parcel in the land surveys of the 1600s?
    From about a tenth of an acre to under half an acre, in the two registers read. On the page transcribed from a 1602 register of Iwamuro village in Omi, the eight paddy lots in cultivation run from 3 se 22 bu to 1 tan 8 se 1 bu, about 370 to 1,800 m2 (~0.09-0.44 acre), and five of them are over a tan. In the 1684 register of Kami-Baba village one parcel of middle-grade paddy is 16 by 10.5 ken, 5 se 18 bu, about 555 m2 (~0.14 acre). No page we read gives an average.
  • How big was a single dry field in an Edo-period survey register?
    Dry fields in the registers ran to similar sizes. The register of Yaho village, of 1678, gives single dry fields of 1 se 12 bu (its sides 7 and 6 ken by 6), 14 by 7 ken (3 se 8 bu) and 41 by 10 ken (1 tan 3 se 20 bu), about 0.03, 0.08 and 0.36 acre.
  • A planned plot of about a quarter acre, one tan, was laid out before Meiji consolidation made it the standard, but only in planned layouts.
    The one-tan plot of 10 by 30 ken, about a quarter acre, laid out for horse plowing, first appears as a replanning plot in the Meiji Restoration years, at Inazato in Gifu in 1869. Twenty years earlier, in 1849, a replanning of about 8 cho in the same district laid out plots of 8 by 39 ken, 1 tan 12 bu, about the same area and also meant for horse plowing, and the ancient jori grid's half-fold plot was a tan of 12 by 30 ken. It was not the plot of the irregular fields most paddies were.
  • The small-plot paddies of Yayoi and Kofun Japan are tiny.
    The small-plot paddies of the Yayoi and Kofun periods, well over a thousand years before the Edo period, are plots of under 100 m2 (~1,076 sq ft), the smallest about 5 m2 (~54 sq ft), hundreds and sometimes thousands of them together.
  • Terraced paddies can be tiny.
    At Shiroyone Senmaida, on the Noto peninsula, 1,004 small paddies run over a slope of about 4 ha (~10 acres) facing the sea. A paddy there averages about 18 m2 (~194 sq ft), and the smallest is about 50 cm (~20 in) square. An old counting song has nine hundred and ninety-nine paddies planted and the last one hidden under a straw raincoat.
  • A terrace's bed is held by a riser, and made as wide as the slope allows.
    A traditional bench terrace is a level bed held by a riser 0.8 to 1.5 m (~3-5 ft) high, with stones built into it where they are to hand, and topped by a small lip of 20 to 25 cm (~8-10 in). Beds are usually 2 to 6 m (~7-20 ft) wide, made as wide as they can be without risking slips, to save labor.
  • How deep did the water stand on a terrace bed?
    On a bench terrace in general, not in Japan alone, the water stands 10 to 15 cm (~4-6 in) deep when the bed is fully flooded.

What grew in and beside a paddy kept up its soil, or was a crop of its own.

  • In China, the water fern azolla has been grown in rice paddies for over a thousand years to feed nitrogen to the rice.
    Azolla lives with a cyanobacterium that fixes nitrogen from the air, and as the fern dies it releases that nitrogen into the water for the rice plants. It has been grown in rice paddies as a companion plant for at least a thousand years; the earliest written record of the practice is in the Chinese farm manual Qimin Yaoshu of 554.
  • In Japan, a legume that feeds the soil nitrogen, Chinese milk vetch, was grown in the paddy over the winter.
    The vetch (renge), a legume, came to Japan long ago and was grown as a green manure for the paddy, its root nodules fixing nitrogen from the air. It was sown among the rice around September, grew on after the harvest, wintered in the paddy and flowered the next spring, the paddy's off-season crop.
  • Were the soybeans on the bunds grown to feed the paddy?
    On this page's reading, no: they were a crop of their own. Soybeans called azemame, "bund beans", were sown along the bunds after the rice was transplanted and harvested at the same time as the rice. A legume the paddy took for its soil was the winter vetch sown in it.
