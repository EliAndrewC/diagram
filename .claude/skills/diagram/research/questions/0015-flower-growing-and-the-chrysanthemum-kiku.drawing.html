How our maps draw the chrysanthemum field (kiku)

One map draws a chrysanthemum field: Hirameki, a walled county seat, keeps one inside its west rampart, standing for the Emperor in a town that changed hands in war, and no public street is cut through it. No other map draws flower plots in a town's ring of farmland.

  • In the setting, the chrysanthemum is the flower of the ninth month's festival.
    That is Choyo, the Double Ninth, when "Gardens, temples, shrines, and fine homes display fine specimens of the seasonal flowers".
  • An Imperial field in a provincial town is a deliberate deviation.
    No page we read describes a planted field of chrysanthemums kept for a ruler or a court, so the field departs from the history on purpose.
  • The field takes the place of houses, so Hirameki is excused from housing its declared population.
    It stands inside the wall where a planned town of the same size would put dwellings (What every settlement map is told before it is drawn, and when it may break a rule).
  • Our maps draw no flower plots in a town's ring of farmland.
    Every Japanese case we read of flowers grown for sale lies on the edge of a great city, Edo, and none in the farmland around an ordinary provincial town; nothing read describes flowers grown for a shrine or a temple in a plot of their own. So the one flower field drawn is the Imperial one.
  • The field is drawn about 220 by 340 ft, some 1.7 acres.
    That size is measured on our own map and is a GUESS: no page we read gives the size of a historical flower field to set it against.
