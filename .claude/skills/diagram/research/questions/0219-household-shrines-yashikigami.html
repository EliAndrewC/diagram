Household shrines (yashikigami)

The yashikigami is the guardian god of a household's own ground, and the word names its shrine too. In a corner of the plot - which corner varied from place to place - a farm kept a small shrine of stone, of wood or with a thatched roof, in which it enshrined the guardian ujigami, its ancestral gods, or a share of the spirit of a god it held in particular faith. In some places every house had one; in others only certain old families did, and in the one twentieth-century count we have, they were rare.

  • Two patterns are recorded: a household shrine at every house, or at only certain old families' houses.
    A Tokushima library bulletin finds both, and the Japanese Wikipedia article on the yashikigami names the same two: every house in the settlement with its own, or only certain old families.
  • How common was a household shrine in the twentieth century?
    Rare, in the one count we have. A 1972 count of outbuildings in a town of Miyagi, in Tohoku in the northeast, finds about one shrine for every hundred houses (0.01 a household) over all its houses, and none among the houses built by 1944.
  • Most household shrines were small shrines of stone or of wood.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on the yashikigami calls most of them small shrines of stone or of wood, which almost never had a shrine hall of the kind an ordinary shrine has.
  • One measured stone shrine in Tokushima is about 40 cm (~16 in) wide, deep and high.
    The Tokushima bulletin records it: a stone shrine called Ohimachi-san, with a round stone inside it that is held to be the god's own body.
  • Some households marked the god's place with a tree or a stone instead of a shrine.
    The Tokushima bulletin finds both a tree or a stone as the mark, and a small shrine.
  • The shrine stood in a corner of the plot.
    Accounts of the yashikigami put its small shrine in a corner of the grounds.
  • Which corner of the plot did it stand in?
    The accounts differ. The Japanese Wikipedia article puts it most often at the northwest or the northeast; a folk-house museum's page puts it especially at the northeast, the kimon; and the Tokushima bulletin finds it usually at the southwest.
  • In a folk survey of Kameyama, two houses kept the shrine at the northwest corner, and one moved it from the northeast to the southwest.
    The Kameyama city history's folk survey finds a shrine at the northwest corner of one house, and another built into the stone wall at the northwest corner of a second; at a third the shrine stood at the northeast corner until the house was rebuilt, and was then moved to the southwest.
