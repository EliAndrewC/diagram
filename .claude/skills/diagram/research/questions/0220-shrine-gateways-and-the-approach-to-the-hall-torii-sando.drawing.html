How our maps draw shrine gateways and the approach to the hall (torii, sando)

On our maps a shrine's approach runs up to its hall under an avenue of one, three or seven torii. The number is chosen at random, and a long avenue is likelier in a larger settlement. The arches stand 12 ft apart, the innermost 12 ft off the hall's face. A row of arches before an ordinary shrine is a deliberate deviation: before 1868 a row is recorded only at a great Inari shrine. A few great named sites, and the small shrines inside a household's grounds, follow rules of their own.

  • A row of arches before an ordinary shrine is a deliberate deviation.
    Before 1868 a row of donated arches is attested only at a great Inari shrine, Fushimi Inari in Kyoto, where the custom arose in the Edo period; at a village shrine, or any ordinary shrine, the early dated arches are single gates, and a row is attested only in modern times. The arch itself is historical: a single stone arch at a shrine's approach is dated by its inscription to 1679, and another is thought older still. This setting mixes periods, so it uses a real form more widely than history did.
  • A shrine gets one, three or seven arches, and a long avenue is likelier in a larger settlement.
    The number is chosen at random from the map's seed, weighted by the size of the settlement (a knob):
    settlementone archthreeseven
    village60%30%10%
    town30%60%10%
    city30%40%30%
    capital10%60%30%
    The shares are this project's choice.
  • The counts of one, three and seven are a deliberate deviation.
    History has shrines with a single arch, shrines whose approach passes under a first, a second and a third, and a row of about 10,000 at Fushimi Inari. The maps keep to one, three and seven because those numbers carry power in this setting, seven most of all.
  • The count follows patronage, not the room in front of the shrine.
    An avenue could be sized to the space before it, on the reasoning that a shrine with a long approach would fill it. It is not: an arch was a gift, so the count records what worshippers gave, and sizing by space would rule out three arches, the count a town gets most often. The larger the settlement, the likelier a long avenue: a larger place has more givers. What the ground does govern is the avenue's line, which the map's author draws to fit the ground it crosses.
  • How far apart should the arches stand?
    12 ft, on every map, at every scale and in every size of settlement. That is two ken, the building measure of about 6 ft, and the middle of the band of 10 to 13 ft this project keeps to, which fits the one small rural row whose spacing can be estimated, with its arches about 10 to 14 ft apart. The figure is a GUESS inside that band.
  • Why 12 ft, rather than 10 or 13?
    At 10 ft, at three feet to the pixel, the arches would stand barely three pixels apart, too close to read as separate. Nothing favored 13 ft over 12. A real row, about 1.3 m (~4 ft) an arch over a whole climb, is packed tighter than arches can be drawn apart at these scales; that limit is the drawing's, not a finding about how gates were spaced.
  • A few great named sites are drawn with a long, dense row of donated arches.
    A row like Fushimi Inari's, around 800 arches on its main path, is not how an ordinary approach in this setting works. It belongs to a handful of named great sites - Shinden Togashi, the Temple of Amaterasu, the Ki Rin Shrine and others of their rank - and a map of one of them is drawn as an explicit outlier, exempt from the 12 ft spacing.
  • A major shrine's first and second gates, which can stand hundreds of feet apart, are not drawn as a pair.
    A shrine's first and second gates can stand about 300 m (~984 ft) apart, which puts the pair off the map at every settlement scale.

Where the avenue begins and ends is set by the shrine's ground and its hall, and the hall's label is kept off it.

  • The first arch stands where the approach enters the shrine's ground.
    A torii marks the entry to a shrine's grounds, so on our maps the first arch stands at the edge of the shrine's wood (Village shrines: where they stand, and how big their grounds are).
  • The innermost arch stands 12 ft off the hall's nearest face, the same as the spacing between arches.
    An avenue can be perfectly spaced and still stand yards from the shrine it serves - three arches up a street beyond another building, or with a label and two rows of houses between it and its hall - and then it reads as marks near some other building, not as that shrine's approach. So the whole run is moved, keeping its direction, its curve and its spacing, until its innermost arch stands 12 ft off the nearest face of the hall, measured to the building's outline rather than its center, so that a run drawn off to one side slides onto that flank. No page gives the distance from a village shrine's innermost arch to its hall, so this is a map drawing convention.
  • Where the first arch and the innermost cannot both keep their places, the innermost holds.
    The first arch moves with the run, off the edge of the shrine's wood.
  • A hall's label never covers an arch.
    A hall's label and its approach both want the ground at the hall's face. The label tries three places in a fixed order - the side the map's author asked for, that same side moved past the far end of the avenue, then the opposite side - and takes the first that touches no arch. Moving past the avenue is needed because a graveyard may sit on purpose behind a temple, so flipping the label off the approach would only bury it in the graves.

A compound shrine - a small shrine inside a household's or an office's grounds - has a smaller ground and its own rules.

  • How far before its hall does a compound shrine's arch stand?
    About 5 ft where the plan is tight, up to 12 ft - the distance of a village shrine's innermost arch from its hall - where there is room. The arch marks the edge of the shrine's ground, and a compound shrine's ground is small; no page gives a distance from any torii to its hall, so the figure is a GUESS.
  • Two shrines on one ground share one arch or have one each - a knob.
    Both are attested: a precinct's arch stands before everything inside it, subsidiary shrines included, and a subsidiary shrine may have an arch of its own. Each map chooses one at random from its seed.
  • A compound shrine may have no arch at all.
    A household shrine that is carefully kept "may even have a torii", so an arch is a mark of care, not a fixture, and a map may leave it out.
