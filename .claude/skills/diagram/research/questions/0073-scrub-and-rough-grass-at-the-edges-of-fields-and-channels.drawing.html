How our maps keep scrub off fields, channels and open water

Scrub on our maps stands 6 ft off the edge of every paddy and dry field and 6 ft off the drawn edge of every irrigation channel, and they draw nothing on open water. Before 1868 grass was cut for green manure, and the paddies needed wide grass-cutting ground around them; cleared land that is no longer cut goes over to scrub. So the band of cut grass between a crop and the scrub is historical, and its 6 ft is this project's choice.

  • Why does scrub stand 6 ft off a field's edge?
    The 6 ft stands for the bund and a strip of cut grass beside it. It is wider than the bund and strip, about 1.5 ft a field, that an Edo-period land survey took out of a field's area, and inside the wide grass-cutting ground a historical dictionary places round the paddies, whose width no old record we read gives. The figure is a GUESS.
  • The 6 ft margin is a flat-ground figure.
    Where a field stands above a terrace riser, the kept-cut slope face below it can be far wider, as the research section above says (Scrub and rough grass at the edges of fields and channels); our maps do not set that width, and keep the 6 ft there too.
  • Trees stand back further than scrub.
    The scraggly pines and the woodland trees stand back from the margin by as far as their drawn crowns spread, so nothing leans over a crop.
  • A grass tuft's blade tips may lean a few feet past the margin.
    At the coarser map scales the tip of a drawn blade can reach a few real feet over the margin line. That a bund's grass grew long enough between cuttings to overhang its edge is this project's GUESS, and the overhang is too small to see on the map.
  • The reed marsh takes no margin.
    Our maps take wet ground to start at the polder dike, so where a field's low bund stands against the dike, reeds are drawn right up to it. That reeds ran up to the dike is a GUESS.

The irrigation channels that run beside the fields get a margin of their own.

  • Scrub stands 6 ft off the drawn edge of every irrigation channel.
    Without a margin of their own, the narrow strips of ground between a supply channel and the dry plots at a field's edge would carry tufts of scrub in their middle, crowding the channel. Together, the channel's margin and the field's margin clear any such strip up to about 12 ft wide, which covers every one our maps lay out (3-9 ft on the Inashiro map).
  • Is a channel's bank kept like a bund?
    Our maps take it so, by analogy with the paddy levee: a levee is maintained ground, walked and kept up, its grass mown periodically, and where a channel runs beside a paddy its bank is that paddy's bund (Where a field meets its ditch). No page we read gives the width a channel bank's grass was cut to, so the bank's 6 ft is the crop margin's figure reused, a GUESS.
  • Natural streams take no margin.
    A natural stream's banks are grown with water-loving plants, and a bare strip along a brook would be wrong. Only the channels people dug and keep are maintained ground, which is this project's reasoning from the levee pages, not a source's statement.
  • Our maps draw nothing on open water, out to a channel's full drawn width.
    Every drawn watercourse counts at its drawn, tapering width - the supply channels run up to about 14 ft wide - so no grass tuft stands on a head race or crowds its banks inside the drawn stroke. Keeping plants off the water is a map drawing convention.

The rule the map follows: scrub of every kind stands 6 ft off every paddy and dry plot, and 6 ft off the drawn water edge of every irrigation channel at its drawn width (both GUESSES); the scraggly pines and woodland trees stand back further by their drawn crowns' reach; nothing is drawn on open water; natural streams and the reed marsh take no margin; a grass tuft's blade tips may lean a few feet past the margin line.
