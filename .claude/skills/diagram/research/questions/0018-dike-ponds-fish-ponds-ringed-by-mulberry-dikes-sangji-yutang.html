Dike-ponds: fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes (sangji yutang)

A dike-pond was a fish pond ringed by a dike planted with mulberry. The mulberry leaves fed silkworms, the silkworms' droppings fed the fish, and the mud dredged from the pond was banked back up around the mulberry. Dike-ponds were kept in the Pearl River delta, in the far south, and in Jiangsu and Zhejiang. In modern practice most ponds in the Pearl River delta are rectangles of an acre or so and 7 to 10 ft deep, inside dikes some 20 to 33 ft wide.

Water and dike were kept in proportion, so that the dike's mulberry could feed the fish in the water beside it. Modern writers put the split at about six parts water to four of dike, though some give it the other way round. A pond lay inside the polder around it, whose villagers watered and drained their land through its dikes, creeks and sluice gates, and reworked its ponds to raise fish.

  • Where the mulberry alone fed the fish, a modern working paper puts dike and water equal, one to one.
    The paper, on integrated fish farming, says that from the experience of the Pearl River delta's farmers one mu of mulberry yields the worm waste, pupae and waste water to feed one mu of pond, so where the mulberry alone feeds the fish, dike and water are equal, one to one.
  • How much of a dike-pond was water?
    About six parts in ten, in a modern working paper on integrated fish farming. Its worked example, a pond of the Dongshan commune near Suzhou, from China's people's commune era, has 3.6 mu of water to 2.4 mu of dike, water to dike six to four. Where the fish had feed or fertilizer from beyond the dike, it says, the split could be six to four or seven to three, though in practice it was more complicated and was generally governed by tradition.
  • Accounts differ on which way round the water-to-dike split goes.
    An essay on the dike-pond says the old pattern of "six dike, four pond", six parts dike to four of pond, can no longer be found in the Pearl River delta, while a modern working paper on integrated fish farming has water to dike at six to four. The Guangdong gazetteer office names its split water-to-dike and gives it as three-seven to four-six. Read in the order it names, that is three or four parts water to seven or six of dike, the dike-heavy order again, though a split written this way does not by itself fix which side comes first. The sources carry both orders and a seven-to-three variant, so the difference may be regional rather than an error.
  • Every page read that writes the water-to-dike split as a number is modern.
    The six-to-four split is a worked example in the modern working paper, and the gazetteer office's present-day article gives its three-to-seven and four-to-six as the split in general, dated to no period. A modern essay calls "four water, six dike" a scene of former days that survives only in documents, but it names no document and gives no date. No text older than the twentieth century that gives the split as a ratio was found.

The oldest figures found are a seventeenth-century writer's, for one township of the Pearl River delta.

  • Late in the seventeenth century, Qu Dajun wrote that fish took half of the township of Jiujiang's land, and elsewhere that its fish ponds took eight tenths.
    Qu Dajun's account of Guangdong was completed in 1678, early in the Qing dynasty. He says of the township of Jiujiang that its land was cramped and the fish took up half of it, the ponds raising fish and the dikes planted with mulberry. In another passage of the same chapter he puts the township's fish ponds at eight tenths of its land and its fields at two.
  • What split of water and dike do Qu Dajun's two figures give?
    About five parts water to three of dike, if they are read together, and that reading is this project's GUESS. He never sets the two figures side by side, and neither says whether the ponds' eight tenths counts their dikes. Taking the fish's half as the water, and the ponds' eight tenths as water and dikes both, leaves the dikes about three tenths of the land: water to dike about five to three, the water-heavy order and close to six to four. No premodern text read states it.

A pond's size and its dike's width are measured mostly in modern times.

  • How large was a pond?
    Most modern ponds are 0.4 to 0.6 ha (~1-1.5 acres) and 2 to 3 m (~7-10 ft) deep. Before modern times only the largest are measured: Qu Dajun, writing of Guangdong in 1678, says that in the villages of Guangzhou's great counties the larger ponds below the planted dikes reached several tens of mu, and ponds built out into the sea were reckoned by the hundred mu. At a modern fish-farming paper's reckoning of six mu to 0.4 ha (~1 acre), the modern pond is six to nine mu, by this project's arithmetic, well below his larger ponds. He gives no ordinary size.
  • How wide was a pond's dike? Modern sources differ.
    An account of the Pearl River delta's dike-ponds puts its dikes at usually 6 to 10 m (~20-33 ft) wide, standing 0.5 to 1 m (~2-3 ft) above the water. A modern fish-farming manual keeps the dikes between ponds within 5 m (~16 ft), and makes only those carrying pigsties, cow-shed piping or traffic 5 to 10 m (~16-33 ft) wide. So the delta account's usual dike is wider than the manual's ordinary one, and both are modern.
  • Were the dikes once wider?
    In one Pearl-delta village, Ruxi, a 2024 study reports dikes that used to be 20 m (~66 ft) wide worn to under 4 m (~13 ft) as the ponds grew. No page we read gives a dike's width before modern times.

How thickly the dike's mulberry stood, and the fruit trees and tea that stood on pond dikes before it and beside it, are at Mulberry and other crops on pond dikes (sangji, guoji).

A pond's water came and went through the polder it stood in.

  • In the Pearl River delta a polder's sluice gate was called a dou, and one old dou still bears its carved name.
    The villagers of the Sangyuan polder watered and drained their land through its dikes, creeks and sluices, reworking ponds to raise fish and planting mulberry beside them to raise silkworms. The Minle dou, in Xiqiao town, is one of the polder's many old sluice gates, its name carved above its opening in the fourth year of the Guangxu era (1878). A local saying, "Minle has a dou, and every household the sound of spinning and weaving", is remembered as witness to the silk weaving of the district in those days.
  • In Jiangnan, a polder's sluice gates were opened in drought to let river water in, and shut in flood to keep it out.
    Fan Zhongyan, in a Northern Song memorial quoted by an article on the history of the Jiangnan polders, says that a polder had gates on its outer side, opened in drought to draw the river's water in and shut in flood to keep it out. How a polder as a whole was drained is at Polders: fields diked against the fluctuating water (weitian, waju).
  • In modern practice the ponds are drained two or three times a year, and their mud dredged onto the dikes.
    A modern account of the delta's dike-pond system says so. In the seventeenth century Qu Dajun has the ponds below the planted dikes of Guangzhou's villages drained at year's end and sown with rice seedlings in spring.
  • Did each pond have a sluice through its own dike?
    Only a modern fish-farming training manual describes one: a protected opening in the pond dike, closed with wooden boards to set the water level and screened against losing fish, its inside width best kept to 0.80 m (~31 in) at most. The manual describes no place and no period. No page we read from before 1912, when China's last dynasty fell, gives a sluice in each pond's dike, or a pond fed at its high side and drained at its low side.
