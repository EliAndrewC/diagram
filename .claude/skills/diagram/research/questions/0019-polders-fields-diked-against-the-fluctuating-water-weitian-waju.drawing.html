How our maps draw polders (weitian, waju)

Every polder on our maps is enclosed by its dike all the way round, and water crosses the dike only at gated sluices: an inlet at the polder's head and an outfall at its tail, which this project reads as its high and low ends. In the one polder whose count we found, a waju, the dike had many openings, drains outnumbering intakes. How many a polder on our maps gets is a calibrated liberty set by its size, and a village polder gets two, enough, in this project's reading (a GUESS), for a small polder.

The rule the map follows, for where the polder and its village sit:

  • Both polders in the pool are drawn against the landward shore.
    The wild pond that feeds the polder stands to its northwest, reed marsh lies along its low south side where the outfall drains, and its dry landward shore lies to the east.
  • Both polders carry a reed fringe along their west flank.
    So the map shows that the west dike holds water back, as every flank of a polder but the landward one does.
  • The village is drawn on the dry ground just off the dike, to the east.
    Houses on a polder's raised margin, which was also its dike, are recorded. Where a polder was built against a shore, with dry ground along its landward side, this project's reading, a GUESS, is that its village stood on that dry ground a few steps off the dike instead.
  • A village is drawn hard against its own polder.
    In this project's reading, a GUESS, a polder's community kept its own dike, so its houses press up to its fields.
  • Dike-top housing is a knob no map uses yet.
    A polder with water on every flank and no shore to build on would seat its farmhouses in single file on the dike crest, this project's drawing of the houses on the margin that are recorded. The generator can draw it, but no settlement in the pool uses it.
  • Where a map takes it, each dike-top house stands on a widened stretch of the crest.
    The band bulges round each house instead of the house overhanging the water. The Lake Tai village's raised margin was wider where it held the houses (Polder dikes), but no page we read gives the ground a house took on a dike, so the widened stretch, the house and about 14 ft by 10 ft more, is a GUESS.
  • No dike-top house stands over a sluice notch.
    A house is kept back from each gap where water crosses the dike, about 34 ft on a hamlet map and 68 ft on a village map, so the notch stays open. That a house kept off the notch is a GUESS: no page we read says where on a dike the houses stood relative to its sluices.
  • No yard or garden is drawn up on the crest.
    The crest serves each house as its dooryard and its lane, and the gardens lie down on the plots beside the dike. The Lake Tai villagers had only very small gardens beside their houses and grew vegetables under the mulberry (The dike-pond hamlet), but no page we read says where on a dike a house's yard or garden lay, so drawing none on the crest is a GUESS.
  • Enokida's village falls inside the household range guessed for a small polder.
    Its 16 households sit inside the ten to twenty households this project guesses, a GUESS, for a small polder.

The rule the map follows, for the water inside:

  • A ring canal is drawn round the inside foot of the dike, on the field side.
    From outside in, a map draws the wild water, the dike, the ring canal, the field ditches and the paddies, and no channel runs outside the dike. Channels inside the dike are recorded; a single ring at the dike's foot is not, so its place is a drawing convention.
  • The canals are drawn in long, nearly straight reaches that waver gently, with rounded corners.
    On this project's reasoning a dug canal wavers with the ground and with repair, and the finer field ditches are drawn crookeder still. The line is a drawing convention.
  • The horita is not drawn yet.
    It would suit a waterlogged polder, in place of ordinary paddy (Polders: fields diked against the fluctuating water (weitian, waju)): long raised strips of rice with water channels between, recorded for Japanese polders from the eighteenth century.
  • The chain pump is not drawn.
    Nothing we read says where it stood on a polder or how many a polder had (Polders: fields diked against the fluctuating water (weitian, waju)).

On our maps a rice polder and a dike-pond block lay out their insides differently: the rice polder on a regular grid of straight canals, the dike-pond block as a mosaic, its lattice of ditches bent out of line so that its four-sided ponds skew to uneven shapes.

  • A rice polder is drawn on a surveyed grid.
    The lower Yangtze polders were laid out on a lattice of channels, so the pool's rice polder keeps the grid.
  • A dike-pond block is drawn as a mosaic; the chessboard of ponds, recorded too, is not drawn.
    The grid is recorded for the canals. For the ponds two layouts are recorded: a mosaic, in a modern essay on the delta's past, and a chessboard, in Qu Dajun's account of Jiujiang in 1678, its dikes the black lines and its ponds the squares. Today the chessboard is also the view from the air by Lake Tai, and in one Pearl-delta village a modern plan would make the ponds regular. With two layouts recorded, the layout would be rolled block by block; our maps draw only the mosaic, so the chessboard of ponds is a known gap.
  • The mosaic bends the lattice but keeps every pond four-sided.
    The interior lattice is pushed out of line by a smooth drift that fades to nothing at the fixed outer edge. Neighboring crossing points move together, so the lines stay continuous but bend, the interior ditches become meandering creeks, and the parcels skew to trapezoids while each pond stays four-sided, as the record has most of them.
  • How strongly the lattice bends is a calibrated liberty.
    It was set on the Kuwabata map, which is drawn with it.
