How our maps size and draw a city's temples (garan)

Our maps draw an ordinary temple at the size history gives it, about 35,000 sq ft, and each of a city's great temple complexes at about twice that. The great complexes follow the plan shared by every great temple we found described, but on a small fraction of a historical great temple's ground. The setting also gives its city temple complexes dozens of monks, where most historical temples had a handful, a deliberate deviation. How many temples our maps give a city, and where, is at How our maps place a city's temples and its temple quarter (teramachi).

  • An ordinary temple precinct is drawn at about 35,000 sq ft, a square some 190 ft on a side.
    This is accurate: an Edo temple of about 1,000 tsubo was called one of average size, and a Chinese county seat's temple stood on 3,300 m² (~35,500 sq ft). It is the size of a county town's temple and of a capital's modest temples.
  • An ordinary precinct holds a gate, a main hall, a kuri that is also the temple office, and a bell tower.
    At an ordinary temple the kuri often doubled as the office, so one building serves for both. Our maps draw the lecture hall, the sutra house, the monks' hall and the corridors only at a great temple.
  • How big is an ordinary temple's main hall drawn?
    About 60 by 40 ft. It is a GUESS from the one measured hall, Jorokuji's of 1629, 61 by 39 ft, which was built as its temple's abbot's quarters and later became its main hall.
  • A city's two great temple complexes are drawn at about 73,000 sq ft each, far smaller than history's great temples.
    That is twice the ordinary precinct, and far less than history's great temples: Quanzhou's Kaiyuan temple stands on 78,000 m² (~19 acres), and Kan'ei-ji held over 305,000 tsubo (~250 acres). Keeping the great temple's plan at a provincial city's size is a deviation.
  • A great temple is drawn with its gates and main halls in one line.
    This is accurate: every great temple we found described, Japanese or Chinese, of any sect, sets its gates and principal halls on one line and its other buildings to the sides.
  • How big is a great temple's main hall drawn?
    About 90 by 70 ft, between an ordinary temple's 60 by 40 ft and the main halls of Japan's greatest temples, about 150 ft across and 113 to 138 ft deep - a GUESS, since no page we read gives a provincial great temple's main hall.

The rule the map follows: a great city temple is a walled precinct on one axis:

  • an outer gate, the main gate, then the main hall (and in a Zen house a dharma hall behind it) in a line, with a cloister or corridors round the central court;
  • the bell tower and the sutra house to the sides of that court, the kuri and the monks' quarters to one side, the abbot's quarters behind the main hall;
  • sub-temples as separate walled lots below or around the core, and the lords' tombs or the graveyard outside the cloister at the back;
  • where the ground slopes, the gates and sub-temples, the central court with its halls, and the founder's hall or tomb behind climb it as terraces.

A temple's tall buildings are its bell tower and, at a great temple, its pagoda.

  • Every temple precinct carries its own bell tower, within its gate.
    A bell tower is part of the full plan in Japan, and a Chinese city temple kept its own apart from the city's, so this is accurate. It is drawn apart from the city's civic tower (The bell-and-drum tower - one per walled seat).
  • How big is a temple's bell tower drawn?
    About 20 ft square, a GUESS between a Japanese tower a single bay square and the Zhihua temple's bell tower, of the same form as its drum tower of 7.1 m (~23 ft) square.
  • Which temples carry a pagoda?
    Only a great temple's precinct: the temples of the domain capital's grand abbots and of a provincial city's provincial abbot, the senior monks the Ministry of Rites posts to each. It is a GUESS: no page we read says how common pagodas were at ordinary temples, every pagoda we found measured stands at a great or famous temple, and the pagodas Emperor Shomu ordered in the 740s stood at each province's official temple (kokubunji).
  • How big is a pagoda drawn?
    About 16 to 20 ft square at its base and 60 to 110 ft tall, for three stories. It is a GUESS from the three-story pagodas measured: Kohfukuji's 19.1 m (~63 ft) on a first story 4.8 m (~16 ft) across, Hokkiji's about 24 m (~79 ft) and Yakushiji's about 34 m (~112 ft).

The setting's city temples keep far more monks than history's ordinary temples did.

  • Every city temple complex in the setting is a major house of many monks, a deliberate deviation.
    Historically most temples were small: a count late in the thirteenth century gives about five clergy to a Chinese temple on average.
  • How many monks does a city temple complex keep?
    About 15 to 30 in a provincial city (~3,000 inhabitants) and 50 or more in a domain capital (~12,000). This is this project's own scale, a GUESS: no page we read gives a monk count for any Ming city monastery. The nearest Ming figure we found, a frontier monastery founded by imperial order in 1442 and given fifty-five official monks, is the size of a domain capital's complex, though fifty-five was the number it was allotted, not a count of the monks living there. History's great temples were of another order: the two whose monks are counted, Zojoji in Edo and Daxiangguo in Kaifeng, kept thousands.
  • How large a share of a provincial city are its clergy?
    Its two complexes, 30 to 60 monks between them, put clergy at about 1-2% of its population, two to four times the 0.46% of China under the Song dynasty. In the setting the city's temples serve the whole province's worship, not just the walled city. Across the population as a whole, the setting puts monks at under 1%.
