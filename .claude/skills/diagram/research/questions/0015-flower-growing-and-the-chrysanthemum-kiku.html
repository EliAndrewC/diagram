Flower growing and the chrysanthemum (kiku)

One of our maps, the walled county seat of Hirameki, keeps a field of chrysanthemums for the Emperor inside its west rampart; in Rokugan the chrysanthemum is the flower of Choyo, the ninth month's festival. A field kept for a ruler in a provincial town is this setting's own, and the research below shows where chrysanthemums and other flowers were actually grown.

The chrysanthemum came to Japan from China as a medicine and an ornament, and became the emblem of the imperial house. Its festival on the ninth day of the ninth month was kept into the Meiji period and lives on at the imperial garden party, and in the Edo period, before 1868, growers bred it into many distinct lines. In China the festival had long been a city's show of the flower.

Flowers were also grown to sell, on the edge of Edo: nursery villages there raised chrysanthemums, and one of them had some eighty nurseries at its height. No page we read describes a field of the flower kept for a ruler or a court, or flowers grown for a shrine in a plot of their own.

  • The chrysanthemum became Japan's imperial emblem by custom.
    In the Kamakura period the retired emperor Go-Toba took the flower as his own mark; later emperors carried it on, and by custom it became fixed as the imperial crest.
  • The chrysanthemum came to Japan from China as a medicine and an ornament.
    It reached Japan as a medicinal herb and an ornamental plant.
  • In twelfth-century China the Double Ninth festival filled the capital with chrysanthemums.
    A memoir of Kaifeng, the Northern Song capital, says that at the Double Ninth the city viewed chrysanthemums of several named kinds, that there was nowhere without them, and that the wine shops bound them into doorways.
  • The chrysanthemum festival was kept into the Meiji period, and lives on at the imperial garden party.
    The Choyo festival, on the ninth day of the ninth month of the old calendar, was held until the Meiji period, and lives on as the imperial garden party for viewing the flower.
  • The chrysanthemum has long been an altar and offering flower in Japan.
    It has long been used as an altar flower and an offering flower.
  • The chrysanthemum's great breeding began in the Edo period.
    Breeding took off from the Edo period, above all from the end of the seventeenth century, with distinct groups of varieties in Edo, Ise, Kyoto and Kumamoto.
  • Was a field of chrysanthemums ever kept for a ruler or a court?
    No page we read describes one.

Around Edo, flowers were grown to sell and to be viewed, and the record of it reaches back into the Edo period itself.

  • Somei's nurserymen grew chrysanthemums near Edo by the early nineteenth century.
    Somei village, now Komagome in Tokyo, was a home of nurserymen, whom a catalog of Edo's plants and products published in 1824 named as a source of primroses, azaleas and chrysanthemums.
  • Sugamo, on Edo's edge, grew chrysanthemums in some eighty nurseries at its height.
    A guide to Edo's flowers of 1827 put chrysanthemums at Sugamo, "where there are nurseries here and there". Sugamo's growing began with the flower beds of the mid-Edo period and had three great booms in the mid-to-late period, at their height some eighty nurseries.
  • Horikiri's gardens for viewing irises began at the end of the Edo period.
    The Katsushika city history dates them so, and says they are said to have been the first in Japan.
  • A later iris garden at Horikiri was turned into rice paddy in 1942, to grow food in the war.
    Horikiri-en, begun in the Meiji period, became paddy when food ran short in the Pacific War.
  • How early did flowers from the Boso peninsula reach Edo?
    By the middle of the Edo period, on a record the city page for the peninsula does not name: it tells of narcissus from there sold to Edo's samurai houses and townhouses, and reads the senior councillor (roju) Matsudaira Sadanobu's travel diary of 1792 as showing narcissus already grown in quantity around Hota and Motona.
  • When did flower growing become a trade at the peninsula's southern tip?
    Lilies for cut flowers and export bulbs were grown from 1886, and the same page dates the first success at flower growing in the Awa district to the Taisho period.
  • Was the farmland around a provincial town ever planted with flowers for sale?
    Not on any page we read: every case we found of flowers grown for sale is on the edge of a great city, Edo.
  • Were flowers grown for a shrine or a temple in a plot of their own?
    No page we read describes such a plot.
  • How large was a flower field?
    No page we read gives the size of any historical flower field or plot; the only areas found are of today's parks.

In China the chrysanthemum is grown today as a medicinal crop, and how far back that growing goes is not settled.

  • Tongxiang in Zhejiang grows the chrysanthemum as a crop today.
    Its dried flower head is one of the province's traditional medicinal herbs, sold across the country.
  • How old is Tongxiang's chrysanthemum growing?
    Tradition has officials of the Southern Song settling there and planting it in gardens, but no page we could read dates Tongxiang's fields of the flower before modern times. So Tongxiang's is a modern case, and the market growing found in the past is Edo's.
