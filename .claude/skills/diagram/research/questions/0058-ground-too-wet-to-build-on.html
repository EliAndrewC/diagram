Ground too wet to build on

On a river plain in Japan the old settlements stood on the natural levees, which are higher and drier than the ground around them, while the poorly drained back marsh behind them was farmed as paddy. Graves, too, were urged onto high ground: a Chinese classic of about 239 BC asks it of every burial, out of the damp of the groundwater, and a Japanese rule of 1884, early in the Meiji era, sites a new graveyard on high, dry ground.

The well is the exception. No rule we found from before modern times (before 1868 in Japan) keeps a well off wet ground; a Ming book on wells says that in marsh country a well finds water anywhere. Whether a shrine was kept off wet ground, no page we read says. What a marsh was, and where it lay, is at Marshes and wetlands (shitchi).

  • On Japan's river plains, settlements stood on the dry natural levees, and the wet back marsh behind them was farmed chiefly as paddy.
    A Japanese encyclopedia describes the natural levee as sandy, higher and drier than its surroundings, with settlements, fields and roads standing on it since long ago, while the back marsh, poorly drained, has been used chiefly as paddy. Where wet rice predominates, as in Japan, the back marsh has been cleared and traditionally used as paddy.
  • Was the low ground just below a field's drain wet, like the back marsh?
    The levee and the back marsh are a river plain's pattern; a single field has a low edge of its own, where its water drains out. Very likely, the ground below that edge was wet. A drainage standard lays the channel that carries a field's water away mainly in the low-lying ground, and low ground of that kind, a river plain's back marsh, drains poorly. That standard is modern and written for upland fields, and the back marsh is a river plain's, not a field's; carrying both to the ground below a field's drain is this project's reading.
  • Villages on the levees are an old pattern: in the Heian period, the Nagano basin's large villages stood on the levees of the Chikuma River.
    In the Nagano basin in the mid-Heian period, the late 9th and 10th centuries, large villages stood on the natural levees of the Chikuma River and similar slightly raised ground, and are thought to have farmed the wet ground behind them as paddy. The city history calls that siting a tradition seen from the Yayoi period on.
  • Did the small Heian villages keep to the dry ground as well?
    Not necessarily. The Nagano city history has small villages of the Heian period, unlike its large central villages, spreading over almost the whole of the city, and new land thought to have been opened first on low wet ground and other ground unsuited to paddy. The levee villages are one basin's archaeology, and the dry ground was the rule for the large villages, not for every house.

Graves were sited away from wet ground too. For them we read a Chinese text on funerals and a modern Japanese rule for graveyards.

  • A Chinese classic of about 239 BC puts every burial on high ground, away from the groundwater.
    The Lushi chunqiu, compiled about 239 BC, says a shallow grave is dug up by foxes and a deep one reaches the groundwater, and so every burial must be on high ground, to escape the foxes and the damp.
  • The Lushi chunqiu also lets a burial lie on low wet ground.
    It argues for modest funerals and speaks of high ground in general, not of a marsh or a village graveyard, and its next chapter lets a burial lie on low wet ground so long as the grave blends into it. High ground is a preference there, not a ban on wet ground.
  • A modern Japanese rule, of 1884, sites a new graveyard on high, dry ground.
    A Home Ministry instruction of 1884, early in the Meiji era, puts a new graveyard on high, dry ground that does no harm to drinking water. How far from houses and from water a village's burial ground lay is at Village burial grounds (bochi).

For wells we read one Chinese book on well-digging and a museum leaflet on Japan's earliest excavated wells.

  • A Ming book on wells ranks the mountain foot first, then the sunny ground of gardens and houses, then open country.
    The one premodern Chinese book on well-digging we read, Xiong Sanba's of 1612, ranks its sites that way; the catalog calls its author a man of the West, so the ranking may owe as much to European practice as to Chinese, a guess of ours.
  • Was a well kept off wet ground before modern times?
    No rule we found says so. Xiong Sanba's book of 1612 says that in low marsh country a well-digger finds water anywhere, and never warns against wet ground. No other page we read from before modern times sites a well against wet ground.
  • Japan's earliest wells were found at lowland sites.
    They appear at the end of the Early Yayoi period, excavated at lowland sites on the Saga and Chikugo plains, though the museum's leaflet says nothing about where on that ground a well was sited.
  • Was a shrine kept off wet ground?
    No page we read, from before modern times or dating the custom, says where a shrine was sited against wet ground.
