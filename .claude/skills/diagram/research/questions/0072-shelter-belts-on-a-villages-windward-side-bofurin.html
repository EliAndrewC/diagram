Shelter belts on a village's windward side (bofurin)

A shelter belt is a band of trees planted to stop the wind, in Japanese 防風林 (bofurin, "windbreak forest"), and the farmhouse groves called igune stood on the side of the house the winter wind comes from. The winter wind over Japan blows from the northwest, and on the Sendai plain the farmhouse groves stand on the north or the west against it, often leaving the south or the east open, as they have for several hundred years. Not every region did the same: on the Izumo plain the grove once ringed the whole house, and on the island of Tarama a village belt planted in 1742 still rings its settlement.

A southern Chinese village kept its woods as separate patches at set places around it, with its rice paddies open between them. Nearly every Japanese record we read is of the grove round a single farmhouse; the belts we found that sheltered a whole village are Tarama's and the Chinese villages' woods. A belt was not one kind of tree in a row. A Japanese farmhouse grove held some nine to eighteen kinds of tree, in three of the four regions surveyed led by cedar, and a Chinese village wood was a mixed broadleaf forest. Old belts are described by the side they stood on, not by a compass bearing, and where an old way in is recorded, it came by the grove's open side or went round the grove, not through it.

  • The winter wind over Japan blows from the northwest.
    In winter the northwesterly wind out of the Siberian high prevails over Japan, as the southeasterly out of the Pacific does in summer.
  • Cold air drains downhill, so high ground can give a place a cold wind of its own.
    Cold air is denser than warm air, so it flows down a slope.
  • On the Sendai plain a farmhouse grove stood on one or two windward sides, not round the house.
    A 2020 study of the Sendai plain's farmhouse groves (igune) gives the rule and its reason: the plain's winter winds are mainly strong north-northwesterlies, so the grove is formed on the north or the west side of the homestead and often lacks the south or the east side. The Japanese Wikipedia article on homestead groves says the same of the igune: many stand on the north and west of the house, as a windbreak and a snowbreak.
  • Sendai's groves have stood on the north and west for several hundred years.
    Date Masamune, the first lord of the Sendai domain, encouraged the planting when homesteads were built on newly opened plains, to shelter them from the winter wind that blows down from the Ou Mountains; the trees were planted in many layers on the north and the west, and kept as a windbreak since.
  • On the Izumo plain the grove once ringed the whole house, before Japan began to modernize in 1868.
    The Izumo plain's pine grove (tsuijimatsu) enclosed the whole house before the Meiji period, and later shrank to a hook over the north and west sides alone, as floods grew fewer and building styles changed. The ring is that one region's form; the page gives no date for the change.
  • In 1625, early in the Edo period, a document issued in the Takada domain ordered cedar planted round the homestead.
    A document Matsudaira Mitsunaga issued in the Takada domain in 1625 directed that cedar be planted round the homestead, and camellia and dwarf bamboo on its south side.
  • A Sendai city page defines igune as a grove round the house, but the groves themselves stood on one or two sides.
    A city conservation page defines igune as trees planted so as to encircle the homestead, but that is a gloss on the word, not a description of how the groves were built; the study of the groves themselves finds them on the north or the west.

Belts that sheltered a whole village are recorded in southern China and in the Ryukyu islands.

  • A southern Chinese village kept its woods as separate patches, not as one wall.
    A study of fengshui forests draws the idealized village with its woods at named places: the back grove on the master mountain behind and above the houses, groves at the water head and the water mouth along the river above and below, and groves at the mountain gaps where the winds are strongest, with the rice paddies in the open ground between them. How large those groves were is at Groves around a southern Chinese village.
  • On the island of Tarama a village belt planted in 1742, under the Ryukyu kingdom, still rings its settlement.
    Of the village tree belts (hogo) of the Ryukyu kingdom, today's Okinawa, the best preserved is Tarama's, made in 1742, about 1.8 km (~5,906 ft) long and still protecting the settlement and its farmland. A local paper gives it as 10 to 15 m (~33-49 ft) wide today, formed so as to surround the settlement. What such belts meant for a town's farms is at Farmsteads at a town.
  • Who ordered Tarama's belt?
    Probably Sai On, one of the kingdom's chief ministers, who ordered its survey. In 1741, on his orders, the head official of the Miyako islands surveyed Tarama's forests, its village belt and its shore belt, and reported to the royal government at Shuri in 1742; the belt is thought to have been planted then.
  • The belt closes the settlement's west, south and east; a wood stands at its back, to the north.
    The village belt rings the settlement on the west, the south and the east, and in the wood to the north, the koshiate-mori, the tall trees are mainly Alexandrian laurel (terihaboku).
  • How wide was it, and what was it planted with?
    About 16 m (~52 ft) wide when planted, 12 m (~39 ft) today after a road was widened. It is thought to have been planted with fukugi, an evergreen, and Alexandrian laurel; other trees have seeded in since, and its upper story today holds fukugi, Alexandrian laurel, mokutachibana and tabu among others. The largest fukugi in it stands 10.36 m (~34 ft) high and is reckoned about 275 years old.
  • Who kept it, and what could villagers take from it?
    The kingdom's yamadome, a set season when entering the forests to cut trees was forbidden, seems to have been kept on Tarama for ten days from set days of the second and eighth months; by the account of one ward's head, until 1972 its officers patrolled the ward's forests against theft. Today the island's two wards keep it where it is ward land, clearing it about once a year - today's arrangement, which the dossier does not date. What penalty the kingdom set for cutting a belt's trees, and what a villager could take from one, no page we read says.

Which side a belt stood on is one thing; which way it ran across the land is another.

  • Was a belt laid at right angles to the wind before modern times?
    Today's textbooks lay it so: windbreak belts are built at right angles to the wind they stop, and an orchard's main belt stands against the prevailing wind with its secondary belts mostly at right angles to it. Both describe modern planting - the textbook's example is Hokkaido's field belts, laid out on American advice - and no premodern source we found lays a belt at a bearing to the wind: the old belts are described by the side they stand on or by the settlement they ring.

What a belt was planted with differed between Japan's farmhouse groves and China's village woods. Every survey of the Japanese form is of a single farmhouse's grove; how those groves stood round each house is at Groves of trees around farmhouses (yashikirin).

  • A Japanese farmhouse grove mixed many kinds of tree, led by cedar.
    A 2004 survey of homestead groves in four regions - Isawa in Iwate, Iide in Yamagata, Tonami in Toyama and Hikawa in Shimane - counted on average 13.3, 9.8, 17.8 and 8.8 kinds of tree per homestead; in the three regions of summer-green forest, cedar grew at every homestead and was the dominant tree. The Tonami research institute puts it the same way: its plain's groves are made up of many kinds of tree round cedar.
  • Miyagi's (the Sendai plain's) groves stand on a frame of four tall trees.
    Cedar, zelkova, alder and black pine, 20 m (~66 ft) high or more, stand mainly on the northwest of the homestead, with many middle and low kinds among them, their seeds carried in by birds.
  • At Iide and Tonami the grove was planted in rows.
    The four-region survey found that at Iide the row was thin enough to see the house through, and at Tonami thick enough to hide it.
  • Was any grove planted with a single kind of tree?
    The nearest was the clipped pine wall of the Izumo plain. The Izumo grove is mainly black pine, planted on the west and north of the house and clipped to a set height every four or five years, so that it stands as a trimmed wall. At Hikawa, on the Izumo plain, the four-region survey found a holly (mochinoki) and a stone oak (matebajii) among the three commonest trees with the pine, and describes the grove as clipped like a hedge.
  • Southern Chinese village woods surveyed today are mixed broadleaf forest.
    Thirty-two well-protected fengshui patches in the Pearl River Delta held 46.8 kinds of tree each on average, and their measures match a well-developed evergreen broadleaf forest rather than a conifer planting.
  • What share of a grove's trees were conifers?
    In one hamlet's farmhouse groves, cedar alone was nearly half. A 1987 survey of Kashima, a hamlet of 46 households on the Tonami plain, counted 1,542 trees at least 10 cm (~4 in) across, and 735 of them, 48%, were cedar (Groves of trees around farmhouses). That count is of one hamlet's farmhouse groves; no page we read gives the share for a belt sheltering a whole village. Cedar was the dominant tree in the three summer-green regions, so our GUESS is that it was the commonest tree in a village belt as well; no count says so.

A grove still had to let its household reach the house, and a village its fields.

  • Was a belt's planted side unbroken?
    No old record we found shows a gap left in a planted side. The Izumo plain's grove once ringed the whole house, a 1625 order had cedar planted round the homestead, and at Fangkeng the water-mouth grove stood like a city wall, with the path going round it; neither the ring nor the order says how the way in passed the trees.
  • Was the way in on the grove's open side?
    On the Tonami plain it was: the homestead's east side was its entrance, planted with gardens and ornamental trees, while tall, sturdy trees, mainly cedar, stood on the west and south against wind and snow. The page gives that layout no date. For the Sendai groves, planted on the north and the west, no page we read says what their open side held.
  • A Huizhou village's road came in at its water mouth.
    The water mouth was a village's key passage to the outside world, so its road and its bridge were there.
  • At the village of Fangkeng the path went round the water-mouth grove, not through it.
    In a villager's account the chapter does not date, the grove planted on the water mouth's earth dam stood like a city wall, with only the stream and the flagstone path going round it.
  • How was a lane cut through a planted belt?
    No old record we found says, nor gives an opening's angle to the wind. A modern United States windbreak manual sets a lane at an angle to the prevailing wind, 100 to 500 ft from the belt's end; that is modern design practice, and no earlier page says the same.
  • A modern windbreak manual warns that a gap in a belt funnels the wind.
    A modern United States windbreak manual warns that a gap concentrates the flow, so that downwind of it the wind often blows faster than over the open field. The manual is modern, but the physics of air squeezed through a gap holds in any century.
  • No source we found gives the width of an opening in a belt.
    None, Chinese or Japanese, historical or agronomic, gives one - for an open side, a gate, or the water-mouth passage that a stream and a road already run through.
