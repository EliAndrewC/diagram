The ground outside a city gate: gate suburbs (guanxiang), tile kilns and the built-up land a city took in

Outside the gates of a walled city, a built-up strip of streets grew along the roads that came in. It was common enough to have its own name in Chinese, guanxiang: the streets outside a city gate and the area around them. By the nineteenth century few walled cities lacked such a suburb outside at least one gate, and in many the built-up ground outside the walls was larger than that within. A gate funneled the traffic of one sector of the countryside, so the ground just outside it drew markets and the trades that served country people, and the gates on the long-distance roads drew inns. The measured places run to over a hundred houses, or a street over a thousand feet long.

Beyond the shops and inns, a city kept at its edge the works it could not keep inside. In the two capitals whose records we read, Edo and Beijing, the tile kilns stood by the city: Edo's at Imado on its river, and Beijing's just outside its old wall, until a new wall took their ground into the city and they moved out. The one lime order we read went the other way: Edo's castle lime was burned in villages well out from the city and carried in. In time Edo took the built-up ground around it into the city as town ground.

  • The ground outside a gate drew markets, and the gates on long-distance roads drew inns.
    A study of Chinese walled cities finds the ground immediately outside a gate a favored site for markets and for businesses serving the rural inhabitants, and inns and other services for travelers and traders outside the particular gates that gave on the long-distance routes.
  • The largest suburb lay on the road that carried the most traffic.
    The study of Chinese walled cities finds the biggest suburb in the direction of the main traffic on an important route.
  • At the busiest gates, the suburb often grew before the ground inside the wall had filled.
    The study of Chinese walled cities also notes that in many if not most cities the suburbs outside the most frequented gates developed before the walled ground had been completely built up.
  • Outside Beijing's Desheng Gate, a horse market grew on the trunk road north.
    At Madian, on the trunk road north from the Desheng Gate, beyond its gate suburb, the tribute horse trains sold their culled animals on the spot, the merchants who dealt in horses, cattle, sheep and camels lodged there, and a market formed of itself. The horse market later moved into the gate suburb itself, which at its height held forty-eight horse inns.
  • At Zhangjiakou, the gate suburb was set up because the ground inside the fort had filled.
    The suburb of the Zhangjiakou fort was set up in 1480 because the ground inside the fort could not house its added inhabitants, and it grew streets of its own.
  • How big was a gate suburb?
    No page we read counts the buildings of a provincial seat's gate suburb; the one count for a gate suburb is the forty-eight horse inns of a capital's, which counts one trade alone. The nearest counted place, not quite a provincial seat's gate suburb, gives the order of size. At Odawara, a castle town and post town on the Tokaido, the ward made beside the castle town's entrance when the highway was re-routed was lined with inns for villagers on official business and with tea shops, and held 124 houses in the early 1830s, late in the Edo period - a count of houses, not shops.
  • How long did a gate suburb's street run?
    The one measured street is a market town's, and it takes its name from a checkpoint set up in it, not from a city gate. In the market town of Xiashi, in Zhejiang, the street district named Nanguanxiang ("south guanxiang") runs about 350 m (~1,148 ft) by 50 m (~164 ft), shops with homes, the shop in front and the river behind.

What else stood at a city's edge - its tile kilns, where its lime was burned, and the built-up ground it took in - is known from two capitals, Edo and Beijing. A potter's kiln works, with its potters living at it, is a different thing, at Kiln works; the kiln quarters and official kilns a capital kept are at How a capital differs from a provincial city.

  • In Edo, tile firing was the specialty of Imado, on the Sumida river northeast of Asakusa.
    The period's guidebook to Edo says tile firing had become Imado's specialty, and Imado stood on the west bank of the Sumida. At the end of the shogunate about fifty households at Imado and Hashiba fired Imado ware of every kind, roof tiles among it. That Imado lay at the city's edge is this record's reading of its place northeast of Asakusa.
  • Beijing's glazed-tile works moved out when a new wall took their ground into the city.
    The works reached north to the moat of the Inner City, Beijing's original walled city. When the wall of the Outer City, its southern extension built in 1553, made that ground city ground, the kilns, unsuited to firing inside a city, moved out to Liuliqu village, in what is now Mentougou district.
  • How many tile kilns did a provincial city keep?
    No page we read counts them. The fifty households of Imado fired for a capital, and not tiles alone.
  • When Edo's castle was built, its lime was burned in villages far from the city and carried in by horse.
    In 1606 the lime for Edo's castle was ordered from the kiln owners of the villages of Kami-Nariki and Kita-Osoki, burned there, and carried in by relays of horses from the districts along the road. The road ran out across empty country: a survey of it from Tanashi to Ome, quoted on the same page, counts a stretch of six ri across the moor with no houses on it. That one urgent order has the lime burned at its source; it says nothing either way about lime burned at a city's edge.
  • Edo in time took the built-up ground around its old core in as town ground.
    In 1662 the districts around the city that had become built up were put under the town magistrate as town ground. That these were farm villages built over is this record's reading of "had become built up".
