Field drains (akusuiro)

A field drain - in Japanese an akusuiro, a channel that carries away unneeded water - takes the water away from the rice paddies once it has watered them. Because water runs only downhill, the drain lies on the lowest ground of the fields, running along their foot and across the fall of the land, so that it gathers what runs out of every paddy above it. When the Minuma reservoir was reclaimed in 1728, in the Edo period, its supply canals were set along its raised edge and its drain on the paddies' lowest ground.

The drain let its water go into a river or a natural watercourse, where the villages below could take it up again. Where it started, at the top of its run, a drain needed little width; the oldest small field channels we have figures for run from about nine inches, on this record's reading of an ancient unit, to about three feet across. It widened as it gathered water. How a drain widens along its run is at How wide canals and ditches are: the ladder of channel widths, and how a whole field's canals and drain are laid out at Irrigation canals and how they are laid out (yosuiro).

  • Water could leave a paddy in three ways.
    Japanese planning names three layouts of a paddy's water: field-to-field irrigation, in which water passes over the bunds from one plot to the next; the combined type, in which one channel both supplies and receives; and the separated type, with a supply channel above the plots and a drainage channel below.
  • Before Japan's postwar land consolidation, one channel could both water and drain the paddies.
    In a village of the delta of the Tone River before the 1950s, the canal network spread over the paddies both supplied their water and received what they let out.
  • Paddy districts reused their drainage water until none went to waste.
    A combined channel was sometimes an old river course, so the water was used again of itself. Where it was not, works were built to return the drainage to the supply canal or to use it directly, until every paddy district had a reuse system that let out no wasted water at all.
  • A separate supply ditch and drain for every plot came with modern land consolidation.
    Separating the supply canal from the drainage canal, giving every plot its own inlet and outlet, and cutting the drain about 1 m (~3 ft) below the field is the standard method of modern field consolidation. In the Tone delta it came with the consolidation that followed World War II, the irrigation and drainage canals separated and laid along the farm roads; across Japan, the Farm Consolidation Projects begun in 1963 laid out blocks with a road, an irrigation channel and a drainage channel beside each one.
  • An Edo-period reclamation already set a district's trunk supply canals and its drain apart.
    When the Minuma reservoir was reclaimed for paddies in 1728, its supply canals were set along its edge and a drainage channel in its center.
  • Separating supply and drainage made a district need more water.
    Once each plot drew and drained on its own, the water left over in it went straight into the drain, and there was less chance to use it again.
  • Japanese counts a drain as a different thing from a supply channel.
    A supply channel (yosuiro) is a channel made to draw water in; a channel whose purpose is drainage alone is not normally counted among them.

Where the drain ran on a field followed from what it had to do: gather the water of every paddy above it.

  • A collector drain runs along the low end of a field, across the fall.
    A modern drainage manual sets it out: the small field drains run down the steepest slope, and they empty into a collector drain built at the lower end of the field, at right angles to them. A drain that ran straight downhill would follow one column of plots and gather the water of no other - this project's reading of why the collector crosses the slope.
  • When the Minuma reservoir was turned into paddies in 1728, its drain went on the lowest ground.
    A historian of the district places that reclamation's supply canals on the terrace edge a step higher than the paddies and the drain, the Shiba River, on their lowest ground, so the drain ran along the low line, across the fall of the paddies between.
  • No page we read gives the angle at which a collector met the stream it emptied into.
    The right angle in the drainage manual is the one between the field drains and the collector. The manual gives no angle for where the collector joins the main drain, which it says is often an improved natural stream, and an angle there would be this record's own inference.

Once the drain had gathered the paddies' water, it had to let it go somewhere lower.

  • A drain let its water go into a river or a natural watercourse.
    A modern Japanese design standard describes a drainage channel as one that gathers the runoff of the collector drains and lets it go into a river or a natural watercourse, laid mainly in the low ground. A farm drain in Saitama did the same: it was built as the drain of a district that canals watered in the Edo period, its source the fields' spent water and its mouth a river.
  • The water a drain carried off was used again downstream.
    In a drought, the drainage one district returned to the river was taken up in full by the irrigation below it. In a valley paddy, the water used in the paddies and in daily life flows on into the river, and in the end into the lake.
  • Every drain we read of emptied into a river or a watercourse, none into a pond of its own.
    No page we read describes a drain ending in a pond, so a drain that does is a GUESS.
  • A walled Chinese city carried water through its wall.
    The page we read on the wall of Nanjing, in China, is about rivers rather than drains: a sluice led a lake's water into the city through a conduit laid in the foundation of the wall, and a river left the city by a water gate.

A drain gathers its water as it goes, so it is narrowest where it starts.

  • A classical Chinese text sizes the smallest field channels by the spade that cuts them.
    The book of artisans of the Rites of Zhou gives a spade five cun wide; two spades working together cut the finest channel, one chi wide and one deep, and the channel at the head of the field is doubled, two chi wide and two deep. It is an ideal scheme, not a survey of dug ditches.
  • How wide were the Rites of Zhou's field channels in feet?
    About nine inches for the finest and about 1.5 ft for the one at the head of the field, on this record's arithmetic. The chi of that time was about 23 to 24 cm (~9 in), but before the Qin standardized measures each state kept its own, and which state's foot this text used is not settled here, so the inches are this record's reading, not the text's.
  • An excavated drain from the start of the Yayoi period was 0.5 to 1 m (~1.5-3 ft) wide.
    At the Hattori site in Shiga, among paddies of the earliest Yayoi period, a drain enclosed by bunds ran east, 0.5 to 1 m (~1.5-3 ft) wide. It is one site, far older than the farms our maps show, and it does not say where along the drain its narrowest point lay.
  • A modern Chinese design standard sets its narrowest canal floor at 0.3 m (~12 in).
    No canal bottom is to be narrower, so that it can be run and maintained once it is built. It is a modern rule for lined canals, but its reason - what a worker with a hoe can maintain - holds for a dug ditch, and it lands near the older figures.
  • On this project's reckoning, the head of a drain needed almost no width for the water it carried.
    At its head a drain takes the runoff of a single paddy, about a tenth of a hectare (~1/4 acre), a fraction of a liter a second even when the paddy is drained after a storm, so its width comes from the tool that cuts it rather than from its flow.
  • A channel narrower than the finest one the Rites of Zhou describes was, on this project's reading, a seasonal furrow rather than a ditch.
    A channel under about nine inches across is taken here as the furrow cut again in the paddy at each midseason drawdown, not a ditch kept from year to year.
