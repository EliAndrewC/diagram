How our maps draw farm storehouses (kura)

About one farmhouse in eight on the hamlets our generator lays out carries a storehouse, and they are the largest farmhouses; the hand-drawn maps carry more. It is drawn as a tiled annex against the house wall, 18 to 27 ft long. The share follows the one village count the record has from before 1868, at Kakimochi, and the storehouse marks a well-off farm at a glance because most farms lack one.

  • How many farms carry a storehouse?
    About one in eight on a generated hamlet, set from Kakimochi, a village whose households were counted before 1868: 2 in 16 had one. The Miyagi survey of 1972 was not used, because it is modern; the alternative, about 30%, a little above that survey's count of about one storehouse for every four older farmhouses, was not kept for that reason. It is one village, so the figure is a calibration rather than a finding; it keeps a storehouse uncommon enough to mean something at a glance.
  • A generated hamlet gets one storehouse for every eight farmhouses, rounded to a whole house.
    One storehouse on a hamlet of ten or eleven farmhouses, two on twelve to nineteen, three on twenty. That comes to 11 of 82 farmhouses over the five hamlets our generator has drawn.
  • The hand-drawn maps keep the higher share they were drawn with.
    They carry 324 storehouses on 1,126 farmhouses, about 29%, near the modern count the generated hamlets do not follow. They are kept as they were drawn, as finished exhibits that are never redrawn.
  • The storehouses go to the largest farmhouses, measured by the main house's floor area.
    No farmhouse without one is larger than a farmhouse with one. Making a strict rule of what one village's count shows as a strong tendency is this project's decision, stricter than the record: the map never leaves a great house without a storehouse while a smaller one has it, though Kakimochi's largest house, at 45 tsubo, had none. The alternative, a draw weighted toward the larger houses, would let a great house go without now and then; it was not taken, because the larger houses first is the tendency the record shows, and a draw would sometimes put a hamlet's storehouse on one of its middling houses.
  • Between two farmhouses of one size, a draw decides.
    Where two farmhouses are the same size the map has nothing else to tell their households apart by, so which of them has the storehouse is a random draw keyed to the map's seed, and the same map drawn twice gives the same answer. What decided it between two such households the records read do not say, so that is a GUESS.
  • A village headman's house always carries a storehouse.
    The powerful farmers who served as village officials owned the houses with the most outbuildings, so the headman's house is never drawn without one. Our generated hamlets have no headman, so on them the largest houses take them all.
  • The storehouse is drawn as an annex against the house wall, a drawing convention.
    It stands on the west wall of a scattered farmstead, and on the shaded north wall in a clustered village, where the sunny walls belong to the garden and the north is the one side the garden never takes. The record contradicts this: the storehouses in the sources read stood free of the house, in front of it or behind, on no fixed side, and few were built against it.
  • The storehouse is drawn at a farm shed's size, not a storehouse's, part of the same drawing convention.
    The annex is 18 to 27 ft long and 1.5 to 1.8 times as long as deep, the band of the two farm sheds measured from the end of the Edo period (Farm sheds and barns (naya)). The commonest storehouse in a new-field village west of Edo was about 15 by 18 ft, and Kakimochi's two were 2 by 3 ken, about 12 by 18 ft, so the annex is drawn as long as a storehouse was or up to half again as long, and at the short end of its band 10 to 12 ft deep, no deeper than Kakimochi's. The shed's size is kept because drawing the storehouse at its own, about 12 to 15 by 18 ft, would shift the houses of every generated hamlet.
  • Were storehouses tiled even beside a thatched farmhouse?
    Both storehouses in the records we read are tiled, so the maps draw every storehouse tiled, even where its farmhouse is thatched. That is this project's reading of two examples.
  • A storehouse's ground is set aside with the rest of its farm.
    When a hamlet is laid out, nothing else is placed on it.
