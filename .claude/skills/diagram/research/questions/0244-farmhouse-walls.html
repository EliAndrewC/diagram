Farmhouse walls

A Japanese farmhouse's outer wall was built in one of two ways: between the posts, so that the posts showed on its face (shinkabe), or over them, so that the posts were plastered in (okabe). The first was commoner in eastern Japan, the second in western. A plastered wall was earth laid on a lattice of bamboo or wood, and board walls and, on poor houses in the south, walls of bark were seen as well.

  • Japan's folk houses had two kinds of outer wall, each commoner in one half of the country.
    A Japanese encyclopedia divides the outer walls of folk houses into the shinkabe, its posts left showing on the outside, and the okabe, its posts plastered into the wall; the shinkabe was commoner in eastern Japan and the okabe in western.
  • Some outer walls were of thatch, or built thick in the storehouse style.
    The same entry names walls of thatch and walls in the storehouse style as the special kinds of outer wall.
  • How was a farmhouse's earth wall made?
    At the former Ikegami house, a large farmhouse in Saitama built at the end of the Edo period or the first years after it, the inner walls are earth walls. A lattice of bamboo or wood was set up as their base, earth was plastered on one face and then on the other, and a wall to be finished in white plaster took a coat of sand, water and konnyaku flour and then the plaster. That is one well-off house; how far a poorer farmhouse's walls were finished, the page does not say.
  • Outer walls of boards were common in the early Meiji years.
    An American zoologist who lived in Japan then describes outer walls of thin boards nailed on across the frame, or set upright with strips over their seams; plastered outer walls were very common too, though not durable, and a broken one with its bamboo laths showing stood for poverty in picture-books. He writes of Japan's houses in general, not of farmhouses.
  • Poorer houses in the south could be walled with bark.
    In the southern provinces the same observer saw rough walls of wide slabs of bark set upright and held by thin strips of bamboo nailed across them, a style common among the poorer houses.
  • How common was each kind of wall, and what were an ordinary small farmhouse's walls made of?
    No page we read counts how many farmhouses had each kind of outer wall, or describes the walls of an ordinary small farmhouse rather than a large survivor.
