How our maps place and draw notice boards (kosatsuba)

Every settlement on our maps, down to a hamlet, carries a notice board. It stands beside the busiest main road or street its map has, its face turned broadside to the traffic, as the boards stood where traffic was heaviest. It is drawn as a roofed frame 16 ft long and 6 ft deep, the size of the boards measured at two post towns, on a stone footing inside a fence, as the reconstructed board at Kanagawa stands. A city draws several boards and labels one.

  • Even a hamlet carries a board.
    The record reads the sources as putting a board in every town and village, and a board needs only one reader in a place: the headman copied out the circulars and had to read and write. So the maps give a board even to a hamlet of 50 inhabitants.
  • The board stands on a main road or street.
    It must catch the eye of everyone passing through. Every road counts as main, and so does the street a map declares as its avenue from the gate to the magistrate's office. Where a map has either, the search looks only along those, so a board on a side lane cannot arise.
  • Where a map has no main road or street, the board goes where the most buildings stand nearby.
    A web of lanes, or a town none of whose streets is marked as main, has nothing "main" to stand on, so the search counts the buildings near each possible site instead.
  • At a hamlet or a village the board stands 6 ft from the edge of the road.
    Every site the record names is on a road or street - a verge, the front of a gate, the foot of a bridge - and never on open ground set back from it. The 6 ft is this project's own figure.
  • In a town or a city, or where no roadside site fits, the board may stand up to 60 ft from the road.
    The 60 ft is this project's own figure, not a finding.
  • The board is squared to the road it faces.
    Its face meets the traffic broadside rather than edge-on, within 30 degrees of some stretch of that road inside the siting distance. That still shows about seven eighths of the face, and it clears the worst case on the drawn maps, a board taking a road bend's bearing about 18 degrees off its nearest stretch. The 30 degrees is this project's own figure, a calibration against the drawn maps.
  • A city draws a set of boards.
    The principal board stands at the central market, and there is another on the approach to every main gate, each within about 800 ft of its gate. The 800 ft is a calibration against the drawn maps.
  • In a city, the label goes to whichever board has room for it.
    An unlabeled board fits a tight verge by a gate where a labeled one cannot.

To find a board's site, the map weighs what those who set up a board weighed: the passers-by it would meet. Nakatsugawa's board was set at a crossroads some 20 m (~66 ft) up the slope from where its replica stands, so that more passers-by would see it.

  • Every board is sited by the map's own search, never picked by hand.
    A hand-picked site loses the traffic the board exists for: on two town maps, the sites the search finds stand among 19 and 29 buildings within 250 ft, against 3 and 10 for the sites a hand would choose.
  • When the map chooses a board's site, it looks for room for the board's label as well.
    The drawn board is small and fits almost anywhere, but its caption does not, and the busiest frontage is exactly where there is least room for one. A search for ground big enough for both walks away from the traffic: on one town map it put the board across the bridge from the town, 120 ft past the last shop, among 8 buildings within 250 ft against 23 on the high street. So the search scores the caption instead: a site whose caption fits outranks any whose caption does not, and among those the busiest wins. A board with nowhere to put a caption is still placed.
  • Our maps site every board for the traffic, never between the halves of a divided village.
    A village divided between two lords once set its board at about the same distance from each half's headman's house; our maps draw no such divided village.
  • The board is the last thing placed, after the map is cropped to its frame.
    A board's site depends on what already stands around it - the crossroads, the busiest frontage - so the board is placed once everything else on the map is drawn.
  • The board clears no ground.
    The one ground a real board claimed was its own site, closed in with a palisade, a stone wall or a bank of turf, and no page we read says a board's site was cleared of trees.
  • A board may stand under a tree's canopy, because its label is drawn above every crown.
    The label says where the board is even where a crown hides it, on the picture of a map as much as the interactive one.

Once sited, the board is drawn at the size the record measured.

  • A board is drawn as a roofed frame 16 ft long and 6 ft deep, on a stone footing inside a fence.
    Its long side runs parallel to the road it faces, and the footing is about 2 ft wider all round, with a fence line at its edge. The size is that of the frames measured at Kanagawa-juku and Totsuka-juku, about 16 to 18 ft along the road and 5 to 6 ft deep. The roof, the stone footing and the fence are those of the reconstructed board at Kanagawa.
  • A village's or a hamlet's board is drawn at the same size - a GUESS.
    No village board was measured on any page we read.
  • The search tests a smaller board than the one drawn.
    When it checks whether a board fits and faces the traffic, it uses a face of 7 by 3 ft, this project's own figure and shorter than every frame measured. The frame drawn is 16 by 6 ft.
  • At city scale the board is drawn as an oversized marker - a drawing convention.
    At town scale the true size can be drawn; at city scale the true frame is too small to see, so a larger marker stands for it and the label carries the read.

One board stands apart from a settlement's own: the one at a magistrate's gate.

  • Would a magistrate's office have had a notice board at its gate?
    A Chinese county office posted edicts, the magistrate's own notices and bans - wanted notices among them - and verdicts on the walls flanking its gate, so a "bounty board" at the gate has a counterpart before modern times. In Japan the boards hung before the gates of village officials' houses, but no page we read puts one at an intendant's office or a magistracy. Our maps join the two: a freestanding board, as in Japan, at the office's own gate, as in China - a GUESS.
