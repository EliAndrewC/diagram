How our maps draw timber yards and log booms (kiba)

A town or city on our maps keeps its timber in one of two forms, chosen by a knob from the map's seed: a timber yard on the water, or a retail timber dealer's shop. On the water, the stock is drawn only in the forms recorded before modern times - rafts moored along a bank, or a pond dug off the river - and never as a floating log boom, which we found in East Asia only from modern times.

  • A town or city with a river or a channel may keep its timber on the water.
    It is drawn as a yard on the bank with the logs held in the water beside it, as on a river port's waterfront.
  • Any town or city, river or not, may instead keep a retail timber dealer.
    Retail dealers were found in every region of Japan, so a town without a river is not without timber; it is without the log pond.
  • What does a dealer's yard look like?
    A shop with a small yard of stacked sawn timber behind it. The dealer is recorded as keeping sawyers, but no page we read says where they sawed or how the timber was stored, so the yard and its size are a GUESS.

Where the timber is kept on the water, it takes only the forms on record before modern times.

  • Where a town keeps its timber on the water, the stock is drawn as raft-mats moored along a bank, or as a pond off the river.
    Those are the forms on record before modern times: rafts moored at a stretch of bank, and ponds joined to the waterways by which the rafts came in and went out. The moored stock lies close along a bank where the current runs slow, its bank edge on the shore line, off the timber yard it feeds; a pond is dug off the river and joined to it by a canal.
  • No floating log boom is drawn round the stock.
    The only fence of floats strung on a rope we found on record in East Asia is a modern one, and our maps draw nothing known only from modern times.
  • The moored stock takes about a third of a navigated river's width, and never more than two fifths.
    So the boats keep their channel, as the nineteenth-century log-boom works on the St. Croix River, in the American Midwest, left a navigation channel beside their log channels. The shares are this project's choice, a GUESS: no source gives one.
  • The catch rope (tsunaba) is never drawn at a port.
    The rope across the water belongs upstream, on a timber river's middle course, where logs floated loose down a mountain river came out of its gorge and were made up into rafts, and a rope across a river would close it to boats.
  • On the Minami map, rafts moor along the Hayakawa's bank.
    The Hayakawa is 120 ft wide and navigated, with a wharf, a dock basin and a cargo canal, so the city's timber lies moored along its bank. The catch rope is off the map, at the catch-works where the river leaves its gorge upstream, where the loose-log drive is made up into the rafts that reach the city.
