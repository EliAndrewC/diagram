Reed beds and the marsh's edge (yoshihara)

A marsh does not end in a line. From open water it grades through a belt of reed and then, left alone, into a wet woodland and at last dry ground; on a floodplain, sedge marsh and wet meadow stand in bands by how often the ground floods. In Japan alder grows on excessively wet ground and takes over a mire as it dries, and the red pine, a tree of dry ridges and sandy ground, is not recorded there. The marsh itself, where a village kept one and why, is at Marshes and wetlands (shitchi).

The reed beds (yoshihara) were not left alone. Reed and grass for thatch were cut every year from ground kept for them, wetlands among it, and on Lake Biwa the reed was cut each winter and its stubble burned in spring. The cutting is what kept a reed bed open: it stops old reed building up into soil on which willow then grows.

  • A marsh grades from open water through reed to wet woodland.
    The textbook hydrosere runs from open water through the shallows and the reed marsh to alluvial forest, laid out along a gradient from the shore. Reed starts it by colonizing the shallows and building up its own litter until the ground dries out. In the British examples the forest at its end is a wet woodland of alder, birch and willow, where the reed thins out among saplings and trees. The dry ground past that woodland is this record's own last step.
  • Sedge marsh and wet meadow stand in bands by how often the ground floods.
    On the floodplains of Northeast China a sedge marsh stands along the streams and in the hollows, flooded each spring; a meadow marsh of another sedge on the middle terraces, flooded in part; and a wet meadow of a tall grass (Calamagrostis, called reed grass but not the reed) on the higher terraces, flooded only now and then.
  • Reed grows mainly in the lower reaches of Japan's rivers.
    Japan's national classification of river vegetation lists a reed community found mainly in the lower reaches, and beside it a community of wild rice and a bulrush-like sedge (ukiyagara) that grow singly or mixed.
  • Alder is a woodland of Japan's wet ground.
    The same classification lists an alder community established on excessively wet ground, and a study of a mire in Hokkaido names alder among the few trees that can grow tall in a mire. A review of wetland classification lists alder woodland among the vegetation of nutrient-rich mires in a Central European scheme, and the alder swamp among the wetland types named in Japan.
  • As the Kushiro Mire dried, alder took it over from the reed.
    The Kushiro Mire in Hokkaido has been drying since the rivers around it were improved. In 1977 reed covered about 87% of it and alder stood only at its upstream edge; alder covered about 35% by 1996 and about 45% by 2004.
  • What holds the alder off?
    Standing water, very probably. At Kushiro the reed held the points that were under water the whole time, and the study judged it highly likely that alder can hardly establish itself where the water stands 0.4 m (~1.3 ft) or more above the ground. Alder gains instead where the groundwater lies below the surface and rises and falls widely, around the rivers and the old channels, where the study expects it to go on spreading.
  • Did willow grow at a marsh's edge?
    Where the reed went uncut, very likely, though no Japanese source we read names willow as a step between reed and dry ground. Japan's river classification places a willow community (tachiyanagi) on the sand-and-mud deposits of the lower reaches. The British wet woodlands hold willow beside alder, and on Lake Biwa willow is what grows on a reed bed that is not cut. Such a wet wood of alder and willow at a marsh's edge is a carr.
  • Did pines or dry-ground brush grow at a marsh's edge?
    None of the pages we read puts either there; the sequence from reed to wet woodland above has no place for them. The red pine grows even on dry, poor soil along ridges, and, where it is rare in the far east of Russia, on rocky slopes, sandy soils and seashores. That it is absent from wet lowland is this record's reading of those habitats, not a finding of either page.
  • Reed and grass for thatch were a crop, cut every year from ground kept for it.
    The Japan Thatching Culture Association says that the plants used for thatch - susuki grass most of all, and reed among others - can be gathered year after year by cutting them every year, and that a thatch field was a place set aside for that. Grassland left alone turns into forest, and the thatch fields were kept from it by cutting every year and by burning. They lay in the hills around a settlement or in its wetlands, used in common by the settlement.
  • On Lake Biwa, reed was cut in winter and its stubble burned in spring.
    There the dead stems above ground have been cut in winter from old times and made into reed goods. After the cutting, sedge and other weeds from the same bed are laid on the stubble, left some days to dry, and burned. The tourism office of Shiga, the prefecture around the lake, puts it in one line: the reed was part of daily life from old times, and the beds were kept healthy by cutting in winter and burning in spring.
  • The cutting is what kept a reed bed open.
    Cutting stops old reed piling up into soil and trees such as willow from growing on it. Even now, a reed bed on dry ground that is not cut seems often to go to ruin.
  • Did a farming village cut its own marsh the same way?
    Very likely, though no page says so of a village marsh. The sources speak of thatch fields in general, kept in common in a settlement's wetlands among other places, and of Lake Biwa's reed beds; that a small village worked its own marsh as they were worked is this record's interpolation from them.
  • The one recorded use of a reed bed's sedge is as fuel for burning the stubble.
    On Lake Biwa the sedge of a reed bed was laid on the cut stubble and burned with it. No page we read says the sedge was cut for fodder; that use, though plausible, was searched for and not found.
  • A reservoir pond's reed is not recorded as cut.
    The cutting is recorded of Lake Biwa's reed beds and of thatch fields in wetlands. Every page we read on reservoir ponds names reed only as a plant growing there, and none carries the harvest to a pond's margin. The reed harvest is recorded of marshes and lakeside reed beds, not of the ponds at Reservoir ponds (tameike).
