Moving goods: carts, packhorses and river landings (kashi)

The tax rice of Japan and China went by water. In Edo-period Japan new river landings (kashi) were built to carry it to the cities of Edo and Osaka, and in Ming China it went up the Grand Canal to the capital. Overland a load went on a packhorse's back, roughly 300 lb to a horse, and Japan kept its carts to the towns and off its highways (Village lanes).

This setting differs. Its wagons and carts travel the roads between towns, and its canals are dug only by a domain with an inland river worth canalizing.

  • In the Edo period, new river landings were built to ship Japan's tax rice.
    When the Edo period began, the shogunate and the domains improved the rivers on a large scale, and new landings were set up under those lords to carry the tax rice and other goods from the provinces to Edo and Osaka.
  • In Edo-period Japan, a river landing grew into a commercial settlement of its own.
    Wholesale merchants and their storehouses gathered at the landings. What a town's landing held is at Wharves and landings: piers, quays and stepped landings (kashi, gangi).
  • In Edo-period Japan, a packhorse's load was set at thirty-six kan, about 135 kg (~298 lb).
    That was the rule for the da, the unit of what one horse carried on its back.
  • In the chuma hauling trade, one man ordinarily led three or four horses at once.
    Together they carried around 100 kan, about 375 kg (~827 lb), at a time.
  • In Edo, goods moved by hand cart, horse and ox cart.
    A Japanese government reference panel on Edo's streets, which cites no sources, names those three.
  • An 1815 report says the hand cart was first made in Edo after the city's great fire of 1657.
    By that report, from a ward headman of Edo, ox-cart carpenters there first made the hand cart (daihachiguruma) when the city was rebuilding, and it had since come into use in Kyoto, Osaka and the castle towns of other provinces.
  • Did Osaka use the Edo hand cart?
    The 1815 report has it in use in Osaka, but a Japanese encyclopedia on the same page says it was not used there, and that Osaka used the beka-guruma, with disc wheels and a plank bed.
  • In the late Edo period, the hand cart spread beyond the castle towns.
    The dictionary page that carries the 1815 report says so, without naming where.
  • The Edo hand cart's bed was about 8 by 2.5 ft.
    It was 8 shaku long and 2 shaku 5 sun wide, a sun being a tenth of a shaku.

China moved its tax rice by water too, but by canal rather than by river.

  • Ming China's tax rice went to the capital by the Grand Canal.
    The grain was a tax levied in the lower Yangtze provinces and shipped along the canal to the capital. In 1472 eight provinces were ordered to send four million shi of rice a year to Tongzhou, the canal's terminal near Beijing.
  • The Grand Canal replaced the sea route for Beijing's grain.
    As Beijing's demand for grain grew, carriage by sea became impractical, and in the end the canal carried it all.

The setting departs from Japan on the road and from China on the canal.

  • In this setting, wagons and carts use the roads between towns.
    The setting's notes have a caravan's wagons of tea crossing a bridge, a charcoal merchant robbed of a cartload on the road, and hay hauled in wagons behind teams of oxen. Carts on the highways are the setting's own departure from Edo-period Japan.
  • In this setting, a canal is a domain's work, never the Empire's.
    A domain has canals only where it has a sizable inland river worth canalizing, as the Lion Clan's canal from Toshi Ranbo to the Drowned Merchant River. Unlike Ming China's Grand Canal, the setting has no canal the Empire keeps.
  • In this setting, a coastal clan moves its goods by sea.
    A coastal clan such as the Crane ships its goods and carts them only the last few miles, so it has little need of canals.
