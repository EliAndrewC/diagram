The five sizes of settlement: hamlet, village, town, provincial city and capital

The setting these maps are drawn for divides each domain by office: into provinces, each under a governor who lives in its provincial city; provinces into counties, each under a magistrate who lives in its town; and counties into village districts, each under a peasant headman who lives in its main village, with small farming hamlets outlying. The setting's population table ranks its places in five sizes, from a hamlet of 50 to 100 inhabitants to a daimyo's capital of about 12,000, and each size keeps institutions the one below it lacks. Every figure is counted in households of about five.

These sizes are the setting's own canon, from its population tables and budget notes, not a historical finding. The history they simplify is that of Japan's villages in the Edo period, before 1868, where a small branch hamlet answered to a parent village for its taxes and its officials, though some branch hamlets kept officials of their own.

  • A hamlet is 10 to 20 households, 50 to 100 inhabitants.
    That is the setting's figure. Below ten households the place is an outlying farmstead or two; above twenty it is a small village.
  • A hamlet is a cluster of farmhouses, often around a common field.
    That is how the setting's budget notes describe it, and a village as several such clusters around larger fields.
  • A hamlet has no headman, no shrine and no tax-free plot of its own.
    It falls under the headman of the village district it belongs to, who lives in the main village and not in the hamlet. Each village district has one country monk, on one plot of tax-free land, serving in the village shrine. That is the setting's rule; some branch hamlets in history did keep officials of their own (below). Whether a hamlet in history kept its own shrine is at Village shrines: where they stand, and how big their grounds are, and where its dead lie at Where a village buries its dead: its own ground, the temple yard, the fields or the home plot.
  • A village is 40 to 100 households, 200 to 500 inhabitants.
    That is the setting's figure, around 350 inhabitants in the ordinary case.
  • A village has the district's headman, its shrine and its country monk's tax-free plot.
    These are what a hamlet lacks: the headman oversees the whole village district from the main village, and the district's one country monk serves in its village shrine, on a plot of tax-free land. The headman's household is slightly larger than the others.
  • The setting counts about six hamlets to every village.
    Its table gives a domain about 1,296 hamlets and 216 villages, with about 40% of the domain's inhabitants in a hamlet.
  • A village district takes its main village's name.
    The setting's place names follow one pattern across its levels, and one of its pairs is a village and its district, so the district shares its village's name. The district a hamlet belongs to is named for the village its lanes lead to.
  • Naming a district for its village is a deliberate departure from how real places were named.
    The setting's notes say so themselves: real capitals are not named for their countries, but keeping the names the same spares a reader many extra names.
  • A town is a county seat of about 1,200 inhabitants in about 238 households.
    That is the setting's figure for a town, from its population table.
  • A town's figure counts the farmers of the fields around it.
    The setting counts a town's inhabitants in and around it, so even the ordinary town is about two-thirds farmers: about 156 of its 238 households farm.
  • A town's figure does not count its county's villages.
    The setting's domain table lists towns, villages and hamlets as separate lines that sum to the domain, so a town's farmers are only those of its own fields.
  • A town's other 82 households are merchants, laborers, servants, burakumin and samurai.
    They are about 24 merchant, 29 laborer, 13 servant, 12 burakumin and 4 samurai households, by the setting's budget tables. Who they are is set out at Who lives in a town, and in how many houses?
  • A town is the smallest place with samurai in it.
    Villages and hamlets are peasant-only. The samurai of a county live in its town, where the state is present as the county magistrate in the magistrate's manor rather than as a peasant headman.
  • A samurai family's country estate is never part of a village or hamlet.
    A family may keep an estate near farmland, but it stands apart from the village or hamlet and never under its headman.
  • A provincial city is a governor's seat of 2,000 to 4,000 inhabitants inside its walls.
    That is the setting's figure for a provincial city, about 3,000 inhabitants in 600 households in the ordinary case, and it counts only those living within the walls.
  • A provincial city counts no farmers.
    The farmers who work a city's land live in the villages and hamlets around it and are counted there.
  • Who works the fields inside a provincial city's wall?
    On this project's reading, the city's own households, beside their other work and staying in their own castes: some townsmen of small castle towns farmed alongside their trade. More at Who works the fields inside a city's wall, and where do they live?
  • A provincial city's households are about 40% laborer, 20% servant, 25% merchant, 10% samurai and 5% burakumin.
    That is about 240 laborer, 120 servant, 150 merchant, 60 samurai and 30 burakumin households, by the setting's budget tables.
  • A provincial city's samurai are its provincial government.
    They are the governor, the six provincial ministers and their deputies, the clerks under them, and rank-and-file bushi on administrative and military duty. How many of a city's samurai live inside its wall is at How many of a city's samurai live inside its wall?
  • A small Imperial post in each provincial city answers to the Imperial Magistrate, not the governor.
    A handful of yoriki from other clans audit the tariff collection there, since a daimyo's own samurai cannot be trusted to audit the daimyo, and they report to the Imperial Magistrate at the domain capital.
  • A domain capital is the daimyo's castle-town seat of about 12,360 inhabitants.
    The setting's population table rounds it to about 12,400; its budget notes give 12,360, the settled 12,000 plus the samurai families who move to the capital from the domain's towns.
  • A household is about five, over two or three generations.
    That is the setting's median, and every population figure above is counted from it.
  • How large was a household in history?
    No page we could read gives a historical figure. The entry on the demographer Hayami Akira says he studied household size from the annual household listings and found a long-run trend toward smaller households, but gives no figure; the one study we found of a single Tohoku village's household size would not open.

History is less tidy than the setting's ladder, and on some points silent.

  • In the Edo period, a branch hamlet (edago) hung from a parent village (hongo).
    A settlement that split off from an older village was called its edago, and the village it came from the hongo; the parent usually gave the branch a share of its assessed yield. The branch was subordinate to the parent in its administration: its land register and tax notice stood in the parent's name, it could pay its land tax only through the parent, and it needed the parent's consent to petition its lord.
  • Why did a branch hamlet usually have no headman of its own?
    On this project's reading, because there was no separate tax unit for one to administer: the tax ran through the parent village. The English Wikipedia article on the village headman gives one to each village, but does not say whether it means a settlement or the administrative village that could take in several.
  • Some branch hamlets did keep their own officials.
    Some had a shoya and kumigashira of their own and were treated on a par with the parent village.
  • The setting's rule that a hamlet has no headman is a deliberate simplification of this history.
    The setting's notes state it twice, once in the hamlet's definition and once in a direct answer whose caveat is about how much ground one headman oversees, not about hamlets having one.
  • How common were clustered and scattered villages?
    No page we read gives a national count or share of any form of settlement. The encyclopedias call the clustered village the general form in Japan and the scattered village a regional one, and put no number on either (Clustered and scattered villages (shūson, sanson)).
  • A study of Kishu found villages of several settlements commoner in the mountains than on the plain.
    The study, in the Japanese Journal of Human Geography and read in its abstract, looked at the administrative village of Kishu, a district on Japan's Pacific coast: the unit placed jointly under the tax obligation as one village. It was mostly a single settlement on the plain and mostly several in the mountains, more of them the deeper the valley.
  • Did villages farm mostly wet rice?
    No source we read gives the share of paddy against dry field. The kokudaka, the assessed yield in rice, looks like such a figure and is not: it estimated the whole yield of the land, with other crops converted to their value in rice, so it covers dry fields too.
