Religion and the dead: the research behind the shrine, torii and funerary rules

The findings behind the shrines, torii, monasteries and burial grounds on these maps, the decisions they drove, and - where no generator draws the feature yet - the rules a map follows.

City temple size - the deliberate L7R liberty

Sources: religion-song-enwiki (the 1221 census: 400,000 monks and 61,000 nuns);1 the "90,000+ Edo temples" and the Shaolin count were found in no page read (ja.wikipedia's page on the terauke temple-registration system (寺請制度) gives only 6,000 Jōdo temples in 1696);3 daxiangguo-enwiki ("several thousand" monks)27

Historically the median temple was tiny: Edo Japan ran 90,000+ parish temples at 1-2 priests each, and small hereditary Chinese temples held a handful of monks; only the famous pilgrimage complexes ran large (the great Song public monasteries several hundred (unsourced), Daxiangguo and Shaolin 1,000+). L7R deliberately over-sizes - every city temple is a major complex in a way history would usually lack. A provincial city (~3,000 inhabitants) has ~15-30 monks per complex (a serious Ming urban monastery's scale); a domain capital (~12,000) has ~50+ monks per complex. That puts clergy at ~1-2% of city population against the historical ~0.46% (Song China: 461,000 clergy in 1221 against ~100 million inhabitants)33 - a deliberate 2-5x density, softened in-world by the temples serving the whole province's worship, not just the walled city.

The census behind that ratio: 1221, ~461,000 clergy (400,000 monks + 61,000 nuns).2

Many modest temples per walled city is the historical norm

Sources: kanazawa-teramachi, takada-teramachi, takayama-teramachi, jokamachi-wiki, pingyao-chenghuangmiao

What determines it in reality: patronage and institutional history. Each temple is a separate FOUNDATION with its own patron, sect and endowment, so temple count tracks how many distinct patrons a place has accumulated - which is why it does not scale smoothly with population.

What the research found. The expectation going in was that eight temples in a 2,600-person seat would need a disclosed liberty. The opposite is true. Japanese castle towns concentrated their temples into a designated teramachi5 at the outer rim of the jokamachi, where the spacious precincts formed part of the city defenses:97 Kanazawa ~704, Kyoto ~80, Takada ~25 relocated in one 1614 wave6, and - the size-matched anchor - Takayama, a SMALL castle town, "over 10 temples and shrines"8. On the China side the standard Ming county-seat kit carried the Confucian school-temple (wenmiao) and the City God temple (chenghuangmiao) as separate mandatory foundations before any Buddhist or Daoist house was counted (unsourced), and Pingyao's City God temple is itself a complex of THREE distinct temples on one site.10

The decision it drove. Eight modest precincts is inside the attested band, not a liberty. The existing "two major complexes" default is where the real liberty sits - see "City temple size", which records the deliberate 2-5x clergy over-density. We keep the two-complex default for non-Fox cities anyway, because it is a game-legibility choice (a few named, visitable temples beat a dozen anonymous ones), not a historical claim.

What was NOT relied on. No per-county-seat temple census was found in a Ming-Qing gazetteer study; searches returned categorization practice (Three Officials temples filed under "Altars and Shrines" rather than "Buddho-Daoist Temples") but no counts. The band therefore rests on the Japanese teramachi figures, with China supplying the mandatory-foundations STRUCTURE rather than a number.

Temples as economic institutions with hereditary householder clergy

Sources: tontine-monastery-lending, pawnbroking-history, kannushi-wiki, tricycle-temple-wives

What determines it in reality: endowment decides the TRADES (a monastery with land, dependents and tax exemption must deploy its capital), and the ordination rule decides the HOUSING (celibacy means cloister; hereditary ordination means householders).

The economy. Chinese Buddhist monasteries were major economic actors:14 their land and dependent labor funded mills, oil presses and other enterprises11; the Inexhaustible Treasuries were interest-earning endowments,12 the Chang'an one liquidated by Xuanzong in 713 as fraudulent banking;13 pawnbroking before the Tang was limited to Buddhist monasteries15, and by the Song lay investors partnered with monasteries specifically to open pawnshops inside monastic tax exemption (a 1202 document records ten men forming a ju to fund one).34 The GM's campaign notes' Fox economy - the Seven Temples holding forest usufruct, moneylending with Fukurokujin and Ebisu (the setting's Fortunes of wisdom and mercy, and of honest work), wedding loans with Benten (the Fortune of romantic love) - is a direct match, down to which institution lends.

The decision it drove. The eight precincts distribute by trade across the quarters rather than gathering into one belt, because each is an economic house sited where its business is. The Japanese rim-belt teramachi is the considered alternative and is deliberately NOT taken: it is a castle-town DEFENSIVE arrangement, and these temples are commercial institutions first. A trade-sited precinct is sometimes the only temple in its quarter, so the rule that a temple neighborhood carries shrines applies per CLUSTER and a lone precinct is exempt - the same treatment Tango's converted-estate Bishamon already gets.

The housing inversion. China first: Zhengyi Daoist priests are almost always married - marriage is required for the highest rank - and they RESIDE IN HOUSEHOLDS rather than a monastery, transmitting ordination hereditarily.16 Japan corroborates twice: Shinto priests marry and their children inherit,17 some shrine priesthoods held in one family for as many as 100 generations (unsourced) (the hereditary office was abolished in 1871 but persists by preference);18 and Jodo Shinshu was the one tradition the Tokugawa exempted so a temple head could marry and keep priestly status,35 producing the bomori19 and eldest-son succession.20 The GM's campaign notes' Fox rule - only the Three Bonds celibate, every other position hereditary, "the temple's revenue sources are effectively family businesses passed down along bloodlines" - is essentially that arrangement with a small celibate administrative core. So a Fox precinct is a small walled workplace ringed by its temple families. Departure, disclosed: the drawn monk house stays deliberately identical to a laborer house - the eye should not be able to pick temple families out of the fabric, because in reality it could not.

Who lives inside a city temple's walls, and who lives outside them?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-24; the GM's campaign notes for the vows and the initiate tier; the householder-clergy finding recorded above

The decision. The GM ruled on 2026-07-24 that a city temple draws exactly like the governor's yamen: a walled compound with a deliberately blank court, because the interior is the subject of its own building plan when the players visit it. On the city sheet the big rectangle IS the temple. This superseded an earlier rule that drew no clergy housing at all.

Who is implied inside. Celibate monks, resident initiates and a few lay servants - some 25 to 45 of them in a provincial-city complex - live in dormitories and cloisters implied within that wall. They are implied population, counted on top of the dwellings the map draws rather than housed in the street fabric, and they sit outside the lay caste bands, because clergy are not a lay caste.

Who lives out. Adepts may marry and raise children, so a share of each complex's 15 to 30 monks are householders whose families keep two or three ordinary homes in the temple's own neighborhood. Departure, disclosed: those homes are drawn deliberately identical to a laborer's house, with no label and no glyph of their own - the eye should not be able to pick the temple families out of the fabric, because in reality it could not. Each complex also carries about twice its monk count in initiates ("many are called, few are chosen"), of whom only a small fraction sleep inside: roughly 5 to 10 at provincial-city scale, 10 to 20 at a domain capital. The rest are laborers, carters, servants and shopkeepers engaged in religious study while they work ordinary jobs, and on the map they are ordinary houses of their own caste, never buildings of their own.

The rule the map follows: on a city or capital sheet (3 ft per pixel) a major temple is a walled compound of wall, gate and empty court. Two or three clergy homes stand within about 500 ft of each major complex , and none stands stranded away from every temple; a precinct whose clergy are hereditary householders carries five to nine instead, ringing its wall. Beyond those, at most two exception features stand apart - a retired abbot's hermitage, or a small daughter chapel with two to four monks of its own - drawn precisely because they are exceptions.

What kind of shops stand at a temple gate?

Sources: the GM's correction of 2026-07-20; Chinese temple accounts and the Japanese amulet custom, neither traced to a readable page; this project's own anchor for the lay-house band

The correction that shaped it. Amulets, charms, talismans, incense and lamp oil were temple-direct sales and a major revenue stream: Chinese temple accounts book an annual oil-lamp and incense income, and a Japanese omamori or ofuda is bestowed by the temple for an offering rather than wholesaled to a shop. (Both of those rest on general reading; no source is cited.) So there is no third-party religious-goods shop competing at the temple's own gate, and none is drawn - the GM's correction of 2026-07-20, and the easiest thing here to get wrong.

What did cluster at the gate. Food and drink - teahouses, eateries, sweet-sellers - is the main draw and the bulk of the neighborhood's commerce, because worshippers and pilgrims need feeding and resting and the temple does not do that itself. Then souvenirs and regional specialty goods (meibutsu): local crafts, sweets and keepsakes, secular goods branded by the place and quite distinct from the sacred objects the temple keeps to itself. Then the funeral trades, which cluster by the graveyard rather than by the hall - coffin-makers, grave-marker cutters, and the China-first detail, the makers of spirit money and paper effigies - serving the parish death economy the temple presides over but does not manufacture. Then the service trades: fortune-tellers and diviners at the gate, and the retained temple artisans (carpenters, gilders, painters) who work for the temple on its endless repair cycle and live in the district in workshops that are also dwellings, not storefronts.

A map drawing convention. At this scale none of those has a glyph of its own - a teahouse and an eatery are both the generic shop - so the story is told by PLACEMENT (a shop beside the temple reads as a teahouse) and never by the icon. Nothing on the map tries to be an amulet shop; everything sacred is inside the compound.

The rule the map follows: on a city sheet, size the lay service band at about 40 to 80 houses around the two complexes of a provincial city (30 to 60 resident monks between them), which is roughly one to two lay service households per resident monk for a city whose worship traffic is regional. That band is ordinary zoning inside the existing caste bands, not extra population. A pilgrimage destination inverts the ratio to ten lay households or more per monk - the gate-town pattern - and that is what an especially pious city looks like on the ground. No source gives a ratio: the band is this project's own anchor, a guess calibrated on the drawn cities.

Does the temple forecourt do anything the map cannot show?

Sources: the temple-market pattern named in this project's own doctrine, traced to no readable page

On fair days the great temple's forecourt hosted the periodic market, the temple renting out the stall space. (This rests on general reading; no source is cited.) It is a good beat for a game - the temple district BECOMES the market for a day - and it is invisible on a static map, which is the reason to write it down where the map's reader can find it.

The rule the map follows: at town and city scale the forecourt inside a temple's gate is drawn as open ground and carries no market furniture. The fair-day market is a use of ground the map deliberately leaves clear, not a feature the map draws.

The graveyard ceiling does not scale with temple count

Sources: reasoning from this project's own funerary doctrine and the Fox precincts' canon - a decision to REJECT a change

What determines it in reality: parish structure and burial LAND, not the number of foundations. A burial ground needs suitable ground outside the built area and serves a catchment; several foundations routinely shared one.

What was reasoned. The tempting change is to scale the 2-4 ceiling with the eight precincts. That is wrong twice over: the Fox precincts are economic institutions holding forest usufruct, not eight parishes, so they have no separate congregations of dead; and the existing city funerary doctrine already sites burial grounds by ground suitability (off the fields, downstream, across the water where the bank allows), which constrains LAND rather than counting temples. Eight small precincts sharing three burial grounds is the ordinary arrangement.

The decision it drove. The ceiling stands unchanged. This is a case where the research led to REJECTING a change.

Torii are VOTIVE DONATIONS - the count records patronage

Sources: fushimi-inari-senbon (~10,000 on the mountain36, ~800 in the Senbon Torii (unsourced), donated from Edo28 into Meiji (unsourced)). The 1 / 2-3 / dozens distribution is assembled from normative statements - no census exists

What the research found: torii were VOTIVE DONATIONS, not shrine construction - a shrine's count records its accumulated patronage21 (ujiko subscriptions, grateful merchants, the local lord), a culture that boomed in the Edo period. The historical distribution: 1 torii is the overwhelming mode (~90%+ of true village shrines); 0 is the norm only BELOW the shrine tier (hokora/wayside god-shelves, which vastly outnumbered real shrines - a proper shrine with none was rare enough that each had a story); 2-3 is the ranked-shrine tier (the numbered ichi/ni/san-no-torii on long approaches, 3 the conventional ceiling); and the dozens-plus tail is specifically the Inari donation-row cult (Edo-onward, a handful to a few dozen at a modest rural Inari, hundreds only at famous cult sites, ~10,000 at Fushimi). No scholarly per-shrine census exists - the shape is assembled from normative statements plus attested examples.

A rule that was tried and retired. An earlier rule sized a torii avenue to the space in front of it, on the reasoning that a temple with a long approach would fill that approach. It was removed on 2026-07-24 and should not be re-added: the count is PATRONAGE rather than street room, and sizing by space banned a count of three, which is exactly what a town rolls most of the time. What the approach space does govern is the avenue's LINE, which the author of a map draws to fit the ground it crosses.

Torii spacing - two regimes and nothing in between

Sources: the ~400 m run length, Kasuga's 1.3 km (1.2 km elsewhere) and Meiji Jingu's ~10-minute walk are guesses - no readable page supports them; meiji-jingu-jawiki counts 8 torii, so "three" there is the ranked set (一・二・三の鳥居), not the total29

  • How far apart, and how far from the hall - the spacing research (GM asked 2026-07-25). What the research found: real torii spacing has exactly two regimes and nothing in between. (1) Donation rows: Fushimi Inari's Senbon Torii section runs ~800 gates (unsourced) along ~400 m in two parallel rows - a pitch on the order of 0.5-1 m - and where the row loosens further up the mountain the gates are described as "at times tightly packed and at times irregularly spaced and several yards apart", i.e. ~3-9 m at the loose end. (2) Ranked gates (ichi- / ni- / san-no-torii) are landmarks strung along a whole approach, not a corridor: Nagao Shrine's ichi->ni is 200 m, Meiji Jingu's three span a ~10-minute walk (~250 m apart),22 and Kasuga Taisha's ichi-no-torii stands 1.3 km from the shrine. At 1-3 ft/px a ranked pair is off the map at every settlement scale. The decision: since Rokugan's 1/3/7 set is neither regime, the pitch is an explicit house rule (~20 ft, cap 32 ft) rather than a copied fact - a deliberate invention, with no source behind it. The approach GAP, by contrast, is grounded: a village shrine's arch sits ~20 ft off its hall (a small keidai with the hall right there), and a great precinct may legitimately keep a courtyard between its OUTERMOST arch and its main hall, comfortably inside the norm for a proper precinct (a Nara-period provincial temple's kokubunji precinct ran ~218 m per side). Where the INNERMOST arch stands is a separate question, and the GM settled it below.

A donation row is a designated-site special case (the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25). The Fushimi-style row - dozens to thousands of arches, given one at a time and standing almost shoulder to shoulder - is not how an ordinary approach in this setting works. It belongs to a handful of named great sites: Shinden Togashi, the Temple of Amaterasu, the Ki Rin Shrine and others of that rank. Everywhere else the 1 / 3 / 7 set is the whole vocabulary. A map of one of those sites is therefore drawn as an explicit outlier, exempt from the pitch cap; and drawing its row honestly would want the one-arch-span FLOOR relaxed as well, because a real donation row is packed tighter than a 16 ft glyph allows at these scales. That relaxation has not been taken, and the reason it would be needed is recorded here so the next such map does not read the floor as a finding about how gates were spaced.

And the avenue STARTS at its hall (the GM's ruling of 2026-07-27): "the distance from the front of the temple should be the same as the distance between each torii arch." Fixing the stride had left the other half of the same defect standing - an avenue can be perfectly spaced at 20 ft and still stand yards away from the temple it serves. One city temple's approach stood 139 ft off its hall, three arches up a street on the far side of a flophouse; another city's began 120 ft south of its temple, with the caption and two rows of houses in between; the eight precincts of a third ran 44 to 93 ft. None of them read as that temple's approach - they read as red marks near some other building. So the threshold is owned alongside the count and the stride: the whole run is moved bodily, keeping its direction, its curve and its stride and changing only its distance, until the innermost arch stands one pitch off the nearest FACE of the hall (measured to the footprint rather than to the center, so a run drawn off to one side slides onto the flank it stands off rather than diagonally into the middle of the building). The rule is an upper bound rather than an equality, deliberately: a village path seats its arches at 0.6 to 0.9 of its 30 ft stride, and the village rule governs that end, so the two meet without forcing cosmetic change on maps the GM has already approved.

And a caption never stands in the approach (the same conversation: an arch must "never be covered by the 'temple of X' label"). A hall's own caption and its approach both want the ground at the hall's face, which is where the collision came from. The caption tries three baselines in a STRICT ORDER - the side the map's author asked for, that same side pushed past the far end of the avenue, then the opposite side - and takes the first that touches no arch. Stepping PAST the avenue has to exist because a parish graveyard may sit deliberately at the back of a monastery, so flipping the caption off the approach would only bury it in the cemetery. An order rather than a score, for a drawing-order reason: a hall goes down early, so a scorer can see almost nothing at that point - a fire tower, the clergy houses and the graveyard's neighbors are all drawn later - and scoring blind put one caption on a fire tower that did not exist yet. The author of the map knows what is on each side; a scorer at that moment does not.

Where does a village put its shrine, and how big is it?

Sources: the Chinese earth-god shrine's siting, traced to no readable page; the GM's ruling of 2026-07-21 on the footprint band; the water-mouth grove, recorded in the vegetation research

What the research found. A Chinese village's tutelary earth-god shrine (tudi miao, 土地庙) stood near the entrance, in the most strategic feng shui position. (This rests on general reading; no source is cited.) In that reading the entrance is the shuikou, the water mouth: the feng-shui-critical point where the water leaves and the connector track sets off for the road, and where the water-mouth grove already grows. The Japanese surface of the same idea is the tutelary chinju standing in its grove.

The decision. The village's one shrine nestles there inside its sacred wood, fronted by an arch facing the village, and it is drawn SMALL: a modest hall, not a temple. It carries no label - a vermilion hall with an arch in front of it reads as a shrine on sight, and the marker rows beside it read as a graveyard, so neither is labeled. A village site is never called a temple; where the word is used of one, it means a shrine.

The rule the map follows: on a village sheet (2 ft per pixel) the ordinary earth-god or water-mouth hall is about 60 by 48 ft, some 275 m² , a touch larger than a plain farmhouse; a showcase primary hall runs to about 490 m² , and 600 m² is the ceiling, because the monastery and temple tier of 1,000 m² and up belongs to towns and cities. The hall stands BESIDE a lane, never on one, since a building sits off the way; the arch is the deliberate exception, because a gateway straddles the approach and the way runs under it. The wellhead a shrine needs stands beside it, never on the hall and never under an arch. Hall and arches sit in a cleared opening in the grove rather than under the canopy, which is why they are seated before the grove is planted.

Is the ground around a shrine or a grave swept clear of scrub?

Sources: the swept forecourt, the tended approach and the grave-tending custom, none of them traced to a readable page; the GM's question of 2026-07-19

What the research found. The ground immediately around a sacred or a funerary feature was kept clear of wild scrub while the waste around it stayed scrubby - a tended verge, not a big bald radius. A shrine's keidai, the forecourt, was a swept raked-gravel or flagged surface, purity being the point, and in the Chinese case a village earth-god shrine or an ancestral hall fronted a hard swept plaza used for grain drying, market and opera. The approach path and the ground under each arch were kept clear and maintained the length of the sando. Graves are the partial case: they sat on marginal scrubby back-slope or waste ground, but grave tending - Chinese tomb-sweeping at Qingming, Japanese haka-mairi - clears the weeds off the mound and a modest collar, while the waste even a short way out stays scrubby. So: clear AT the graves, wild BETWEEN them. (All four of those rest on general reading; no source is cited.)

The one nuance that shaped the design. A Japanese shrine's setting is a deliberately PRESERVED grove (chinju no mori) - a swept precinct set inside kept trees, the opposite of cleared ground. So the swept collar holds off only the loose scrub and reed scatter. It never holds off the grove, which is a drawn feature in its own right, and it never governs where a building may stand: a shrine's back grove stands untouched right up to the swept precinct, exactly as in reality.

The rule the map follows: on a village sheet (2 ft per pixel) a small swept collar reaches about 115 ft beyond the hall , about 60 ft around each arch and its footing, and about 60 ft around a grave plot . It is deliberately modest - "a little bit of clearance" - and the scrub commons is unchanged a stride out. Loose scatter (the scrub of the commons, the reeds of a marsh) keeps off that ground; the grove, the buildings and every placement rule ignore it.

Why does that swept ground have a ragged edge?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-23; this project's own measurement of the drawn pool

The ruling. The collar had been the feature's own footprint grown outward as a rectangle, and because the scatter skipped it, that rectangle was visible: a crisp bare box in the scrub around every shrine and every graveyard. The GM ruled on 2026-07-23 that "such clearage rarely conforms to precise geometric shapes". The why: swept ground is PRODUCED by tending - brooms, feet, the traffic of the approach - radiating outward from the feature, and its edge sits wherever the tending peters out into the scrub that season; nobody surveys it. Ruled edges belong to what is surveyed or built - a wall, a paddy bund, a house plot - which is the same reasoning that gives the perimeter dike and the common burial ground their shapes.

Bays inward, and no lobes outward. The irregular edge is cut INTO the padded rectangle: shallow bays, deeper along the sides than at the corners. Outward lobes were tried and rejected, and both reasons are worth keeping. A collar is a maintenance CLAIM, so making it irregular should mean the sweeping falls SHORT of the surveyed ideal; it should never annex ground the tending does not reach. And a lobe can newly cover scrub that legitimately predates the clearing - the first lobed draft did exactly that on one village map, where the graveyard's lobe reached the toe marsh, which had been scattered several stages earlier. Bays-only makes every collar a subset of the rectangle the ground already had, so the change moved nothing else on any map.

The rule the map follows: at every tier the swept ground is DRAWN as an irregular patch bitten out of the collar, never as a rectangle, and the same feature always draws the same patch. The ground it RESERVES - what placement and clearance rules test against - stays the plain rectangle, so the shape is a drawing decision and never a placement one.

Burial-ground shape - Japan organic, China surveyed

Sources: louzeyuan-zhwiki (1104,30 high barren ground, fenced, numbered plots, continued through Ming), toribeno-jawiki (no defined boundary),31 adashino-jawiki (an exposure-burial (風葬) ground, later cremation; Rendaino (蓮台野) has no article;32 the Osaka seven graveyards (大坂七墓) page is gone)

What the research found - the two reference cultures split. Japan (the organic pole): commoner burial grounds were unplotted and terrain-following everywhere - Kyoto's great burial fields (Toribeno, Adashino, Rendaino) were open hillside/riverbank grounds shaped purely by terrain; a village kept an unsurveyed sanmai on waste land at its edge; Edo-period urban burial fragmented into many small temple graveyards packed incrementally (Osaka's excavated "seven graveyards" show crowded overlapping pits, not plots). Premodern Japan offers essentially NO surveyed rectangular commoner cemetery. China (the split case): ordinary family graves were scattered organically over unfarmable hillsides by feng shui - no communal commoner cemetery at all - BUT the institutional "common burial ground" for the poor and unclaimed dead genuinely WAS a surveyed rectangle: the Song state pauper cemeteries (louzeyuan 漏泽园, proposed by Cai Jing in 1104 - 建議, a proposal, not an imperial edict37 - and continued as Ming yizhong 义冢 charity graveyards)26 were fenced compounds (圍欄, a fence, not a wall) on high barren ground23 with plots in ordered rows (unsourced), numbered against the Thousand Character Classic,24 a drainage ditch every three rows (unsourced), and a resident caretaker monk.25

How much ground does a village burial ground need, and whose dead lie in it?

Sources: the Edo hamlet burial patches, the parish registration and the two-grave regions, none of them traced to a readable page; a pre-mechanization farm survey of China for the Chinese contrast, of which no publicly readable copy carries the passage; an excavated commoner ground for the cross-check

What the research found. Edo Japan actually buried PER SETTLEMENT: burial patches at the fringe of even twelve- to fifteen-household hamlets (shuraku bochi, the grandfathered minashi bochi of today) were ubiquitous, while the RITUAL center was central - temple registration, the funeral and the standing stone at the main village's temple. The two-grave regions (ryobosei) formalized exactly that split: the corpse in the local burial patch, the venerated stone at the center. Ming and Qing China is the deliberate contrast - family graves sited by feng shui and scattered through the farmland, with communal ground only as pauper charity; Buck's pre-mechanization farm survey of 1929-33 put some 2% of ALL farm area under graves. (Each of those rests on general reading; no source is cited for any of them.) So the single discrete communal ground these maps draw is the Japanese surface convention, which is the setting's China-geography, Japan-surface split working as intended.

Two things make one central ground MORE defensible here than in historical Japan. The setting cremates and then inters, and an urn is carried to the district ground in a way a corpse procession is not. And a hamlet's own patch would be tiny - roughly 750 to 2,450 sq ft for full-body burial, less for urns, which is a blob thirty to fifty ft across on a village sheet - legitimately below the resolution of these maps, so a hamlet that draws nothing stays honest even where local patches exist in the fiction.

The decision. The drawn village ground is the marked, ritually active DISTRICT ground, and it is sized to the district rather than to the village that hosts it: about 800 inhabitants, at roughly 25 to 30 deaths per 1,000 a year, with a plot reused after about 30 years, gives some 600 to 720 active urn plots; at 10 to 20 sq ft each that is about 0.15 to 0.30 acre. The cross-check is an excavated commoner ground, which came out at about 15 to 17 sq ft per body including its paths. Sizing the same ground to the village's own roughly 350 inhabitants would give about half of that, and it is the district the ground serves.

The rule the map follows: a village draws ONE burial ground, between about 0.12 and 0.38 acre (the district band with glyph rounding allowed both ways), and a ground sized only to the village itself is too small. A hamlet draws none: its dead go to the village district's ground, just as it has no shrine and no headman. The ladder stays monotone with the population served - the district's ~800 sits below a town's ~1,200, and 0.15 to 0.30 acre nests under a town's 0.25 to 0.75.

Does a graveyard stand beside its temple or its shrine?

Sources: the GM's campaign notes for the merged orders and the village shrine's resident brother; the parish-temple registration, traced to no readable page

Why the graveyard is the hall's own yard. The state merged the Buddhist strand with Fortune worship, so ANY temple may host a graveyard, and a graveyard is a parish ground that sits inside a temple precinct. A village SHRINE hosts one too, which is the churchyard rule and the thing most likely to look wrong at first glance: real-world Shinto shuns death pollution (kegare), but this setting's country shrines are officially of the Buddhist-analog order even while they house Fortune deities, and the resident brother performs the funerary rites. The shrine IS the death-handling institution - the parish-temple logic, in which a household is registered to the temple that buries it (this rests on general reading; no source is cited) - so its burial ground is its churchyard rather than a plot held away. That unifies burial across the tiers: the graves lie by the monk-run building everywhere, the shrine in a village and the monastery or temple in a town or a city.

The exceptions, and what they are worth. A HILLTOP shrine keeps no graves: graves do not climb the sacred hill, and a prominent hill shrine is not the humble earth-god monk's funerary base. That is a judgment about the setting, not a finding: no source was found either way. A ground may also be marked as belonging to no parish - a village-style burial ground not attached to a hall at all, which is how a farming quarter inside a city wall gets a ground of its own. And divided remains (a portion interred at a great head temple, the rest in the home village) are real and simply not drawn, because nothing of the practice is visible in this view.

The rule the map follows: in a VILLAGE the burial ground lies in the shrine's own yard, within about 500 ft of the hall , unless the shrine stands on a hill, in which case the village's ground lies elsewhere. In a TOWN or CITY a graveyard lies in its monastery's or temple's precinct - about 690 ft of the hall on a city sheet, the same drawn reach being about 230 ft on a town sheet - and at city scale that binds only a ground INSIDE the walls, since an extramural common ground belongs to the whole city. The hall and its arches themselves stay clear: the graves fill the yard AROUND them and never lie on them. Any WALLED settlement keeps a ground inside AND outside its walls, and the exterior common ground is at least 1.3 times the largest interior one, because there is room beyond the wall while the parish plots inside are hemmed in; an unwalled town's grounds are all parish grounds. A town, having no pauper ossuary to take the poor, carries proportionally more in its common ground than a city does and draws it larger.

How far from water does a burial ground lie?

Sources: this project's own funerary doctrine - death pollution and flooding - with no measured set-back found in any source

A burial ground by big water floods out, so the bigger the watercourse, the further back the dead must lie; and because even narrow open water floods a grave, the ladder has a solid floor rather than starting at nothing. A flooded paddy counts as open water - rice grows submerged, and a grave against a field edge is a real flood hazard - while a thin irrigation channel does not. Two refinements earn their place. The CREMATION ground may sit nearer the water than the graves, because fire and its washing rites want water at hand and a fire site is not flood-sensitive, so the burial ground naturally lands beyond the crematory as seen from the water. And a ground INSIDE the wall is exempt from the moat term, the moat being outside and the rampart between; streams and ponds still count on either side of a wall. A walled crypt precinct is exempt entirely - a crypt compound is not a flood-sensitive open plot. No source was found giving a distance: the ladder is reasoned from the hazard, and its numbers are this project's own.

The rule the map follows: the set-back scales with the watercourse and never falls below its floor. On a city sheet (3 ft per pixel) that floor is about 225 ft from a stream, rising to about 390 ft from the moat, which is the heaviest water a map carries at about 78 ft wide, and capped at about 420 ft for a river or a canal; a flooded field edge takes about 150 ft of its own. The same drawn bands read about two thirds of those figures on a village sheet at 2 ft per pixel . A cremation ground keeps a fixed modest margin of about 90 ft instead and is exempt from the field-edge margin altogether.

Where do the crematory, the ossuary and the clan mausoleum stand?

Sources: the GM's campaign notes for the cremate-then-inter canon and the caste standing of a religious order; the pollution and fire reasoning behind a marginal crematory, traced to no readable page

The setting always cremates and then inters the bones, so a graveyard here is a compact field of stone markers rather than a body-burial sprawl. Smoke, fire risk and pollution put the crematory OUTSIDE the walls, at a town's edge and clear of the dwellings, and set back from the trunk road: the pyre is marginal, polluting land reached by a minor funeral path, not high-street frontage (this rests on general reading; no source is cited). It also never stands between a temple and the road, because nobody should walk past the pyre to reach worship - behind or beside the hall, never on its ceremonial approach. And it adjoins an EXTRAMURAL burial ground, because the body is burned and its bones interred next door: the two are one funerary complex, and a graveyard inside the walls does not answer for it. Monks officiate with burakumin assistants, a religious order standing outside the caste system, so handling the dead does not pollute its caste.

Note the split that is easy to miss. A temple's own graveyard sits beside the temple wherever the temple is, and may perfectly well face the road; it is the CREMATORY that is the marginal exception, kept off the road and off the hall's approach. Minor town streets trigger neither rule - only the trunk road does.

The other two. The pauper OSSUARY is a low earthen mound with one weathered stupa holding the communal bones of the poor and the unconnected dead, and it stands outside the walls by the crematory; it is a city feature, which is why a town's common ground has to absorb the same dead itself. The clan MAUSOLEUM is the opposite in every way: the ruling clan's ancestral crypts in a walled precinct with a stone hall and memorial stupas, INSIDE the walls by the samurai and government quarter, a prestige monument near the seat of power, since ancestor veneration is central to samurai identity. City only.

The rule the map follows: a TOWN draws a cremation ground at its edge, clear of the dwellings; a CITY draws the cremation ground and the pauper ossuary outside the walls, beyond a gate, and the clan mausoleum inside them by the samurai quarter. The crematory stands at least about 390 ft from the trunk road and, where a monastery or temple is within about 1,200 ft of it , at least as far from that road as the hall is, less a tolerance of about 120 ft for "beside it, a little away" . Its burial ground is extramural and within about 210 ft of it edge to edge . Where a wall of a walled compound (a mausoleum, a manor, a merchant estate) abuts a neighborhood fence, the compound YIELDS that side: the fence is drawn over the coincident wall and is that side of the precinct, the compound's own wall running underneath - a map drawing convention that keeps two clashing parallel walls off the sheet.

How large are the gates, walls and funerary features drawn?

Sources: the Edo warrior-residence gate schedules, a Fukui survey of cremation grounds, the Musashi provincial gazetteer on the Yoyogi crematory, an excavation study of Edo commoner cemeteries (1992) and a study of the Chinese charity graveyard - none of them yet traced to a readable page; the GM's ruling of 2026-07-19

Why this matters at all. A walled compound - a manor or estate, the governor's yamen, a merchant estate, the mausoleum - draws its wall, its gate and a deliberately blank court, because the interior is the subject of its own building plan. The wall and the gate ARE the feature, so they have to be honest, and a funerary feature drawn to the wrong size reads as a different kind of place.

The anchors. A samurai residence gate opens about 9 to 12 ft, and a grand yamen gatehouse about 18 to 24 ft; a gate gap drawn as a fixed distance on the sheet rather than as a real width once opened 204 ft at city scale , which is most of a wall missing. Compound walls run about 1.5 to 2 ft thick and gate posts about 2 ft square. A cremation ground's cleared core runs 30 to 80 ft at village and town scale and about 80 to 160 ft at city scale; even the crematory serving metropolitan Edo at Yoyogi was about 900 tsubo, some 180 ft square, with a pyre platform of about 15 by 10 ft. A pauper bone mound runs 10 to 30 ft across; Kyoto's monumental ear mound has a base of about 50 ft, and cremated bone takes almost no volume, so even the hundred thousand and more dead of the great Edo execution ground never made a great mound. Burial grounds size to the population served at about 10 to 20 sq ft per packed urn grave, with about one generation of active plots before reuse: a village district 0.15 to 0.30 acre, a town 0.25 to 0.75, a city 0.75 to 2 split across its yards. Chinese full-body burial runs four to six times that, and this setting follows the Japanese cremate-then-inter mode. (Every figure in this paragraph rests on a work the record has not yet read; none is cited.)

A license that was retired, and must not come back. The bone mound was once drawn at about 40 ft, with a visibility floor under it that rendered 54 real ft at city scale - a feature inflated until it was easy to see. That license was retired on 2026-07-21 and the band tightened. A to-scale map does not enlarge a feature for legibility; where a glyph would otherwise vanish, the help is the thinnest mark the sheet can carry, disclosed as the drawing convention it is.

The rule the map follows: a compound's gate opening is drawn at its real width for the building it serves, about 9 to 12 ft for a samurai residence and 18 to 24 ft for a yamen gatehouse. A compound wall is drawn at its true thickness, or at the thinnest line the sheet can carry where that is wider , and a gate post at about 2 ft square under the same kind of floor - map drawing conventions, so that a wall one and a half ft thick still reads at 3 ft per pixel. A cremation ground's cleared core is 30 to 80 ft at village and town scale and 80 to 160 ft at city scale, with a pyre platform of about 15 by 10 ft; a pauper bone mound is drawn at about 22 ft, with a small floor under it so that it never vanishes , and anything outside 8 to 32 ft is wrong. Burial grounds are sized to the population they serve, and the ladder stays monotone: a village district ground below a town's, a town's below a city's.

What religious and funerary features does each size of settlement carry?

Sources: the GM's tier rule and the clan patron Fortunes, from the campaign notes; the wayside shrine at a threshold, traced to no readable page; the temple counts recorded above

Type and rank track each other, and the two faiths divide the work. A HAMLET has no religious building of its own and shares its village district's, exactly as it has no headman. A VILLAGE has a Shinto shrine, tended by the one tax-free religious figure of the district, whose dwelling may double as the shrine itself. A TOWN has monasteries, each dedicated to a patron Fortune of the clan whose holdings include it, with an arch in front. A CITY has full temples, its temple neighborhood dotted with small wayside shrines. An arch fronts any shrine, monastery or temple that a way runs straight up to.

Where the small shrines go. The floor of three wayside shrines near a cluster of temples is a minimum, not a pattern to repeat: additional ones placed at meaningful spots elsewhere read truer than one more shrine crowded by the halls. Historically a wayside shrine stood exactly at a threshold - a landing, a crossing, a gate, a well (this rests on general reading; no source is cited) - so a shrine at the wharf, fittingly to the Fortune of fishermen and honest commerce, or at a bridge or a gate, is placed where the city's life turns.

The rule the map follows: a HAMLET draws no shrine, no arch and no burial ground. A VILLAGE draws one shrine with its arches and one burial ground in the shrine's yard. A TOWN draws its monasteries - two by default, one for each patron Fortune of the clan that holds it - with one or two graveyards in their precincts, a graveyard inside and outside the walls if it is walled, and a cremation ground at its edge. A CITY draws its temples - two major complexes by default, more only under a declared exception - with two to four graveyards and one in every temple precinct that has not opted out, at least three small wayside shrines by a cluster of two or more temples (a lone precinct among houses needs none), the clan mausoleum inside the walls, and the cremation ground with the pauper ossuary outside them, an extramural funerary complex beyond a gate.
