Religion and the dead: the research behind the shrine, torii and funerary rules

The findings behind the shrines, torii, monasteries and burial grounds on these maps, the decisions they drove, and - where no generator draws the feature yet - the rules a map follows.

City temple size - the setting's deliberate liberty

Sources: religion-song-enwiki (the 1221 census: 400,000 monks and 61,000 nuns);1 the Edo temple count and the Shaolin count each say at their own sentence that no page read carries them (ja.wikipedia's page on the terauke temple-registration system (寺請制度) gives only 6,000 Jōdo temples in 1696);3 daxiangguo-enwiki ("several thousand" monks).27

Historically the median temple was tiny: Edo Japan ran something on the order of 90,000 parish temples at one or two priests each, a figure on no page read,107 and small hereditary Chinese temples held a handful of monks; only the famous pilgrimage complexes ran large (the great Song public monasteries several hundred,101 Daxiangguo and Shaolin held a thousand or more, on no page read either108).136 The setting deliberately over-sizes - every city temple is a major complex in a way history would usually lack. A provincial city (~3,000 inhabitants) has ~15-30 monks per complex (this project's own scale - no page read gives a monk count for any Ming urban monastery); a domain capital (~12,000) has ~50+ monks per complex.137 That puts clergy at ~1-2% of city population against the historical ~0.46% (Song China: 461,000 clergy in 1221 against ~100 million inhabitants)33 - a deliberate 2-5x density, softened in-world by the temples serving the whole province's worship, not just the walled city.

The census behind that ratio: 1221, ~461,000 clergy (400,000 monks + 61,000 nuns).2

Many modest temples per walled city is the historical norm

Sources: kanazawa-teramachi, takada-teramachi, takayama-teramachi, jokamachi-wiki, pingyao-chenghuangmiao, temple-of-confucius-enwiki, chenghuang-zhwiki, lin-2001-miaohui, danka-system-enwiki

What determines it in reality: patronage and institutional history. Each temple is a separate FOUNDATION with its own patron, sect and endowment, so temple count tracks how many distinct patrons a place has accumulated - which is why it does not scale smoothly with population.135

What the research found. The expectation going in was that eight temples in a 2,600-person seat would need a disclosed liberty. The opposite is true. Japanese castle towns concentrated their temples into a designated teramachi5 at the outer rim of the jokamachi, where the spacious precincts formed part of the city defenses:97 Kanazawa ~704, Sakai ~60 and ~50 in its two wards, Akita 43 in 1663,138 Takada ~25 relocated in one 1614 wave6, and - the size-matched anchor - Takayama, a SMALL castle town, "over 10 temples and shrines"8. On the China side the standard county-seat kit carried the Confucian school-temple (wenmiao, decreed for every county school under the Tang in 630) and the City God temple (chenghuangmiao, at every seat of local government, its gods enfeoffed by the Ming founder at four administrative levels) as separate state foundations - that they came before any Buddhist or Daoist house was counted is this page's framing,102 and Pingyao's City God temple is itself a complex of THREE distinct temples on one site.10

The decision it drove. Eight modest precincts is inside the attested band, not a liberty. The existing "two major complexes" default is where the real liberty sits - see "City temple size", which records the deliberate 2-5x clergy over-density. We keep the two-complex default for non-Fox cities anyway, because it is a game-legibility choice (a few named, visitable temples beat a dozen anonymous ones), not a historical claim.

What was NOT relied on. No per-county-seat temple census was found in a Ming-Qing gazetteer study; searches returned categorization practice (Three Officials temples filed under "Altars and Shrines" rather than "Buddho-Daoist Temples") but no counts.134 The band therefore rests on the Japanese teramachi figures, with China supplying the mandatory-foundations STRUCTURE rather than a number.

Temples as economic institutions with hereditary householder clergy

Sources: tontine-monastery-lending, chinaknowledge-tang-econ (monastic manors carrying oil mills, spinneries and breweries, worked by dependent labor), lai-2003-daoism-today (the married Zhengyi priest living at home, and the monastic order he is being contrasted with), lagerwey-1988-taoist-lineages (the office descending in a family, shown in dated genealogies), contemporary-chinese-culture-daoism (the Zhengyi priest who does not live in the temple he serves), pawnbroking-history, kannushi-wiki, tricycle-temple-wives, shake-jawiki

What determines it in reality: endowment decides the TRADES (a monastery with land, dependents and tax exemption must deploy its capital), and the ordination rule decides the HOUSING (celibacy means cloister; hereditary ordination means householders).133

The economy. Chinese Buddhist monasteries were major economic actors:14 their land and dependent labor funded mills, oil presses and other enterprises11; the Inexhaustible Treasuries were interest-earning endowments,12 the Chang'an one liquidated by Xuanzong in 713 as fraudulent banking;13 pawnbroking before the Tang was limited to Buddhist monasteries15, and by the Song lay investors partnered with monasteries specifically to open pawnshops inside monastic tax exemption (a 1202 document records ten men forming a ju - a partnership of investors who put up capital together for one venture - to fund one).34 The GM's campaign notes' Fox economy - the Seven Temples holding forest usufruct, moneylending with Fukurokujin and Ebisu (the setting's Fortunes of wisdom and mercy, and of honest work), wedding loans with Benten (the Fortune of romantic love) - is a direct match, down to which institution lends.

The decision it drove. The eight precincts distribute by trade across the quarters rather than gathering into one belt, because each is an economic house sited where its business is. The Japanese rim-belt teramachi is the considered alternative and is deliberately NOT taken: it is a castle-town DEFENSIVE arrangement, and these temples are commercial institutions first. A trade-sited precinct is sometimes the only temple in its quarter, so the rule that a temple neighborhood carries shrines applies per CLUSTER and a lone precinct is exempt - the same treatment Tango's converted-estate Bishamon already gets.

The housing inversion. China first: most Zhengyi Daoist priests lived a married life at home rather than in a temple,99 and the office often passes from generation to generation within a family;100 they RESIDE IN HOUSEHOLDS rather than in a monastery, normally affiliated with a temple without living in it.85 Both works describe clergy in China and Taiwan in the twentieth century rather than in a premodern period, and what they carry back is the shape of the institution rather than its numbers. Japan corroborates twice: Shinto priests marry and their children inherit,17 some shrine priesthoods held in one family for scores of generations103 (the hereditary office was abolished in 1871 but persists by preference);18 and Jodo Shinshu was the one tradition the Tokugawa exempted so a temple head could marry and keep priestly status,35 producing the bomori19 and eldest-son succession.20 The GM's campaign notes' Fox rule - only the Three Bonds celibate, every other position hereditary, "the temple's revenue sources are effectively family businesses passed down along bloodlines" - is essentially that arrangement with a small celibate administrative core. So a Fox precinct is a small walled workplace ringed by its temple families. Departure, disclosed: the drawn monk house stays deliberately identical to a laborer house - the eye should not be able to pick temple families out of the fabric, because in reality it could not.132

Who lives inside a city temple's walls, and who lives outside them?

Sources: sodo-jawiki; the GM's ruling of 2026-07-24; the GM's campaign notes for the vows and the initiate tier; the householder-clergy finding recorded above

The decision. The GM ruled on 2026-07-24 that a city temple draws exactly like the governor's yamen: a walled compound with a deliberately blank court, because the interior is the subject of its own building plan when the players visit it. On the city sheet the big rectangle IS the temple.

Who is implied inside. Celibate monks, resident initiates and a few lay servants - some 25 to 45 of them in a provincial-city complex - live in dormitories and cloisters implied within that wall.131 They are implied population, counted on top of the dwellings the map draws rather than housed in the street fabric, and they sit outside the lay caste bands, because clergy are not a lay caste.

Who lives out. Adepts may marry and raise children, so a share of each complex's 15 to 30 monks are householders whose families keep two or three ordinary homes in the temple's own neighborhood. Departure, disclosed: those homes are drawn deliberately identical to a laborer's house, with no label and no glyph of their own - the eye should not be able to pick the temple families out of the fabric, because in reality it could not.130 Each complex also carries about twice its monk count in initiates ("many are called, few are chosen"), of whom only a small fraction sleep inside: roughly 5 to 10 at provincial-city scale, 10 to 20 at a domain capital. The rest are laborers, carters, servants and shopkeepers engaged in religious study while they work ordinary jobs, and on the map they are ordinary houses of their own caste, never buildings of their own.

The rule the map follows: on a city or capital sheet (3 ft per pixel) a major temple is a walled compound of wall, gate and empty court. Two or three clergy homes stand within about 500 ft of each major complex , and none stands stranded away from every temple; a precinct whose clergy are hereditary householders carries five to nine instead, ringing its wall. Beyond those, at most two exception features stand apart - a retired abbot's hermitage, or a small daughter chapel with two to four monks of its own - drawn precisely because they are exceptions.

What kind of shops stand at a temple gate?

Sources: the GM's correction; xiangyouqian-zhwiki (incense and lamp oil given to a Chinese temple as a donation, and then money in their place); kotobank-monzenmachi (the eating houses of the gate town); miyagegashi-jawiki (the sweets sold at the teahouses along the highway); kotobank-miyage (the early-modern souvenir shops that formed a gate town, and what they sold); lin-2001-miaohui (the diviners at a village temple fair); omamori-enwiki (the amulet as a donation to the shrine or temple that issues it); monzenmachi-jawiki (who gathers before a great temple's gate, and how the trades there shifted); qingming-enwiki (the spirit money and paper replicas a family burns for its dead); Chinese temple accounts, traced to no readable page; this project's own anchor for the lay-house band

What the temple sells itself. Amulets, charms, talismans, incense and lamp oil reach the temple directly, with nobody in between, and, in this page's reading, are a major part of what keeps it.128 On the Chinese side that flow is a DONATION rather than a sale: believers gave incense sticks, candles, lamp oil and gold paper to temples and monasteries for use in the offerings, and later gave money in their place, which is why the money itself is called incense-oil money.86 What no page read carries is a temple's own accounts booking an annual oil-lamp and incense income.38 The Japanese side is the same relation: an omamori is issued by a shrine or a temple and is viewed mainly as a donation to the place that issues it, so it is a gift of the institution rather than a line of stock.39 Manufacture away from the precinct, and charms issued by no shrine at all, belong to the modern trade and to no part of this setting.129 So there is no third-party religious-goods shop competing at the temple's own gate, and none is drawn - the GM's correction of 2026-07-20, and the easiest thing here to get wrong.

What did cluster at the gate. A gate town forms when tradesmen and craftsmen doing business with the temple's own people and with its worshippers gather in front of a large shrine or temple that draws many pilgrims; lodging is the oldest of those trades, and the eating houses and souvenir shops a visitor meets today are a later composition, after the railways brought day-trip worship.40 So the gate carries food, drink and lodging - the inn above all, and the eating-and-drinking establishments a reference work names as what was added to the gate town's permanent festival market82 - because worshippers and pilgrims need feeding and resting and the temple does not do that itself. The sweets are attested a step away: the teahouses along the highway sold the confections travelers carried home and talked about, which is how a province's named sweet grew,87 and no page read puts a sweet-seller at the gate itself. Souvenirs follow, and they are premodern: a signed encyclopedia entry has the Kumano shugenja selling souvenir goods in the early modern period, and specialist souvenir shops coming into being as demand grew and forming the gate town - talismans and amulets above all, with lucky dolls, toys, picture books and sacred medicines beside them.83 That sits against the gate-town article's attribution of the eating houses and souvenir shops a visitor meets today to the railway era: the early-modern shop is attested, the crowd of them is later. The word meibutsu for them is this project's own and stands on no page read.41 Then the funeral trades, which cluster by the graveyard rather than by the hall - coffin-makers, grave-marker cutters, and the China-first detail, the makers of the spirit money and the paper replicas of houses, servants and goods that a family burns for its dead42 - serving the parish death economy the temple presides over but does not manufacture; that those makers stood in a row of shops by the burial ground is this project's reconstruction, and rests on nothing read.43 Then the service trades. Diviners are attested at a temple: a Qing county gazetteer has the physicians, diviners, astrologers and physiognomists putting up sheds at a village temple's annual fair, beside the storytellers and the conjurors44 - a fair rather than a permanent shopfront, and a village temple rather than a great urban one. The retained temple artisans (carpenters, gilders, painters) who work for the temple on its endless repair cycle and live in the district in workshops that are also dwellings, not storefronts, are this project's own: nothing read describes a temple housing the trades it retains.91

A map drawing convention. At this scale none of those has a glyph of its own - a teahouse and an eatery are both the generic shop - so the story is told by PLACEMENT (a shop beside the temple reads as a teahouse) and never by the icon. Nothing on the map tries to be an amulet shop; everything sacred is inside the compound.

The rule the map follows: on a city sheet, size the lay service band at about 40 to 80 houses around the two complexes of a provincial city (30 to 60 resident monks between them), which is roughly one to two lay service households per resident monk for a city whose worship traffic is regional. That band is ordinary zoning inside the existing caste bands, not extra population. A pilgrimage destination inverts the ratio to ten lay households or more per monk - the gate-town pattern - and that is what an especially pious city looks like on the ground. No source gives a ratio: the band is this project's own anchor, a guess calibrated on the drawn cities.

Does the temple forecourt do anything the map cannot show?

Sources: lin-2001-miaohui (the fair held on the temple's own ground, and the traders renting its buildings and plots)

On fair days the great temple's forecourt hosted the periodic market, and the temple rented out the stall space: a study of Chinese temple fairs has the fair-going traders renting the temple's buildings and plots of ground between fairs, and quotes a Song account of the Xiangguo Temple opening five times a month for the common folk to trade.45 The renting sentence rests on a 1991 compilation on Republican-era society that the study cites, while the fair's span from Tang to Qing rests on Tang tales and Song memoirs at one end and on Qing and Republican gazetteer editions at the other.127 It is a good beat for a game - the temple district BECOMES the market for a day - and it is invisible on a static map, which is the reason to write it down where the map's reader can find it.

The rule the map follows: at town and city scale the forecourt inside a temple's gate is drawn as open ground and carries no market furniture. The fair-day market is a use of ground the map deliberately leaves clear, not a feature the map draws.

The graveyard ceiling does not scale with temple count

Sources: ryobosei-jawiki; reasoning from this project's own funerary doctrine and the Fox precincts' canon - a decision to REJECT a change, kotobank-kyodo-bochi

What determines it in reality: parish structure and burial LAND, not the number of foundations. A burial ground needs suitable ground outside the built area and serves a catchment; several foundations routinely shared one.126

What was reasoned. The tempting change is to scale the 2-4 ceiling with the eight precincts. That is wrong twice over: the Fox precincts are economic institutions holding forest usufruct, not eight parishes, so they have no separate congregations of dead; and the existing city funerary doctrine already sites burial grounds by ground suitability (off the fields, downstream, across the water where the bank allows), which constrains LAND rather than counting temples. Eight small precincts sharing three burial grounds is this page's own arrangement, on no page read.125

The decision it drove. The ceiling stands unchanged. This is a case where the research led to REJECTING a change.

Torii are VOTIVE DONATIONS - the count records patronage

Sources: fushimi-inari-senbon (~10,000 on the mountain,36 ~800 along the main path, donated from Edo28 into Meiji (unsourced)). The 1 / 2-3 / dozens distribution is assembled from normative statements - no census exists, fushimi-inari-jawiki, torii-enwiki, hokora-jawiki

What the research found: torii were VOTIVE DONATIONS, not shrine construction - a shrine's count records its accumulated patronage21 (ujiko subscriptions, grateful merchants, the local lord), a custom that began in the Edo period.123 The historical distribution: 1 torii is the overwhelming mode (~90%+ of true village shrines); 0 is the norm only BELOW the shrine tier (hokora/wayside god-shelves, which vastly outnumbered real shrines - a proper shrine with none was rare enough that each had a story); 2-3 is the ranked-shrine tier (the numbered ichi/ni/san-no-torii on long approaches, 3 the conventional ceiling); and the dozens-plus tail is specifically the Inari donation-row cult (Edo-onward, a handful to a few dozen at a modest rural Inari, hundreds only at famous cult sites, ~10,000 at Fushimi).124 No scholarly per-shrine census exists - the shape is assembled from normative statements plus attested examples.

An alternative, declined. A torii avenue could be sized to the space in front of it, on the reasoning that a temple with a long approach would fill that approach. It is not: the count is PATRONAGE rather than street room, and sizing by space bans a count of three, which is exactly what a town rolls most of the time. What the approach space does govern is the avenue's LINE, which the author of a map draws to fit the ground it crosses.

Torii spacing - two regimes and nothing in between

Sources: meiji-jingu-access (the ~10-minute walk from each entrance to the main hall), nara-nagao-jinja (about 300 m between Nagao Shrine's first and second gates, on an approach a little over 7 m wide); Kasuga's 1.3 km is this page's reading, on no page read; meiji-jingu-jawiki counts 8 torii, so "three" there is the ranked set (一・二・三の鳥居), not the total29, sanuki-kokubunji-jawiki, shimotsuke-kokubunji-jawiki

  • How far apart, and how far from the hall. What the research found: real torii spacing, as this page reads the attested cases, falls into two regimes with nothing between them.141 (1) Donation rows: Fushimi Inari's Senbon Torii section runs ~800 gates104 along ~400 m in two parallel rows - a pitch on the order of 0.5-1 m - and where the row loosens further up the mountain the gates are described as "at times tightly packed and at times irregularly spaced and several yards apart", i.e. ~3-9 m at the loose end. (2) Ranked gates (ichi- / ni- / san-no-torii) are landmarks strung along a whole approach, not a corridor: Nagao Shrine's ichi->ni is about 300 m, on an approach a little over 7 m (4 ken) wide,88 Meiji Jingu's three span a ~10-minute walk from the entrance to the main hall - the first gate at the approach's mouth, the second where the southern and northern approaches meet, the third in front of the worship hall - so the ~250 m pitch beside it is this project's own arithmetic on those ten minutes,22 and Kasuga Taisha's ichi-no-torii is said to stand 1.3 km from the shrine, which no page read states.89 At one to three feet per pixel a ranked pair is off the map at every settlement scale. The decision: since Rokugan's 1/3/7 set is neither regime, the pitch is an explicit house rule (~20 ft, cap 32 ft) rather than a copied fact - a deliberate invention, with no source behind it. The approach GAP, by contrast, is grounded: a village shrine's arch sits ~20 ft off its hall (a small keidai with the hall right there), and a great precinct may legitimately keep a courtyard between its OUTERMOST arch and its main hall, comfortably inside the norm for a proper precinct (Sanuki's Nara-period provincial temple, its kokubunji, had a precinct of 240 by 220 m, and that is the small end - Shimotsuke's ran 457 by 413 m).122 Where the INNERMOST arch stands is a separate question, and the GM settled it below.

A donation row is a designated-site special case (the GM's ruling of 2026-07-25). The Fushimi-style row - dozens to thousands of arches, given one at a time and standing almost shoulder to shoulder - is not how an ordinary approach in this setting works. It belongs to a handful of named great sites: Shinden Togashi, the Temple of Amaterasu, the Ki Rin Shrine and others of that rank. Everywhere else the 1 / 3 / 7 set is the whole vocabulary. A map of one of those sites is therefore drawn as an explicit outlier, exempt from the pitch cap; and drawing its row honestly would want the one-arch-span FLOOR relaxed as well, because a real donation row is packed tighter than a 16 ft glyph allows at these scales. That relaxation has not been taken; the floor is a drawing limit, not a finding about how gates were spaced.

And the avenue STARTS at its hall (the GM's ruling of 2026-07-27): "the distance from the front of the temple should be the same as the distance between each torii arch." An avenue can be perfectly spaced at 20 ft and still stand yards away from the temple it serves - three arches up a street on the far side of a flophouse, or with the caption and two rows of houses between it and its hall - and then it does not read as that temple's approach; it reads as red marks near some other building. So the threshold is set alongside the count and the stride: the whole run is moved bodily, keeping its direction, its curve and its stride and changing only its distance, until the innermost arch stands one pitch off the nearest FACE of the hall (measured to the footprint rather than to the center, so a run drawn off to one side slides onto the flank it stands off rather than diagonally into the middle of the building). The rule is an upper bound rather than an equality, deliberately: a village path seats its arches at 0.6 to 0.9 of its 30 ft stride, and the village rule governs that end, so the two meet without forcing cosmetic change on maps the GM has already approved.

And a caption never stands in the approach (the same conversation: an arch must "never be covered by the 'temple of X' label"). A hall's own caption and its approach both want the ground at the hall's face, which is where the collision came from. The caption tries three baselines in a STRICT ORDER - the side the map's author asked for, that same side pushed past the far end of the avenue, then the opposite side - and takes the first that touches no arch. Stepping PAST the avenue has to exist because a parish graveyard may sit deliberately at the back of a monastery, so flipping the caption off the approach would only bury it in the cemetery. An order rather than a score, for a drawing-order reason: a hall goes down early, so a scorer can see almost nothing at that point - a fire tower, the clergy houses and the graveyard's neighbors are all drawn later - and a scorer working blind can put a caption on a fire tower that does not exist yet. The author of the map knows what is on each side; a scorer at that moment does not.

Where does a village put its shrine, and how big is it?

Sources: tudigong-enwiki (the earth-god shrine inside a walled village's entrance gate), fengshui-woodland-enwiki (the head and mouth of a village, and the earth-god shrine grove), kejia-taiwan-zhwiki (the Hakka tutelary's hall built at the village water mouth, with a named instance of 1760); the GM's ruling of 2026-07-21 on the footprint band; the water-mouth grove, recorded in the vegetation research

What the research found. A Chinese village's tutelary earth-god shrine (tudi miao, 土地庙) at a village's entrance is attested as one instance rather than as a rule: a walled village of the Hong Kong New Territories keeps its earth-god shrine within the entrance gate, while the same page puts the god's other shrines inside temples and houses, beneath the main altar or below the door.46 The fengshui vocabulary of village groves keeps head for a village's entrance and mouth, the shuikou or water gate, for its exit, and it files the earth-god shrine grove among the temple groves rather than among the village ones.47 Seating the hall AT the water mouth is attested: the temples of a Hakka village's tutelary are usually built at the village's shuikou, and a named instance of 1760 stands on a stream bank in what is now New Taipei.48 Two things beside it are not: the deity so sited is a Hakka tutelary rather than the earth god, and calling the site the most strategic feng shui position is this project's own.92 On these maps the entrance is therefore the shuikou: the point where the water leaves and the connector track sets off for the road, and where the water-mouth grove already grows. The Japanese surface of the same idea is the tutelary chinju standing in its grove.142

The decision. The village's one shrine nestles there inside its sacred wood, fronted by an arch facing the village, and it is drawn SMALL: a modest hall, not a temple. It carries no label - a vermilion hall with an arch in front of it reads as a shrine on sight, and the marker rows beside it read as a graveyard, so neither is labeled. A village site is never called a temple; where the word is used of one, it means a shrine.

The rule the map follows: on a village sheet (2 ft per pixel) the ordinary earth-god or water-mouth hall is about 60 by 48 ft, some 275 m² , two farmhouses' worth under one roof; a showcase primary hall runs to about 490 m² , and 600 m² is the ceiling, because the monastery and temple tier of 1,000 m² and up belongs to towns and cities. The drawn hall is the country shrine in its one-roof form - the villagers' hall and the monk's home in one building, the form the GM chose - and its 275 m² lies inside the 200 to 330 m² of the one attested example, Kaie-ji's hall-and-kuri of 25.2 by 8.0 to 13.0 m,176 a form its own record calls unusual177 (labeled accurate; the measured halls and the one-roof example are on this page under "How big is a country shrine, and what stands in its precinct?"). The 490 and 600 m² figures above it remain this project's own calibration: no source gives a village shrine a precinct or a ceiling.49 The hall stands BESIDE a lane, never on one, since a building sits off the way; the arch is the deliberate exception, because a gateway straddles the approach and the way runs under it. The wellhead a shrine needs stands beside it, never on the hall and never under an arch. Hall and arches sit in a cleared opening in the grove rather than under the canopy, which is why they are seated before the grove is planted.

Does the country monk live at the shrine? Yes - and that is the historically accurate form

Sources: kuri-jawiki and kuri-kitchen-enwiki (the kuri, the priest's dwelling), jushoku-jawiki (the unstaffed temple), jinja-jawiki, kannushi-wiki and miyaza-jawiki (the village shrine with no priest), shake-jawiki (the hereditary priest's house near the shrine), betto-jawiki, bettoji-jawiki, jinguji-enwiki and shinbutsu-bunri-enwiki (the shrine-temple and its resident monk, and its end in 1868), mianning-village-temples, tudimiao-zhwiki and temple-chinese-enwiki (China), l5r-fandom-seido and l5r-fandom-shinden (the RPG's own setting); the GM's ruling of 2026-09-19

What the research found. Three bodies of evidence agree once the setting's fusion of the Fortunes and Shinsei is read as what it is - the pre-1868 Japanese fusion of the kami and the Buddhas. At a Japanese village's Buddhist parish temple the priest lived on the premises: the kuri is "the place where a temple's monks live",143 a suite of raised-floor living rooms with an earthen-floored entry that "surviving examples" share "with traditional vernacular houses of the same period"144 - which is to say, a farmhouse. The one limit is the unstaffed temple, where "no one resides and a head priest from far away commutes".145 At a village's Shinto shrine, by contrast, nobody lived: "small shrines often had no dedicated priest; the parishioners managed them in rotation",146 "most village shrines during this period were too poor to have a dedicated priest",147 the miyaza "keeps no full-time priest; the members ... take the priest's role in yearly rotation",148 and where a hereditary priestly family existed it "set up their houses near the shrine" - near, in houses of its own.149

The form the setting draws is the third one, which was the ordinary one before 1868: the shrine with a small temple attached, "the temple where the bettō lived",150 from which the shrine's own rites "such as sutra-reading before the kami, were performed in Buddhist form" by "the head of the shrine monks".151 That monk "was tellingly called shasō (社僧) or 'shrine Buddhist monk', and was both a shrine priest and a Buddhist monk".152 How ordinary the arrangement was is measured by what it took to end it: the 1868 order "ordered the defrocking of shasō and bettō (shrine monks performing Buddhist rites at Shinto shrines)",153 who "were told to become 'shrine priests' (kannushi) and return to their shrines",154 and "an estimated 30,000 Buddhist structures were demolished between 1868 and 1874".155 A country monk living at a village shrine and serving its rites is that arrangement exactly.

China, the setting's first model, has both forms side by side. A Qing village temple in Sichuan "engaged monks and Daoists to reside and keep watch, responsible for guarding the property, cleaning, and managing the temple's incense offerings",156 under elected lay heads; the earth-god shrine proper, "from a few square meters at the small end to over ten",157 housed nobody, and popular temples are "established and administered by local managers, village communities, lineage congregations".158 The tabletop game's own setting says the same of Rokugan: "there was no village without some manner of shrine",159 its priests "the shrine's administrators and primary caretakers",160 and of temples that "the smallest ... relied on visiting monks ... but most had at least one resident monk".161

The decision. The country monk lives at the shrine, and the sheet draws the fused site: a sanctuary, a hall for the villagers, and the monk's home - under the hall's roof by default (the GM's words: "the shrine is both their home and the place where the villagers come"), which the next section shows is an attested if uncommon form. This is labeled accurate: the GM's ruling and the sources coincide, so no knob is needed for residence. Disclosed beside it are the two forms history also had and these sheets do not draw - the pure Shinto village shrine with no resident, tended by the parishioners' rota, and the unstaffed rural temple with a commuting priest.

The rule the sheet follows: a country shrine's precinct holds the monk's dwelling; in the one-roof form (the default) the dwelling is the rear end of the hall, and in the two-building form it is a farmhouse-class house beside it. The district's registers - the temple issued the travel certificate,188 the village headmen compiled the ledger189 - live in a writing room or a record chest in the dwelling, never in an office building, because a small shrine had none: "business was frequently carried on at the private home of the administrator".184

How big is a country shrine, and what stands in its precinct? Larger than a farmhouse, because it contains one

Sources: nagarezukuri-jawiki and nagare-zukuri-enwiki (the one-bay sanctuary), ehime-pref-honden-56 (a village honden and haiden, measured), saitama-kannonji-kannondo, nara-byakugoji-hondo and chikusei-saishoji-yakushido (measured halls), homemate-shichido-garan (the minimal temple), kawasaki-chonenji-kuri, kawasaki-chonenji-hondo, sakai-kaieji, ohtawara-daioji and bunka-tokuunji-kuri (the priest's dwelling, and the one under the hall's roof), xietang-tudimiao-zhwiki and gantang-temple-thepaper (China), jinja-jawiki and jaanus-shamusho (the parts of a precinct), jochi-jawiki, shuinchi-jawiki, danka-seido-jawiki, terauke-seido-jawiki, shumon-ninbetsu-jawiki, lijia-zhwiki and mianning-village-temples (the land, the income and the records)

What the research found - the buildings. The sanctuary is tiny. The flowing-roof honden "is the most common form of shrine honden in the whole country",162 it is a one-bay building "if the bay spacing along the ridge (the front) is one ken",163 and "the most common sizes are 1 and 3 ken";164 a designated village honden of 1789 measures "1.98 m along the ridge and 1.82 m across the beams",165166 about 6 ft square. The villagers' hall is the precinct's largest building, and on measured village examples it runs from about 20 ft square to 35 ft: the same shrine's haiden of 1790 is "9.78 m along the ridge and 10.62 m across the beams",167 a thatched village Kannon hall of 1778 is "6.540 meters" on each side, "three bays square",168169 and a temple main hall of the early 17th century "5 ken" by "5 ken";170 the smallest hall read, a subsidiary hall of 1685, is "3.83 m" square.171 A minimal temple had no more than that: "small temples with only a 'main hall' and a 'bell tower' are by far the most common",172 which is why the bell is a knob and the office building is not on the sheet at all.

The dwelling, and the one roof. Every designated kuri that could be read is a rich temple's - "9 ken along the ridge and 5 ken across the beams",173 with "the head priest's living room ... a twelve-mat room",174 beside a hall of "7 ken" by "7 ken";175 the largest, "12 ken along the ridge by 8 ken", says of its own form that it resembles "the honmune-zukuri farmhouse characteristic of this region".180 No small kuri was measured,193 so the map draws the monk's dwelling at the farmhouse's own footprint, 46 by 28 ft: accurate in form, a guess in size. The form the GM asked for - the hall that is also the home - is attested once and called rare: at Kaie-ji in Sakai the "main hall and kuri" are "one building: ridge span 25.2 meters, beam span 8.0 meters on the left side, 13.0 meters on the right side",176 and "a main hall and kuri in one building is unusual for temple architecture of the beginning of the Edo period".177 That is 83 ft long by 26 to 43 ft deep, some 200 to 330 m²; a lordly hall for contrast is "25.5 meters" by "16.3 meters".178 China's earth-god temples span the same range from a hut to a hall: the surviving main hall of a large one is "three bays wide and one bay deep, 10 meters long and 7 meters wide",181 and one village temple's compound "covers about 2.5 mu",182 the one precinct area the pass could read; no Japanese one could be.192

What stands in the precinct. "Beside the approach stand the temizuya, for 'purifying the body'",183 and the arch marks the sacred ground; at a small shrine no office, its "business ... frequently carried on at the private home of the administrator";184 the grove around it and the burial ground beside it are the village rule already recorded on this page. The plot the monk lives on is exempt land - "the precincts of temples and shrines and fields and homesteads holding a document of tax exemption"185 - and such grants ran down to "a mere 1 koku";186 the income is the parish's, each household paying "fuse" to the temple it "entrusts all of its funerary and memorial rites to".187 In China the temple's land came "partly bought and partly donated, to serve as a permanent resource for the deity's incense and lamps",191 while the population was the lijia's business, "allocating corvée labor and collecting tax by head-count",190 never the temple's; whether Ming or Qing temple land was exempt could not be read.195 The three-bay village hall that search summaries repeat as the standard could not be found on a page and is not used.194

So: is it bigger than a farmhouse? Yes, because it contains one. The hall alone is farmhouse-sized or smaller; the monk's dwelling IS a farmhouse in form; under one roof the two together make a building of 200 to 330 m², two to three farmhouses, and the sanctuary, the arch, the well, the garden and the privy stand around it inside the fence, with the grove and the graves outside it.

The rule the sheet follows (the bands are the declaration's, rendered in the program catalog): the sanctuary about 6 ft square, one bay, at the back of the precinct on the approach axis (accurate); the villagers' hall 18 to 38 ft on a side (accurate as a band from four measured halls), and under one roof with the dwelling a building of 2,100 to 3,600 sq ft (accurate as a band from one attested example, disclosed as such); the dwelling, where it stands apart, at the farmhouse's 46 by 28 ft (form accurate, size a guess); an arch over the approach where it crosses the fence, a well or basin beside the approach, a fence or hedge and never a wall, the grove around and the burial ground beside, a kitchen garden and a privy at the dwelling, fire-water at the wooden buildings; a bell tower only where the knob says so; no office building. The precinct's own size is a guess bounded by what it must contain.

Is the ground around a shrine or a grave swept clear of scrub?

Sources: cssn-citang-panyu (the pool and plaza before a Cantonese ancestral hall), weilongwu-zhwiki (the drying floor and the fengshui pond before a Hakka hall), sando-jawiki (the purifying gravel of the approach), qingming-enwiki (tomb-sweeping and the groundskeeping at a grave), ryobosei-jawiki (the burial place away from habitation), chinju-no-mori-jawiki (the kept grove around a shrine); the GM's question of 2026-07-19

What the research found. The ground immediately around a sacred or a funerary feature was kept clear of wild scrub while the waste around it stayed scrubby - a tended verge, not a big bald radius.121 That a shrine's keidai, its precinct, was a swept raked-gravel or flagged surface is general reading; no page giving the precinct's surface or its upkeep was found.50 What is attested is the approach. In the Chinese case a Cantonese ancestral hall fronts a body of water and an open plaza, where the clansmen assemble and hold their ceremonies, an arrangement read both as fengshui and as drainage and fire protection.51 Grain drying is the first use the record gives that ground: in front of a Hakka hall-and-wing house lies a rectangular drying floor, the heping, for drying grain and other crops and for the gatherings of the new year, the festivals, the weddings and the funerals, with the half-moon fengshui pond before it holding water against drought and for putting out fire.52 The market and the opera on that plaza, and the same arrangement before a village earth-god shrine, remain this project's own extensions and stand on no page read.93 On the Japanese side the tamajari gravel laid along the sando, the approach, is there to purify the ground it covers53 - the custom behind a clear approach - while the claim that the path and the ground under each arch were swept the whole length of it is again general reading.54 Graves are the partial case: a burial place is often in mountain forest away from human habitation,55 yet grave tending keeps the tomb itself clear - at Qingming the young and the old alike kneel before the ancestors' tombstones, burn incense and paper, sweep the tombs and offer food56 - so the weeds come off the mound and a modest collar while the waste a short way out stays scrubby. How wide that collar ran is in no source read: the figure below is this project's own.57 So: clear AT the graves, wild BETWEEN them.

The one nuance that shaped the design. A Japanese shrine's setting is a deliberately PRESERVED grove (chinju no mori) - a forest kept and maintained around the sanctuary, the approach and the place of worship, so that the swept precinct sits inside kept trees rather than in cleared ground.58 So the swept collar holds off only the loose scrub and reed scatter. It never holds off the grove, which is a drawn feature in its own right, and it never governs where a building may stand: a shrine's back grove stands untouched right up to the swept precinct, exactly as in reality.

The rule the map follows: on a village sheet (2 ft per pixel) a small swept collar reaches about 115 ft beyond the hall , about 60 ft around each arch and its footing, and about 60 ft around a grave plot . It is deliberately modest - "a little bit of clearance" - and the scrub commons is unchanged a stride out. Loose scatter (the scrub of the commons, the reeds of a marsh) keeps off that ground; the grove, the buildings and every placement rule ignore it.

Why does that swept ground have a ragged edge?

Sources: the GM's ruling of 2026-07-23; this project's own measurement of the drawn pool

The ruling. A collar drawn as the feature's own footprint grown outward reads as a crisp bare box in the scrub around every shrine and every graveyard, because the scatter skips it. The GM ruled on 2026-07-23 that "such clearage rarely conforms to precise geometric shapes". The why: swept ground is PRODUCED by tending - brooms, feet, the traffic of the approach - radiating outward from the feature, and its edge sits wherever the tending peters out into the scrub that season; nobody surveys it.120 Ruled edges belong to what is surveyed or built - a wall, a paddy bund, a house plot - which is the same reasoning that gives the perimeter dike and the common burial ground their shapes.

Bays inward, and no lobes outward. The irregular edge is cut INTO the padded rectangle: shallow bays, deeper along the sides than at the corners. Outward lobes were tried and rejected, for two reasons. A collar is a maintenance CLAIM, so making it irregular should mean the sweeping falls SHORT of the surveyed ideal; it should never annex ground the tending does not reach. And a lobe can newly cover scrub that legitimately predates the clearing - a graveyard's lobe reaching a toe marsh that was already there. Bays-only makes every collar a subset of the rectangle the ground already had, so nothing else on the map moves.

The rule the map follows: at every tier the swept ground is DRAWN as an irregular patch bitten out of the collar, never as a rectangle, and the same feature always draws the same patch. The ground it RESERVES - what placement and clearance rules test against - stays the plain rectangle, so the shape is a drawing decision and never a placement one.

Burial-ground shape - Japan organic, China surveyed

Sources: louzeyuan-zhwiki (1104,30 high barren ground, fenced, numbered plots, continued through Ming), toribeno-jawiki (no defined boundary),31 adashino-jawiki (an exposure-burial (風葬) ground, later cremation; Rendaino (蓮台野) has no article;32 the Osaka seven graveyards (大坂七墓) page is gone), osaka-nanabaka-jawiki, fengshui-enwiki

What the research found - the two reference cultures split. Japan (the organic pole): commoner burial grounds were unplotted and terrain-following everywhere - Kyoto's great burial fields (Toribeno, Adashino, Rendaino) were open hillside/riverbank grounds shaped purely by terrain; a village kept an unsurveyed sanmai on waste land at its edge; Osaka's Edo-period dead were gathered out of graveyards scattered through the city into seven grounds on its outer rim, one of which yielded more than 1,500 skeletons and 350 ossuaries to excavation.117 Premodern Japan offers essentially NO surveyed rectangular commoner cemetery.118 China (the split case): ordinary family graves were scattered organically over unfarmable hillsides by feng shui119 - no communal commoner cemetery at all - BUT the institutional "common burial ground" for the poor and unclaimed dead genuinely WAS a surveyed rectangle: the Song state pauper cemeteries (louzeyuan 漏泽园, proposed by Cai Jing in 1104 - 建議, a proposal, not an imperial edict37 - and continued as Ming yizhong 义冢 charity graveyards)26 were fenced compounds (圍欄, a fence, not a wall) on high barren ground23 with plots in ordered rows,105 numbered against the Thousand Character Classic,24 a drainage ditch every three rows,106 and a resident caretaker monk.25

How much ground does a village burial ground need, and whose dead lie in it?

Sources: kofukuroman-sanmai (the sanmai at the hamlet's edge and the visiting graves in the hamlet's own temples), bochi-jawiki (the village graveyard held in common), sumida-miyakodori-33-2 (ten graves in a 40 m² trench at an Edo commoner graveyard), ryobosei-jawiki (the two-grave split, and where each grave stands), tanigawa-1992 (the sizes of excavated Edo commoner graves); a pre-mechanization farm survey of China for the Chinese contrast, of which no publicly readable copy carries the passage, feng-yizhong-jiangnan

What the research found. Edo Japan actually buried PER SETTLEMENT. A Fukui record of the funeral custom puts the sanmai, the cremation and burial place, at the edge of the hamlet while the visiting graves stand in two temples inside it,59 and the Japanese graveyard vocabulary keeps a category for exactly that ground: the graveyard a village settlement's own inhabitants hold in common and manage themselves.60 The form is the usual one - most often a graveyard held in common by each settlement, its ground sometimes divided into plots by household or by age and sometimes under no rule at all - and a national folklore survey counted the settlements keeping the two-grave custom prefecture by prefecture: 60 in Shiga, 59 in Nara, 45 in Mie, 42 in Kyoto, 39 each in Hyogo and Fukui, few elsewhere.61 Those counts are of the two-grave custom rather than of village graveyards in general, they lean heavily to the Kinki region, and they count settlements confirmed at survey time in the twentieth century, so they are a floor rather than a rate. Whether a twelve- to fifteen-household hamlet kept one, and the grandfathering that leaves them standing today, are in no page read.94 The two-grave regions (ryobosei) formalize the split: the body goes to a burial place mostly of earth burial, often in mountain forest away from habitation, while the visiting grave - a stone pagoda holding neither body nor bone, raised for grave-visiting at Bon and for the memorial of the ancestors - commonly stands inside a temple's precinct.62 Ming and Qing China is the deliberate contrast - family graves sited by feng shui and scattered through the farmland, with communal ground only as pauper charity;116 Buck's pre-mechanization farm survey of Chinese farming of 1929-33 (buck-survey) is said to put some 2% of ALL farm area under graves, and no readable copy of it carries the figure, so it is carried here as a guess.63 The Chinese burial-ground measure here is the Qing charity graveyards below. So the single discrete communal ground these maps draw is the Japanese surface convention, which is the setting's China-geography, Japan-surface split working as intended.

Two things make one central ground MORE defensible here than in historical Japan. The setting cremates and then inters, and an urn is carried to the district ground in a way a corpse procession is not. And a hamlet's own patch would be tiny - roughly 750 to 2,450 sq ft for full-body burial, less for urns, which is a blob thirty to fifty ft across on a village sheet - legitimately below the resolution of these maps, so a hamlet that draws nothing stays honest even where local patches exist in the fiction.

The decision. The drawn village ground is the marked, ritually active DISTRICT ground, and it is sized to the district rather than to the village that hosts it: about 800 inhabitants, at roughly 25 to 30 deaths per 1,000 a year, with a plot reused after about 30 years, gives some 600 to 720 active urn plots; at 10 to 20 sq ft each that is about 0.15 to 0.30 acre.139 The plot figure is of the order the excavated Edo commoner graves themselves reach: jar burials in wooden burial chambers run 85 cm to 1.4 m along the major axis, and the wooden coffin graves that match the graves found in villages 1 to 2.4 m.64 One excavation does give a density: a 40 m² trench at a commoner temple graveyard in Edo's Honjo held the traces of ten graves, one box coffin and nine barrel coffins - about 4 m², some 43 sq ft, a grave.65 That is the trench rather than the whole ground, and those are body burials in coffins rather than the packed urn plots drawn here, so it sits above the 10 to 20 sq ft band and corroborates the Chinese full-body figure instead; the step from a grave's size to an urn plot's footing is still this project's own. Sizing the same ground to the village's own roughly 350 inhabitants would give about half of that, and it is the district the ground serves.

The rule the map follows: a village draws ONE burial ground, between about 0.12 and 0.38 acre (the district band with glyph rounding allowed both ways), and a ground sized only to the village itself is too small. A hamlet draws none: its dead go to the village district's ground, just as it has no shrine and no headman. The ladder stays monotone with the population served - the district's ~800 sits below a town's ~1,200, and 0.15 to 0.30 acre nests under a town's 0.25 to 0.75.

Does a graveyard stand beside its temple or its shrine?

Sources: the GM's campaign notes for the merged orders and the village shrine's resident brother; danka-terauke-encyclopedia (the household as the registered unit, and the funerals and memorial rites the temple renders it), bunkotsu-jawiki

Why the graveyard is the hall's own yard. The state merged the Buddhist strand with Fortune worship, so ANY temple may host a graveyard, and a graveyard is a parish ground that sits inside a temple precinct. A village SHRINE hosts one too, which is the churchyard rule and the thing most likely to look wrong at first glance: real-world Shinto shuns death pollution (kegare), but this setting's country shrines are officially of the Buddhist-analog order even while they house Fortune deities, and the resident brother performs the funerary rites.140 The shrine IS the death-handling institution - the parish-temple logic, in which a household is registered to the temple that buries it - the registered unit being the household rather than the person, and what the temple renders it being the funerals and the continuing memorial rites for all its members66 (that arrangement is the Tokugawa danka registration, a state anti-Christian apparatus; this setting reaches the same outcome by another route) - so its burial ground is its churchyard rather than a plot held away. That unifies burial across the tiers: the graves lie by the monk-run building everywhere, the shrine in a village and the monastery or temple in a town or a city.

The exceptions, and what they are worth. A HILLTOP shrine keeps no graves: graves do not climb the sacred hill, and a prominent hill shrine is not the humble earth-god monk's funerary base. That is a judgment about the setting, not a finding: no source was found either way. A ground may also be marked as belonging to no parish - a village-style burial ground not attached to a hall at all, which is how a farming quarter inside a city wall gets a ground of its own. And divided remains (a portion interred at a great head temple, the rest in the home village) are real and simply not drawn, because nothing of the practice is visible in this view.115

The rule the map follows: in a VILLAGE the burial ground lies in the shrine's own yard, within about 500 ft of the hall , unless the shrine stands on a hill, in which case the village's ground lies elsewhere. In a TOWN or CITY a graveyard lies in its monastery's or temple's precinct - about 690 ft of the hall on a city sheet, the same drawn reach being about 230 ft on a town sheet - and at city scale that binds only a ground INSIDE the walls, since an extramural common ground belongs to the whole city. The hall and its arches themselves stay clear: the graves fill the yard AROUND them and never lie on them. Any WALLED settlement keeps a ground inside AND outside its walls, and the exterior common ground is at least 1.3 times the largest interior one, because there is room beyond the wall while the parish plots inside are hemmed in; an unwalled town's grounds are all parish grounds. A town, having no pauper ossuary to take the poor, carries proportionally more in its common ground than a city does and draws it larger.

How far from water does a burial ground lie?

Sources: paddy-field-enwiki; this project's own funerary doctrine - death pollution and flooding - with no measured set-back found in any source

A burial ground by big water floods out, so the bigger the watercourse, the further back the dead must lie; and because even narrow open water floods a grave, the ladder has a solid floor rather than starting at nothing. A flooded paddy counts as open water - rice stands in water, and a grave against a field edge is a real flood hazard - while a thin irrigation channel does not.114 Two refinements earn their place. The CREMATION ground may sit nearer the water than the graves, because fire and its washing rites want water at hand and a fire site is not flood-sensitive, so the burial ground naturally lands beyond the crematory as seen from the water.113 And a ground INSIDE the wall is exempt from the moat term, the moat being outside and the rampart between; streams and ponds still count on either side of a wall. A walled crypt precinct is exempt entirely - a crypt compound is not a flood-sensitive open plot. No source was found giving a distance: the ladder is reasoned from the hazard, and its numbers are this project's own.

The rule the map follows: the set-back scales with the watercourse and never falls below its floor. On a city sheet (3 ft per pixel) that floor is about 225 ft from a stream, rising to about 390 ft from the moat, which is the heaviest water a map carries at about 78 ft wide, and capped at about 420 ft for a river or a canal; a flooded field edge takes about 150 ft of its own. The same drawn bands read about two thirds of those figures on a village sheet at 2 ft per pixel . A cremation ground keeps a fixed modest margin of about 90 ft instead and is exempt from the field-edge margin altogether.

Where do the crematory, the ossuary and the clan mausoleum stand?

Sources: the GM's campaign notes for the cremate-then-inter canon and the caste standing of a religious order; kasoba-jawiki (burning in open country away from habitation, and the fire bed beside a burial ground), tokyo-kasoba-history (the 1884 set-back from dwellings and crowded ground, and the 1667 removal of the temple cremation grounds), shinagawa-kaizoji-muentou (the bone mound with its image, raised over the unconnected dead); the funeral path itself, traced to no readable page, zuihoden-jawiki

The setting always cremates and then inters the bones, so a graveyard here is a compact field of stone markers rather than a body-burial sprawl. Smoke, fire risk and pollution put the crematory OUTSIDE the walls, at a town's edge and clear of the dwellings, and the marginal siting is attested: the dead were burned in open country away from habitation, and where a permanent fire bed was made it lay beside a burial ground.67 The set-back is a stated rule where the record speaks: the model standard issued with the burial regulations of 1884 put a cremation ground at least 120 ken, about 216 m, from dwellings and from places where people congregate, on ground that is not upwind, walled, with a chimney and a device against the foul smoke.68 The premodern instance of the same hazard is Kanbun 7 (1667), when the smoke of the cremation grounds scattered through Asakusa and Shitaya reached the shogun on his way to visit a grave, and some twenty-odd of them were moved together to designated ground by the Kozukappara execution place.96 That the pyre is reached by a minor funeral path rather than by high-street frontage is this project's own reasoning from the same hazard, on nothing read.95 It also never stands between a temple and the road, because nobody should walk past the pyre to reach worship - behind or beside the hall, never on its ceremonial approach.112 And it adjoins an EXTRAMURAL burial ground, because the body is burned and its bones interred next door: the two are one funerary complex, and a graveyard inside the walls does not answer for it. Monks officiate with burakumin assistants, a religious order standing outside the caste system, so handling the dead does not pollute its caste.

Note the split that is easy to miss. A temple's own graveyard sits beside the temple wherever the temple is, and may perfectly well face the road; it is the CREMATORY that is the marginal exception, kept off the road and off the hall's approach. Minor town streets trigger neither rule - only the trunk road does.

The other two. The pauper OSSUARY is a low earthen mound with one weathered stupa holding the communal bones of the poor and the unconnected dead, and that form is on the record: a ward's cultural-property listing has the bones of those who died in the Shinagawa holding jail gathered up at a temple in 1708, a burial mound raised over them and a Kannon image set on top, with stupas for the unconnected dead raised beside it in 1865 and 1873.84 Where it stands - outside the walls by the crematory - is this project's own;90 it is a city feature, which is why a town's common ground has to absorb the same dead itself. The clan MAUSOLEUM is the opposite in every way: the ruling clan's ancestral crypts in a precinct with its own halls and gates - the attested instance, Sendai's Zuihoden, faces the castle's main keep across the river - drawn here INSIDE the walls by the samurai and government quarter as a prestige monument near the seat of power, a placement of this project's own; that ancestor veneration is central to samurai identity is on no page read.111 City only.

The rule the map follows: a TOWN draws a cremation ground at its edge, clear of the dwellings; a CITY draws the cremation ground and the pauper ossuary outside the walls, beyond a gate, and the clan mausoleum inside them by the samurai quarter. The crematory stands at least about 390 ft from the trunk road and, where a monastery or temple is within about 1,200 ft of it , at least as far from that road as the hall is, less a tolerance of about 120 ft for "beside it, a little away" . Its burial ground is extramural and within about 210 ft of it edge to edge . Where a wall of a walled compound (a mausoleum, a manor, a merchant estate) abuts a neighborhood fence, the compound YIELDS that side: the fence is drawn over the coincident wall and is that side of the precinct, the compound's own wall running underneath - a map drawing convention that keeps two clashing parallel walls off the sheet.

How large are the gates, walls and funerary features drawn?

Sources: kojodan-dobei (the thickness of a rammed-earth wall and of its lighter cousin), tokyo-kasoba-history (the recorded site areas of village and city cremation grounds), kyototuu-mimizuka (the ear mound's width across and its height), feng-yizhong-jiangnan (mu and coffin counts for Qing charity graveyards), tokyohakuzen-yoyogi (the Musashi gazetteer's 900 tsubo at Yoyogi), kasoba-jawiki (what a pyre actually was), kozukappara-jawiki (the Edo execution ground's dead and its extent), tanigawa-1992 (excavated grave sizes); the gate widths and the cremation-ground band, traced to no readable page; the GM's ruling

Why this matters at all. A walled compound - a manor or estate, the governor's yamen, a merchant estate, the mausoleum - draws its wall, its gate and a deliberately blank court, because the interior is the subject of its own building plan. The wall and the gate ARE the feature, so they have to be honest, and a funerary feature drawn to the wrong size reads as a different kind of place.

The anchors. A samurai residence gate is drawn at about 9 to 12 ft and a grand yamen gatehouse at about 18 to 24 ft. Neither figure is a finding: the official commentary on the nagaya-mon, the gate a samurai of middle rank and above was permitted, describes its servants' quarters and its stable and gives no width,69 and the best-preserved county magistrate's office in China is described by its forty thousand square meters and its hundred-meter axis and not by its gate,70 so both are this project's own calibration on the drawn sheets. What the anchors exist to prevent is real enough: a gate gap drawn as a fixed distance on the sheet rather than as a real width opens 204 ft at city scale , which is most of a wall missing. Wall thickness is the one figure in this paragraph the record does measure, and what it measures is a heavier wall. A Japanese rammed-earth tsuijibei was built a full meter thick and three meters high, and the lighter neribei about 30 cm71 - castle walls, both, and they bracket the 1.5 to 2 ft these compounds are drawn at without containing it, so the drawn figure is a choice about a lesser wall and not a measurement of one. A cremation ground's cleared core is drawn at 30 to 80 ft at village and town scale and about 80 to 160 ft at city scale, and the recorded sites bracket that band: village cremation grounds of 10 tsubo 5 go (34.65 m²) and 42 tsubo, and the large urban one at 600 tsubo (1,980 m²), about 145 ft square.72 Those are Meiji through Showa municipal facilities rather than Edo ones. The one real extent is Edo's own: the crematory at Yoyogi is recorded in the Musashi gazetteer at 900 tsubo, some 180 ft square, with three houses of the men who worked it.73 The pyre itself is not a built platform at all: in open country it was firewood stacked on the ground, the permanent form was a shallow trench about one size larger than a coffin framed in stone and earthenware, and the roofed forms are two: a fire bed on leveled ground with posts at its four corners carrying a simple roof, and, in snow country, a hut of about four to six tatami with a full tiled roof on stacked cut-stone walls and a channeled fire bed dug at its center.74 A pauper bone mound is drawn at 10 to 30 ft across with nothing behind the band,75 while the base of Kyoto's monumental ear mound is measured: 30 ken, about 49 m, across, standing 4 ken (about 7.2 m) high under a 5-ken stone pagoda, on a regional encyclopedia site that names no source for the figures.76 Cremated bone takes almost no volume, which is why the great Edo execution ground - more than two hundred thousand put to death between its founding and its abolition, on a plot about 108 m across the front and a little over 54 m deep77 - is not remembered for a mound; no page read describes one raised there.78 Burial grounds size to the population served at about 10 to 20 sq ft per packed urn grave, with about one generation of active plots before reuse.109 The excavated Edo commoner graves are of that order - jar burials in wooden chambers 85 cm to 1.4 m along the major axis, wooden coffin graves 1 to 2.4 m79 - and the four-to-six-times multiple for Chinese full-body burial is measured now: a Kangxi endowment of 4 mu of Jiangnan field took 573 coffins, about 4.7 m² - some 50 sq ft - a grave, inside the 40 to 120 sq ft the multiple implies, in charity graveyards that ranged from 3 fen to 10 mu.80 Those are grounds for the unclaimed and the drowned, packed more tightly than a family grave sited by fengshui.110 The acreage ladder those plots build to (a village district 0.15 to 0.30 acre, a town 0.25 to 0.75, a city 0.75 to 2 split across its yards) is still this project's own arithmetic, on no source.97 This setting follows the Japanese cremate-then-inter mode.

A to-scale map does not enlarge a feature for legibility; where a glyph would otherwise vanish, the help is the thinnest mark the sheet can carry, disclosed as the drawing convention it is.

The rule the map follows: a compound's gate opening is drawn at its real width for the building it serves, about 9 to 12 ft for a samurai residence and 18 to 24 ft for a yamen gatehouse. A compound wall is drawn at its true thickness, or at the thinnest line the sheet can carry where that is wider , and a gate post at about 2 ft square under the same kind of floor - map drawing conventions, so that a wall one and a half ft thick still reads at 3 ft per pixel. A cremation ground's cleared core is 30 to 80 ft at village and town scale and 80 to 160 ft at city scale, with a fire bed about a coffin's length and a little wider, roofed where it is permanent - four posts and a simple roof over the bed, or, in snow country, a walled hut of about four to six tatami, roughly 8 to 10 ft square; a pauper bone mound is drawn at about 22 ft, with a small floor under it so that it never vanishes , and anything outside 8 to 32 ft is wrong. Burial grounds are sized to the population they serve, and the ladder stays monotone: a village district ground below a town's, a town's below a city's.

What religious and funerary features does each size of settlement carry?

Type and rank track each other, and the two faiths divide the work. A HAMLET has no religious building of its own and shares its village district's, exactly as it has no headman. A VILLAGE has a Shinto shrine, tended by the one tax-free religious figure of the district, whose dwelling may double as the shrine itself. A TOWN has monasteries, each dedicated to a patron Fortune of the clan whose holdings include it, with an arch in front. A CITY has full temples, its temple neighborhood dotted with small wayside shrines. An arch fronts any shrine, monastery or temple that a way runs straight up to.

Where the small shrines go. The floor of three wayside shrines near a cluster of temples is a minimum, not a pattern to repeat: additional ones placed at meaningful spots elsewhere read truer than one more shrine crowded by the halls. Historically a wayside shrine stood exactly at a threshold: the deity of the way is enshrined at spatial and social boundaries - a village's entrance and exit, a mountain pass, a crossroads, the foot of a bridge81 - which gives the landing, the crossing and the gate, while no page read puts one at a well98 - so a shrine at the wharf, fittingly to the Fortune of fishermen and honest commerce, or at a bridge or a gate, is placed where the city's life turns.

The rule the map follows: a HAMLET draws no shrine, no arch and no burial ground. A VILLAGE draws one shrine with its arches and one burial ground in the shrine's yard. A TOWN draws its monasteries - two by default, one for each patron Fortune of the clan that holds it - with one or two graveyards in their precincts, a graveyard inside and outside the walls if it is walled, and a cremation ground at its edge. A CITY draws its temples - two major complexes by default, more only under a declared exception - with two to four graveyards and one in every temple precinct that has not opted out, at least three small wayside shrines by a cluster of two or more temples (a lone precinct among houses needs none), the clan mausoleum inside the walls, and the cremation ground with the pauper ossuary outside them, an extramural funerary complex beyond a gate.
