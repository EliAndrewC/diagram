Hu Jian (胡健), "山西发现17方宋代漏泽园墓志" ("Seventeen Song-dynasty Louzeyuan epitaphs found in Shanxi", title translated), People's Political Consultative Conference Net (人民政协网), 17 February 2017, from China News Service (in Chinese; http://www.rmzxw.com.cn/c/2017-02-17/1348496.shtml?n2m=1)

What it is: A short news report that Jincheng Museum in Shanxi confirmed seventeen epitaph bricks of the Song-dynasty Louzeyuan, the official burial ground for the poor and unclaimed dead, found on both banks of the Baishui River in the south of Jincheng (Zezhou). It describes the bricks, who was buried, and when.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives physical facts for a Song public cemetery: epitaphs on strip bricks 31 by 16 by 4.5 cm, burials dated from 1110 to 1118 under Emperor Huizong, and the dead identified as banished soldiers, patients who died at an infirmary (Anji fang), soldiers, commoners, unnamed men who died at inns and posts, and poor people who died of illness. It cites the Song History that Louzeyuan were established in the third year of Chongning, and says the find fixes the cemetery's site and shows the Zezhou charity institutions. It reports a find of seventeen epitaphs from one prefecture over nine years, gives no cemetery size, plan or count of burials, and describes the carving of the epitaphs as hasty and error-ridden.
