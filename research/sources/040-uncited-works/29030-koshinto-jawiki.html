koshinto-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 庚申塔 ("koshinto", the Koshin stone tower, title translated) (in Japanese; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/庚申塔)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the koshinto, the stone monuments raised under the Koshin belief and its all-night vigils (koshinmachi), with their forms, carvings, distribution across Japan and history. It includes the count of 77 stone koshinto found in Tanto town, Toyooka, Hyogo.

Why it applies, and its limits: It says koshinto were set along highways with directions carved on them, at village boundaries as guardian-of-the-road deities, and at crossroads and village entrances, and that they became widespread from the early Edo period, so it can support a roadside or boundary stone at a village edge. Its oldest surviving example is a board stele in Saitama dated 1471, and its main spread is from the 1600s on, and it says the Meiji government removed many along the highways and that road widening from the high-growth era removed or moved most of the rest, so many surviving stones now stand in temple or shrine grounds or on private land; its crossroads and village entrances are where such stones are seen today, not a dated siting. Its only local count is from one town in Hyogo, and it notes that the Kanto region has many and Kyoto and Osaka relatively few. The article is marked as a stub, and its only reference is that town's 1999 board-of-education booklet, so its siting along highways, at village boundaries and at village entrances is uncited.
