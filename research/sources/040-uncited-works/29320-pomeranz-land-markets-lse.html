Kenneth Pomeranz, "Land Markets in Late Imperial and Republican China" (draft conference paper), London School of Economics, Global Economic History Network conference 6 (https://lse.ac.uk/economic-history/assets/documents/research/gehn/gehnconferences/conf6/conf6-kpomeranz.pdf)

What it is: A conference paper, marked as a draft not to be quoted, cited or distributed without the author's permission, so its published form may differ, surveying how land was sold, pawned, rented and ruled over in Ming, Qing and Republican China, drawing mostly on other scholars' studies of contract collections, lawsuit records and 1920s-1940s surveys. It covers live sales, permanent tenancy (one field, two masters), lineage trusts, middlemen and how well the markets worked, with most weight on the Qing.

Why it applies, and its limits: It describes who owned and rented land and on what terms in Jiangnan, Fujian, Guangdong, Sichuan and North China, and says the one-field-two-masters system was concentrated in the developed southeast and far from universal. Its evidence runs from the Ming to the 1940s, so it speaks to late imperial China and not to a Japan-like setting; its figures are estimates, not counts: live-sale prices of 60-80% of an outright price are other scholars' (McAleavy, Buoye, Macauley), and tenants on "perhaps 15%" of North China land against almost half in the Yangzi Delta is the author's own uncited rough figure, and it is about legal and economic arrangements, not about how a village or field looked.
