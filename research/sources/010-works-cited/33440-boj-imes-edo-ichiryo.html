Bank of Japan Institute for Monetary and Economic Studies, Currency Museum, 「江戸時代の１両は今のいくら？」 ("What is one ryo of the Edo period worth today?", title translated), a four-page teaching sheet, September 2017 (in Japanese; https://www.imes.boj.or.jp/cm/history/historyfaq/mod/1ryou.pdf)

What it is: A handout of the Bank of Japan's currency museum explaining why a ryo of the Edo period has no single modern value, with worked sums of a ryo's worth in yen of 2016 by rice, a carpenter's wage and a bowl of soba, and a table of everyday prices in copper mon and a few wages, given as examples of the first half of the 1800s.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the price of a persimmon, with other village goods, for the villages of the Kanto in the first half of the 1800s. It says the items come from several different documents, because it is hard to find the count or quantity and the price together in one document, and it names none of those documents (its last page gives a reading list of works on Edo prices, such as the Mitsui Bunko price series and Ono Takeo's 江戸物価事典, but ties no item to any of them), so no single item can be traced to its year, place or record. Its table gives only 柿 1個 6文: it does not say whether the persimmon was fresh or dried, or in what season, and the sheet itself warns that farm prices rose and fell with the harvest and the season. Its short list of the shogunate's official rates of exchange gives the rates of law, which, it says, the market rates moved away from day by day. It also gives the rates of 1609 and 1700 alike as 4,000 mon of copper without saying which coin. The decree of 1609 counted those 4,000 mon in 京銭, worn and privately cast coins, equal to 1 kanmon of Eiraku coins. The Kan'ei tsūhō, the coin of the 1700 rate and of the 1800s prices, was first cast in 1636. So the two rates are not 4,000 of the same coin. Its sums of a ryo's worth today are its own worked examples in yen of 2016. It takes 1 ryo as 1 koku of rice (about 150 kg) in the 1700s, an assumption rather than a recorded rate. It prices that rice at the 2016 shop price of polished rice, 2,100 yen for 5 kg, although, it says, Edo prices were for brown rice, and gets about 63,000 yen. It takes one case, which it does not trace, of a ryo hiring 23 carpenters for a day in the later 1700s, prices them at an assumed 15,000 yen a day, and gets 345,000 yen. That the two differ more than five times over is its point: no one figure is a ryo's worth. Its rice by period, about 350 kg to the ryo early in the period, about 150 kg in the middle and late periods and about 15 to 30 kg around 1867, is a round guide, and the last falls after the ports were opened, when, it says, prices rose steeply within a single year.

Used for: the price of a persimmon in the Kanto countryside, and the official rates of 1609 and 1700; a ryo's worth today by rice and by a carpenter's wage, by period, and its warning on harvests (Money and prices)
