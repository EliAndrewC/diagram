shimane-toshuko-lecture

Shimane University, rural-engineering lecture notes on irrigation facilities, chapter 3, head works (頭首工) (https://zoukou.life.shimane-u.ac.jp/ruraleng/shisetsuken/12kougisiryou/15.pdf)

What it is: A printed textbook chapter on the head works, hosted as course material by Shimane University's rural engineering program: their parts, a line on their history (small timber-and-stone works up to the end of the Sengoku period), the rule of one-bank against two-bank intake, where the intake mouth is set, and the intake court between the mouth and the canal.

Why it applies, and its limits: It states two rules a map needs plainly - the intake is on one bank, the side of the land it serves, on torrent and steep rivers, and as the rule on gentle ones, and it stands where the current's deep line touches that bank - and it dates the small timber-crib form to the period before the early modern age. Its limits are that it is teaching material for modern design: the one-bank rule, the siting where the deep line touches the bank and the intake court (a tapering transition with guide walls) are rules for designing a modern gated intake, and it does not say premodern intakes followed them, though the silt and scour they answer are the same. Its history is one uncited paragraph, which says the intakes up to the end of the Sengoku period were mostly small timber-and-stone works and that stacked-stone and pitched-stone weirs were built from the early Edo period, so the timber crib is not the only form for an Edo-modeled place. Its velocities and depths belong to concrete works, and it gives no distance from the intake to the first division.

Used for: intake works up to the end of the Sengoku period as small timber frames packed with stone; one-bank intake on the benefited side, the practice on torrent and steep rivers and the rule on gentle ones; the intake where the current touches the bank; the intake court between mouth and canal
