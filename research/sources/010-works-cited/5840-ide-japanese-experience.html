ide-japanese-experience

IDE, Passing on "The Japanese Experience", rural society (https://d-arch.ide.go.jp/je_archive/english/society/wp_je_unu4.html)

What it is: "Development of local culture and the irrigation system of the Azusa Basin" by Akira Tamaki, issued in 1979 as number 4 of the “Japanese Experience of the UNU Human and Social Development Programme” series, and kept in the institute's online archive. It studies the Azusa River basin near Matsumoto in Nagano: the customary rules by which villages shared irrigation water, the difference between the dry fields on the river terraces and the wet paddy below them, and the repeated attempts to bring water up onto the terraces.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for the distinction between dry field (hatake) and wet paddy (suiden) and for why a settlement sitting above the water is stuck with the former: the late-Tokugawa schemes to irrigate the Hata terraces were defeated again and again by the downstream villages who would have lost the water. Its limits: it is a close study of one river basin in one province, so its customs are local before they are national, and the terrace-against-paddy contrast is a fact of that valley's landform rather than a general rule; and it runs from the Tokugawa period through twentieth-century modernization, so only its account of the older arrangements bears on our settlements. The 1655 figure of 589 cho watered by the Wada canal is hearsay: the paper gives it as "it is said" and cites no register for it. It is also the area of the basin's dominant canal, which had the best intake on the river and a village under the shogun's direct rule, and Hata village drew water from it free on top of that area. So it is the upper end of what one canal shared among three villages could water, not a typical village's paddy.

Used for: hatake vs suiden; the defeated late-Tokugawa hata irrigation schemes; the Wada canal's 589 cho shared among three villages in 1655 (Fields)
