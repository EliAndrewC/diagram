liu-2022-china-wages-1530-1840

Ziang Liu, "Wages, Labour Market, and Living Standards in China, 1530-1840", London School of Economics, Economic History working paper 339, 2022 (https://www.lse.ac.uk/Economic-History/Assets/Documents/WorkingPapers/Economic-History/2022/WP-339.pdf)

What it is: An economic historian's estimates of the day wages of unskilled and skilled workers in China from 1530 to 1840, estimated by a regression on about 5,700 wage quotations pooled from across China: Ming gazetteers' pay for county doormen and runners (1530-1640), government payrolls chiefly in Beijing and on the Grand Canal (1571-1840), Lower Yangzi temple woodblock engravers' piece rates (1601-1686), and Qing judicial-archive cases (1724-1840). It also discusses the silver and copper money the wages were paid in. It is the 2022 working-paper draft of an article published, revised, in Explorations in Economic History 92 (2024).

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the one unskilled wage series found that reaches back into the Ming, half-century by half-century, and states the weight of the tael and how the capital's cash was counted. Its series is the Lower Yangzi's unskilled building laborer, not Beijing's. It is the regression's predicted baseline, not wages recorded for such laborers: before 1640 it rests chiefly on the budgeted stipends of conscripted doormen and runners, which the author takes as a cost of living rather than a market wage, and in the later 1600s on engravers' piece rates and Beijing payrolls. It is a working paper whose full regression results were not released with it, and the published 2024 version revises its half-century figures. Beyond that, its earliest half-century averages only 1530-1549 and its last only 1800-1840; and its levels run well below the Allen et al. estimates it sets beside them, a disagreement it explains by arguing that Allen's Lower Yangzi series is closer to skilled wages. Its tael weights and exchange rates are taken from other works it cites, not measured.

Used for: the Lower Yangzi laborer's day wage from the 1500s to the 1800s, the weight of the tael, and the capital's double-counted cash (Money and prices)
