ihns-bunongshu

Zhang Lüxiang, 补农书 (Bu nongshu, "Supplement to the Farm Manual", title translated), completed 1658 by its author's preface, full text on the agricultural-history site of the Institute for the History of Natural Sciences (in Chinese; http://agri-history.ihns.ac.cn/books/bns.htm)

What it is: A full transcription of the farm book of Zhang Lüxiang (1611-1674), a schoolteacher who also farmed in Tongxiang county, Zhejiang, with the farm manual of a Mr. Shen of Huzhou (his given name lost), written in the last years of the Ming, printed before it and a modern editor's notes after each part.

Why it applies, and its limits: It describes the household garden of a rice-and-silk farm of the Lower Yangzi in south China: fenced with a thorny hedge, beside the house, and a mu of it feeding ten people their vegetables all year; and it has some farmers of the author's own village, in Tongxiang county, making a trade of the sweet chrysanthemum, a flower steeped with tea, and taking it to market, at double the profit of beans if planted to a whole mu. Its limits: it is one scholar's advice on how a farm should be kept, not a survey of how farms were - Zhang was a schoolteacher of reduced fortune who farmed ten-odd mu with his household, in the Jiaxing-Huzhou country, among the most heavily manured and commercial farmland in China, so its mu of garden feeding ten may be smaller than a garden elsewhere would need; it names the mu without giving its size; and the online text is a modern collation, on an 1897 print, of a book that went around in manuscript copies that differ, with no page references and characters it could not type written out as their component parts. A hired hand's rice comes from Mr. Shen's part, not Zhang's: it is the old rule of his section on working the fields, a late-Ming Huzhou employer's reckoning of what each hired hand ate, in some of the highest-wage and most commercial country in China, and it is given in sheng and ge whose size the book does not state. Its prices of spirit and of the field hands' wine are Mr. Shen's too; only the ratio of bought to home-brewed wine is Zhang's, of 1658. Its rainy-day work and the cloth bought are Mr. Shen's as well, from his calendar of each month's tasks in fair weather and rain and the stores to lay in: one late-Ming Huzhou manager's schedule for a farm that hired year-hands, and the cloth he lays in is bought ramie cloth. So are the rope twisted in a day and the 120 bolts of silk two women weave in a year, which is silk woven for the market in the silk country, not a household's clothing; only the common man's ten mu and his wife's day of cloth or yarn are Zhang's.

Used for: the fenced garden beside a south Chinese farmhouse, its thorny hedge, and one mu of garden to a household of ten (Homesteads); the sweet chrysanthemum grown for market in Tongxiang county in 1658 (Fields); a hired hand's daily rice, averaged over the year; bought wine against home-brewed, spirit retail and in bulk, and the field hands' shop wine; hired hands' rainy-day work, rope twisted in a day, women weaving and spinning, and the cloth the Shen farm bought (Money and prices)
