clark-2006-gpih-england-prices-wages

Gregory Clark, "English prices and wages, 1209-1914", a spreadsheet for the Global Price and Income History Group, provided by Clark and reformatted and converted to metric by Peter Lindert, 10 April 2006, from the series of Clark's "The Price History of English Agriculture, 1209-1914" (2004), "The Condition of the Working-Class in England, 1209-2004" (2005) and "The Long March of History" (2006) (https://gpih.ucdavis.edu/files/England_1209-1914_(Clark).xls)

What it is: One row a year from 1209 to 1914, and a column for each of some eighty goods and three day wages in England, in English pence and English units, then again in grams of silver and metric units, with the sources and the compiler's caveat on a sheet of their own.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a comparative source: the yearly prices of grain, fuel, salt, lamp oil and cloth and the day wages of one European country under metal money, read for how far each moved from year to year. Its figures are Clark's as of 2006 and, by its own caveat, subject to revision; many goods have years with no price, and some repeat a price from one year to the next without the file saying whether it held or was filled in. The file refers its reader to Clark's publications for the data's descriptions and sources. A draft of the first of those publications, his paper on the agricultural series, among them grain, peas and firewood (https://faculty.econ.ucdavis.edu/faculty/gclark/papers/Agprice.pdf, 9 October 2003; the published version of 2004 was not read), says each good's price was joined into one series by a regression with a term for each year, unit and place or quality, from published collections (Thorold Rogers, Beveridge, Farmer and others), printed accounts of churchwardens, town treasurers and the Carpenters Company, and archival records, among them King's College, Cambridge's for hay, firewood and timber after 1780. The earlier prices mostly come from accounts running from Michaelmas to Michaelmas, and one without a date in the year is set to the year of the January after its Michaelmas. So a year's figure is an estimate across many places and buyers, not one market's price, and one town's prices may well have swung more; the sources of the salt, charcoal, lamp oil, cloth and wage series were not read.

Used for: how far single goods and wages swung from year to year in England, 1209-1869 (Comparative money: other societies)
