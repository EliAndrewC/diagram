liq-takayasu-daidokoro

Otsubo (TAKAYASU Co., LIQ), 台所の歴史 ―キッチンと呼ばれるまで― (The history of the kitchen, until it was called a kitchen) (in Japanese; https://www.liq-takayasu.com/blog/53)

What it is: A blog post (24 February 2023) by the public-relations staff of TAKAYASU, a Kyoto builder of custom houses, tracing the Japanese kitchen from prehistory to the modern era, with pictures from the National Diet Library's digital collections, among them the kitchen of the Ishiyamadera engi scroll, and a photograph of the kamado in the middle-rank samurai house reconstructed at Bōsō no Mura, Chiba Prefecture's open-air museum.

Why it applies, and its limits: It says plainly that a samurai house's kitchen was board-floored and that, its doma being small, the kamado could stand on the board floor, which is the choice a residence's kitchen hearth is drawn from. It names no source for that sentence, and its general rule that the kamado stood on the doma is written beside a passage on commoners' rowhouses. Its samurai sentence is in its Edo-period section and is illustrated by a middle-rank retainer's house, so it speaks for the kitchens of middle and lower samurai, not for a great lord's residence, whose large kitchen (the post's own header picture is a daimyo's ōdaidokoro) is another scale of place. It says nothing of shrines; its one religious house is a temple's kitchen, the kuri of Myōhōin, where a late-sixteenth-century kamado site was found in the ground and some 800 monks are said to have been fed in 1595, and it says nothing of how that kitchen was floored or of a priest's dwelling. The kamado on a shrine dwelling's doma rests on three things the post says of other kitchens: the kamado on the doma and the irori on the board floor in the 1351 Boki ekotoba scroll (which it says early-Edo samurai kitchens still resembled), its rule that the kamado basically stood on the doma (said of rowhouses), and its remark that farmhouses had larger doma than samurai houses. For a shrine dwelling it is a carry-over from those houses, not a statement about shrines.

Used for: the samurai kitchen's board floor and the kamado on it or on the doma (Estates and other compounds); the kamado on a shrine dwelling's doma (Religion and the dead)
