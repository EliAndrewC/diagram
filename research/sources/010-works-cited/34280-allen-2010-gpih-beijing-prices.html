allen-2010-gpih-beijing-prices

Robert C. Allen, Jean-Pascal Bassino, Debin Ma, Christine Moll-Murata and Jan Luiten van Zanden, "China 1730-1933", a spreadsheet for the Global Price and Income History Group (January 2010), the data of their "Wages, Prices, and Living Standards in China, 1738-1925" (https://gpih.ucdavis.edu/files/China_1730-1933_Allen_et_al.xls)

What it is: The data files of the study of Qing wages registered here as allen-2009-china-wages: a year-by-year "Beijing price data base" of the goods of a living-standard basket in grams of silver, and the silver wages of unskilled laborers in Beijing, Suzhou and Canton.

Why it applies, and its limits: It prices nearly every good of a year's basket in Beijing in every year from 1738. But before 1900 the prices are a reconstruction, not records: the study carries its Beijing shop prices of 1901-1904 back by wholesale grain prices of Zhili province, its rice by the Yangzi delta's rice price, its meat and oils by the price of wheat flour, and holds its cloth at one price through the 1700s, carried unchanged to 1830 and interpolated to an imported-cloth price of 1871, as its working paper's appendix says. Its Zhili grain series are five-year moving averages, so a single year's price is smoothed. Some goods have no Chinese price at all: candles are set at the price of lamp oil, soap at half of it "based on European precedents", and rice wine by a Japanese ratio of sake to rice; fuel rests on Zhili charcoal prices of 1769 and one Beijing coal price of 1816, interpolated between. Every level is the capital's retail price, not a market town's. Its Beijing wage of the 1700s is a regression estimate.

Used for: the reconstructed Beijing prices of the 1730s-1750s and the 1820s; how far the basket cost stood apart in Beijing, Suzhou and Canton (Money and prices)
