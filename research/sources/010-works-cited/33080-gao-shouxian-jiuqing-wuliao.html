gao-shouxian-jiuqing-wuliao

高寿仙 (Gao Shouxian), 「清代《九卿议定物料价值》的分类整理——兼比较明代《工部广库须知》同类物品的价格」 ("Sorting the Qing-Dynasty Material Value as Agreed by the Nine Chief Ministers: In Comparison with Similar Items in the Ming-Dynasty Essential Knowledge of the Ministry of Public Works' Workshops and Warehouses", title translated by its author), Gugong Xuekan 2022, pp. 23-37 (in Chinese; the Palace Museum, https://www.dpm.org.cn/Uploads/File/2023/02/10/u63e60647c73ae.pdf)

What it is: A historian's tables of the official price list for the materials the Ministry of Works bought in Beijing, settled by the Nine Chief Ministers and submitted to the emperor in 1736, sorted by kind, with the wages the list sets for craftsmen and laborers, and set beside the like items of the Ming Ministry's 1615 handbook (Gongbu changku xuzhi), whose prices Beijing officials had likewise assessed against Beijing's market.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives Beijing's official prices of silk and cotton cloth by width and length, of clothing, of paper and ink, of bows and arrows, and of salt (0.012 tael a jin, among the foods of the author's first table), of charcoal (among the fuels of his second table) and of a kitchen cleaver, with a laborer's and a craftsman's day wage, in the same list. The craftsman's rate is the one for the ministry's wood, stone, brick, tile and painting craftsmen; the list's other grades are scaffolders (140 cash) and rammers (100). So it is a building craftsman's wage on government works, not a smith's or a weaver's. The wages are the day rates the ministry set for the men it hired on its own works, not a record of what other Beijing employers paid. This list also cut and evened out the older rates: a craftsman's went from 180 cash (hired long) or 140 (hired short) to 154, and a laborer's from 80 or 60 to 75. The prices are those the ministry would pay, based on the market but set by officials; the wages are in copper cash, which the author converts at the 800 cash to the tael that a Board of Revenue memorial of 1737 reports for Beijing (the official rate was 1,000, and the market rate varied); and most of its garments are costume pieces for the yangge folk dance, which it marks as differing somewhat from everyday dress. Its silks run from plain raw silk at 0.36 tael a zhang to palace-made golden-yellow satin with five-clawed mang (the dragon of rank robes) at 7.935; this project takes the dragon satins for court silk and only the plain grades as what a townsman might buy. These are the imperial capital's prices, which the author judges should broadly reflect Beijing's market; a provincial town's ran differently, and the 1768 provincial price regulations he names (1,557 jurisdictions, Beijing excluded) are the closer record for those. Its salt is a single entry at one grade, and it does not follow a free market: under the state salt monopoly, each salt district's price was set. Beijing drew its salt from the coastal works nearby, so a town in another salt district paid that district's price, and one farther inland paid more. This figure is the low end of an inland range, not its middle. Its charcoal is the price at which the ministry bought fuel in bulk for its works in the capital, not what a household paid buying a little at a time; the 1615 handbook's prices were likewise official prices for the capital, so the two lists compare with each other better than either compares with a provincial town. Its foods are the few the ministry bought as materials: flour, bean flour, glutinous rice and sesame oil, but no ordinary rice; and the 1615 salt and coal it sets beside the 1736 list come from two other books of the Wanli years, not the handbook.

Used for: Beijing's silk and cotton prices, its clothing prices, its paper, ink and arrow prices, its kitchen cleaver, its salt, its charcoal (with the 1615 handbook's beside it), and its laborer's and craftsman's wages in 1736; its flour, glutinous rice, sesame oil, firewood and coal of 1615 and 1736, the 1615 handbook's wages, and the Qing official and Beijing rates of cash to the tael; Peng Xinwei's index of silver's purchasing power, as cited (Money and prices)
