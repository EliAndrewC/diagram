What it is: Peasant Life in China: A Field Study of Country Life in the Yangtze Valley by Fei Hsiao-tung (Routledge, 1939), the classic village ethnography of Kaixiangong, a silk-and-rice village on the lower Yangtze delta near Lake Tai. It records the place household by household: how the houses stand along the streams, what land a family held, boats, manure pits, and silkworms reared indoors on stacked trays.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a first-hand, room-by-room account of exactly the kind of settlement these maps draw, and almost everything we take from it - where the manure pit sits, that nearly every household kept a boat, that the village grew along its water, where rice was threshed - is arrangement rather than technology, and so belongs to the older village as much as to the one Fei walked through. Its household work is the exception. Women free of farm work is, by Fei's own account, a characteristic of the silk-producing area, where women's reeling once earned about as much as the farm, and it does not hold where there was no reeling: in neighbouring villages with larger farms women had already gone into the fields. Even in Kaihsienkung women worked the pumps whenever the fields had to be watered or drained in a hurry, and by 1936, with reeling taken over by factories, Fei found the freed women's time going to leisure and to leaving for the town rather than to the farm. And the cloth bought by 1936 is the industrial break, not the older practice: nearly every house still had a wooden loom, but Fei found only two of them working, the rest of the village's cloth coming from outside, mainly linen and cotton. The limits are real: it is 1936, with railways, motor roads and a silk economy already disrupted by mechanized filatures, and Fei chose this village because an industrial reform of its silk-making was under way there, which is a bias toward exactly the silk arrangements we take from it; his fieldwork was two months of one summer, by his own statement not a full year's cycle, so the seasonal claims we draw on are a summer observer's; it is one village in one delta rather than a survey; and the copy we can read is a scanned text whose garbles the footnotes carry.
