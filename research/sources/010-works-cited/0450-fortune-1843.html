fortune-1843

Robert Fortune, eyewitness account, 1843 (https://www.gutenberg.org/files/54720/54720-h/54720-h.htm)

What it is: Three Years' Wanderings in the Northern Provinces of China by Robert Fortune, the Scottish botanist and plant collector sent to China in 1843, published in London in 1847; it is his own travel narrative of the tea, silk and cotton districts, with an account of Chinese agriculture and horticulture. We quote his description of where tea was planted in what he calls the north of China (Zhejiang and the lower Yangzi): always on the lower and most fertile sides of the hills, never on the low lands, on small farms of one to four or five acres, every cottager having his own.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is an eyewitness account of tea growing before mechanization, before state terracing programs, and before the twentieth century rearranged the hillsides, which makes it a close premodern observation of how tea gardens sat on a slope. The limits are that it is one traveler's narrative written for a Victorian British readership rather than a survey, and that it covers only the treaty-port provinces he happened to pass through, from Canton up the coast to Shanghai. His "north" is as a rule Zhejiang and the lower Yangzi, rice and tea country, where he traveled; it reaches the dry wheat and millet north only in passing. The lower-slope rule is his for that north alone: he says that in hotter Fujian tea is generally grown at a high elevation among the hills. His "always" and "never" are a traveler's generalization, his picture of the contented tea farmer is idealized, and the "northern districts" he says made the greater part of the tea for foreign markets also take in Fujian's black-tea hills near Fuzhou, so how common tea was there may owe something to that trade. His remark on the poorer classes' blue cotton dress is said in passing, of Shanghai and all the other towns in what he calls the north of China, in the 1840s, and with no count. His Gossypium herbaceum is a species name of 1847, and a later classification puts Chinese Asiatic cotton under G. arboreum, so the record carries it only as what he called the plant, not as its species.

Used for: tea in the north of China on the lower and most fertile hillsides, never the low lands; the poorer classes' blue cotton dress in the 1840s; his naming of the Chinese cotton plant as G. herbaceum (Money and prices)
