ye-mengzhu-yueshibian

葉夢珠 (Ye Mengzhu), 閱世編 ("Notes on the world as I have seen it", title translated), chapter 1, 水利 ("Water control") and 田產一 ("Landed property, part 1"), chapter 7, 食貨一 to 食貨六 ("Food and goods", parts 1 to 6), 種植 ("Planting") and 錢法 ("The coinage"), and chapter 8, 冠服 ("Caps and dress"), Wikisource text (in classical Chinese; https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/閱世編)

What it is: A Shanghai county writer's notes on his own prefecture, Songjiang, now within Shanghai, from the last years of the Ming into the Kangxi reign, recording what land sold for a mu, what rent it was let at, the price of rice in many of those years, and the prices of goods sold there, cloth and salt among them.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives land prices and rents in the same passage, from someone who watched them move, so a price can be set against a rent, though only in 1680 does he say in words that a price is for land of a stated rent (七斗起租之田); his early Shunzhi prices name the land by a figure (石五六斗田, 六七斗田) that on his own scale of rents reads as its rent, without the word. Its figures are one observer's for one prefecture, recalled in round ranges, and read here in a Wikisource transcription with slips of its own (丁末 for 丁未 in chapter 1), so a single numeral or year may be the copyist's; of his land prices only the Chongzhen ones are from ordinary years, and the rest are those of crises: the dear rice after the Ming fell, the tax burden of the early 1660s and the dear rice of 1680. The best land of Huating and Qingpu (Huating and Lou in 1680) sold for two to five times as much as Shanghai's, but it was not the same land. In 1680 it was let at about twice the rent of the Shanghai land he prices (1.4 or 1.5 shi a mu in Huating and Lou, 7 dou in Shanghai), so that year its price against its rent differs far less than its price. His other rents are given once, by grade and undated (from above 1 shi to 1.5 or 1.6 in Huating, Qingpu and Lou; at most 1.1 or 1.2 on Shanghai's best, 6 dou on its worst), tied to no price, and on them the best land's rent is only about a third above Shanghai's best, so the Chongzhen gap in price is only partly explained by rent. So is the early Shunzhi gap, on that sentence's own figures: Huating and Qingpu land of 1.5 or 1.6 shi at 15 or 16 taels a mu, Shanghai land of 6 or 7 dou at 3 or 4, about two and a half times the rent and four or five times the price, and that Shanghai land is of his lower grades, not its best. No one figure speaks for the prefecture. Songjiang was taxed, as he says himself, at the empire's highest rate, so its land sold low against its rent; and he does not say whether a rent was counted in husked rice or in unhusked grain, or what tax the owner paid out of it. His pongee is Shandong pongee (山東繭綢), a silk named for Shandong, whose own was the best; but he says that Shandong kind had died out, and the cloth he prices is a thinner one, looser than floss silk, woven everywhere in Jiaxing, Huzhou, Suzhou and Songjiang, so it is a Jiangnan cloth's price, not the Shandong silk's. He prices it by the chi, not the bolt. He gives its price undated, only as what it sells for "now" against what it fetched under the Ming, so "the late 1600s" is inferred from when he wrote. And it is the price of a cloth he says was getting cheaper every year as more of it was woven and worn, not a steady price. His salt is the salt of a coast that made its own, boiled from the sea with reed fuel, so its price is a salt-making district's, not what salt cost far from the coast. Those prices too mix crises with ordinary years: one qian of silver a jin in the spring of 1651, six or seven fen after the fourth month, and about one fen a jin after that, a rate he gives for no one year; three fen two li in the long rains of 1683; six or seven li in 1688. He gives the late Ming's too: under 1.6 qian of silver for a hundred jin early in Chongzhen (by a scale that weighed about 120), under a fifth of a fen a jin, and five fen in a flood of 1642 or 1643 (the reign year and cyclical year in the text disagree), so his Qing "about one fen" is several times the price of the Ming's ordinary years. In 1683, he says, all the salt was official salt carried in from the prefectural and county towns and private salt had vanished, which he calls a change, so the salt of his other years was at least in part private salt. His rates of copper cash to the tael are never named for his prefecture: they are the capital's against the other provinces' (京師, 外省), the Three Wu's (三吳) or no place's, swinging with the fall of the Ming and the bans on private coin, one of them under a reign year and a cyclical year that disagree (十三年戊寅, 1640 or 1638), and his rice and wheat prices are given now in silver, now in cash; he records no day wage: the nearest is in chapter 1 (水利), 2.5 taels of 工食 silver for each man hired to dredge the Wusong, in a work begun in the twelfth month of Kangxi 10 (about January 1672) and finished in five months, which the landholding households "doubled" (倍加其值); he does not give the days per man, and the men were raised at about one for each jia of land (計田一甲，出夫一名), so it is the rate of a work levied by landholding, not a market wage. His word on cotton's acreage is two undated remarks, impressions and no count: in 食貨四, that in his county it was as widely planted as rice (種植之廣，與粳稻等), and in 種植, that his county's land being high and short of water, farmers grew millet and beans and cotton about half and half (粟菽、棉花參半), which gives no share for the rice he treats apart. His dress by rank under the Ming is written decades after the dynasty fell, from his memory of its last years, as what he lived to see (此余所及見前朝冠服之制也), and it is not the dynasty's code. So he can be cited for what was worn as he recalls it, not for the rule.

Used for: land prices against rents in Songjiang from the 1630s to 1680; the price of pongee in Songjiang in the late 1600s; the price of salt there from 1651 to 1688; its rice, soybeans, wheat, cotton cloth, firewood and pork of the 1670s and 1680s, the rates of cash to the tael he gives, named for the capital, the provinces and the Three Wu rather than for Songjiang, from the 1620s to 1690; and cotton's acreage in his county and dress by rank under the Ming (Money and prices)
