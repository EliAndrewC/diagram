Hokuriku Regional Development Bureau (MLIT), Aga River office, "The Aga River and traditional river engineering" (in Japanese; https://www.hrr.mlit.go.jp/agagawa/river/history_gijyutsu05.html)

What it is: The transcript of a lecture, "Traditional river engineering and its modern evaluation", given on 19 July 2001 by Chino Yasuaki, a lecturer in civil engineering at Nihon University, to the Aga River office's council on traditional river engineering, and published by that office of the national river administration. It describes the two standard pre-Meiji tools: the ushi, a timber trestle weighted with stone baskets, and the waku, a square mortise-jointed timber frame filled with stones thrown in from above. Its drawings of the kinds of trestle and frame come from Doboku ko yoroku, a Meiji-era compilation of traditional methods, and its drawing of the Hamura weir from Josui ki, an Edo-period record of the Tamagawa Aqueduct; others come from the Edo-period manual Sanpo jikata taisei and from modern histories.

Why it applies, and its limits: It describes what the Edo-period frames were and says weirs were built of them, of stone and of reed facing. That sentence follows its account of DIVERSION weirs, which must do their work in flood where an intake weir need only control the ordinary water; but it also names the Hamura intake weir on the Tama River as built of ushi and waku, so on this page frames were not limited to diversion weirs. It names no weir as a village's intake on a brook: the Hamura weir took the Tama River's water into the Tamagawa Aqueduct as water supply, and it does not say how large the rivers its other weirs stand on were. Its definition of the square frame is given under groynes, and the one dimension it gives for a frame is for the largest kind of trestle, the daiseigyu, about 9 meters long and 5 meters high. It is a modern specialist's overview of period manuals and drawings, given as a talk, not a referenced paper.

Used for: that Edo-period weirs were built of timber frames, stone and reed facing; what a square timber frame (waku) was; the Hamura intake weir built of frames (Water)
