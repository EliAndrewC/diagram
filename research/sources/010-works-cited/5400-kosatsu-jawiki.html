kosatsu-jawiki

ja.wikipedia 高札 ("public notice board", title translated) (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/高札)

What it is: Japanese Wikipedia's article on 高札 (kosatsu), the boards on which laws, carriage-fee schedules and prohibitions were written up and posted publicly, and on the kosatsuba where those boards stood.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the edict-board as an institution, the shogunate's order siting the boards at conspicuous places - points of heavy traffic, barrier stations, ports, the ends of large bridges, and the entrances and centers of towns and villages - and Edo's six great boards, Nihonbashi first among them, which is what puts a notice board on every map and tells us where. Its limits are that it has been flagged since 2019 as lacking sufficient citations and cites nothing for its siting rule, though the signed Heibonsha and Nipponica entries on Kotobank (kotobank-kosatsu) bear that rule out in substance (a town's central crossroads, its entrances and its bridge ends; the key points of towns and villages); that its counts - the six great boards and 35 others - are Edo's, the six hedged even there; and that its notices posted at the gates of courts and prefectural offices are Meiji practice, from the boards' abolition in 1873 to the official gazette of 1883, under a modern state's institutions and after the period the setting is modeled on.

Used for: the edict board institution - standing law, rate tables, ban edicts; Nihonbashi as the first of Edo's six great boards; where the boards stood; notices at the gates of courts and prefectural offices after the boards ended; a board written in ink on wood, re-inked only with permission, and the lords' own boards (Estates and other compounds; Trades and services)
