jawiki-yashikigami

ja.wikipedia 屋敷神 ("homestead deity", title translated); minka-en.com 屋敷神 ("homestead deity", title translated); satologue.com 屋敷神様 ("homestead deity", title translated) (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/屋敷神)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on 屋敷神 (yashikigami, the guardian deity of a household's own plot), together with matching pages on the same subject from two Japanese folk-culture sites, minka-en.com and satologue.com. They cover the patterns of household worship - the Wikipedia article gives three, after the folklorist Naoe Hiroji (every household keeping its own; only the old main house; the kin gathering at the main house), which come to two, every household or the main house alone - and the form the shrine takes, which is almost always a small stone or wooden hokora rather than a building.

Why it applies, and its limits: It tells us the form of the household shrine - a small stone or wooden hokora, almost never a shrine hall - and where it commonly stood, which the page gives as the northwest or the northeast of the plot without preferring either; the page goes on to say that treating the northeast as the unlucky demon gate is a later overlay from Onmyodo and a comparatively recent custom, so our maps take a corner and not a compass rule. Its limits are that it is a summary which Japanese Wikipedia itself flags as under-sourced and possibly original research, a heavier discount than the usual encyclopedia caveat; that it describes a folk custom that varied by region and household, so it gives a common pattern and not a rule; and that it dates none of it beyond an 1803 picture of a yashikigami, so nothing here fixes the practice to the era our maps depict. The order it does give puts both things we take from it late: the hokora is a later change from a bare ritual site with an old tree or a natural stone, and the shrine spread from the main house to every household only as branch houses rose. It also says the custom is absent where Jodo Shinshu prevails, and that some households worship in woods a little way from the house rather than in the plot.

Used for: the two patterns; a small stone or wooden hokora at the plot's corner, often the NW or NE.
