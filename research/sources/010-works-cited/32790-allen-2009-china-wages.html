allen-2009-china-wages

Robert C. Allen, Jean-Pascal Bassino, Debin Ma, Christine Moll-Murata and Jan Luiten van Zanden, "Wages, Prices, and Living Standards in China, 1738-1925: in Comparison with Europe, Japan and India", London School of Economics, Economic History working paper 123/09, July 2009 (https://www.lse.ac.uk/asset-library/information/wp123.pdf)

What it is: A study by five economic historians of what unskilled workers earned in Qing China, from the wages the Dutch East India Company paid at Canton, the guild records of Suzhou's cloth calenderers, government regulations and other sources, priced against a basket of goods so that they can be compared with Europe, Japan and India.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the day wage of a laborer in Canton and the Yangzi delta, a farm day laborer's wage and a year-hired farm hand's earnings, with the price of rice that turns them into food. Its baseline wage is a regression estimate pooling many sources, not a recorded wage; its farm hand's year is another historian's reckoning it quotes; and its 250 working days a year is a convention it adopts for comparison. Its city wages come from the empire's two greatest trading and cloth cities: dock and ship-repair workers paid by the Dutch company at Canton, and Suzhou's calenderers, strong men paid a little above laborers, whose day wage it converts from piece rates using a study of the early twentieth century. These city wages run about twice its empire-wide baseline. Its farm day wage of 0.045 taels is an average it expects to run high, since day laborers were hired mostly at planting and harvest, and it does not know whether food was given on top.

Used for: the Qing laborer's day wage in the cities and the countryside, and the 250-day working year as a convention; Beijing's wages of 1700 and 1865 beside a mule hire, and Suzhou's beside pongee (Money and prices)
