allen-2009-china-wages

Robert C. Allen, Jean-Pascal Bassino, Debin Ma, Christine Moll-Murata and Jan Luiten van Zanden, "Wages, Prices, and Living Standards in China, 1738-1925: in Comparison with Europe, Japan and India", London School of Economics, Economic History working paper 123/09, July 2009 (https://www.lse.ac.uk/asset-library/information/wp123.pdf)

What it is: A study by five economic historians of what unskilled workers earned in China from 1738 to 1925, chiefly under the Qing, from the wages the Dutch East India Company paid at Canton, the piece rates of Suzhou's cloth calenderers carved on stone tablets after their wage disputes, government regulations and other sources, priced against a basket of goods so that they can be compared with Europe, Japan and India.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the day wage of a laborer in Canton and the Yangzi delta, a farm day laborer's wage and a year-hired farm hand's earnings, with the price of rice that turns them into food. Its baseline wage is a regression estimate pooling many sources, not a recorded wage; its farm hand's year is another historian's reckoning it quotes; and its 250 working days a year is a convention it adopts for comparison. Its city wages come from the empire's two greatest trading and cloth cities: dock and ship-repair workers paid by the Dutch company at Canton, and Suzhou's calenderers, strong men paid a little above laborers, whose day wage it converts from piece rates using a study of the early twentieth century. These city wages run about twice its empire-wide baseline. Its farm day wage of 0.045 taels is an average it expects to run high, since day laborers were hired mostly at planting and harvest, and it does not know whether food was given on top. Its Beijing wage of 1700 is also not a recorded wage: it is the regression's predicted value for the capital region, whose wages it found well above the rest of the country, perhaps because the government favored the capital. Its Beijing wages of 1807-1902 come from one rural fuel store's account books near Beijing, kept in a local count of copper cash that changed about 1860, which it converts to silver. The authors call that wage extraordinarily low, though the workers were also fed, and use only its trend, never its level. This record uses its level all the same, and only as an order of magnitude, because it runs low: it is a cash wage without the food; it is the year-round average, which the authors chose as the lowest of Gamble's three series because it takes in the winter slack; and it is halved on the authors' reading that the local cash was worth half the official count, a conversion they call a major hurdle.

Used for: the Qing laborer's day wage in the cities and the countryside, and the 250-day working year as a convention; Beijing's wages of 1700 and 1865 beside a mule hire, and Suzhou's beside pongee; the rural Beijing laborer's wages of 1807-1902 beside rents and theater prices (Money and prices)
