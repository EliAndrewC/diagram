nordhaus-1996-lighting-price-indexes

William D. Nordhaus, "Do Real-Output and Real-Wage Measures Capture Reality? The History of Lighting Suggests Not", in Timothy F. Bresnahan and Robert J. Gordon (eds.), The Economics of New Goods, University of Chicago Press for the NBER, 1996, chapter c6064 (https://www.nber.org/system/files/chapters/c6064/c6064.pdf)

What it is: An economist's measure of the true price of light, from Babylonian and earlier lamps to the compact fluorescent bulb of 1992, set against the American price indexes of 1800 to 1992, with a rough sorting of American consumption in 1991 by how much each kind of good has changed since the early nineteenth century.

Why it applies, and its limits: It shows which goods an ordinary price index carries well over two centuries and which it does not, the question behind carrying a basket that far. Its sorting of goods is, in its own words, extremely rough, and its evidence is chiefly American and British. The sorting is of an American budget of 1991, so its shares (about 28 percent of spending little changed, 37 percent changed past recognition) are a modern household's, not a premodern one's. What carries to the setting is which kinds of goods an index follows well (food, clothing) and which it misses (light, heating, transport, medicine), not those proportions.

Used for: which goods a price index carries over two centuries and which it misses (Money and prices)
