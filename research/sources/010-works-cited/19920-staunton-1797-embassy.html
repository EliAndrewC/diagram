staunton-1797-embassy

Sir George Staunton, An Authentic Account of an Embassy from the King of Great Britain to the Emperor of China, volume 2 (London, 1797), full text on the Internet Archive (https://archive.org/stream/authenticaccount02stau/authenticaccount02stau_djvu.txt)

What it is: The account of the British embassy to the Emperor of China by its secretary, taken chiefly from the papers of the ambassador, the commander of the expedition and other members of the embassy, and printed in London in 1797; the second volume follows the embassy from the Pei-ho toward the capital and to the northern frontier, then south through the interior to Canton, and describes Chinese farming along the way.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is an eyewitness account printed before modern times, so what it saw of farm practice is a premodern attestation - here, large earthen vessels buried to the rim on farms and near paths and roads to collect night soil. Its limits: the travelers were foreigners passing through; the manure passage is written of Chinese practice in general and names no province, so it cannot place the jars in any one region; it calls them only large earthen vessels set out for labourers and passers-by, and gives no size, number or spacing and no observer, so it attests the form and cannot size or space it on a map; its statement that the lower orders wore cotton is a passing generalization by travelers, with no count behind it; the online text is an uncorrected machine reading of the scan, with broken words and run-together letters.

Used for: manure vessels sunk in the ground on farms and by paths and roads in eighteenth-century China (Field archetypes); the lower orders' cotton clothing in the 1790s (Money and prices)
