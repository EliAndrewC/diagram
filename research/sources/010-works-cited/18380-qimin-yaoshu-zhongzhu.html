qimin-yaoshu-zhongzhu

賈思勰 齊民要術 卷第五 ("Essential Techniques for the Common People, Scroll Five", title translated) (Jia Sixie, Qimin Yaoshu, c. 540), 種竹第五十一 (the chapter on planting bamboo), 種榆白楊第四十六 (the chapter on planting elm and white poplar) and 種槐柳楸梓梧柞第五十 (the chapter on planting pagoda tree, willow, qiu, catalpa, paulownia and oak), Chinese Wikisource (https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/%E9%BD%8A%E6%B0%91%E8%A6%81%E8%A1%93/%E5%8D%B7%E7%AC%AC%E4%BA%94)

What it is: Book 5 of the sixth-century Chinese agricultural treatise, on trees and useful plants, in a Wikisource transcription; its bamboo chapter says what ground bamboo wants, which shoots to lift, and where in the garden to set them, with the author's own note giving the reason; its elm and white-poplar chapter says that no grain grows in an elm's shade, that the shade reaches east, west and north as far as the tree, and that elms are sown on the north edge of garden ground (園地北畔) and raised there as a thicket, to be moved in their third year; its chapter on timber trees sets catalpa and qiu two paces apart each way and sows an oak wood broadcast on a hillside.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a primary text from long before the setting's period that places bamboo on one side of a planted plot - the northeast corner of a garden, because bamboo spreads toward the southwest and will fill the garden in a few years. It describes North China in the sixth century and a garden or orchard (園), not a homestead or its windbreak, and says nothing of Japan, of a kitchen drain or of service sheds. The elm chapter is the same: its shade rule is the author's rule of thumb, not a measurement, and its grain is the dry-farmed grain of the north, not paddy rice; its north edge is a seedbed, where elms are sown and grown as a thicket until they are moved in their third year (it does not say where to), not a standing row or a village's windbreak; the elms grown as a crop for wood, pods and leaves get a plot of their own near a market, and the same chapter warns against elms along a field's edge (they bring sparrows that eat the grain, and grow crooked) and advises giving them a plot of their own. Its chapter on timber trees gives a spacing for catalpa and qiu grown for sale in North China, two paces each way (catalpa 'must grow large, so is not planted close'), and for qiu a count, 120 trees to a row of two mu and 600 to five rows, sold after ten years at that spacing - the count of a ten-year plantation on ploughed ground, not of an old or natural wood - and its oak wood is sown broadcast with no spacing at all; the length of the pace it uses is not stated.

Used for: bamboo set in a garden's northeast corner so that it spreads southwest (Homesteads); from its elm chapter, that no grain grows in a tree's shade, that the shade reaches east, west and north as far as the tree, and that elms are planted on the garden's north edge (Vegetation and terrain); from its timber-tree chapter, catalpa and qiu planted two paces apart each way, and an oak wood sown broadcast on a hill, cut for rafters and purlins and sprouting again (Vegetation and terrain)
