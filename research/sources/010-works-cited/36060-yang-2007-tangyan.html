Yang Guoan, 「Ponds, Weirs and Irrigation: Water Management Organizations and Popular Order in Rural Southern Hubei in the Ming and Qing」 (translated; original: 「塘堰與灌溉：明清時期鄂南鄉村的水利組織與民間秩序」), Journal of History and Anthropology 5(1) (2007) (in Chinese; https://schina-publication.hkust.edu.hk/sites/default/files/pubs/Jour-05.1.A03.pdf)

What it is: A historian's study of Chongyang county in southeastern Hubei, built on the Huapi weir's own register of water documents (recompiled in 1948 from documents of 1583 onward), with the county gazetteers, lineage genealogies and land deeds. It gives the land the county's old ponds and its great weir watered, how the weir's upkeep was shared out by acreage, and how each paddy was tied to the pond or weir that watered it.

Why it applies, and its limits: Its figure for the Huapi weir (7,940 mu, through three canals) comes from the county gazetteer of 1866, and the rule for sharing its upkeep by acreage comes from Ming documents copied into the weir register. Its figures for single ponds (210 to 730 mu) are the county's five old named ponds, the largest it has, and the paper takes them from the county gazetteer of 1991 without dating the land they watered: they bound a pond's reach from above, not the land an ordinary village pond watered. It is one hilly county of the middle Yangtze, where ponds and weirs ordered the farmland; the weir's acreage is the one the Qing gazetteer registers, in the mu of its day. That every paddy belonged to one pond or weir is the author's generalization, shown by a single deed of 1865 from Yuezhou in neighboring Hunan, whose plots are each tagged to a pond but could all also draw stream water by waterwheel.

Used for: the land single ponds and the Huapi weir watered, and every paddy's tie to one pond (Fields)
