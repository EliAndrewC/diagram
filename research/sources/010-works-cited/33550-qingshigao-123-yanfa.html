清史稿 (Draft History of the Qing, title translated), volume 123, 食貨四, 鹽法 ("Food and goods, part 4: the salt laws"), Wikisource text (in classical Chinese; https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%B8%85%E5%8F%B2%E7%A8%BF/%E5%8D%B7123)

What it is: The treatise on the Qing salt monopoly in the draft dynastic history compiled after the dynasty fell, summarizing its districts, taxes and reforms from the 1600s to 1911 and quoting the memorials and edicts behind them.

Why it applies, and its limits: It quotes the officials' own figures for what salt cost at the saltworks, what the state fixed it at for the Changlu district, what a censor said it sold for in copper cash, and what a person spent on it a day. Each figure is one district's and one year's, given in an argument over salt policy, not counted in a market: the saltworks price (1 or 2 cash a catty) is Tao Shu's for the Lianghuai coast about 1830, given against taxing salt at the works; the Changlu price is the one fixed in 1688; the censor's copper prices (16 cash a catty under Kangxi, still 16 in 1728 with some sold down to 13 or 14) are for Changlu; and the 1 or 2 cash a day is the Jiaqing emperor's, in an edict of 1800 refusing a rise. The history was compiled in the 1910s and 1920s from the court's records, not from markets. The censor's rates of 1,400 to 2,000 cash a tael are about twice those other sources give for the same decades, and he does not say how he counted his cash, a doubt that reaches his 16-cash price as much as his rates.

Used for: the price of salt at the saltworks and at the official price in Qing China; the Changlu censor's rates of cash to the tael under Kangxi and in 1728; the cuts of 1791 and 1848 and the ticket reforms of 1832 and 1849 (Money and prices)
