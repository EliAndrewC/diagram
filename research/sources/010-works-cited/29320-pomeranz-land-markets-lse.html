Kenneth Pomeranz, "Land Markets in Late Imperial and Republican China" (draft conference paper), London School of Economics, Global Economic History Network conference 6 (https://lse.ac.uk/economic-history/assets/documents/research/gehn/gehnconferences/conf6/conf6-kpomeranz.pdf)

What it is: A conference paper, marked as a draft not to be quoted, cited or distributed without the author's permission, so its published form (Continuity and Change 23:1, 2008) may differ; undated, about 2005 by its latest references, surveying how land was sold, pawned, rented and ruled over in Ming, Qing and Republican China, drawing mostly on other scholars' studies of contract collections, lawsuit records and 1920s-1940s surveys. It covers live sales, permanent tenancy (one field, two masters), lineage trusts, middlemen and how well the markets worked, with most weight on the Qing.

Why it applies, and its limits: It describes who owned and rented land and on what terms in Jiangnan, Fujian, Guangdong, Sichuan and North China, and says the one-field-two-masters system was concentrated in the developed southeast and far from universal. Its evidence runs from the Ming to the 1940s, with Republican-era surveys among it; its figures are estimates, not counts: live-sale prices of 60-80% of an outright price are other scholars' (McAleavy, Buoye, Macauley), and tenants on "perhaps 15%" of North China land against almost half in the Yangzi Delta is the author's own uncited rough figure, and it is about legal and economic arrangements, not about how a village or field looked. The 60-80% is for the southeast's live sales (the studies it cites are of Fujian and Guangdong); the paper itself gives North China's 1940s dian at half to two-thirds of the market price (Pepper, from land-reform reports) and one Sichuan live sale at under a quarter, so the ratio varied by region and era. Its village interest rates are not measured: 36% a year is the rate it says "is often used", uncited, and 12% a deliberately low assumption, both inside one worked example; it gives no return on land as a share of its price (only rent at about half the main crop), so a comparison of credit with land's return takes the land side from elsewhere.

Used for: a live sale's price against an outright sale, and village credit's rates against a return on land (Money and prices)
