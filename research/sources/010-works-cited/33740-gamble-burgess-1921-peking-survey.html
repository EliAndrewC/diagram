What it is: A survey of Peking made by two Americans, under the Peking Chinese Young Men's Christian Association and the Princeton University Center in China, with Chinese field workers between September 1918 and December 1919, from questionnaires, interviews and official reports; its chapter on commercial life describes the city's gilds, their wages and their strikes, with a table comparing each gild's wages then with those of 3 to 50 years before.

Why it applies, and its limits: It gives the only craftsmen's wages of late Qing Beijing found in this record, but the past wages are what the gilds told the surveyors in 1918-1919, not entries in any account, and the table dates them only as so many "years ago". It prints them in dollars, cents and taels without saying how the earlier ones were paid or counted, and labels most of them with no period, which this record reads as monthly from the survey's own account of gild wages. That same account says the ordinary worker of 1918-1919 also had his room and board from his employer, so the figures are most likely cash paid on top of keep, not a worker's whole income (the dyers had no board but bought rice from their employers far below the market price; the painters boarded themselves); the survey does not say whether board was given in the earlier years. And they are the rates of the imperial capital's gilds, which a smaller town need not have paid.

Used for: Beijing's gild wages of about 1868 to 1894, and a jade-workers' piece rate of 1885, beside rents and theater prices (Money and prices)
