ehime-kenshi-shuso-shuraku

Ehime Prefecture, Ehime kenshi: chishi II (Toyo tobu) (1988), chapter 2 "Shuso heiya", section 4 "交通・集落" ("Communications and settlements", title translated), in the database Ehime no kioku (in Japanese; https://www.i-manabi.jp/system/regionals/regionals/ecode:2/32/view/4555)

What it is: The prefectural history's subsection on alluvial fans and where settlements sit on them, in its geography of the Shuso plain: which settlements sit at the head of each fan and which at its foot where the water that sank into the fan rises again, with three villages described in detail and their water supply (Tataki at the head of the Sekiya river fan, Naka-Nagano at its foot, and Furuta on a small fan laid out anew after a fire of 1805).

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a scholarly regional geography that names the settlements strung in a line along a fan's foot, where the water that sank into the fan comes up again - the spring line of the textbooks, seen on the ground. Its limits: it describes the landscape of 1988 and does not date the arc of villages at the fan's foot; its Edo-period history (Tataki moved down the fan in 1655-58, Furuta laid out anew after the fire of 1805) is local tradition, and its accounts of daily water use reach back only to the Meiji and Taisho eras. The spring line is its general statement: the one fan-foot village it describes, Naka-Nagano, had its groundwater 5-6 m down and drew its water from shared wells and, into the Taisho era, from an irrigation channel off the Nakayama river, its main houses on the spots of shallow groundwater, so it supports a row that follows the fan's foot and its shallow groundwater, not houses standing at springs on the surface. Its row is a row of villages - the one it counts, Naka-Nagano, had some fifty households - not a row of houses, and it gives no frontage or spacing.

Used for: settlements lined up in an arc at the foot of an alluvial fan, where the spring water rises (Homesteads)
