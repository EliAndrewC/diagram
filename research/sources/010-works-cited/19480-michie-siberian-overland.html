michie-siberian-overland

Alexander Michie, The Siberian Overland Route from Peking to Petersburg, London: John Murray, 1864 (Project Gutenberg; https://www.gutenberg.org/files/45167/45167-h/45167-h.htm)

What it is: A British merchant's account of his journey in 1863 from Beijing across Mongolia and Siberia to St Petersburg, beginning with the voyage from Shanghai to Tianjin, the road on to Beijing, where he lodged at a city inn and hired pack mules and mule litters for the road to Kalgan, and on to Nankou, the village at the mouth of the pass, where he stayed at the inn the night before crossing it, and then across the Gobi, where he notes the Mongols' brick tea.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is an eyewitness of north China's inns in the late Qing, in 1863. One is a city inn in Beijing whose courtyard held travelers' horses and mules. The other is the village inn at Nankou, on the road north, whose courtyard was crowded with travelers' gear and animals and where he slept in what he calls our dormitory. He never says that room was shared with strangers, so a common dormitory is our reading of his word, not his statement. It is one foreigner's passing impressions, written with a traveler's disdain, of a few inns on one road in the north; it gives no plan and no size. It also records one hire, on 16 August 1863: eight pack mules at four taels each and three mule litters at eight taels each, for the four days' road to Kalgan. He gives the total as sixty taels, though those rates add up to fifty-six; a few lines later he says they somehow had nine mules instead of eight, which at four taels would make sixty, though he does not say the ninth was paid for. By his own account the dealers held out until he was ready to pay "an extortionate price", so this was a price asked of foreigners in a hurry. The litters were not a luxury: he says they were the only way through the Nankou pass, and luxurious only beside a cart. It is not what a local traveler or carrier paid, and it is a ceiling rather than a going rate. Its brick tea is a single remark made in the Gobi at the start of September 1863: that silver is not much used among the Mongols, "their only real currency being very coarse brick tea." It describes herders on the Mongolian steppe, outside China's farming country. In the same passage a lama is paid in sycee silver for a camel, and Michie adds that the Mongols are not ignorant of the value of money. So it shows tea bricks used as money on the steppe. It does not show that silver was unknown there, and it says nothing of tea as money inside China.

Used for: the inn yard holding travelers' animals and the common dormitory, in 1863 (Estates and other compounds); the hire of pack mules and mule litters from Peking to Kalgan, and brick tea as the Mongols' currency, in 1863 (Money and prices)
