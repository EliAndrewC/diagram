bernstein-fire-and-earth

Andrew Bernstein, "Fire and Earth: The Forging of Modern Cremation in Meiji Japan", JJRS 27/3-4 (2000), full text: https://nirc.nanzan-u.ac.jp/journal/6/article/1039/pdf/download (2026-09-29)

What it is: Andrew Bernstein's article "Fire and Earth: The Forging of Modern Cremation in Meiji Japan", published in the Japanese Journal of Religious Studies 27/3-4 (2000), a scholarly account of the Meiji government's short-lived national ban on cremation and its repeal, drawn from the Council of State's records, Tokyo newspapers of the day and the work of Japanese historians (its count of Senju's cremation grounds is itself taken from Asaka and Yagisawa 1983). The journal's full text is a public scanned PDF; we cite its account of Edo's cremation grounds clustered at Senju (p. 303) and of the Tokyo police's proposal of 22 May 1873 to remove the crematories from the crowded wards, because the smoke and "severe stench" injured health (p. 307).

Why it applies, and its limits: It bears directly on where a city puts the burning of its dead and why, which is the rule a capital map needs when it places a crematory ground relative to the built-up area. Its limits: its subject is Meiji Japan, later than the period our settlements are drawn from, so it shows the Edo arrangement coming under pressure rather than describing it at length; and it is one historian's reading of Tokyo, the one city, not a survey of provincial towns. The health reason given in 1873 is, by the author's own account (p. 308), a Meiji concern for hygiene. The Edo-period precedent he gives had a different reason: in 1669 the shogun moved cremation out to Senju because the smell offended him at his family's tombs in Ueno, which protected a sacred and elite ground rather than the townspeople. So the article supports keeping the smoke away from what the city most values, but does not show a premodern public-health rule. Cremation was also a minority practice even in Edo, where most of the dead were buried whole (p. 303), and the article shows it condemned in Song China and banned under the Ming and Qing (pp. 304-306). A crematory ground on a capital map therefore draws on the Japanese half of the setting only.
