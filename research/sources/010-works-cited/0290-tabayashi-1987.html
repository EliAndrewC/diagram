tabayashi-1987

Akira Tabayashi, "Irrigation Systems in Japan", Geographical Review of Japan Series B 60(1): 41-65 (1987) (https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/grj1984b/60/1/60_1_41/_article/-char/en/)

What it is: A peer-reviewed article by Akira Tabayashi, "Irrigation Systems in Japan", in the Geographical Review of Japan, Series B volume 60 number 1 (1987), pages 41-65. It classifies Japan's irrigation systems by their water source - river, pond, lake, groundwater, torrent - maps where each kind predominates, and traces when each was built, from the Kofun and Nara period works of the west to the Sengoku and Edo works of the east.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is the right kind of source for what it is used for - a Japanese geographer's account of Japanese irrigation from the third century to 1867, at national scale and with named systems at village scale, describing the gravity-fed logic that is premodern in origin - so the siting of a reservoir above its fields and the tapering of canals as they are tapped rest on it directly. It does not show supply and drain separated before modern times: in its delta example, Magabuchi on the Tone, the same canals carried irrigation and drainage until the projects after World War II, and its irrigation channel and drainage channel beside every 30-are block belong to the Farm Consolidation Projects begun in 1963, so it dates that separation as modern and supports no premodern separation. Its other limits are that the straight secondary canals of its Tedori fan example were rearranged under the Arable Land Reorganization of the early 1900s (the 1899 law) and are mapped as they stood in 1976; that the torrent systems it describes, small streams dammed and led through short canals, were built largely in the Meiji period, though the form is older; that its Toro figures (a canal over 370 m and 7.5 ha of paddy) are a third-century excavation reported secondhand from Sugihara (1968), the extent excavated rather than a whole tract; that it gives no paddy depth; and that it does not say a storage pond is tapped by a single outlet - its largest pond, Mannoike, emptied through five drain gates into three main canals - so that claim on our maps rests elsewhere.

Used for: tameike siting above its fields; one outlet; canals taper as they are tapped; supply/drain separation at system level; small streams dammed and led through short canals; a diversion dam feeding a main canal that division works tap; the Toro canal of over 370 m and its 7.5 ha of paddy, and a branch canal serving a whole village, for the size of one tract (Fields); the separation of irrigation from drainage canals dated to the postwar consolidation projects
