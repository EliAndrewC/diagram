Federico Marcon, The Knowledge of Nature and the Nature of Knowledge in Early Modern Japan (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2015), chapter 13, "Nature as Accumulation Strategy: Sato Nobuhiro and the Synthesis of Honzogaku and Keizaigaku", as it stands in a pack of readings on the University of Warwick's website (https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/arts/history/ghcc/event/events/marcon_readings_-_all_files.pdf)

What it is: A history of nature studies in early modern Japan. Its last chapter sets out the Satsuma domain's economy as the background of the thinker Sato Nobuhiro, and in passing gives the rice the domain paid the villages of its southern islands for their sugar crop, from the second half of the 1700s, within the quota and over it.

Why it applies, and its limits: It is a second statement, in English and from another Japanese study (Matsui Masato's 1985 book on the lord Shimazu Shigehide, which was not read), of the rate at which Satsuma took sugar for rice. The rate is an aside in a book on another subject: it gives no year, names no island, and says the rice was paid for a kin of "sugarcane", where the domain's own laws pay rice at nearly the same rate for a kin of sugar.

Used for: the rice Satsuma paid for a kin of the islands' sugar crop in the later 1700s (Money and prices)
