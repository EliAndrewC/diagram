buck-1930-farm-economy

John Lossing Buck, Chinese Farm Economy: A Study of 2866 Farms in Seventeen Localities and Seven Provinces in China, University of Chicago Press, 1930, full text on the Internet Archive (https://archive.org/stream/chinesefarmecono0000john/chinesefarmecono0000john_djvu.txt)

What it is: The first of the two great farm surveys of republican China by John Lossing Buck and his students at the University of Nanking: 2,866 farms in seventeen localities of seven provinces, visited in 1921-1925, with tables of land, crops, livestock, labor and living standards.

Why it applies, and its limits: It counts, farm by farm, how many kept each kind of animal, which is the measure the record needs for how common the farmyard chicken was. Its limits are that it describes the 1920s, after the empire, read back here into the late imperial countryside - a countryside that by then also sold eggs to an industrial export trade through the treaty ports' egg-processing plants, which Buck's East Central localities sat nearest; Buck himself says only that chickens were commoner there "perhaps because of the nearness to markets", and the export trade is this record's reading of what those markets were, so its 82 per cent may run above an imperial-era share; that its farms were gathered by his students and field investigators, not drawn at random; that seventeen localities in north and east-central China are not the whole country; and that the Internet Archive's text is an OCR whose tables are garbled, so only its prose and the legible average row of its table of land not cultivated are quoted. Its farmstead figure, buildings and threshing floors on two per cent of the farm area, comes from that same table, which covers only 1,353 farms in seven localities of four provinces, not all 2,866; it is a share of a whole farm's land, scattered fields included, not the size of a house lot; and Buck names Su Hsien, Anhwei, where many large farms were sampled, as the exception. Its grave figures carry the same limits of era, sampling and region (the egg trade aside), and more: the 2.6 per cent of farm land in graves is computed from only five localities having only level land, 9.1 per cent the highest (Wuchin, Kiangsu); the two checks by his students, 7.8 and 5.2 per cent, were made in 1929 on 135 fields in Kweiteh, Honan and on 8.2 square kilometres near Changchow; Buck himself says grave land is often reported inside the crop area; and his graves placed "in every conceivable position in a field" are those of regions having no hill land - where hills were near, as at Kiangning, the graves went to the hill land - so graves scattered through the fields fit a place on the plain, not one beside hills. The shares are also those of long-settled, crowded plains after centuries of burial; a younger place would hold fewer. Its labor figures are of the same farms and years. Its "about one-fourth of the year" on productive work is Buck's inference from his man-work units, each a ten-hour day of crop or livestock work only, so it leaves out everything but crop and livestock work. Buck himself allows only for "some other work", rainy days and holidays, says these "could scarcely account" for the gap, and concludes that farmers had "several idle months each year"; that housework, spinning and other side work, fuel and manure gathering and marketing filled much of that time, so that the figure understates the working year, is this record's reading against his. Its women's share of the work, a little over a fifth, is of farm work alone and is 31.6 per cent in East Central China against 11.8 in the north, a gap Buck puts down, probably, to foot-binding there. Of its winter work, the felt shoes made at home for sale are household handicraft, which Buck says was done only "to a very limited extent" and places particularly at Su Hsien, Anhwei; the young men's going to the cities for winter jobs is the 1920s cities' labor market, not the empire's. Its clothing count covers 2,370 households in 13 localities of six provinces in 1922-1925, not all 2,866. It gives the share of households having clothing from raw material off their own farm, 23.5 per cent (37 in the north, 4.5 in East Central China), and the share purchasing clothing, 93.5; the two overlap, and they count households, not how much of a family's cloth came from either. The farm's material was mostly cotton, and Buck says how far it was spun and woven is not known and that it probably went largely to padding winter garments and a limited amount of yarn, so the 23.5 is not a share of households weaving. The count also comes after factory yarn and cloth had reached the countryside through the treaty ports, which is this record's reading and not Buck's, and his table does not divide purchased cloth into hand-woven and factory-made; its purchased share may run above an imperial-era one, and it is the nearest count to a premodern share, not a premodern share.

Used for: the share of Chinese farms keeping chickens and the ground a farm's buildings and threshing floor took (Homesteads); the share of Chinese farm land under graves, and where in the fields the graves stood (Religion and the dead); productive farm work as a share of the year, women's share of farm work, winter work, and farm households' clothing as the nearest count to any premodern share (Money and prices)
