ja.wikipedia 家庭菜園 ("home vegetable garden", title translated) (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/家庭菜園)

What it is: The Japanese Wikipedia article on the household kitchen garden, a tertiary entry which records that ground a Japanese household cultivates to feed itself is called yashikibatake, the homestead field. It also gives the plot a short history: its origin can be traced as far back as the Yayoi period, and by the Edo period such a plot stood inside the residences of the warrior class as well as those of farmers.

Why it applies, and its limits: We use it for the object and its name - the bed beside the house that grows a household's own food, as distinct from the fields it farms for the tax and the market - which is what the garden on our homesteads is, and its two dates are what tell us the plot is far older than the modern hobby gardening, the European royal kitchen gardens and the Javanese pekarangan the rest of the article is about. Its limits are sharp and worth stating: the article gives no area and no count, so it cannot support the size band our maps hold a garden to, and that band is labeled a guess on the page; its history is one sentence carrying two dates rather than an account, so it fixes that the plot existed early and nothing about what it looked like; and though it names a corner of ground joined to the homestead first, and reports Yanagita Kunio calling the plot the field attached to the house, it says such cultivation is also often done on odd scraps of ground away from the residence (beside the paddies and cash-crop fields, at the edge of a riverbed), so it supports the homestead's ground as one usual place for the plot, but not that every household's plot lay there, still less against the house itself. It names no crops, only "crops for the household's own eating", so it sets the plot apart from the tax-rice paddy and the cash-crop fields but cannot tell us that no grain grew there, or that no grain field lay beside a house; that part of our drawing is our own inference. Its three local names for the bed (Senzaibata, Saenba, Kado no hatake) are said only to vary by region, with no region or date given for any of them, so they serve as names a Japanese household might use and cannot be tied to one kind of country or one period. And its short account of the plot rests on one modern folklore dictionary (Mori Takao, ed., Jū no minzoku jiten, 2019) and on twentieth-century folklorists (Yanagita Kunio, Miyamoto Tsuneichi), so apart from its one dated sentence (the Yayoi origin and the Edo warrior houses, cited only to that dictionary, with no period evidence given for either) it describes the plot as folklorists recorded it in modern times, not as period records show it.

Used for: the name of the household's own cultivated ground, the yashikibatake; and that the plot a household works beside its own house is that ground for its own consumption (research/contents.json#fields, "Dry fields and their crops (hatake)"); the page says nothing of which crops, so that no grain plot stands beside a house is our inference, not this source's; the bed's local names, and that it was often on spare ground away from the house (Homesteads)
