ming-huidian-136

明會典 (Collected Statutes of the Ming, title translated), volume 136, Board of Punishments, part 11, the Siku Quanshu edition (in Chinese; Wikisource transcription; https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/明㑹典_(四庫全書本)/卷136)

What it is: A volume of the Ming government's collected statutes (ordered in 1497, finished in 1502, printed in 1509) on the stolen goods and fines its courts recovered, ending with a table of the value at which each kind of stolen thing was reckoned, from gold and silk to rice, livestock, fruit and clothing, in the state's paper money.

Why it applies, and its limits: Its table values persimmons, pears, small apples and dried persimmons in the same paper money as rice and silver, so their worth can be set against each other. The table is a court's standard for judging a theft, though its own rule says goods are valued at the price current where the crime was done; it carries no year, standing after a ruling of 1497, and it gives no wage in the same money. Its paper money is the court's unit of account: the table's 80 strings to an ounce of silver is a legal rate, far above what the notes then fetched in the market, so its figures set goods against one another and are no price of the notes themselves.

Used for: the Ming valuation of fruit against rice and silver (Money and prices)
