What did an empty charcoal bale cost, and who wove it?

The setting's price list prices charcoal by the bale and says nothing of the bale itself: the woven bag, its end caps and its ropes. The findings below give what the historical record holds on the empty bale in Edo-period Japan - its price, its makers, the trade in it - and on the basket that held charcoal in China.

No price for an empty charcoal bale of the Edo period was found. What was found is the empty bale of no stated kind bought by the hundred for Edo's flood works, relief sheds and canal works between 1802 and 1808, at 6 to 12 mon each, a twentieth and a thirteenth of the laborer's pay in the accounts of 1802 and 1808. In the Tama hills west of Edo in 1814 the charcoal burners' own women made the bales, of kaya grass. In Beijing in 1736 the basket for 60 jin of black charcoal cost about a fifth of a laborer's day. What charcoal itself cost is at What did charcoal cost?, and what a bale was at Straw bales of rice and charcoal.

An empty bale's price

  • What did one empty charcoal bale cost, in coin or in days of work?
    No source found says. The two charcoal contractors' accounts read give carriage and rope and no line for bales, and no price list read names a charcoal bale apart from its charcoal. The nearest figures are for an empty bale whose kind is not stated, below.
  • Edo's wards paid 6 mon for an empty bale in 1802.
    After the drainage canals of Honjo overflowed in the seventh month of 1802, the ward headmen's account entered 245 empty bales, made into earth bags, at 1 kan 530 mon, "6 mon a bale", and 36 laborers at 4 kan 500 mon, "124 mon a man". Two more entries of the same account price 180 and 60 empty bales at the same rate. A bale was about a twentieth of the laborer's day, by this project's arithmetic.
  • In 1806 Edo's town office paid 12 mon, or reckoned twelve to the hundred mon.
    For the relief sheds built after Edo's great fire of 1806 the town office entered 3,410 empty bales, used on the sheds' roofs, at 42 kan 624 mon, "12 mon a bale". A later entry for the same sheds, beside the mending of leaks, reckons 560 more at twelve to the hundred mon. The sheds' 78 laborers were paid 2.5 monme of silver a man, a rate entered as covering early and late work, by day and night; with the bales in copper and the pay in silver, no count of days is given.
  • A canal-works account of 1808 has 11 mon.
    A list of payments for works on the Tatekawa canal, in a volume the library catalogs under 1808, entered 50 empty bales at 572 mon, "11 mon a bale", and seven laborers at 1 kan, "141 mon a man". A bale was about a thirteenth of that laborer's pay, by this project's arithmetic.
  • What kind of bale were these?
    The accounts do not say what the bales had held, or whether they were new. A handbook of village administration of the 1790s, in its standard for carrying empty bales and rope, counts as an empty bale one "of the 4-to size, with their end caps", the size of a rice bale (Straw bales of rice and charcoal). That is the handbook's standard, not a statement about these purchases, so these stay bales of unstated kind and are not charcoal bales.
  • Why do the sums not match the rates?
    Each copper sum in the 1802 and 1806 accounts is larger than its rate times its count by about a twenty-fourth: 245 bales at 6 mon are 1,470 coins, entered as 1 kan 530 mon. That fits a hundred mon counted as a string of 96 coins, which is this project's reading of the arithmetic; the accounts do not say so. By it the laborer's 124 mon was 120 coins, and the shares above are unchanged.

Who made charcoal bales, and of what

  • In the Tama hills in 1814 the charcoal burners' women made them, of kaya grass.
    An official who walked the cliff road to Nippara village in the ninth month of 1814 wrote that its men cut wood and burned charcoal as their first trade, while its women cut kaya and made charcoal bales, or carried charcoal on their backs to Hikawa village every day; a local historian quotes him. The historian's own summary is that the Edo-period charcoal bale was made by women in the same mountain villages as the charcoal, from each village's own kaya. It is one district, at second hand.
  • Does that say where charcoal was baled?
    It bears on this record's guess that charcoal was baled at the kiln (Charcoal yards and charcoal stores): at Nippara the bales were made in the burners' own village and the charcoal left it already in bales, on the women's backs. The passage does not say where the charcoal was packed into them.
  • Did village straw-workers weave charcoal bales in winter for sale?
    No Edo-period source found says so. The nearest is an undated recollection, of living memory: the speaker learned to weave charcoal bales from their mother, and the charcoal dealer of Tosayama came to buy them. Rope, mats and sandals as a farm household's night and rainy-day work are at What work did a farm household do that was never paid?, and the straw they came from at After the rice harvest.
  • What were the body, the end caps and the ropes made of?
    The body was kaya at Nippara in 1814. For the ends and ropes only modern evidence was found. Folklore of the same hills, from a book the historian cites without a date, has the women weaving the bale from kaya the way a reed blind is woven, the kaya cut from the plots grown to thatch the village's houses; in the recollection that names the Tosayama dealer the kaya was woven on rope made from straw. Tokyo prefecture's standard of 1935 set a round bale weighing 2.5 kg (~6 lb) empty for 15 kg (~33 lb) of charcoal, its ends stopped with leafless brushwood or the like and tied with doubled straw rope, its body roped in three places.
  • A charcoal contractor bought his rope at market in 1763.
    Soemon, who contracted to supply Edo castle's charcoal from the hills behind Hanno, went to Hanno in the second month of 1763 and bought 348 bundles of rope for 1 kan 80 mon, about 3 mon a bundle by this project's arithmetic; a local-history writer quotes his record and guesses the rope was for making charcoal bales. The record as quoted names no bales bought, and no wage of Hanno in 1763 was found.

How long one took to weave

  • How many hours of work went into one charcoal bale?
    No Edo-period source found says. Two later recollections are the nearest. In the undated folklore of the Tama hills the women are said to have finished 14 or 15 in a short winter's day, when much charcoal was being burned; the count is of woven pieces, and whether a piece is a finished bale is not said. In the recollection that names the Tosayama dealer, making one took less than an hour, in an account that begins with making the straw rope. Neither counts cutting the grass.

Were empty bales bought and sold?

  • Empty bales were bought in Edo by the hundred and the thousand.
    The purchases above run from 50 bales to 3,410 at a time. After the main enceinte of Edo castle burned in 1859, a contractor ordered to deliver 5,000 empty rice bales found them hard to buy, they being scarce in the city, and asked to buy them from the rice dealers by private agreement; no price is given.
  • Edo's jail sold its empty bran bales every month.
    Until the reform of 1843 the empty bran bales of the Kodenmacho jail's kitchen were a perquisite of its kitchen staff; after it they were sold monthly and the money added to the jail's budget. In one later year the sale had brought 6 ryo 3 bu 2 shu and 718 mon; the number of bales is not given.
  • For river works, villages supplied empty bales as a levy.
    A shogunal order of 1743 on the materials for river-bank and weir works lists empty bales, rope, straw matting, vines and wisteria rope as village service, owed by the villages.
  • Were charcoal bales sold empty to charcoal makers or dealers?
    No Edo-period source found says so, by the hundred or otherwise. The one text found is the Tosayama recollection, in which the charcoal dealer came to buy them. At Nippara in 1814 the burners' households made their own.

The bale's share of a full bale's price

  • What share of a bale of charcoal's price was the bale?
    No Edo-period source found gives it. Dividing Edo's 6 to 12 mon of 1802-1808 by Edo's charcoal prices of 1842 would set a bale of unstated kind against charcoal of another decade, so no share is offered from them.
  • The nearest figure is of 1914: baling was about 6.5 percent of the price.
    The forestry bureau's survey put the standard cost of baling white charcoal in Japan's home territory, which the survey counts without Hokkaido, in 1914 at 8.38 yen for 100 bales of 10 kan, in a total cost of 115.67 yen and beside a standard settlement price of 128.31 yen: 6.5 percent of the price, by this project's arithmetic. At Hikawa in the Tama hills it was 20 yen in a price of 200, a tenth. The figure is the whole cost of baling, not divided into bale, rope and work, and it is half a century after the Edo period.

Rice bales, straw mats, rope and sandals

  • Empty rice bales: traded, with no price found.
    The order of 1859 above names empty rice bales and leaves their price to private agreement. No price for an empty bale stated to be a rice bale was found.
  • A straw mat cost 29 mon in Edo in 1806, and 25 mon in a Mino village early in 1766.
    The relief-shed account of 1806 entered 1,150 straw mats at "29 mon each", beside the empty bales at 12. At Kashimo village in Mino a household bought 8 mats for 200 mon in the last days of the lunar year Meiwa 2, early in 1766, 25 mon each by this project's arithmetic; the same village's farm day wage of 1774, eight years later, is at What did salt cost?
  • Rope cost 67 mon a bundle in Edo in 1806.
    The same account entered 370 bundles of rope, for tying the sheds, at "67 mon a bundle"; it gives no length or weight for a bundle. The Hanno contractor's bundles of 1763, above, are a different measure at about 3 mon.
  • What did straw sandals cost?
    No price, and no count of a day's output, was found.

China: the charcoal basket

The Chinese counterpart found is a basket, and its one price is the Qing court's.

  • In Beijing in 1736 the basket for 60 jin of black charcoal was priced at 0.0195 tael.
    The official price list the Ministry of Works settled in 1736 priced, among household goods, a basket for black charcoal holding 60 jin at 0.0195 tael and a basket for Hongluo charcoal holding 10 jin at 0.0066. It does not say what the baskets were woven of.
  • How much of the charcoal's price, and of a day's pay, was that?
    The same list's black charcoal at 0.5 and 0.6 tael the 100 jin and its laborer at 75 cash a day are at What did charcoal cost? By them the 60-jin basket was 5.4 to 6.5 percent of the charcoal it held, and about a fifth of a laborer's day; the Hongluo basket was about 6 percent of its charcoal. This is this project's arithmetic.
  • And under the Ming?
    No Ming source found prices what charcoal was packed in. The nearest is the Ming statutes' list for valuing stolen goods, which sets a reed mat at 1 guan of paper money and 10 jin of charcoal at the same; these are legal valuations, not market prices, and name no charcoal basket.
