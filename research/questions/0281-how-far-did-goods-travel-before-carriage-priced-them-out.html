How far did goods travel before carriage priced them out?

In Rokugan, goods such as charcoal and tea travel hundreds of miles from where they are made, and a good is dearer the farther it has gone. The findings below give what the historical record holds instead: an old rule of thumb, what carriage cost against the good itself, and how far some goods are known to have gone, in China first and then in Japan.

An old Chinese proverb held that firewood was not worth trading beyond 100 li, nor grain beyond 1,000, but by the Ming and Qing everyday goods went far, and mostly by water. No source read gives a distance at which carriage came to cost as much as the good. What survives is carriage as a share of the good - from a quarter to four-fifths of a shi on top of each shi of tax grain carried to the Ming capital, a few percent of the rice for a Japanese domain shipping by sea - and the lengths of some hauls. What carriage cost by the li or the ri, by water and by land, is in What did carrying goods cost by water, against by land?

China: the rule of thumb

  • Firewood not beyond 100 li, grain not beyond 1,000.
    Sima Qian quotes it as a proverb in his history of the Han dynasty's money-makers, as advice on what to trade.
  • Did Ming and Qing trade keep to it?
    No, as the historian Xu Tan (xutan-2000-market-network) puts it: long-distance trade had once carried mainly luxuries, as the proverb has it, but in the Ming and Qing everyday goods became the main body of large-scale, long-distance trade. Water was the most convenient and cheapest way to carry them, Xu Tan writes, so the great trading cities lay mostly along the main waterways.
  • How far did a villager walk to market?
    3.4 to 6.1 km (~2-4 miles), and about 4.5 km (~3 miles) in the commonest case, as the farthest villager's walk to the market town, in G. William Skinner's study of rural markets.
  • A hill near water was worth double.
    Qu Dajun described the firewood of Xining in Guangdong, carted down from the hills on small carts and floated out on rafts at the flood; anyone buying fields had to take a firewood hill with them, and a hill near water cost twice as much.

China: carriage against the good

  • What did carrying tax grain to the Ming capital cost the farmer?
    A surcharge in grain on every shi, set in 1431 by distance: 8 dou (eight-tenths of a shi) from Huguang, 7 from Jiangxi and Zhejiang, 6 from South Zhili and 5 from North Zhili, and only 4 where farmers brought it themselves to Huai'an. It was later cut to no more than 6 dou for the far provinces and as little as 2 dou 5 sheng for the near ones, two-thirds paid in rice and one-third in other goods. So carrying a shi to Beijing cost a quarter to four-fifths of a shi more, this project's arithmetic. The surcharge is grain, so no wage prices it.
  • And the worst hauls in the old histories?
    As the Ming minister Qiu Jun quotes them in 1487: the Qin dynasty's army supply to the northern frontier spent on average 30 zhong of grain to deliver one shi, 190 hu for one; and under the Han emperor Wu, carrying grain on men's backs to the southwest cost more than 10 zhong a shi delivered. These are military hauls, not trade.
  • What did the Tang canal cost?
    In 764, rice from Yangzhou to Heyin cost 120 cash a dou to carry, as Qiu Jun quotes the history. The history he quotes gives no rice price beside it, and no Tang wage is in this record.

Where did carriage equal the price?

  • No source read gives a distance.
    No Chinese or Japanese source, and no modern study read, states the distance at which carriage equaled or doubled a good's price.
  • What do the official rates imply?
    An estimate, this project's arithmetic: the Qing statutes of 1738-1744, as Ruoran Cheng (cheng-2024-transport-cost) prints them, paid 0.001 tael a shi a li for flat land in several provinces, 0.0015 for porters in Jiangsu, 0.002 to 0.003 for hill roads, and 0.01 a shi for 100 li on a secondary river; and he gives the grain price of 1680 as 32.22 g of silver for 100 liters. Taking a shi of grain as about 100 liters, a guess, carriage equals the grain's price after about 860 li on flat land, 580 by porter, 290 to 430 in the hills and some 8,600 by river. At his 500 m to the li, that is about 430 km (~270 miles) on flat land and 145 to 215 km (~90-135 miles) in the hills. The rates are 60 years later than the price, and are the state's.

How far particular goods went

  • Firewood into Kyoto: about 15 km on a woman's head.
    The Ohara women carried brushwood, firewood and produce to sell in Kyoto from Ohara, about 15 km (~9 miles) to the north, a practice that ran from the Kamakura period to the early Showa period. They walked over 20 km (~12 miles) there and back, with 30 to 40 kg (~66-88 lb) on the head as a rule and as much as 50 kg (~110 lb), it is said.
  • Rice: millions of shi down the canal.
    The largest good through the Huai'an customs house in the Qing was grain, "no less than several million shi" a year shipped down to Jiangnan, Xu Tan writes. The Hagi domain shipped its rice to Osaka by sea (What did carrying goods cost by water, against by land?), and farmers of Shinano took rice and firewood on their own horses toward Edo and Nagoya and brought back salt, fish and cotton; neither source gives the distance.
  • Did the farther district pay more?
    In the Hagi domain's schedule of 1790, freight on rice to Osaka ran from 5.9% of the rice for Oshima district to 6.9% for Maeotsu, and on rice to the domain's own storehouses at Hagi from 1.4% for Maeotsu to 6.9% for Oshima and four other districts; its historian writes that the rates were set high or low by the distance from the district to the destination. Distance was not all: from 1776 rice to Hagi, the nearer port, paid more than rice to Osaka, because the winter voyage on the Sea of Japan was dangerous and the ships shunned it.

  • Silk, cloth and tea went across the empire.
    Suzhou and Hangzhou silks were sold to the provinces of the north, center, southwest and northwest, and abroad to Southeast Asia, Europe, America and Russia; Suzhou was also the center for finishing cotton cloth; and Beijing's markets drew grain, silks, cotton cloth, paper and tea from outside. No source read gives the distances these went, or their freight.
