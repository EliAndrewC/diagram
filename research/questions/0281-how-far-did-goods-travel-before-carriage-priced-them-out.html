How far did goods travel before carriage priced them out?

In Rokugan, some goods travel far from where they are made: the Fox burn charcoal, easier to carry than their lumber, and their white charcoal commands a premium throughout the Empire. The findings below give what the historical record holds on how far goods went: an old rule of thumb, what carriage cost against the good itself, and how far some goods are known to have gone, in China and in Japan.

An old Chinese proverb held that firewood was not worth trading beyond 100 li, nor grain beyond 1,000, but by the Ming and Qing everyday goods went far, and the great trading cities lay along the waterways. No source read gives a distance at which carriage came to cost as much as the good. What survives is carriage as a share of the good - from a quarter to four-fifths of a shi on top of each shi of tax grain carried to the Ming capital, a few percent of the rice for a Japanese domain shipping by sea - and the lengths of some hauls. In Japan a handbook of village administration made the villages carry their tax rice the first 5 ri to the boats and the lord pay beyond. China: the rule of thumb

  • Firewood not beyond 100 li, grain not beyond 1,000.
    Sima Qian, the historian of the Han court (shiji-129-huozhi), quotes it as a proverb in his chapter on the money-makers, as advice on what to trade.
  • Did Ming and Qing trade keep to it?
    No, as the historian Xu Tan (xutan-2000-market-network) puts it: long-distance trade had once carried mainly luxuries, as the proverb has it, but in the Ming and Qing everyday goods became the main body of large-scale, long-distance trade. Water was the most convenient and cheapest way to carry them, Xu Tan writes, so the great trading cities lay mostly along the main waterways.
  • How far did a villager walk to market?
    3.4 to 6.1 km (~2-4 miles) in most market areas, and about 4.5 km (~3 miles) in the commonest case, as the farthest villager's walk to the market town, in G. William Skinner's (skinner-2002-etudes-rurales) study of rural markets in China.
  • A hill near water was worth double.
    Qu Dajun (guangdong-xinyu-25) described the firewood of Xining in Guangdong, carted down from the hills on small carts and floated out on rafts at the flood; anyone buying fields had to take a firewood hill with them, and a hill near water cost twice as much.

China: carriage against the good

  • What did carrying tax grain to the Ming capital cost the farmer?
    A surcharge in grain on every shi, set in 1431 by distance: 8 dou (eight-tenths of a shi) from Huguang, 7 from Jiangxi and Zhejiang, 6 from South Zhili and 5 from North Zhili, and only 4 where farmers brought it themselves to Huai'an. It was later cut to no more than 6 dou for the far provinces and as little as 2 dou 5 sheng for the near ones, two-thirds paid in rice and one-third in other goods. So the surcharge alone came to a quarter to four-fifths of a shi on each shi carried to Beijing, this project's arithmetic. It may not have been the whole cost: the army was also given silver for the rapids, the locks and the transfers between boats, though the chapter does not say there who paid it. The surcharge is reckoned in grain, so no wage prices it.
  • And the worst hauls in the old histories?
    As the Ming writer Qiu Jun (daxue-yanyi-bu-33) quotes them: the Qin dynasty's army supply to the north spent on average 30 zhong of grain (a zhong of 6 hu 4 dou, a hu of 10 dou) to deliver one shi, which Qiu Jun reckons at 190 hu for one; and under the Han emperor Wu, carrying grain on men's backs to the southwest cost more than 10 zhong a shi delivered. These are hauls for an army and for the state's road-building, not trade.
  • What did the Tang canal cost?
    Before Liu Yan took charge of grain transport in 764, rice from Yangzhou to Heyin had cost 120 copper cash a dou to carry, and his new boats cut that by 90 cash, as Qiu Jun quotes the history. The history he quotes gives no rice price beside it, and no Tang wage is in this record.

Where did carriage equal the price?

  • No source read gives a distance.
    No Chinese or Japanese source, and no modern study read, states the distance at which carriage equaled or doubled a good's price.
  • What do the official rates imply?
    An estimate, this project's arithmetic: the Qing statutes of 1738-1744, as Ruoran Cheng (cheng-2024-transport-cost) prints them, paid 0.001 tael a shi a li for flat land in several provinces, 0.0015 for porters in Jiangsu, 0.0015 to 0.004 for hill roads, and 0.01 a shi for 100 li on a secondary river; and he gives the grain price of 1680 as 32.22 g of silver for 100 liters. Taking a shi of grain as about 100 liters, a guess, carriage equals the grain's price after about 860 li on flat land, 580 by porter, 220 to 580 in the hills and some 8,600 by river. At his 500 m to the li, that is about 430 km (~270 miles) on flat land and 110 to 290 km (~67-179 miles) in the hills. The rates are 60 years later than the price, and are the state's.

Japan: the five-ri rule for tax rice

  • Who paid to carry the tax rice to the boats?
    The village for the first 5 ri, and the lord beyond. The Jikata hanreiroku (kotobank-jikata-hanreiroku), a handbook of village administration that Oishi Hisataka, a district officer of the Takasaki domain, began in 1791, calls it a fixed rule, as the archivist Nakamura Ken (nakamura-2020-fukui-nengu) quotes it: when tax rice was sent out, the village paid the horse and boat charges to the loading landing within 5 ri as the farmers' duty, and the lord paid the horse charges beyond 5 ri, at 24 mon a ri for one horse-load of two bales. 5 ri is about 20 km (~12 miles), this project's arithmetic. The rule is for tax rice; it says nothing of a merchant's goods. One dictionary says the modern edition Nakamura quotes follows the Tojo text, a revision made at the request of the senior councilor Mizuno Tadakuni, so the wording quoted may be the reviser's.
  • Did a domain follow it?
    The Fukui domain did, Nakamura writes, though how its farmers actually carried their tax, he adds, has not been worked out.
  • What was 24 mon a ri against a day's wage?
    No wage of the 1790s was found for Fukui or Takasaki. The nearest in this record are Osaka's, both in copper mon: a building helper's 236 mon a day in 1785, six years before the handbook was begun, and a carpenter costed at 420 mon a man, by a historian's conversion, in an estimate for rebuilding a temple that burned in 1801 (What did a skilled craftsman earn against a laborer?). Against them one horse-load's carriage beyond the five ri came to about a tenth of a helper's day for each ri; a helper's day paid for about 10 ri of it and a carpenter's for about 17 or 18, this project's arithmetic across cities and years. How far particular goods went

  • Firewood into Kyoto: about 15 km on a woman's head.
    The Ohara women carried brushwood, firewood and produce to sell in Kyoto from Ohara, about 15 km (~9 miles) to the north, a practice that ran from the Kamakura period to the early Showa period. They walked over 20 km (~12 miles) there and back, with 30 to 40 kg (~66-88 lb) on the head as a rule and as much as 50 kg (~110 lb), it is said.
  • Grain: millions of shi down the canal.
    The largest good through the Huai'an customs house in the Qing was grain, most of it soybeans, "no less than several million shi" a year shipped down to Jiangnan, Xu Tan writes. The Hagi domain shipped its rice to Osaka by sea (What did carrying goods cost by water, against by land?), and the Shinano pack-horse trade is said to have begun with farmers of the Ina region taking rice, firewood and the like on their own horses toward Edo and Nagoya and bringing back salt, fish and cotton; neither source gives the distance.
  • Did the farther district pay more?
    In the Hagi domain's schedule of 1790, freight on rice to Osaka ran from 5.9% of the rice for Oshima district to 6.9% for Maeotsu, and on rice to the domain's own storehouses at Hagi from 1.4% for Maeotsu to 6.9% for Oshima, Kaminoseki, Kumage and Tsuno; its historian writes that the rates were set high or low by the distance from the district to the destination. Distance was not all: from 1776, for the districts on the Inland Sea, rice to Hagi, the nearer destination, paid more than rice to Osaka, because their ships shunned the dangerous winter voyage on the Sea of Japan, which had also paid less.

    The prices of salt near the works and inland are in What did salt cost?
  • Charcoal.
    No source read gives how far charcoal usually or at most went, or its freight. The one far-carried charcoal in this record is Kumano charcoal from Kii province selling in Edo in 1716, at about two and a half times the price of the Tama district's, in What did charcoal cost?
  • Silk went across the empire and abroad; cloth and tea reached Beijing.
    Suzhou and Hangzhou silks were sold to the provinces of the north, center, southwest and northwest, and abroad to Southeast Asia, Europe, America and Russia; Suzhou was also the center for finishing cotton cloth; and Beijing's markets drew grain, silks, cotton cloth, paper and tea from outside. No source read gives the distances these went, or their freight.
