How our maps draw vegetable gardens at samurai houses and government offices (saien)

Every residence and walled compound on our building plans has a vegetable garden. Its size and its site are both knobs, from a plot of about a thousand square feet to a field over half the grounds, each value one the record attests, and the sun rules out any site where the garden's bed would lie in shade. The shadows are counted by the same rule as a farmhouse's kitchen bed, at How our maps keep yards and gardens in the sun, and where the rest of the house's service stands is at How our maps lay out samurai residences and their rooms (buke yashiki).

  • A garden's size is a knob between two attested forms.
    At the low end it is a soup-greens plot of about 100 m² (~1,076 sq ft), like the one at the Takei samurai house of Boso-no-mura; at the high end it is a field over about half the grounds, like the Yokota samurai house's at Matsushiro. Each settlement picks its own at random, and each hand-drawn plan takes its own.
  • A garden's site is a knob among four, three recorded at Japanese houses and offices and one from China.
    No page we read seats a Japanese residence's vegetable garden north of the house, so the rear site rests on a Chinese list alone.
    • West of the residence, as at the Takei house.
    • South, beside the formal garden, as the Higuchi house at Matsushiro kept its field south of its pond.
    • In the rear service ground, as a Chinese county office listed its plot behind the residence gate.
    • On a parcel of its own, as a daikan's office registered its field apart from its residence lot.
  • Our maps rule out a site where a full-sun bed gets under six hours of direct sun in autumn.
    That a household chose among its sites by the sun is a GUESS: no page we read gives a household's reason for putting its plot where it did. Among the sites left, a generated plan picks one at random and a hand-drawn plan takes one.
  • On the three hand-drawn magistracy plans, the bed stands south, in the inner court's garden.
    At Ochiba, Hayakawa and Ubame the west and the rear lie in the shade of the kitchen and the residence, so the only site left inside the walls is the south. Each bed takes sunny ground in the inner court's garden, south of the house, as the Higuchi house's field lay south of its pond.
  • At Ubame, the bed is a soup-greens plot.
    The inner court there has room for a bed only at the size knob's low end.
  • Our maps draw a plot beside the formal garden in rows, with no fence or hedge between them.
    This is a GUESS: no page we read names a fence or hedge between the two.
  • Our maps set the plot on the far side of the formal garden from the house, where the court has room.
    This is a GUESS: no page we read says whether the plot lay between the house and the formal garden or beyond it.
  • Our maps put no fence round a single bed.
    This is a GUESS: the walls against boar and deer that were found ring a village's farmland, not a single plot.

Outside the samurai quarter, a country shrine whose keeper lives in the hall gets a plot too.

  • At a country shrine, the resident keeper's plot stands near the hall, on open ground that gets its sun.
    The plot's place and its size are a GUESS: no page we read says where a keeper living in a precinct grew their vegetables.
