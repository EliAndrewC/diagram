How steady were prices in Edo Japan and Qing China, from one day to the next century?

Rokugan's money rules convert a price with one price level for each era, as if prices held still for the era's years. The findings below give what the historical record holds instead: how far prices in Edo-period Japan and Qing China moved in a day, a week, a month, a year, a decade and a century, which moves were trends and which came back, and which goods held steady.

The records of premodern prices in East Asia read here are the Osaka rice market's daily quotes, the Mitsui merchant house's half-yearly account-book prices in Kyoto, Edo and Osaka, and the Qing government's monthly grain price reports. Read together, they show prices that drifted little over a century before 1859 but swung hard from season to season and year to year, with famine spikes of two to four times that came back within a few years. Where a figure below is a standard deviation of log changes, it is this project's arithmetic: roughly, two changes in three fall within that percentage, up or down.

Japan: how far prices moved, scale by scale

  • Within a day: ATTESTED ONLY FOR how a trading day ran; how far the price moved is SILENT.
    The Dojima rice exchange in Osaka had an opening and a closing price, had a trading-halt rule under which a session could be broken off, and often went on trading after the official close. No figure for the size of a move within a day was found.
  • Day to day and week to week: SILENT in the sources; about 2% and 4% by this project's arithmetic.
    The Mitsui exchange office in Osaka wrote down the day's rice price from 1787. From one quoted day to the next its price moved with a standard deviation of 2.0%, and over seven days 3.8%, in 1787-1858.
  • Month by month within a year: ATTESTED for Osaka rice, a few percent in a quiet year, a fifth in the Tenmei famine.
    Within a year, Osaka's spot price of rice varied by a coefficient of variation under 10% in most years from 1756, but 15.8% in 1784, 19.0% in 1786, 20.1% in 1787 and 24.2% in 1851. In the daily quotes a month's change had a standard deviation of 9.0%.
  • Season to season and year to year: ATTESTED, a swing of about 10-17% for rice, less for the price level.
    An index of general prices in Osaka varied within the year by a coefficient of variation of 7.4% in 1802-1829 and 8.1% in 1830-1858, and rice by 6.9% and 11.3%. In the Mitsui books, rice moved by 12-14% from one half year to the next and 15-17% from one year to the next in all three cities before 1859.
  • Five years to fifty: ATTESTED for the trend, by this project's arithmetic for the swing - a wide swing around almost no trend.
    Mitsui's rice moved by 25-34% over five years and 28-38% over ten, but its drift to 1858 was -0.5% a decade in Kyoto (1713, then 1741-1762 and 1791-1858), +0.1% in Osaka (1713, then 1818-1858) and +3.9% in Edo (four years of 1710-1716, then 1818-1858). The first years of each span fall in years of debased silver coin. The Osaka price level began a general rise in 1820, and averaged 36% higher in 1830-1858 than in 1802-1829.
  • Century to century: ATTESTED ONLY FOR 1802-1867 in the sources, steady before 1859, then a new level.
    Over 50 years Kyoto's rice moved by 20%, no more than over ten. After the ports opened in 1859 the price level was 239% above its 1830-1858 average in 1859-1867.

Japan: the shocks, dated

  • Famines doubled or tripled the rice price, and it came back.
    In a yearly table of Osaka rice, which in some years quotes a different brand (rice named for the province or domain that grew it), rice went from 41.5 monme a koku in 1731 to 76-90 in the Kyoho famine of 1732, from 60-75 in 1782 to 77-98 in the Tenmei famine of 1783, 71-116 in 1784 and 101.5 in 1786, and from 71-93 in 1835 to 153-159 in the Tenpo famine of 1836, 258 at its peak in 1837, and back to 58-75 by 1840. The daily quotes give 73.4 for Yonago rice in the sixth month of 1836 and 225 a year later for Higo rice, a different brand.
  • Recoinage and the open ports moved the level for good.
    The shogunate's debasement of the coinage in 1818-1829 and the famine of 1833-1838 made real wages in the Kinai extremely volatile for the two decades after 1820. From 1859 short-run movement became remarkably large; rice stood at 126-129 monme in 1858 and 1,160-1,447 in 1866.

China: how far prices moved, and the shocks

  • Within a month: SILENT.
    The Qing reports give each prefecture a high and a low price each lunar month, nothing finer.
  • Month to month: ATTESTED ONLY FOR the rise between harvests, about 1% a month.
    In 1770-1860, Chinese grain rose by an average 1.1% a month in the months it was stored, weighted by the share of months whose price changed.
  • Half year and year: ATTESTED, steadier than Europe.
    Rice in 121 prefectures, priced in the second and eighth lunar months, swung with a seasonally adjusted volatility of 9.2%, against 12.7% for wheat in 15 European markets. The cost of a bare-bones year of goods in silver moved by 9.6% a year in Suzhou and 8.1% in Canton.
  • Decades: about 20% at ten years and beyond, with a slow rise in silver.
    The Suzhou basket moved by 22% over ten years and 20% over fifty, drifting up by 5.6% a decade in 1730-1833; Canton's by 19% and 15%, drifting 9.4% a decade. In silver, rice prices of the early and middle Qing swung but showed no marked upward trend; Suzhou's rose by 0.067 tael a shi a decade in 1700-1800.
  • The century in copper: a fourfold rise, kept apart from silver.
    In copper cash, rice in Suzhou and its three neighboring prefectures went from 7 a sheng in 1707 to a normal price of 27-35 after 1785; one historian, writing of American silver's effect on prices, puts the 18th century's rise in rice at about fourfold or more, without saying in which money. Cash itself fell against silver, from near 1,000 to the tael in the 18th century to over 2,000 around 1850.
  • Shocks: famine years at several times the normal price.
    In Songjiang white rice reached 5 taels a shi in 1642, after which 2-3 taels was normal; in 1651 it climbed from 3 taels in the second month to nearly 5 in the sixth, and new grain sold at 2 in the seventh; 1669 was the cheapest year, and new rice was 0.6 tael in 1670; prices soared in spring 1680. Rice in Suzhou and its three neighboring prefectures rose more than threefold in the drought of 1707 and rose to 35-36 cash in the famine of 1755 and 56-57 in the drought of 1785. In the Taiping war years rural Beijing's cash fell from 2,205 to the tael in 1853 to 4,970 in 1856; no grain price of 1853-1864 was read.

Which goods were steady and which swung

Against the setting's money rules

  • One price level per era hides a swing of about a quarter for rice.
    Within five years Mitsui's rice moved by 25-34%, lamp oil by 17-19%, firewood by 5-11% and the Edo day wage by 8%; that spread is the honest size of treating an era as one price level.
