Sloping ground and hillsides

Wet rice is grown in level basins of standing water, so a farmed slope is broken into level steps, and the water that floods each step enters on the high side and leaves on the low. On a steep hillside the steps are terraces you can see; on a gently tilted valley floor they are too low to notice, and the fall of the land shows only in which way the water runs. Most of this rests on general reading; no page we read gives it. Mapmakers, for their part, have long had to show high and low ground, called relief, on a flat sheet.

  • On a steep slope the paddies become terraces.
    Paddies can be built into steep hillsides as terraces. In early Korea (the Mumun period) terraced paddies were long, irregular shapes that followed the land's natural contours. That each terrace is a narrow strip with a riser between one and the next, and that terraces appear only where the slope is steep, rests on general reading; no source is cited. The sourced account of hillside rice land is at Terraced and valley paddies (tanada, yachida).
  • On a gently tilted valley floor the paddies' steps are too low to notice.
    The paddies read as a flat patchwork, the level steps between them too slight to see. This rests on general reading; no source is cited.
  • Water enters the paddies high and leaves them low.
    The water's source, a stream or an irrigation pond, lies above the fields. The ditches that share it out fan down from there, and a drainage ditch leaves the fields at their lowest corner. This rests on general reading; no source is cited.
  • Rice grown in flooded fields needs a way for water out as well as in.
    So rice land has drainage outlets as well as intakes. This rests on general reading; no source is cited. Where a field's drains ran, and why, is at Field drains (akusuiro).
  • The oldest way to show a hill on a map is a picture of the hill.
    Hill profiles, drawings of hills seen from the side, are the most ancient form of relief on maps, placed on maps of broad areas.
  • The land surveyors who drew estate maps seldom showed relief at all.
    Few even tried: their purpose was to record boundaries and areas.
  • Hachures, slopes shaded with lines, were first standardized in 1799.
    The German topographer Johann Georg Lehmann standardized them, long after the pictorial hill.
