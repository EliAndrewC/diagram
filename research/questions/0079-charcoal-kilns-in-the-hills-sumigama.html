Charcoal kilns in the hills (sumigama)

Charcoal burning was once an important income for Japan's hill villages. Some burned it for a living, and farming households burned it as a side job in the slack season of the farming year, in one Shikoku district in 1936 from about December to about April, between the autumn reaping and the rice planting of June and July. One kiln of about 1600 excavated in Fukushima was dug into a hillside: a firing chamber about 10 by 7 ft, with a chimney trench rising steeply up the slope from its back.

The village fuel wood on the hill ground beyond the fields is at Village fuel woods and their coppice (satoyama); the charcoal a town bought and stored is at Charcoal yards and charcoal stores.

  • In one Shikoku district, hill villages had more burners by trade and field villages more who burned on the side.
    A Shikoku town's history says that villages with little field and much hill, such as Hashikami, had more who made charcoal their trade; villages with many fields, such as Yamana and Hirata, had more side-job burners.
  • In two Shikoku farming villages, most charcoal was burned between harvest and planting, from about December to April.
    A Shikoku town's history gives monthly figures for 1936 that show Yamana and Hirata, villages of many fields, burning little in the months of rice planting and harvest, and most in the slack season from about December to about April.
  • How many kilns did a village have?
    A Shikoku town's history counts its villages' kilns at the end of 1936: 35 in Hirata, a field village, and 159 in Hashikami, a hill village. These are counts for whole administrative villages, not for one hamlet, so they give no count for one hamlet before 1868, in the Edo period.
  • Kilns excavated at Date, in Fukushima, from around the start of the Edo period, were dug into a slope.
    At a site on the southern slope of a river valley in Date City, in Fukushima Prefecture in northeastern Japan, kilns dated to the late 16th or early 17th century - around the start of the Edo period - were built underground into the sloping ground, though the excavators could not rule out that some were half sunk.
  • How large was a kiln's firing chamber?
    The largest of the kilns excavated at Date, in Fukushima, measured 2.92 m (~10 ft) by 2.10 m (~7 ft). Two kilns of the same type elsewhere in Fukushima were somewhat larger: 3.29 m (~11 ft) by 2.06 m (~7 ft), and 4.00 m (~13 ft) by 2.60 m (~9 ft), each chamber an oval wider than it was deep.
  • In the kilns excavated at Date, in Fukushima, a chimney trench rose steeply from the chamber's back.
    At the largest it ran 2.36 m (~8 ft), rising at 40 to 60 degrees. Chamber and chimney together run about 4.5 m (~15 ft) along the slope, by this page's sum of the two.
  • What charcoal did kilns make in the Edo period, before 1868?
    From the late Muromachi into the Edo period, soft black charcoal that lit easily was made by letting the kiln cool before it was opened. What the source calls rough charcoal (arazumi) was mostly white charcoal, hard, long-lasting and slow to light, drawn red-hot from the kiln and smothered with ash outside it. The firm distinction between the two is said to be modern.
  • In the setting, charcoal burners are hinin.
    The setting's notes list charcoal burners among the hinin, the castes outside the people. That is a DEVIATION from the Japanese hill villages of the record, where farming households burned charcoal too: in the setting, a kiln in a village's wood is a charcoal burner's.
  • Do farmers in the setting also burn charcoal in the slack season?
    The setting's notes do not say.
