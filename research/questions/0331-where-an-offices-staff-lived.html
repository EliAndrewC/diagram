Where an office's staff lived

Where an office's staff lived depended on the office and on who its staff were. Offices and small lords' seats in the provinces kept rowhouses (nagaya) for their junior staff on their own grounds, though at one domain's distant office the staff lived in the town around it instead, and Edo's great town magistracy, in the shogun's capital, housed its officers and constables (yoriki and doshin) in a district of their own. How such a rowhouse was built, sized and divided is at Staff rowhouses and barracks (nagaya).

  • A shogunate intendant's office counted its junior officials' quarters among its parts.
    A Japanese encyclopedia lists, among the parts of an intendant's office, the office itself, the intendant's residence, the storehouses, the jail, and the huts and rowhouses that were the official quarters of its junior officials.
  • A small lord's jin'ya laid out its retainers' official residences inside it.
    They stood among its residence hall, offices and storehouses.
  • At Takayama, an intendant's office, the storehouse keepers' rowhouse stood on the grounds and survives, restored.
    The Takayama jin'ya was restored to nearly its Edo-period condition from a plan of 1830, and the storehouse keepers' rowhouse was one of the parts restored.
  • Did all the staff of a rural intendant's office live on its grounds?
    No page we read counts who lived inside one, or says whether all its staff did. The sources name the junior officials' quarters and the storehouse keepers' rowhouse, and at Omori, the intendant's office that governed the silver mine, a mid-ranking official serving under the intendant lived in a spacious house of his own in the town, not far from the office.
  • At the Sakai town magistracy, about 1728, the magistrate's own retainers are thought to have lived in rowhouses inside its grounds.
    The magistracy's plans, as the architectural historian Takaya Mariko (sakai-bugyosho-kachu-nagaya-takaya) redraws them, place the retainers' rowhouses beside the magistrate's residence, in three places inside the grounds, one of them a single row running east and west, and, it appears, clearly distinguished from the yoriki's lots and from the doshin rowhouse, which stood on the lot adjoining the residence to the north. The size of a doshin's dwelling there is at Staff rowhouses and barracks (nagaya).
  • In the small Sayama domain's jin'ya, the middle and lower retainers lived mainly along its East Gate street and side lanes.
    Uematsu Kiyoshi (sayama-jinya-uematsu), reading the domain's plans, finds its upper retainers on the main street, the Ōmachi, and its middle and lower ones elsewhere, in rowhouses the domain supplied, sized by rank.
  • At Sano, a domain's distant office, the grounds held rowhouses in 1739, yet its staff lived in the town.
    The Hikone domain governed its territory at Sano from a jin'ya in Horigome, one of four towns there. Besides its council office the grounds held a writing office, two rowhouses, an accounts rowhouse, the jin'ya keeper's house and storehouses, but its stipended men, from the intendants down, lived as a rule in the four towns, and none but the keeper inside.
  • Edo's town magistracy housed its officers and constables in a district of their own, Hatchōbori.
    After the temples of Hatchōbori were ordered moved to Asakusa in 1635, the group residences of the town magistrate's yoriki and doshin were, it is said, set up on their sites.
  • Edo's town magistrate lived inside the magistracy, apart from the constables.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on the office says so of the magistrate's residence alone, and not who else lived inside.
  • The lower warriors who came to Edo with their lord lived in rowhouses inside his residence.
    Many retainers followed a lord to Edo on his alternate attendance, and the lower warriors among them lived in rowhouses set up inside the domain's residence there.
