A lord's retreat just outside town (shimoyashiki)

A daimyo of the Edo period kept, besides his main residence, a lower mansion on the outskirts of Edo: mostly a second house with gardens, most of them placed in the suburbs away from the castle. It was a place of gardens for the lord's rest, and it also fed his main house. Lords and senior retainers kept them in some other castle towns too. In at least one castle town it took another form: a street of the outer town lined by the mansion and its retainers' houses. In China, a Ming official's private garden outside a Suzhou city gate is something like it. The sources say what such a retreat was far better than how far out it lay.

  • At Edo, a lower mansion held gardens and kitchen plots.
    A dictionary of Japanese history says that at Edo the lower mansion held gardens for the lord's amusement and rest, and vegetable plots and bamboo groves to supply the upper residence.
  • At Edo, a lower mansion on the water also served as a cargo landing.
    The dictionary of Japanese history adds that at Edo a lower mansion on a shore or a riverbank was also used as a landing place for cargo.
  • Some lower mansions stood on land a daimyo bought, farmland among it.
    A house a daimyo built on land bought from its private owners, farmland among it, was called a kakae-yashiki; these lay generally in Edo's suburbs and served some domains as lower mansions.
  • Lords and senior retainers kept them in some other castle towns too.
    A dictionary of Japanese history says that in castle towns other than Edo there are cases of domain lords and senior retainers keeping one besides the castle or their main house. It gives cases, not a count.
  • Did samurai in provincial castle towns keep smaller retreats like a daimyo's at Edo?
    The dictionary's cases in castle towns other than Edo include senior retainers as well as domain lords, but it gives none of their sizes. That they kept retreats like a daimyo's at Edo, scaled down, is this record's GUESS.
  • In the castle town of Sumoto, a lower mansion and its retainers' houses lined a street of the outer town.
    A gazetteer of Japanese place names, printed on the same online dictionary page, describes a street running east to west through the south of Sumoto's outer town, three ken wide and 237 ken long (about 18 ft by 1,400 ft ), with the Inada family's lower mansion and its retainers' houses on both sides. It was a samurai quarter named for the mansion, the Outer Lower Mansion ward. It is a second form of the retreat: a street within the town's outer edge, not a house on its own ground.
  • In China, a Ming official kept a private garden outside a Suzhou gate.
    Suzhou's Lingering Garden stands outside the Changmen gate, northwest of the old city, and was begun in 1593 as the private garden of an official who had been removed from office. The page does not say whether he lived there or in town.
  • How far out did a retreat lie?
    No source we read gives a distance from a castle or a city wall to a retreat: the pages say "the outskirts" and "the suburbs", and name Edo's by district. That a retreat lay within a mile or a few of its town is this record's GUESS.
