How much miso and soy sauce did a household use, and what did they cost, era by era?

Rokugan's money rules price a laborer's year of goods, and the historical basket they lean on counts its soybeans as whole beans standing for miso, soy sauce, tofu and natto. The findings below give what the historical record holds on how much miso and soy sauce a household used, what a measure of beans made, and what the two cost in each era the money rules use.

How much a household used

No yearly count of miso or soy sauce eaten in a city household was found. What survives is soybeans: how many beans a farm household or a domain set aside a person a year for making miso or soy sauce at home, each an average over a household with its children, not an adult's share. A koku of soybeans is taken as 129 kg, the conversion Bassino and Ma use.

  • Beans for miso, a Shinano village in 1760: about 16.8 kg a person.
    A household's precepts written in 1760 in Katakura village, Saku district, in Shinano, reckon a person's yearly food at 1.8 koku of brown rice, 1 koku of barley and 0.13 koku of soybeans for miso, as quoted by Shigematsu; 0.13 koku is about 16.8 kg, this project's arithmetic. Attested only for a farm household's own reckoning; the precepts themselves were not read.
  • Beans for miso, Hirosaki domain in 1816: about 9 kg a person.
    The domain's estimate of 1816 sets aside 14,000 koku of miso soybeans for a population of 200,000; that is 0.07 koku, about 9.0 kg, a person, this project's arithmetic. Attested only for a northern domain's official estimate.
  • Beans for soy sauce, an 1844 farm manual: about 4.6 kg a person.
    Ōkura Nagatsune's Kōeki kokusan kō tells a household of five to get three old sake barrels and set in each 6 sho of soybeans and 6 sho of polished barley, 1 to 8 sho of each in all, with 1 sho of salt and 3 sho of water to each sho of beans; households everywhere set it in during the midsummer days of the sixth month. 1 to 8 sho over five is 3.6 sho a person, about 4.6 kg, this project's arithmetic. That it is a year's supply is this record's reading from the one setting-in a year; the manual does not say so. Attested only for a farm manual's household brewing.
  • The Edo recipes give proportions, and none says how much they made.
    The Honchō shokkan (1697) sets 1 to of soybeans with 1 to of barley, 1 to of salt and 1 to 5 or 6 sho of water; the Wakan sansai zue (1712) 1 to each of soybeans, grain and salt with 2 to 5 sho of water, and says shop soy sauce was all made with wheat. The nearest to a miso is its hishio, a bean paste: 1 to of soybeans, 1 to of grain, 2 sho 6 gō of salt and 1 to of water. How much miso or soy sauce a measure of beans made was not found in any Edo source read.
  • Bassino and Ma's 52 kg names no source.
    Their basket of goods was built to give 1,940 calories and 80 g of protein, and its soybeans stand for miso, soy sauce, tofu and natto, so their amount is more than the 1920s surveys report; the surveys' average for 1921-1925 was 18 kg of beans a person, 12.9 kg of them soybeans. The paper does not say whether the 12.9 kg counts beans already made into miso and soy sauce. The 52 kg is not attested as anything a household used.
  • What is left for tofu and natto is not attested.
    No source read gives the share of a household's soybeans going to tofu and natto. Setting the 1760 miso beans beside the 1844 soy sauce beans, two sources from different places and times, leaves about 31 kg of the 52 kg (about 38 kg with Hirosaki's miso beans), this project's arithmetic.

What a kilogram of beans makes today

Since no Edo yield was found, the yields below are present-day ones, from Japanese recipes, a maker and the industry's own figures. Whether a modern yield may stand for an Edo one is a decision for the setting, not a finding of the record.

  • Miso: about 3 kg a kilogram of dry soybeans, where the koji is no more than the beans.
    Kawaguchi City's recipe takes about 1 kg of soybeans, 1 kg of koji and 500 g of salt, and says it makes about 3 kg of miso, aged about a year. Marukome's salty miso takes 300 g of rice koji, 430 g of soybeans and 155 g of salt for about 1.3 kg, about 3.0 kg a kilogram of beans; its sweet miso, with 250 g of beans, is given the same 1.3 kg, about 5.2 kg a kilogram. Amagasaki City's koji-rich miso, 500 g of dry soybeans to 1,000 g of rice koji and 300 g of salt, makes about 2.5 kg, 5.0 kg a kilogram. Each a kilogram is this project's arithmetic. Attested only for present-day home recipes.
  • The industry's ordinary red rice miso uses less koji than beans.
    The miso industry's committee measures koji against beans as a koji ratio, 10 meaning equal amounts, and gives the salty red rice misos of Sendai, Akita and Tsugaru a ratio of 6 to 10, usually 7, with 11 to 13% salt; it states no yield. The modern recipes are weighed, the Edo ones measured by volume, so the two do not set directly side by side.
  • Soy sauce: no source read states liters a kilogram of soybeans.
    The national science museum's history of soy sauce sets the Edo recipes beside a present-day whole-soybean soy sauce by volume: the 1844 manual about 20% each of soybeans, grain and salt and 40% water, the Wakan sansai zue 18.2% of each and 45.5% water, and the soy sauce of 1990 on about 20% soybeans, 19% wheat, 20% salt and 41% water. At Noda about 1900, raw pressed soy sauce was 65-68% of the fermenting mash, and over 70% with screw presses. Neither is a yield a kilogram of beans.
  • How the findings stand against the money tables' split.
    This project's money tables split the basket's 52 kg of soybeans into about 13 kg of beans for miso, 4.6 kg for soy sauce and the rest whole, turned into product by a modern yield. The 13 kg lies between the 9 and 16.8 kg found, and the 4.6 kg is the 1844 manual's, but all three are rural household averages with children, not an adult's share, and the 1844 soy sauce is brewed with barley, not wheat. The miso yield rests on present-day recipes; the soy sauce yield is not stated by any source read.

What they cost, era by era

The Mitsui merchant house's account books, as transcribed, price miso by 10 kan (37.5 kg) and soy sauce by the koku (180.9 liters), in silver monme, each spring and autumn, in Kyoto, Edo and Osaka. The table gives the means of those half-yearly cells in each era they reach, and grams of pure silver at each era's own coin, from How much silver was a monme, a ryo and a bu, coinage by coinage?; every mean and gram is this project's arithmetic.

  • Which silver rate.
    What did a Kyoto laborer's year of goods cost? gives the same Kyoto cells of 1745-1754 at Bassino and Ma's 3.11 g of silver a monme, 1.28 g a kilogram of miso and 1.57 g a liter of soy sauce; the table here uses each era's own coin, 1.725 g for that era. The Edo sheet heads its prices in silver monme too, and does not say how they were reckoned.
  • 1860-1867 hides a rise of about three times.
    In Edo, miso went from 25 monme for 10 kan in the spring of 1860 to 81 to 85 in 1867, and soy sauce from 118 a koku to 321; three autumns of 1860-1862 are blank. The era's mean is a mean over that rise.
  • 1712-1718 rests on one Osaka cell.
    Osaka's sheet prices soy sauce once in these years, 120 monme a koku in the autumn of 1713, about 1.0 g of silver a liter at 1.5 g a monme; its miso cell is blank. Kyoto's autumn of 1713 has miso at 31.74 and soy sauce at 123.1.
  • 1706-1711 has only Edo prices.
    The four Edo cells of 1710-1711 average 12.30 monme for 10 kan of miso (11.6-12.7) and 104.38 a koku of soy sauce (101.12-106.09), about 0.62 g a kilogram and 1.08 g a liter at 1.875 g a monme; no Kyoto or Osaka cell falls in these years.
  • 1620-1650: a "Soya" by the sho, read as soy sauce.
    Kimura's price series, as transcribed, carries a column headed "Soya", per sho, beside "Soybeans" per koku: 0.5 monme in 1631 and 0.9 in 1636 and 1637, which the sheet itself puts at 0.83 and 1.50 g of silver a liter. That "Soya" is soy sauce is this record's reading, since it is priced by volume apart from the beans; the sheet does not say. It has no miso.
  • 1882-1886: nothing within the years; the nearest is 1887.
    The Finance Ministry's yearly prices, as transcribed, begin in 1887 with "Soy" at 8.29 yen a koku, about 1.15 g of silver a liter at 25 g a yen, this project's arithmetic; that "Soy" is soy sauce is this record's reading. Bassino and Ma's own prices of 1882-1886 hold neither miso nor soy sauce.
  • The other eras are silent.
    No price of miso or soy sauce was found in the years of 1695-1705, 1736-1739 or 1780-1789, nor of 1690-1735 beyond the cells of 1710-1716 above, none of them in that era's own coin of 1722-1735; and no price of soy sauce or of jiang, the Chinese bean paste, in any Chinese era. Beijing's lists price vinegar but not soy: Timkowski's prices of 1821 give salt by the jin and vinegar by the bottle, and the official price list of 1736 vinegar, salt and sesame oil by the jin, with a mold for making jiang, not jiang itself.
