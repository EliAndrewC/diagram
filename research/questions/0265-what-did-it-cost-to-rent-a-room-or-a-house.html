What did it cost to rent a room or a house?

Rokugan's townsmen rent rooms and shops in its cities, and the setting judges a price by how many days of a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give what the historical record holds for comparison: a few rents from Edo, Osaka and Qing Beijing, what an Edo town lot earned against its value, and the nearest day wages found.

On the Mitsui merchant house's lots in Edo, ground and house rents were collected on the last day of every month. The rents found are few: a carpenter's household in Edo about 1820, a popular page's tenement rents, what central Edo's town lots charged by the tsubo and the Mitsui merchant house's Osaka houses by the mat of floor, and in Beijing the government's rent for a house by the bay and the rents of a few shops and one official's house. What property earned against its value is better known: in both cities about 3 to 6% of it a year, in Edo the owner's income from a lot after its charges, in Beijing a bay's rent.

Edo

  • What did a carpenter's household pay?
    10 monme of silver a month in rent, in an account of Edo around the Bunsei era (1818-1830), which gives the carpenter's day's income as 5.4 monme, meals included. The historian who reads the account does not say what kind of house it was or how big.
  • And a room in a back-alley tenement?
    A popular page, which names no source for the figures, puts the rent of a back tenement at about 300 to 500 mon a month in 1804-1830, and cites an unnamed record of 500 to 600 mon for a unit 9 shaku by 2 ken in the closing years of the shogunate, when prices had risen.
  • What did a town lot in central Edo charge by the tsubo?
    On Minami-Denmacho 1 and 2 chome, a street lined with merchants' shops on the Tokaido between Nihonbashi and Kyobashi, the monthly mean house rent on the back of a lot ran 3.3 to 3.8 monme of silver in the Kansei era (1789-1801) and 3.7 to 5.0 in 1840, and the ground rent on its street front 3.8 to 4.5 and 4.0 to 5.5. The historian's table gives them in monme a month under the heads "front tsubo" and "back tsubo"; that each is the rent of one tsubo is read from those heads.
  • Is there a second carpenter's rent?
    A book of 1848, the Ryuan zappitsu, as a historian of money retells it, gives a carpenter 4.2 monme of silver a day and 1.2 for his meals, and, for an assumed household of a couple and a child, 120 monme of rent among a year's spending; it names no city. Those are the same 5.4 monme a day and 10 monme a month as the Bunsei-era account above, so this record takes them as the same figures again, not a second rent.
  • And in Kyoto, or nearer 1750?
    No rent of a room or a house in Kyoto was found, for any year, and none in Kyoto or Osaka for the mid-1700s; the nearest are the Mitsui house's Osaka rents of 1814 below, some sixty years after 1750.
  • Whom did an Edo tenant pay?
    The yanushi. By the Morisada manko, a townsman's record of the 1830s to 1853, the yanushi managed a landowner's ground, collected the ground rents and house rents from the tenants and paid them to the landowner; Edo had 20,117 of them. One who rented both the ground and the house on it was a tanagari, and paid the monthly rent, the tanachin, to the yanushi at the end of each month.
  • What else did a tenant pay?
    In Edo, on first renting ground or a house, or moving in from elsewhere, a tenant gave the yanushi a sum by the house: 2 shu or 1 bu of gold, or as much as 3 or 5 or even 10 ryo. It was called tarudai, "sake money", and seems to have been more or less fixed for each house; in Kyoto and Osaka a tenant gave only a chit for one or two sho of sake. At each of the five festivals, too, an Edo tenant gave the yanushi, by the house, from 50 or 70 mon to 500 or 600, or 2 shu or 1 bu, the sekku-sen, which Kyoto and Osaka did not have. An order of the Tenpo era (1830-1844) forbade both, but they did not stop.
  • And the monthly rent itself?
    The Morisada manko gives no sum for it.
  • A shop, a small house, a samurai's house, a storehouse?
    No rent by the month was found for a street-front shop, a small house, a samurai's residence or a storehouse in Edo, none for a castle town, and none by the week anywhere. Storehouses were let: some of the Mitsui house's waterside lots in Edo were let as storehouses, and storage fees were part of their income, but no rate is given.

Osaka

  • What did a rented house in Osaka cost by its floor?
    The Mitsui merchant house let its Osaka houses by the tatami mat of floor. From 1814, for some years, a house on the street front paid 0.9 monme of silver a mat and a back house 0.7, open ground being let at the same by the tsubo; a building of the house's newly put up in 1846 charged the same by the mat, its back houses' open ground less. The historian reads these as a month's rent: he multiplies them by the mats and open ground of the rented houses the house built on one of its lots in 1852, and calls the total, 406.14 monme, "one month's rent income".
  • Was that the going rate?
    A letter-book put together in 1832 from a man of letters' answers to the questions of Ota Nanpo, a shogunal official who had come to Osaka, gives house rents in silver, high and low averaged, as about 0.7 monme a half tsubo for a back house and about 0.9 for a front one. The historian takes it that the shogunate's control of rents kept Mitsui's at that level into 1848-1854, an assumption he states.
  • What would a back-tenement room cost at that rate?
    A room of 9 shaku by 2 ken, the size the Edo page above names, is 3 tsubo; counted as 6 mats at 0.7 monme, it would rent for about 4.2 monme a month. That is this project's arithmetic on a guess: it assumes a mat is half a tsubo and that the whole floor, the earth-floored entry too, was charged by the mat. The historian's sums count the buildings in mats and do not say how an entry was counted, and no source read says.

Rent against value in Edo

  • What did a town lot earn its owner?
    About 3 to 5% of its value a year. In the historian's earlier study of Edo town lots, they yielded their owner a little over 6% a year early in the 1700s, then 3 to 5%. The forty lots the Mitsui house held, let with their houses, yielded about 4% in the 1770s-1790s and nearly 4.5% in the 1800s.
  • What did the Mitsui house itself reckon?
    A survey of 1811 put the rent on a house-lot taken over for a bad debt at about 4 rin a month on its value. The historian makes that 4.8% a year, and, since half of it was set aside for rebuilding, about 2.4% net.
  • And against the building alone?
    No ratio was found; the yields are against a lot with its houses. What a central Edo lot sold for against its income is in What did farmland sell for against its yield?

Qing Beijing

  • What did a government house rent for?
    By the bay. Under the rule of 1727, a bay of a house of 7 purlins rented for 0.2 tael of silver, of 6 purlins 0.15, of 5 or 4 purlins 0.1, and a bay of 3-purlin veranda 0.05. The Imperial Household Department's own schedule, undated, gives the same rates, with 0.05 for a 3-purlin house too. The department collected the rent monthly, or, for some officials, had the Board of Revenue deduct it from their salaries each quarter, and let its dwelling houses only to Han Chinese officials serving in the capital.
  • And a shop?
    The department's regulations let a government shop-front, where no rent was already set, at 0.3 or 0.4 tael to 3 or 4 taels a bay, by how busy or remote its site. A mutton shop's contract of the Daoguang era (1821-1850) let two shop-front rooms at 1 diao 600 wen of copper cash a month, and from the rents of the shops around it the historian infers about 800 wen to 2 diao a room. In the more commercial districts, from a file of 1871-1911, four government rooms let to a secondhand-clothes shop for 8 diao a month, three to a restaurant for 8, and four rooms on two floors to a bank for 10.
  • And an official's house?
    In 1871 the official Li Ciming rented a house of about 15 or 16 bays for 36,000 wen a month, with a deposit of 10,000.
  • What was a house worth against its rent?
    Before 1731 the department sold its houses by the bay at fixed prices: 70 taels a bay for 9 purlins, 60 for 8, 50 for 7, 40 for 6, 30 for 5, 20 for 4 and 10 for 3, set mainly by the number of purlins; a new schedule of 1802 took account of a house's state of repair. Set against the rents of 1727, a bay rented for about 4 to 6% of its price a year, this record's arithmetic on two administrative schedules, not market prices.

The day's wage beside these rents

  • Beside the Edo rents?
    The carpenter's 5.4 monme a day is a craftsman's wage from the same account as his rent. The Mitsui house's Edo books paid a day laborer 0.9 monme of silver in almost every season of 1818-1829 (What did an unskilled laborer earn?); Kyoto's, in grams of silver, was 4.17 g in 1823. In the same account, a street peddler's daily income, reckoned from what he spent and what he had left, came to about 400 mon, a trader's earnings and not a wage.
  • Beside the move-in and festival payments?
    The Morisada manko gives Edo's wages of the same years: a tobi laborer 300 mon a day, and a carpenter about 5 or 5.5 monme of silver. A festival payment of 50 to 600 mon was a sixth of a tobi's day to two days. At Edo's market rates of copper to gold in the 1830s and early 1840s, about 6,550 to 6,910 mon to the ryo (What was copper coin worth in silver?), the tarudai's 2 shu came to about three of his days, 1 bu to five or six, and 10 ryo to some 220 to 230: this project's arithmetic, at those rates.
  • Beside the Beijing rents of 1727?
    No wage was found for 1727; the nearest are Beijing's of 1736, nine years later: a laborer 75 copper cash a day, and carpenters, masons, bricklayers, tilers and painters 154.
  • Beside the rents of the 1800s?
    No wage of a laborer or a craftsman in the city was found for the Daoguang era or the 1870s. The nearest laborer's is rural Beijing's, from one fuel store's account books, in its own count of copper cash: between 68 and 100 cash a day in the years recorded from 1822 to 1850, and 287 in 1870 and 333 in 1871, the year of Li Ciming's lease, in a count of cash the store changed about 1860. The historians who compiled the series say the wage they derive from it in silver seems extraordinarily low, the hands being also fed (What did an unskilled laborer earn?). The nearest craftsman's is a barber's $3.00, a wage Beijing's barbers' guild recalled in 1918-1919 as paid 50 years before, about 1868-1869, two or three years before the lease and 18 to 48 years after the Daoguang era; the survey that took it down says guild wages were usually fixed by the month, and gives the barbers' present wage, $5 to $10, as a month's with board and lodging; this record reads the $3.00 as a month's. Beside the shop rents of 1871-1911, the guilds' wages of about 1893-1894 are in What did a play or a show cost to see?.
  • Why are rents and wages not set in one unit?
    The rents are in silver and copper and the wages in copper, silver or the dollars the Beijing survey printed. None is converted but the Edo tarudai above, at the market rates in What was copper coin worth in silver?; this record gives no rate between the coins of Qing Beijing.
