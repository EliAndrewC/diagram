Pottery kilns (noborigama)

A noborigama, a climbing kiln, is a long tunnel of joined firing chambers built up a slope: a firebox at the bottom, the chambers climbing above it, and a flue and chimney at the top. The early kilns we found measured ran some 55 to 80 ft up their hills and about 7 ft wide, and the porcelain villages later built shared kilns over 300 ft long. A firing was stoked round the clock for days, and in this project's reading the kiln stood where its clay was.

Roof tiles and everyday pots were fired in kilns built side by side. In this project's reading the households that worked it lived at it, and a kiln district was a village of kiln houses, not a workshop its potters walked out to from town.

  • A climbing kiln is a row of chambers up a slope, unlike a charcoal kiln's low mound.
    Its lowest chamber is the firebox, the kiln mouth, and several firing chambers follow one another up the slope above it; the chambers grow larger toward the top. A charcoal kiln, by contrast, is a low heap of logs built in a hemisphere or cone around a central chimney.
  • How long were the early climbing kilns?
    About 55 to 80 ft, in the two early kilns we found measured. The smallest, thought to have been the hobby kiln of Hosokawa Tadaoki, lord of Kokura at the start of the Edo period, has a stoke mouth and four chambers and is 16.6 m (~54 ft) long. The oldest climbing kiln in Gifu, at Motoyashiki in Mino, is 24 m (~79 ft) long and on average 2.2 m (~7 ft) wide, with fourteen chambers above its firebox.
  • The porcelain villages built kilns of 100 m (~328 ft) and more; Arita's were shared among several kiln houses.
    The newer kilns of Arita were built larger, some 100 m (~328 ft) or more, and used jointly by several kiln houses. Mikawachi fired an east kiln 120 m (~394 ft) long from the end of the 1600s to 1937, and a west kiln of 100 m (~328 ft) from the early 1700s to 1942. A kiln shared that way is a different thing from one works with its own kiln.
  • Did a works' workshop stand beside its kiln?
    At Motoyashiki it did: around the kilns their workshops and waste heaps have been found, and an older kiln there became the workshop of the No. 3 kiln once that was built.
  • No page we read gives how far a kiln stood from the houses round it.
    The nearest is a modern note that smoke makes wood kilns hard to fire in built-up areas.
  • How long did a firing last?
    An anagama, the older one-chamber tunnel kiln, is fired for anywhere from 48 hours to twelve days or more, depending on the kiln's volume, with stoking round the clock, and the kiln generally takes the same time to cool. That a climbing kiln fired the same way is this project's reading.
  • Did a kiln stand where its clay was dug?
    Early kilns were sometimes dug as tunnels into banks of clay, and Jingdezhen, a remote town in hilly country and an unpromising place for potteries, lies close to the best deposits of petuntse, the stone its porcelain was made from. That the clay, a slope for the chambers and the fuel drew digging, throwing, drying and firing to one spot is this project's conclusion.

What the kilns fired was mostly the town's everyday ware; roof tile was a smaller trade.

  • Tile kilns and pottery kilns were built side by side from the Nara period on.
    From the eighth century, kilns for Sue ware were built in each region alongside the kilns that fired roof tiles for the provincial temples.
  • Few houses had tile roofs before the end of the Edo period.
    Even in the Edo period the houses of ordinary commoners were generally roofed with boards, and tile roofing became widespread only at its end.
  • A tile roof was mended one tile at a time.
    A broken tile can be taken out and replaced. No page we read gives how long a tile roof lasts.
  • Did a town use more pots than tiles?
    In this project's reading, yes. Pottery lasts, and survives in the ground as shards long after other things have decayed, but bowls, pots and jars break and are replaced on a steady cycle, which is our premise, not the page's.
  • Where was a heavy storage jar (kame) made?
    At its clay, in this project's reading. Alfred Weber's location theory puts a trade near its raw material when that material weighs more than the goods made from it, and so costs more to carry; that a jar's clay weighs more than the fired jar, and so falls on that side, is this project's application.
  • Stoneware is fired hotter than earthenware.
    Stoneware is fired at about 1,100 to 1,300 degrees C, and earthenware can be fired as low as 600 degrees.
  • Was stoneware a district's specialty rather than every town's?
    In this project's reading, yes. Seto ware takes its name from Seto, where it is made.

Who fired the kilns differed between China, where the state registered its craftsmen, and Japan, where kiln districts grew into villages of kiln houses.

  • China's artisans were registered as households of their own.
    The artisan households formed a class of their own in the registers, were bound to serve in the government's craft bureaus and could not change their registration at will.
  • Did the potters live at the kilns?
    In this project's reading, yes: a kiln district was a village of kiln houses. A firing was stoked day and night for days, so no one walked home in the middle of one, and a kiln stood at its clay, so the digging, throwing, drying and firing were done in one place. At Imado, on the edge of Edo, about fifty households made Imado ware at the end of the Edo period. No page we read says outright where the kiln workers slept.
  • How many kilns did a kiln district hold?
    Medieval Tokoname's kiln sites are said to number over a thousand, and perhaps several thousand, though the true figure is unknown.

Whether a town kept its kilns at a distance is less clear: Edo limited fires within the city, but China's porcelain city kept its imperial kilns at its center.

  • Edo's fire law limited open fires in the town.
    In 1648 the city banned the setting off of fireworks anywhere but the river mouth, and their making in town; in 1653, since the town's bathhouses could not work without fire, it ordered for fire prevention that their fires be kept burning only until about six in the evening. That kilns in particular were pushed out by law is this project's reading.
  • Jingdezhen, China's porcelain city, kept its imperial kilns at its center.
    The imperial kilns stood in the center of the city, with many other kilns four kilometers (~2.5 mi) away at Hutian.
  • Were potters counted among the hinin?
    No page we read puts them there. The hinin of the Edo period were at root beggars; under the old imperial police, the kebiishi, they had also served in cleaning and as executioners. Pottery is not among their offices.
