How our maps place and draw wells (ido)

Our maps draw a settlement's communal wells among the houses they serve, more of them than the record counts, each as a marker larger than life: a square curb on a paved apron, with a well-sweep or a pulley frame over it and, on some, a roof. How many wells a settlement draws is a deliberate liberty; where in a village they stand, which gear lifts the water and which wells are roofed are chosen for each settlement from the map's seed, weighted by what the record found.

  • How many wells does a settlement draw?
    More than the record counts: a town about one well to 20-25 households, a village one to 8-26, a hamlet as many as one to 2-20. So a hamlet draws one or two, a village of 60 households six to nine, a town about sixteen - some two to three times the count this project reads for a real village. These bands are a liberty, not a finding. Ablution wells beside a shrine are not counted among them.
  • Why isn't the number of wells set by how many persons one well could serve?
    A well served several hundred persons - late-Qing Beijing at least 652 to a well - and a village on our maps is about 250 m (~820 ft) across, so neither the crowd at a well nor the walk to it binds. What limits wells is the cost of digging, and, in this project's reading, the queue at the well in the morning.
  • Why doesn't one district's cap on its wells argue for our maps drawing fewer?
    On the Noubi plain the wells the kabu-ido capped were artesian wells dug for irrigation and household use, and the quarrel was their drainage onto the paddy of the villages below, which a draw-well in a house yard does not raise. The case is also, in this project's reading, one district's arrangement rather than a norm, and it ran from the end of the Edo period to the 1930s. It does not show a general pressure toward fewer wells, so the liberty stands.
  • A household counts as watered by a well or by the water it sits beside.
    That is a channel, pond, stream or moat within about 760 ft, measured in real feet on a hamlet's map and a village's alike; a row village spaces its shared wells by the same reach (How our maps draw a row village). A dispersed farm household drew from the water it already had, in this project's reading: the page we read says channel water served domestic and drinking use, not that a farm household drew from the channel beside it. The count makes the wells present; the reach makes sure no household is left dry. A dispersed farm's own well is at How our maps choose and draw clustered and scattered villages (shūson, sanson).

Where a well stands follows from whom it served.

  • Every well stands among the houses it serves, never out in the fields.
    On hamlet and town maps a well must have a dwelling within about 95 ft, and the map places its wells a little closer than that.
  • Whether a village's wells stand by the lane or among the houses is a knob.
    Each settlement takes one of two forms, chosen from its seed. In the lane-side form the wellhead stands on a lane verge, clear of the tread, within reach of the houses; in the cluster form it stands in the open ground among five to ten houses, off every lane - the five to ten houses that each dug well served at Karihama, a coastal village in Ehime on Shikoku. The cluster form is this project's reading of that village; the lane-side form is attested for wayside wells in general, and its use in a village is a GUESS.
  • A communal well sits on a paved apron about 4 ft wider than its curb all round.
    The village of Karihama, in Ehime, paved the ground round many of its common wells; the apron's width is a GUESS.
  • No well is drawn out on open common ground.
    No page we read sets a village's well there.
  • A shrine or temple set apart from the houses keeps its own ablution well.
    Where its nearest dwelling is more than about 150 ft off, a well stands beside the hall, measured from the hall's edge, so a large hall pushes its well outward. It is a well rather than a ditch or a pond by the map's convention, not a finding: no page we read says which waters a shrine used.
  • A hilltop shrine draws no well.
    That rests on the GUESS that its water came from a spring or a cistern. A well could be dug where the water lay far down, so this is a drawing rule rather than a law.

A city's wells follow its quarters.

  • A city's commoner quarters are sprinkled with public wells, one within a short walk of any home.
    No commoner dwelling inside the walls stands more than about 870 ft from a wellhead, and no single well is the nearest for more than 26 households. On our maps servants live among the commoners and share their wells; a gate market outside the wall is not housing and is not counted. A city of about 600 households runs on the order of 47 wells, drawn one by one rather than as a symbol.
  • Why may one city well serve as many as 26 households?
    It is a calibration, not a finding. Coverage can look fine on average while the busiest wells in a dense quarter are swamped, and giving each household to its nearest well over-counts where two wells stand nearly equidistant, so the ceiling sits a little above the ten to twenty households this project reads for a tenement's well. The 870 ft reach is a calibration against the drawn cities too.
  • A city's wellheads stand inside its blocks, and the houses are packed round them.
    They sit clear of the lanes and off every building and compound, and the houses go in afterward, so they end up enclosed. Each well keeps only a small court, so a city's wells do not visibly thin its population, though they do displace some housing.
  • The samurai and government quarter draws no public wells.
    A samurai household could draw from a well inside its own fence, as the Aoyagi house at Kakunodate does, and a public well there would read wrong.

What the map draws at a well is a marker, sized to be seen, carrying the forms the record found.

  • A well is drawn as a marker larger than life.
    At one foot per pixel its curb is drawn at a radius of 9.36 ft, about 19 ft across, so the well is visible at map scale: the marker shows where the well stands, not how much ground it takes. It scales with the map: the mean well's width against the median dwelling's longer side lands between 0.35 and 0.85 on every kind of map, hamlet to city - smaller than a house, but not a dot. The band is a calibration against the drawn maps.
  • How large a curb does the marker stand for?
    About 4 ft square. The one curb we found measured is 118 cm (~46 in) square, and round a premodern shaft about 1 m (~3 ft) across a 4 ft curb leaves a frame about 4 in wide a side. So the 4 ft is calibrated to the premodern shaft, and its exact figure is a GUESS.
  • The lifting gear is a knob, weighted by how deep the water lies.
    On shallow water about three wells in four take a sweep, a pole about as long as the marker is wide crossing the curb from a post outside it; on deeper water about three in four take a pulley frame, two posts and a beam across the curb. The two gears, and the depth that chooses between them, are attested; the three-in-four split is a GUESS.
  • Our maps take a valley floor or river plain to hold shallow water, an upland or terrace deep water.
    That is a GUESS.
  • The shallowest wells, where the bucket hung from a plain pole with no post, are drawn with a sweep all the same.
    That pole had no post, and drawing it as a sweep is a convention.
  • The pulley frame is a premodern form.
    Wang Zhen's windlass stood on a frame set over the well carrying an axle, which reads from above as the map's two posts and a beam. Its drawn proportions are a GUESS.
  • A roof is drawn over about one well in three.
    That share is a GUESS: how many ordinary wells had a roof is not known, and a roof on every well would draw the finished form, not the common one.
  • The roof is a premodern form too.
    In the Ming and Qing every well in the Forbidden City had a pavilion over it.

Wells that watered the crops were real in north China, but they are not the well our maps draw.

  • The well our maps draw is the household's draw-point, so it stays off the crop.
    It is the idobata, the neighbors' gathering place at the water, so the map keeps every well off a dry crop plot: a lone curb in the middle of one would be wrong as drawn. The reason is not that field wells never existed; it is that the drawn well means something else.
  • Our dry plots are not the dryland the field well was built for.
    They are the hem and margin round the wet-rice fields of a monsoon valley, not the loess country of north China, with its 400 to 800 mm (~16-31 in) of rain a year concentrated in the summer, and the water they need arrives, by this project's GUESS, as rain and through the fields' own ditches.
  • Our maps draw no irrigation wells.
    One would be a separate mark - the curb with a windlass frame or a sweep - standing in the dry plot it serves rather than among the houses, in a dry upland county rather than a paddy valley. That siting is this project's GUESS: the pages we read say a well's water went to fields too, not where such a well stood (Communal wells (ido)).
