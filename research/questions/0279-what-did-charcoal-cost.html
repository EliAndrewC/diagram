What did charcoal cost?

The setting's price list guesses what a bale of ordinary black charcoal and of fine white charcoal cost in Rokugan, and weighs each by how many days of a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give what the historical record holds: what charcoal cost by the bale in Edo-period Japan and by the jin in Ming and Qing China, set beside the nearest day wages found.

In Edo in 1842 the ward headmen reported the prices of charcoal, by grade and by the bale, at between 112 and 466 mon after a cut ordered that year. In 1716, as a local historian reports it at second hand, the best Kumano charcoal sold at 500 mon a bale, about two and a half times the white charcoal of the Tama hills. In Beijing in 1736 white charcoal was priced at about twice black, by the official list. No Japanese source found sets black charcoal against white, and none gives a retail price by small measure, a wholesale price at the kiln, or the cost of carrying it.

Edo's charcoal by the bale, 1842

  • Edo's charcoal prices were reported by grade and by the bale.
    In 1842, under the order that fixed the copper rate and cut prices to match, the ward headmen in charge of charcoal and firewood returned the prices of six grades of charcoal, each by the bale, before and after the cut:

    low charcoal364342
    Sakura charcoal144135
    Sakura, middle132124
    Sakura, low120112
    The "before" prices were themselves the prices already cut in the fifth month of that year.
  • What the return does not say.
    It prints no weight for the bale, does not call any grade black or white, and does not say whether these are a shop's prices or a wholesaler's. Sakura charcoal is priced at less than a third of the top grade, and the return gives no reason.
  • What was a day's pay?
    No wage of Edo dated 1842 was found. The nearest unskilled wages are a farm day laborer's 132 mon in the Kazusa village of Nagata in the same year (What did sake cost by the shop and by the cup?), and Edo's building laborers at 300 mon a day in a record of the 1830s to the 1850s that names no year; the nearest craftsman's is an Edo carpenter's 5 to 5.5 monme of silver a day in the same record (What did a skilled craftsman earn against a laborer?).
  • How many days of pay was a bale?
    A bale of the top grade, at 466 mon, was about a day and a half of the Edo laborer's 300 mon and three and a half of the Nagata laborer's; at Osaka's copper rate of 1842, about 105 mon to the monme (What was copper coin worth in silver?), it was about 0.8 to 0.9 of the carpenter's day. A bale of the top Sakura charcoal, at 135 mon, was about half the Edo laborer's day. This is this record's arithmetic, an Osaka rate set on an Edo wage of no stated year.

Kumano charcoal against Tama's, 1716

  • Kumano charcoal cost about two and a half times Tama's.
    In 1716, a year of short charcoal and high prices, the antiquarian Mitamura Engyo found in a merchant's diary that Kumano charcoal from Kii province sold at 500 mon a bale for the top grade and 400 for the next, eight or nine bales to the ryo of 4,000 mon, while Hachioji charcoal, the name Edo gave the charcoal of the Tama hills, sold at about 20 bales to the ryo; so a local historian reports it, at second hand. At that rate a bale of Kumano charcoal was about 440 to 500 mon and a bale of Hachioji charcoal about 200, by this record's arithmetic. The same historian calls Kumano charcoal bincho.
  • Why by the ryo?
    Charcoal was priced by how many bales a ryo bought, the historian writes, thinking this also allowed for a bale's weight not being fixed.
  • Tama's was white charcoal.
    The charcoal of the Tama hills, the historian writes, had all been white charcoal until black charcoal was made on the Musashino plain, so the ratio of about 2.2 to 2.5 between Kumano and Hachioji sets Kumano's charcoal against a white one, not black against white. No Japanese price of black charcoal set beside white was found.
  • What was a day's pay in 1716?
    No wage of Edo or the Tama district in those years was found. The nearest craftsman's wage is the Tama district's carpenters and sawyers at 1 to about 1.7 monme of silver a day in 1749, 33 years later (What did a small shrine sanctuary cost to build?); the nearest unskilled wage is a Kyoto laborer's 2.82 g of silver a day on average over 1745-1754, about 30 years later (What did an unskilled laborer earn?). Both are in silver and the prices in copper, and the record holds no copper rate of 1716, so no count of days is given.

How heavy was a bale?

  • No weight dated to the Edo period was found.
    The record's graded weights are from the Iburi district of Hokkaido, undated: 4 kan (about 15 kg) for the best charcoal, 10 and 8 kan for lower grades (Charcoal yards and charcoal stores; Straw bales of rice and charcoal). None of the sources read for this question gives a weight of the Edo period.
  • Tama's bales were not of one weight either.
    Tama charcoal bales weighed 4 kan, 5 kan or 6 kan 500 monme (about 24 kg), the historian writes without a date, and a record of 1887-1896 gives 5 kan a bale of Hikawa charcoal.

How much work went into a bale?

  • An earth kiln of black charcoal gave about ten times a stone kiln of white.
    Black charcoal fired three or four days in an earth kiln yielded 20 or 30 bales a firing, the historian writes, against 2 or 3 from the small stone kiln of white charcoal. It is the historian's general statement, with no date or source; "about ten times" is this record's arithmetic.
  • How many burner-days, and how much wood?
    No count of the days of work in a firing, and no yield of charcoal from wood, was found.

Retail, wholesale and carriage

  • What did charcoal cost by small measure in a city?
    No price by weight or by small measure was found.
  • What did it cost at the kiln, and to carry?
    No wholesale price at the kiln and no freight charge were found. What was found is a tax and the size of a load. Around Ome a horse-load is said to have been 6 bales, and at Itsukaichi it was 8; the Itsukaichi brokers collected from the sellers 8 mon, later 12, for every horse-load of 8 bales as the tax owed on it, and gave the buyer a charcoal ticket. Farmers of the Tama villages carried charcoal and firewood to the Edo mansions by horse overnight and earned carriage money, a village record of 1863 says, without the amount. In 1726 about 810,000 bales of charcoal reached Edo by ship, by a count a local-history book reports.
  • The nearest price before the Edo period.
    In 1600 the Akita house's account for repairing Minato castle entered 181 kanmon for 120 bales of smith's charcoal, about 1,500 mon a bale by this record's arithmetic, with no bale weight. No wage of Akita in 1600 was found; the nearest are the day laborers of building works in and near Osaka in 1620-1628, at 0.5 to 1.5 monme of silver a day (What did a kitchen knife cost?).

Charcoal in Qing and Ming China

  • Beijing priced white charcoal at about twice black in 1736.
    The official price list the Ministry of Works settled in 1736 priced, by the 100 jin, charcoal at 0.225 tael, black charcoal at 0.5 and 0.6, white charcoal at 1.1 and Hongluo charcoal at 1.06, and charcoal cakes by the piece. The same list paid a laborer 75 copper cash a day and building craftsmen 154, about 0.094 and 0.19 tael at the 800 cash to the tael the historian takes for Beijing.
  • How many days of pay was that?
    White charcoal was about 1.8 to 2.2 times black. A hundred jin of black was about five to six and a half laborer-days, or about three craftsman-days; a hundred jin of white about twelve laborer-days, or six craftsman-days. A laborer's day bought about 16 to 19 jin of black or 8.5 of white. This is this record's arithmetic.
  • And in 1615?
    The Ming Ministry of Works' handbook of 1615 priced charcoal at between 0.12 and 0.75 tael the 100 jin and white charcoal at between 0.49 and 1.113, as the same historian tabulates it; it paid its craftsmen and laborers between 0.03 and 0.07 tael a day, the historian naming no class for either end.
  • Zhili's districts priced charcoal by the jin.
    The Zhili regulation, whose introductory memorial is dated 1769, prices charcoal in 137 rows across its districts, between 0.0018 and 0.008 tael a jin, 51 of them at 0.005, which is 0.5 tael the 100 jin; it names no black or white.   • And at a Ming weapons forge?
    A costing of a matchlock in the military compendium Wubian puts 170 jin of charcoal at 0.51 tael, with the forge's smiths at 0.03 tael a day; a second estimate it quotes puts 500 jin at 0.85 tael, with the tongs-man at 0.04 a day and the ordinary smiths at 0.03. That is about 0.30 and 0.17 tael the 100 jin, or about ten and six smith-days the 100 jin, this record's arithmetic. It names no place of work and no laborer's wage; the nearest is the 1615 handbook's above.
