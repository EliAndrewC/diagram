Towpaths along a river (qiandao)

In China a towpath is the qiandao, and the best known runs beside the Eastern Zhejiang Canal at Shaoxing, raised as an earth embankment in 815, under the Tang dynasty, and close to a hundred li long. Part of it stands not on the bank at all but out in the water, in places a solid causeway, in places stone slabs laid on stone piers.

Along a navigable river the sources show a towpath, and at a landing a place where cargo was loaded and unloaded. A highway beside the river they do not show, and this project reasons it would only have duplicated the boats, which carried bulk goods far more cheaply than carts or porters.

  • A towpath served the boats: haulers walked it to pull them upstream.
    A boat going downstream drifts with the current, and one going upstream has to be pulled; the towpath is where the haulers walked. It served the boats rather than competing with them, and so was narrow, ran on one bank and went only as far as the boats went. This is this project's reading of what a towpath was for; no page we read sets it out.
  • When was Shaoxing's towpath, in eastern China, built?
    A museum account says Meng Jian, the surveillance commissioner of Eastern Zhejiang, raised it as an earth embankment in 815, under the Tang dynasty. The Chinese encyclopedia tells it otherwise: a towpath formed little by little along the bank after the Xixing Canal was cut under the Western Jin dynasty, five centuries earlier, and in 815 Meng Jian renovated it on a large scale.
  • Shaoxing's towpath ran close to a hundred li, some 50 km (~31 miles).
    The Chinese encyclopedia gives its whole length as close to a hundred li. A comparatively well-preserved stretch, in Keqiao district, is 7.5 km (~4.7 miles) long.
  • Shaoxing's towpath ran partly on the bank and partly out in the water.
    Shaoxing's towpath takes two forms, one on the bank of the canal and one out in the watercourse, parallel to the bank. The Chinese encyclopedia divides the second again, into a solid causeway and a deck on stone piers.
  • How high above the water did Shaoxing's towpath stand where it ran on stone piers?
    A news report puts each of its stone slabs, laid on the piers, about 0.5 m (~20 in) above the water. No other page we could read gives the height.

Why a river carried a towpath rather than a road beside it comes down to the cost of carriage, and Japan's rivers show the same as China's.

  • How much cheaper was carriage by water than by land?
    Over long distances a Chinese junk may have had a nine- or tenfold cost advantage over human and animal carriage.
  • In Japan, tribute rice went to Edo more by river boat than overland.
    A study of Japanese river transport says river boats were used more than land transport to carry the tribute rice sent to Edo from every region.
  • Did a trunk road run beside a navigable river?
    The cost of carriage suggests not: a highway shadowing a navigable river would carry what the boats already carried more cheaply. That a road beside such a river was redundant is this project's reasoning from the cost gap, not a source's statement.
  • On the Tokaido, the Oi River had no bridge and no ferry.
    In the Edo period no bridge was built over the Oi and no ferry was allowed, so travelers were carried across by porters. That a road met a river to cross it, not to follow it, is this project's generalization from this case.
  • Why was the Oi River left without a bridge or a ferry?
    The usual explanation is the defense of Edo, or support for the post towns. A study of the crossing argues that technical and social and economic causes weighed even more, and recent research holds that the river's great volume and swift current made it unsuited to a bridge, and that the system protected the profits the crossing brought the river offices and post towns.
  • A river landing in Japan, the kashi, was a loading place and a town in its own right.
    A Japanese dictionary defines the kashi as the bank of a river, and in particular the place where cargo was loaded onto and taken off boats, and adds that administratively a kashi was a town or a village. Its quay served the landing's docks, storehouses and brokers rather than carrying through traffic, which is this project's reading. The wharf itself is at Wharves and landings: piers, quays and stepped landings (kashi, gangi).
