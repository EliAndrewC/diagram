When did cotton become ordinary in India and in Korea?

Rokugan's tax rules set cotton beside common iron as an ordinary, low-rate good, and the setting's list of regional cash crops does not name it. The findings below give what the historical record holds for India, and for Korea, which sent cotton cloth to Japan.

India grew cotton for thousands of years before Korea did. It was being cultivated in the Indus region by about 2500 BCE and was widespread across India by the last centuries BCE. India exported the cloth, and only in the 1800s did imported factory cloth arrive in force, reaching more than half of the cotton cloth India consumed by about 1880, on this project's arithmetic from one economic historian's estimates.

Korea has one find of cotton cloth from the Baekje kingdom and a recorded start to growing the plant in the 1360s. One study has all ranks wearing cotton by the later 1400s, and a poem by a scholar of the 1700s and a traveler of the 1890s say much the same. The same stages for Japan are in when cotton became ordinary clothing in Japan, for China in how cotton reached China and spread across it, and the species in which cottons grew in Asia before 1492.

India

  • South Asia's first record of cotton is fiber and seed at Mehrgarh, by 5000-4300 BCE.
    A review of the finds by Dorian Fuller and colleagues has cotton textile production and use established in Baluchistan by then, whether wild or cultivated being unknown.
  • At first India exported cotton cloth.
    Fuller calls cotton a major export from ancient India. A merchant's handbook of the first century, the Periplus, lists Indian cotton cloth among the exports of Barygaza. In 1795 exports were still only 1-2% of India's own cloth, in the estimate of Tirthankar Roy.
  • India became a net importer of cloth in 1830.
    Michael Twomey, working from the trade figures, puts the turning point there.
  • Did imported cloth ever supply most of India's cotton cloth?
    Yes, for a few decades. Roy's estimates of net imports against all the cotton cloth India consumed make imports about 14% in 1840, 44% in 1860, 63-64% in 1880, 60% in 1900, 31% in 1920 and 6% in 1940, on this project's arithmetic. Twomey's figures give about 14% in 1850 and about 59% in 1880-1884, also on this project's arithmetic, and Twomey's table keeps imports just above half through 1910-14. No source read names the year imports first passed half.
  • What share of India's cloth was imported in the 1880s is disputed.
    W. W. Hunter wrote in 1886 that about three-fifths of the cotton cloth used was woven in the country. Twomey's own estimate of that share is only two-fifths, and Twomey calls the difference puzzling.
  • Imports fell away after 1913.
    Twomey has them peaking in 1913 and at 32% of India's cloth consumption by 1929.
  • Cultivation is inferred from about 2500 BCE in the Indus region.
    That is when seeds begin to appear regularly, from which Fuller infers that cultivation was well established. Seeds appear elsewhere in India from about 1000 BCE, and by the late centuries BCE cotton was widespread across the country.
  • Was cotton a commercial crop as early as the Harappan period?
    Fuller calls it a key cash crop by the Mature Harappan period, on the evidence of cotton at a wide range of sites by 2000 BCE. That is a reading of the archaeology. The first-century handbook is written evidence of the crop grown and of its cloth in trade: the country of Ariaca, on the gulf of Barygaza, yields cotton and coarse cloth made from it.
  • Did India's commoners wear cotton in antiquity?
    The general statements say so, without naming any class. The Greek historian Herodotus says the Indians wore clothing from trees that grow wool, which this project takes to be cotton, and elsewhere that tribes in the river swamps wore clothing of rushes. The 1911 Encyclopaedia Britannica says that for five centuries before the Christian era the clothing of the inhabitants was chiefly cotton. No page read separates commoners from the better-off before the 1800s.
  • Family budgets show poor households buying less cloth than the average in the 1800s.
    Roy's sample has about 4 yards a head in one Madras laborer's family budget of 1810, against 5.1-7.0 for all India, and 3.5 yards in a village survey of Bengal in 1851. Roy also calls cotton more the fiber of choice in India than in Britain or China at the time.

Korea

Korea met cotton far later, and its record is mostly the royal annals and modern studies of them.

  • The first cotton cloth excavated from the Three Kingdoms period came from a Baekje temple.
    Fiber analysis of a small rolled piece excavated at Neungsan-ri showed it to be cotton, and a second remnant was judged the same cloth from its weave. The study dates it to the Baekje period from what was found with it, and places the temple's building in 567. It says it cannot yet tell whether the cloth was woven in Baekje, though it judges from Baekje's long record of spinning that it could have been.
  • Later texts name a cloth the study takes to be cotton, as a rarity.
    The same study reports a Chinese work of about 660 saying the people of Goguryeo made it and a Korean chronicle recording it sent to Tang China in 869, and concludes that it was not a generally used cloth but an extremely rare one, used for gifts.
  • Did cotton reach Korea by trade before the 1360s?
    In-Sung Kim Han writes that cotton had already reached Goryeo through contact with Yuan China, and cites an undated language textbook, probably of the late Goryeo period, whose imaginary scenario has a merchant buy cotton wool in China and sell it at a profit in Goryeo. Han's reading is that cotton had not spread widely before seed was brought, and Han reports that some scholars reject the earlier introduction as a distortion. No page read says what share of Korea's cotton came from abroad.
  • Was cotton general in the Goryeo period?
    An encyclopedia says cotton cloth became general then. Han's account has it not widely spread before the seed was brought, and that was at the end of the Goryeo period, so the two can both stand if the encyclopedia means the last decades of that period.
  • Imported cotton goods were arriving by 1887.
    The encyclopedia has cloth imported by Britons and Chinese at the end of the Joseon dynasty, and Japanese cloth from 1895. A statement printed by the traveler Isabella Bird Bishop values cotton goods imported in 1896 at $3,338,545 (yarn included). Bishop has Japanese cottons rising from 3 percent of the imports in 1887 to about 40 percent in 1894, and also saw coarse, narrow cloth of Korean make on sale. So on Bishop's account the encyclopedia's 1895 is not the start of Japanese imports.
  • The plant's first recorded growing is at Jinju in the 1360s.
    Mun Ik-jeom's obituary of 1398 says that he took about ten cotton fruits when about to return from the Yuan court and reached Jinju in 1364, giving half to Jeong Cheon-ik, of which one lived. By spring 1367 Jeong was sharing seed out. Han gives 1363 for the seed, and reports that the Goryeosa, a history of the dynasty, agrees on the seed and Jeong's planting but omits a foreign monk the obituary credits with the weaving tools. The 1911 Britannica, naming no source, says Korea received its cotton plants from China some 500 years before its own day.
  • Did cotton weaving really cover Korea within ten years?
    The obituary says weaving covered the country in under ten years. It is entered under Mun's death in 1398, thirty years after the event. Lee Ja-yeon has cotton growing actively encouraged from the middle of the 1400s, and cotton spreading and being produced from Pyeongan in the north to Jeolla in the south.
  • In the 1400s cotton cloth is recorded as a state and export good.
    Lee says slaves' tribute held in one government office in the 10th month of 1485 came to 724,500 bolts of cotton, against over 180,000 of hemp. It shows how much the state held, and says nothing about farmers selling a crop.
  • Cotton as a market crop is attested for later Joseon.
    Kim Dae-gil has cloth second only to grain among market goods in the five-day rural markets, Honam and Yeongnam in the south famous for cotton cloth, and many farms paying taxes in cloth and enlarging production for sale. Kim also reports a list from the first half of the 1800s naming raw cotton and cotton cloth among the main market goods. No page read dates the start of that trade.
  • Cotton was still a crop for sale in the 1890s.
    Bishop names it among the great articles of export of the Han valley.
  • When did Korea's commoners wear cotton?
    The sources give three dates, none with a count behind it.
    • The later 1400s: Lee has men and women of all ranks wearing cotton cloth by then, citing an entry of the annals of 1456 that was not read here.
    • The 1700s: the scholar Yi Ik, in a poem, has cotton clothing all under heaven, down to the low and base.
    • The 1890s: Bishop calls a country of at least 12 million all cotton-clad, and notes the serious cost of the farmer's cotton clothing.
  • Cotton did not end the use of other cloth in later Joseon.
    Kim names cotton, hemp and silk together as the main daily necessities of most of the population.

Korea and cotton reaching Japan

  • Korea sent cotton cloth to Japan from around 1400.
    A draft by Masayuki Tanimoto, marked by its author not to be quoted or cited without permission, has Japan's supply around 1400 wholly imported from Korea and China. A Japanese reference article has Korean cotton the largest item of that trade under King Sejong (1418-50), lasting to the reign of Seongjong (1469-94).
  • Cotton overtook hemp among the goods given to Japanese envoys in the mid-1400s.
    In 1423 Lee has 3,325 bolts of cotton against 23,306 of hemp, the government then curbing cotton's outflow, and cotton the main return gift from the middle of the century.
  • How much cotton went to Japan?
    The yearly consignments are in the Japan question. Lee says the return gifts to Japanese ran to over 500,000 bolts a year about 1486, and Kawakatsu Heita's table has 500,000 for that year. Lee's figure is one author's reading of the annals, from a scanned page. For 1475 the two differ slightly: Lee 27,201 bolts paid out in Seoul and Gyeongsang, Kawakatsu 27,208.
  • Lee thinks Japan wanted the cloth because it did not yet produce cotton.
    Lee says that in this period cotton was not yet produced in Japan and was a precious cloth known only by import.
  • Did the plant also come to Japan from Korea?
    The sources differ on the route, and none records a shipment of seed. Kawakatsu says seeds probably came from China by way of Korea or Ryukyu, on different occasions. A Japanese encyclopedia says what spread across the country in the Muromachi period came from around Korea. A local historian has the seed imported from Ming China. The 1911 Britannica says Japan received cotton from India before China, naming no route.
