How our maps draw (and do not draw) pleasure quarters and inn serving women

No map draws a walled licensed quarter (yukaku), and no map gives a post town's serving women (meshimori) a house of their own. In the setting, prostitution is illegal by Imperial decree, a law honored more in breach than observance, and the trade goes on in a city's geisha houses, outside the law. A trade spread through the city rather than walled off is also the form most Japanese pleasure districts took.

  • A capital's licensed quarter is left out, a deliberate deviation from the history.
    Edo, Kyoto and Osaka each had a licensed quarter, but nothing in the setting's notes sets one out, so a capital's entertainment district is drawn instead as teahouses, geisha houses and playhouses among the townspeople's streets.
  • Which form would a licensed quarter take, if a capital had one?
    The history offers three, which would be chosen at random for each capital as a knob: inside the town with a fence on one side, at the town's edge with an earthen wall, or cut off from the town, enclosed by a wall and fields, with a single gate. Each would be laid out on newly opened ground, as Osaka's was.
  • How large would a licensed quarter be?
    Its size would fall between the two sites of Edo's Yoshiwara, the first and the one it was moved to: 720 to 840 ft square, taking the smaller of the two figures the record gives for the second site.
  • A post town's serving women (meshimori) get no building of their own.
    They worked in the inns, which kept them as employees to entertain the lodgers, so the inns already on the map are the feature; there is no separate house, quarter or gate to draw.
