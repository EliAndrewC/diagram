How our maps draw reservoir ponds (tameike), their reeds and their embankments

Every reservoir pond on our maps has a fringe of reeds standing on wet ground at its shore, an embankment beyond them where the growth of grass and trees is held back, one outlet, and an area kept in proportion to the paddy it waters - two or three parts in ten for a field on high ground, by the measure of a Chinese farming manual of the Song dynasty. None of these is a drawing convention. Where the maps simplify, it is this project's choice: every bank is drawn bare, though the same manual plants some with trees, and a pond that a stream tops up is drawn smaller by a guess.

  • A pond's reeds stand on ground drawn wet.
    Reed, wild rice and cattail grow in colonies around a shallow pond, among the large emergent plants that a pond's plants are sorted by water depth into, so the pale wet tint that marks a reed bed at the waterside is drawn under a pond's reeds too, even where the fringe is narrow.
  • No reed harvest is drawn at a pond.
    A pond's reeds stand uncut, with no cut bed, drying racks or stacks, because nothing we read puts a harvest at a pond's margin. It is left out because it is not attested, not because it is known to have been absent.
  • Reeds and the wet tint are kept off the embankment.
    A pond's embankment is managed to keep its strength, the growth of grass and trees on it held back, so no reed or tint is drawn on it, nor on any raised rim around a pond.
  • The bank is drawn bare as the older record keeps it: trodden firm by cattle, not mown and burned as today.
    Our maps draw only what is attested before modern times, so the bank stands for a bank trodden firm by cattle, or turfed as Edo-period river embankments were, not for the mowing and burning of today's ponds, which no page we read shows before 1868.
  • A bank with a few trees on it is not drawn yet.
    A Chinese farming manual of the Song dynasty plants a pond's bank sparsely with mulberry and cudrania, a second form attested beside the bare bank. Our maps draw every bank bare and do not yet vary between the two - a shortfall, not a finding that banks were bare.
  • Each pond has one outlet.
    A pond's water is drawn off through one set of works - an inclined or vertical pipe into a bottom conduit - with a sediment gate at the very bottom for managing the pond and drawing off its bottom water, so the maps draw one outlet and one main ditch leaving it. That a pond had no second way out to its fields is this project's reading.
  • Does a pond's ditch fork below it, as a large canal's does?
    The large canal systems divide at junctions downstream, their main canals tapering as branch canals draw from them, and the maps draw a pond's ditch forking the same way. That a pond's ditch did so is this project's reading.

A pond's size is set by the paddy it waters, and by whether a stream helps water it.

  • A reservoir pond is sized by the paddy it waters.
    Its area is kept in proportion to the area it waters, as a Chinese farming manual of the Song dynasty gave a field on high ground a pond of two or three parts in every ten of its land. That a high field's pond was its only water is this project's reading, and a pond that is its fields' only water is drawn to that measure.
  • No storage figure per acre is used.
    The maps size a pond by its area alone, because no page we read, of any period, gives how much a pond stores for each acre it waters.
  • A pond that a stream keeps topping up is drawn smaller.
    Such a pond need hold less of the season's water itself - this project's reasoning, not a finding - so it is held below the two or three tenths of its paddy that the Song-dynasty manual gives. How far below is a GUESS: no page we read gives how much a feeding stream saves.
  • The pond on our map of Hoshigaoka is about a twentieth of the paddy it waters, because a stream feeds the paddy as well.
    About 1.5 ha (~3.7 acres) of water for 31.8 ha (~79 acres) of paddy, against the two or three tenths the Song-dynasty manual gives a pond that is its fields' only water.
