What did fruit cost?

Rokugan's households and travelers buy fruit, and the setting judges a price by how many days of a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give the few fruit prices the historical record holds for Edo-period Japan and the fuller lists of Qing and Ming China, each set beside the nearest wages found.

Only one fruit price was found for Edo-period Japan: a persimmon at 6 mon in the villages of the Kanto in the first half of the 1800s, from a museum's teaching sheet that names none of its documents. In Beijing the Qing court bought its fruit at fixed prices in silver, a persimmon at 0.008 tael and an apple at 0.03, when a laborer on the capital's official list was paid 75 copper cash a day and a craftsman 154, which the list's historian puts at about 0.094 and 0.19 tael.

Japan

  • What did a persimmon cost?
    6 mon apiece. A teaching sheet of the Bank of Japan's currency museum lists it among the prices of the Kanto villages in the first half of the 1800s, and says it took its items from several different documents, since it is hard to find the count or quantity and the price together in one document; it names none of them, so the persimmon's year and village cannot be traced.
  • And beside a craftsman's day?
    About a thirty-third of it. The nearest craftsman's wage is Kumehara's, a village of Musashi in the Kanto, where thirty-nine villages agreed a carpenter's day wage of 200 mon in 1818 (What did a small shrine sanctuary cost to build?). The same sheet gives a cook's wage of 300 mon a day, from another of its unnamed documents.
  • And beside a laborer's?
    No unskilled wage of the Kanto villages was found. The nearest is the Kyoto day laborer's of 1802-1804, paid in silver, far to the west and in another coin, so it is not set against the persimmon (What did an unskilled laborer earn?).
  • What did fruit cost in Edo or a castle town, or a pear, a native apple or a dried persimmon?
    No price was found for any of them.

China: what the Qing court paid in Beijing

  • Who bought the court's fruit, and with what?
    A licensed supplier, paid from a tax of 0.1 tael a year on each mu of the court's orchard land, which was set aside for buying fresh fruit. These are the prices the court paid, not prices in a market.
  • What did it pay?
    In silver: a persimmon 0.008 tael, an apple (pingguo) 0.03, a crabapple (huahong) 0.003, a yellow pear 0.015, a red pear or a tang pear 0.013, and an orange 0.05. The list does not say what these prices were for; that each is the price of one fruit is this project's reading, since the list names no unit until after its fresh fruit and walnuts, where it begins to price by the slice and the jin.
  • And dried persimmons?
    0.022 tael a jin, in the same list.
  • When?
    The list carries no year. It stands in a compilation of the Qianlong reign, after a ruling of 1748 on weighing the court's nuts and dried fruit.
  • What did a day's wage buy at these prices?
    The nearest wages are Beijing's own, on the official price list of 1736: a laborer 75 cash a day and a carpenter, mason or other craftsman 154. The list's historian converts them to about 0.094 and 0.19 tael, at the rate a memorial of 1737 gives for the capital. At those figures a laborer's day was worth about 12 persimmons, 3 apples, 31 crabapples, 6 yellow pears or 4 jin of dried persimmons, and a craftsman's about twice as many. The fruit prices and the wages are from different lists, years apart.

China: the value set on fruit paid in place of tax

  • What was a pear worth to the court's orchards in Manchuria?
    0.01 tael. By a ruling of 1706, the gardeners of the imperial orchards at Shengjing (Mukden) and Guangning delivered hazelnuts, hawthorns, crabapples and xiangshui pears to be set against their tax, a xiangshui pear credited by the rule at 0.01 tael apiece.
  • And a peach?
    By a ruling of 1729, each of the twenty-six new orchard heads delivered twenty baskets of sweet peaches in place of tax, credited by the old rule at 0.2 tael a basket of twenty, 0.01 tael a peach.
  • Is this a price?
    No: it is the value at which a delivery was set against the tax owed, not what anyone paid. No wage of the orchards was found; the nearest is Beijing's of 1736, thirty years later and in another place.

China: the Ming valuation of stolen fruit

  • What was fruit worth to a Ming court?
    The Ming statutes' table for valuing stolen goods, in the state's paper money, puts persimmons at 1 guan for thirty, pears and peaches at 2 guan a hundred, small apples (linqin), apricots and plums at 1 guan a hundred, and dried persimmons at 1 guan a jin. Its own rule says stolen goods are valued at the price current where the crime was done.
  • Against rice and silver?
    The same table puts a shi of polished or glutinous rice at 25 guan and a tael of silver at 80. So a shi of rice was worth about 750 persimmons, 1,250 pears or 2,500 small apples, and a tael of silver 2,400 persimmons, 4,000 pears or 8,000 small apples.
  • When, and beside what wage?
    The table carries no year; it follows a ruling of 1497. No wage in the same paper money was found, so the table gives only the worth of fruit against rice and silver.
