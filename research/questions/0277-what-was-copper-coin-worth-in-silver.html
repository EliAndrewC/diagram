What was copper coin worth in silver?

This record's historical wages are counted in two monies: some in silver, like the Kyoto laborer's day wage in monme, and some in copper coin, like the Nagata farm laborer's in mon. Setting one beside the other needs the rate between them. The findings below give what the historical record holds for that rate in Edo-period Japan: the rate the law fixed, what copper coin fetched in silver on the Osaka market from 1787 to 1859, and what it fetched in gold in Edo from 1781 to 1842.

Edo-period Japan ran on three monies: gold counted in ryo, silver weighed in monme, and copper coins counted in mon, a thousand to the kanmon. The law fixed a ryo at 60 monme of silver or 4,000 mon from 1700, about 67 mon to the monme. On the Osaka market, a monme of silver fetched about 105 to 110 mon from 1787 to the early 1850s, and about 95 in the later 1850s; by this record's arithmetic from the gold and copper rates together, a ryo came to about 5,900 to 7,100 mon. In Edo, where copper was quoted in mon to the ryo, a ryo fetched between about 4,700 and 6,650 mon from 1781 to 1800 and between about 5,850 and 7,000 from 1801 to 1842.

The rate the law set

  • A ryo was fixed at 60 monme of silver and 4,000 mon from 1700.
    In 1700 the shogunate revised its official rate to 1 ryo of gold = 60 monme of silver = 4 kanmon of copper coin, and forbade money-changers to trade copper at 3,900 mon or fewer to the ryo. That is about 67 mon to the monme, this record's arithmetic.
  • In 1842 it was changed to 6,500 mon to the ryo.
    When the Tenpo coins flooded the market and copper's price collapsed, the shogunate changed its rate to 1 ryo = 6 kanmon 500 mon and pressed the money-changers to hold to it. These are rates of law, not of the market, which, the same article says, the official rate had to follow.
  • Edo and Osaka quoted copper differently.
    Edo quoted it in mon to the ryo of gold, Osaka in monme of silver to the kanmon.

The Osaka market, 1787-1859

  • The Mitsui house's Osaka exchange office wrote down the day's rates.
    Its business diary records, day by day from 1787, the closing rate on the Osaka floor for 1 kanmon of copper coin in monme of silver and for 1 ryo of gold in monme of silver, each as a low and a high. The database has no rates for some years: 1788, 1790-1791, 1795-1796, 1802-1803, 1807, 1815, 1818-1819, 1821, 1826, 1829, 1831, 1833-1835 and 1839.

  • How the means were taken.
    A day's figure is the middle of its low and high, or the one figure where only one is given; a decade's mean is the mean of all its days, each weighted alike; mon a monme is 1,000 divided by the mean monme a kanmon, and mon a ryo is the mean monme a ryo times 1,000 divided by the mean monme a kanmon. Each row rests on 705 to 2,447 trading days.
  • From 1843 to 1850 the rate was a floor, not a market.
    On the twelfth day of the fourth month of 1843 an order forbade buying and selling copper at under 10 monme a kanmon, since its cheapness in the Kamigata bore on the prices of goods; 10 monme or more was allowed. From the next day to the second month of 1850 the diary's rate stands at 10 monme or a hair above, so those days are left out of the means above.
  • Why does the table stop at 1859?
    From the first day of the sixth month of 1859 the Osaka floor quoted gold in the smaller gold and silver coins in circulation rather than in the koban and the ichibu gold piece, so the diary's later gold rates are not the same measure; gold after that day is left out, and the table ends with 1859.

Before 1787, and Edo

  • Copper was dearer before the 1780s.
    On one day in Osaka in 1766, a ryo was 63.82 monme of silver and a kanmon 15.19 monme, a ryo 4,201 mon: about 66 mon to the monme, near the official rate. From the Meiwa era (1764-1772), as the iron one-mon and brass four-mon coins were issued in quantity, copper's price fell until a ryo was about 6 kanmon. No Osaka figure by year was found for the early 1780s; the diary's first year, 1787, averaged 9.26 monme a kanmon, about 108 mon to the monme. Edo's figures from 1781 are below.
  • What did copper fetch in Edo?
    A historian's table of 1966 gives, for each year from 1751 to the fifth month of 1842, two market rates of copper coin in mon to the ryo of gold, nearly all with their months from 1781, taken from a 1933 table of the gold, silver and copper rates of the three cities "centered on Edo"; where that table gave a range he printed its middle. That these are Edo's rates is this record's reading: the table is centered on Edo and quotes copper in Edo's form, mon to the ryo, but the paper does not say so in those words. Some years as printed: 1781, 6,292 in the first month and 6,510 in the eleventh; 1787, 4,697 in the sixth month and 6,055 in the fourth; 1791, 5,030 in the third and 6,002 in the first; 1813, 6,210 in the tenth and 7,010 in the ninth; 1842, 6,665 in the first and 6,995 in the fifth.

  • What happened after 1842?
    The table stops at the fifth month of 1842; the 1933 table it copies notes there the town order of the eighth month fixing copper at 6,500 mon to the ryo, and is blank from then to 1852; the historian supposes no market rate was quoted while the official rate held. No printed Edo market rate from 1842 to the end of the 1850s was found. The nearest printed figures for those years are Osaka's series above, and Kyoto's rates in the first month of 1867: a ryo 107.1 monme of silver, a kanmon 12.66 monme, a ryo 8,460 mon.

The rate in two years the record's wages use

  • 1802-1804, the years of the Kyoto laborer's spring wage.
    The diary has no rates for 1802 or 1803. In 1804 copper averaged 9.22 monme a kanmon in Osaka, about 108 mon to the monme and 6,930 mon to the ryo. In Edo the printed rates were 6,520 to 6,692 mon to the ryo in 1802, 6,660 to 6,760 in 1803 and 6,675 to 6,760 in 1804. At Osaka's rate the Kyoto laborer's 0.92 monme of 1802-1804 (What did an unskilled laborer earn?) is about 100 mon, this record's arithmetic.
  • 1842, the year of the Nagata wage list.
    In 1842 copper averaged 9.53 monme a kanmon in Osaka, about 105 mon to the monme and 6,710 mon to the ryo; Edo's printed rates of 1842, up to the fifth month, were 6,665 and 6,995 mon to the ryo. At Osaka's rate the farm laborer's 132 mon a day in the village of Nagata (What did sake cost by the shop and by the cup?) is about 1.26 monme of silver, this record's arithmetic, an Osaka rate set beside a wage paid elsewhere.
