What did an unskilled laborer earn?

Rokugan's prices are counted in koku and its smaller coins, and the setting's money rules judge a price by how many days of a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give what the historical record holds for an unskilled laborer's pay in Japan, with Qing China beside it.

In Kyoto in the mid-1700s, in the Edo period, an unskilled day laborer was paid about 0.9 monme of silver a day, enough to buy about 2 kg (~4.4 lb) of rice. By about 1800 a farm worker in a village about 40 km (~25 miles) from Kyoto was paid as much. A year of 250 working days, often used to turn a day's pay into a year's, is a convention of modern comparisons, not a count from the time.

  • How much was a Kyoto laborer paid a day?
    About 2.82 g of silver, on average over 1745-1754, from the account books of the Mitsui merchant house. At the paper's 3.11 g of silver to the monme, that is about 0.91 monme.
  • How much rice did a day's pay buy?
    About 2 kg (~4.4 lb). The paper finds that a day's work bought about 2 kg of rice on average through the Edo period, and over 3 kg in the 1880s and 1890s. In 1745-1754 polished rice cost 1.49 g of silver a kilogram in Kyoto, so 2.82 g bought about 1.9 kg (~4.2 lb).
  • Was a laborer in Edo paid the same?
    About the same from 1818, when the Mitsui house's Edo wages resume after a gap of a century: their levels are broadly equal to Kyoto's, though they moved differently over time.

  • Was Edo's wage a market rate?
    The books do not say. Edo's barely moved: all 82 of its entries from 1818 to 1859 lie between 0.87 and 0.97, while Kyoto's moved between 0.83 and 1.35 over the same years.
  • And between 1762 and 1791, or after 1871?
    The Kyoto books have no entries from 1763 to 1790, and both series stop in 1871; no other laborer's day wage was found for the 1780s or for 1872-1879.
  • And in Osaka?
    For 1620-1628 the day wages paid at the rebuilding of Osaka Castle are given in What did a torii, stonework and earthwork cost? For 1690-1739 the table above gives the Kyoto wage of 1713 and the Edo wages of 1710-1716; for the 1780s none was found, as above.
  • Was a laborer paid more in the city than in the countryside?
    At first, yes. Early in the 1700s a day laborer in Kyoto was paid substantially more than one in Kami-Kawarabayashi, a village a little over 40 km (~25 miles) from the city; how much more is not given in the passage read.
  • By about 1800, city and village paid the same.
    In the 1750s and 1760s the two rates came level, and thereafter the city's unskilled rate kept level with farm work. In 1802-1804 the village paid farm workers 1.0 monme a day for spring tasks, and Kyoto's casual laborers were paid 0.92 monme on average in the same season.
  • A skilled worker was paid several times as much.
    In 1802-1804 the village's carpenters were paid 2.6 times a farm laborer's spring rate, and Osaka's carpenters 4.7 times what Kyoto's unskilled laborers were paid.
  • How many days a year did a laborer work?
    No count from the time was found. The studies that turn a day's pay into a year's assume 250 working days, as roughly full-time work allowing for holidays, illness and slack periods, a convention taken from earlier comparisons of Europe's wages.
  • What did a year's pay come to in koku of rice?
    About 3.2 to 3.3 koku, if the convention is accepted. At the 1745-1754 Kyoto wage and rice price, 250 days' pay bought about 473 kg (~1,043 lb) of polished rice; the paper's own conversions put a koku of rice at 150 kg, which makes this 3.15 koku, and a shō at 1.425 kg, which makes it 3.32. The two conversions disagree with each other, since 1.425 kg a shō is 142.5 kg a koku. At an even 2 kg a day the year is 500 kg, 3.33 koku at 150 kg.

Qing China's wages, given here in taels of silver, show the same kind of laborer paid more in the great cities than in the countryside.

  • What was a laborer paid in a Qing city?
    In Canton, the Dutch East India Company's records of the eighteenth century show workers paid mostly between 0.08 and 0.1 taels a day, with no food on top. The study's baseline, its statistical estimate of a construction laborer's wage in the Yangzi delta in 1700, is about 0.0456 taels, about half the wages of Suzhou and Canton.
  • Why did the great cities pay more?
    The study's authors suggest, by analogy with Europe's great cities, that living cost more there, and that the cities had to draw workers in from the countryside.
  • What was a farm laborer paid?
    Farm day laborers in the study's sample averaged 0.045 taels a day, likely more than a year-round rate, since day laborers were hired more often at planting and harvest. Another historian reports an official standard of 0.04 taels a day, a little low against some farms in Zhejiang, in a passage that suggests wages of the 1600s were not far from later ones. A farm hand hired by the year in the Yangzi delta in the mid-1700s earned, by one historian's reckoning, 2 to 5 taels in cash and about 5 shi of rice worth 8.4 taels, 10.4 to 13.4 taels in all, or 0.035 to 0.045 taels a day over 360 days.
  • What was a laborer paid near Beijing in the 1800s?
    The one series found comes from the account books of a single fuel store in rural Beijing, 1807 to 1902: the year's average day wage, which takes in the winter slack and is the lowest of the three averages its historian gave. In copper cash it was 81 a day in 1807, 95 in 1820, 100 in 1842, 94 in 1850 and 110 in 1856. After a debasement about 1860 the store counted its cash another way, so the later figures are in another count: 255 in 1860, 287 in 1870, 410 in 1880, 393 in 1890 and 470 in 1902. The study's authors convert it to 0.041 taels of silver a day in 1807, 0.035 in 1842, 0.011 in 1856, 0.026 in 1870, 0.024 in 1880 and 0.039 in 1902, but say the level seems extraordinarily low, though the workers were also given food, and use only its trend. No craftsman's wage of rural Beijing in those years was found; the nearest are the city's guild wages of about 1868 to 1894, as its guilds recalled them in 1918-1919 (What did a play or a show cost to see?).
  • How much rice did a Qing wage buy?
    About 1.9 kg (~4.2 lb) a day at the baseline. The study puts a kilogram of rice in Canton in the mid-1700s at roughly 0.024 taels, so the baseline of 0.0456 taels, a Yangzi delta wage of 1700 priced here at Canton's rice of the mid-1700s, bought about 1.9 kg, and Canton's 0.08 to 0.1 taels about 3.3 to 4.2 kg (~7.3-9.2 lb).
