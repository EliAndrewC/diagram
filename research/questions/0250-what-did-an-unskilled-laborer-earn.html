What did an unskilled laborer earn?

Rokugan's prices are counted in koku and its smaller coins, and the setting's money rules judge a price by how many days of a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give what the historical record holds for an unskilled laborer's pay in Japan, with Qing China beside it.

In Kyoto in the mid-1700s, in the Edo period, an unskilled day laborer was paid about 0.9 monme of silver a day, enough to buy about 2 kg (~4.4 lb) of rice. By about 1800 a farm worker in a village near Kyoto was paid as much. A year of 250 working days, often used to turn a day's pay into a year's, is a convention of modern comparisons, not a count from the time.

  • How much was a Kyoto laborer paid a day?
    About 2.82 g of silver, on average over 1745-1754, from the account books of the Mitsui merchant house. At the paper's 3.11 g of silver to the monme, that is about 0.91 monme.
  • How much rice did a day's pay buy?
    About 2 kg (~4.4 lb). The paper finds that a day's work bought about 2 kg of rice on average through the Edo period, and over 3 kg in the 1880s and 1890s. In 1745-1754 polished rice cost 1.49 g of silver a kilogram in Kyoto, so 2.82 g bought about 1.9 kg (~4.2 lb).
  • Was a laborer in Edo paid the same?
    About the same from 1818, when the Mitsui records of Edo wages begin: their levels are broadly equal to Kyoto's, though they moved differently over time.
  • Was a laborer paid more in the city than in the countryside?
    At first, yes. Early in the 1700s a day laborer in Kyoto was paid substantially more than one in Kami-Kawarabayashi, a village a little over 40 km (~25 miles) from the city; how much more is not given in the passage read.
  • By about 1800, city and village paid the same.
    In the 1750s and 1760s the two rates came level, and thereafter the city's unskilled rate kept level with farm work. In 1802-1804 the village paid farm workers 1.0 monme a day for spring tasks, and Kyoto's casual laborers were paid 0.92 monme on average in the same season.
  • A skilled worker was paid several times as much.
    In 1802-1804 the village's carpenters were paid 2.6 times a farm laborer's spring rate, and Osaka's carpenters 4.7 times what Kyoto's unskilled laborers were paid.
  • How many days a year did a laborer work?
    No count from the time was found. The studies that turn a day's pay into a year's assume 250 working days, as roughly full-time work allowing for holidays, illness and slack periods, a convention taken from earlier comparisons of Europe's wages.
  • What did a year's pay come to in koku of rice?
    About 3.2 to 3.3 koku, if the convention is accepted. At the 1745-1754 Kyoto wage and rice price, 250 days' pay bought about 473 kg (~1,043 lb) of polished rice; the paper's own conversions put a koku of rice at 150 kg, which makes this 3.15 koku, and a shō at 1.425 kg, which makes it 3.32. The two conversions disagree with each other, since 1.425 kg a shō is 142.5 kg a koku. At an even 2 kg a day the year is 500 kg, 3.33 koku at 150 kg.

Qing China's wage records, kept in taels of silver, show the same kind of laborer paid more in the great cities than in the countryside.

  • What was a laborer paid in a Qing city?
    In Canton, the Dutch East India Company's records of the eighteenth century show workers paid between 0.08 and 0.1 taels a day, with no food on top. The study's baseline, a construction laborer in the Yangzi delta in 1700, was paid about 0.0456 taels, about half the wages of Suzhou and Canton.
  • Why did the great cities pay more?
    The study's authors suggest that living cost more there, and that the cities had to draw workers in from the countryside.
  • What was a farm laborer paid?
    Farm day laborers in the study's sample averaged 0.045 taels a day; an official standard of 0.04 taels a day is reported as a little low against some farms in Zhejiang. A farm hand hired by the year in the Yangzi delta in the mid-1700s earned, by one historian's reckoning, 2 to 5 taels in cash and about 5 shi of rice worth 8.4 taels, 10.4 to 13.4 taels in all, or 0.035 to 0.045 taels a day over 360 days.
  • How much rice did a Qing wage buy?
    About 1.9 kg (~4.2 lb) a day at the baseline. The study puts a kilogram of rice in Canton in the mid-1700s at roughly 0.024 taels, so the baseline of 0.0456 taels bought about 1.9 kg, and Canton's 0.08 to 0.1 taels about 3.3 to 4.2 kg (~7.3-9.2 lb).
