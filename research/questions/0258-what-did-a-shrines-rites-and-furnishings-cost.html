What did a shrine's rites and furnishings cost?

Rokugan's prices are counted in koku and its smaller coins, and the setting's money rules judge a price by how many days of a craftsman's or a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give what the historical record holds for the fees of a shrine's dedication and the price of its small furnishings in Edo-period Japan and, where Japan is silent, in Qing China, each figure set beside the nearest day wages the record gives.

The one fee of the Edo period found is what the priestly houses and the temple of the Fushimi Inari shrine in Kyoto charged for installing a branch of their god in another shrine: a graded fee, of which most buyers chose the cheap grade, 300 hiki before 1864 and 3 bu of gold after. No fee was found for a local monk's or priest's dedication, for a rite purifying the ground in the Edo period, or for depositing remains at a temple, and no price for a shrine's bell and rope, its name plaque, its offering table or its smaller furnishings. What was found for carving is a day rate: a family of carvers northwest of Kyoto was paid about 3 monme of silver a day with 6 bu for meals in the 1810s, more than the carpenters beside them.

Installing an Inari shrine

  • What were the grades before 1864?
    Six, in a schedule of offerings kept by one of Fushimi's priestly houses, undated, with those of related houses dated, two of them tentatively, to before 1800 and one to before 1823: a great rite of one large gold coin, a main rite of five silver pieces, a middle rite of three, a small rite of 500 hiki, an abbreviated rite of 300 hiki, and 200 hiki for one outside the rites.
  • And from 1864?
    Seven, the same in all three schedules found, which the author takes to mean that all of Fushimi's issuers changed to it that year: 20 ryo, 7 ryo 2 bu, 3 ryo, 2 ryo, 1 ryo 1 bu, 3 bu, and 2 bu for the very abbreviated rite. The two lists' lowest three grades line up, the small rite's 500 hiki with its 1 ryo 1 bu, the abbreviated rite's 300 hiki with its 3 bu, and the 200 hiki outside the rites with the very abbreviated rite's 2 bu, which makes 100 hiki a bu, a quarter of a ryo; that equivalence is this project's reading of the lists, not the paper's.
  • Which grade did buyers choose?
    The cheap one. Of 46 certificates that show their grade, 28 were the abbreviated rite, and the rest spread over the others: one very great, two great, four main, three middle, six small and two very abbreviated.
  • Where did such a shrine stand?
    One requester's certificate, of 1821, places its shrine in the back garden of a house plot held in common by a Kyoto street ward.
  • What was the cheap grade in days of pay, about 1800?
    About 50 days of a Kyoto laborer's pay, or about 10 days of a city carpenter's. In 1802-1804 a Kyoto casual laborer earned about 0.92 monme a day in spring, and Osaka's carpenters 4.7 times what Kyoto's unskilled laborers did; at 60 monme to the ryo, a rate this project borrows from a price table of the 1850s, 300 hiki was 45 monme. The small rite's 500 hiki was about 80 laborer-days.
  • And in 1864?
    No wage of Kyoto in 1864 was found. The nearest is the wage a price table gives a carpenter, 0.125 ryo a day, meals included, from 1853 to 1860, four to eleven years earlier; at that rate the 3-bu grade was 6 carpenter-days and the 20-ryo grade 160. The nearest unskilled wage is Kyoto's of 1802-1804, sixty years earlier.
  • Was this what a local priest was paid?
    No. It is the fee for a branch of Fushimi's god, paid to Fushimi; no fee was found for a local monk's or priest's dedication of a shrine.

Other rites and the furnishings

  • What did a rite to purify the ground cost?
    No fee of the Edo period was found. The one figure found is older: an account of building work at Mount Koya in 1544, before the Edo period, as a historical dictionary quotes it, sets down the ground rite for building an offerings hall at 2 kan 500 mon, 2,500 mon. No wage of the 1540s was found; the nearest in this record are Osaka's of 1620 and 1628, in silver, about eighty years later (What did a torii, stonework and earthwork cost?).
  • Was it held with four green bamboo and a rope?
    Not that the record shows for the period. An encyclopedia gives four green bamboo at the corners with a sacred rope strung between them as the rite's present usual form, and judges from Edo carpenters' manuals that the rite took an important place among building rites only from the later Edo period, when its present customs were settled.
  • What did a temple take for keeping a portion of the remains?
    No fee was found.
  • What did a shrine's bell and its rope cost?
    No price was found.
  • What did a painted name plaque cost?
    No price was found, nor the days a carver took over one. The nearest is a carver's day rate (below).
  • What did an offering table cost?
    No price was found, for a new table, an old one refitted, or a flat stone set as one.
  • And a bamboo tube for offerings, a paper wand, a wooden or stone-set offering box, a low fence or a bamboo spout?
    No price was found.
  • And a lattice door, a cover-shed's roof or a stone bearing the shrine's name?
    No price of one alone was found. The nearest are a worship hall's twelve doors and screens of 1841 (the halls page), a sanctuary re-roofed within a whole repair in 1749 (the roof page), a sanctuary repaired with its covering house in 1764-1770 (the sanctuary page), and a shrine's other stonework (What did a shrine's stonework cost?).

Carving, boards and guardians

  • What was a carver paid?
    About 3 monme of silver a day, with 6 bu more for his meals, 3.6 monme in all, in the provinces northwest of Kyoto in the 1810s. That was the standard fee of the Nakai, a family of carvers of shrine and temple ornament, by their historian's reading of their jobs: 66 days for 237.6 monme, meals included, at the Enkoji temple in 1818, and 147 monme for 49 days with 29.4 monme of meal money on a job with the carpenters of Hanakura village. A small sanctuary's carving at Kuto in Tajima, made in the winter of 1816 and the spring after, took 42 days for 151.2 monme, meals included.
  • And beside a carpenter?
    More. Among the few cases the paper found, a carpenter was paid 2 monme a day at a house of 1819-1822, 2.5 at the Kaibara Hachiman shrine's pagoda in 1815, and 1.5 with a sho of polished rice for his meals at the Kamo shrine's worship hall in 1808, and the paper concludes that the specialist carvers were paid more than carpenters. No laborer's wage of the region was found. The nearest is a Kyoto casual laborer's of 1802-1804, about 0.92 monme a day, some fifteen years earlier, at which the carver's 3.6 monme was about 4 days of a laborer's pay.
  • What did carved wooden guardians cost?
    Only the carver's rate: at the Hachiman shrine of Kamibayashi-Nishiya village, done in the summer of 1815, the Nakai carved komainu, the guardian dogs, at 3 monme a man-day, as they did its "sparrow boards" (suzume-ita, a carved part the paper does not describe), and its other carvings at 3.3. The paper gives no count of days and does not say what the komainu were made of; that they were wood is this project's guess, from the carvers' trade.
  • What did a Kyoto carver charge by the piece?
    32 to 225 monme of silver a piece, in a Kyoto carver's written order for the carvings of the Amida hall of the Honganji temple: 57, 32, 40 and 137 monme for carved pieces, 160 for an elephant-nose bracket end and 225 for one with a baku's nose. The order is dated the twelfth month of a dog year, the year not given. None of them is a plaque.
  • What did a board telling the shrine's origin cost?
    No price was found, for the board or for painting it. The nearest is a village's notice board of 1830, whose estimate prints 50 carpenter-days though its sums fit 20 (the sanctuary page). An upper bound comes from Edo: the standing signboards of its shops, some of them finely carved, which stood on the road outside the ditch and so needed the authorities' leave and an inspector, cost with their posts and those fees 70 or 80 ryo of gold at the least and 140 or 150 at the most.
  • What was a signboard in days of pay?
    The same record of Edo's customs gives its carpenter about 5 or 5.5 monme of silver a day and its tobi laborer 300 mon (What did a skilled craftsman earn against a laborer?). At 60 monme to the ryo, a rate this project borrows, and Osaka's 105 to 110 mon to the monme of those years (What was copper coin worth in silver?), a small signboard was about 760 to 960 carpenter-days or 1,470 to 1,760 laborer-days, and a large one about 1,530 to 1,800 or 2,940 to 3,300.

A Qing hall's furnishings

  • What did a Qing temple hall's furnishings cost?
    The accounts of the rebuilding of the Confucian hall at Midu, in Yunnan, cut on its stele in 1902, give fitting out the four sacred tablets, the niche and the window lattices, labor and materials, at 280,000 copper cash; oiling and painting, labor and materials, 105,000; gold and silver leaf for the tablets 6,400; the plinth stones 27,000; and the incense burner, the stele's core stone and its carving together 8,650. The stele gives no wage rate or count of days.
  • What was that in silver?
    The stele counts 48 taels of silver as 62,400 cash, 1,300 cash to the tael, at which the tablets, niche and lattices were about 215 taels and the painting about 81.
  • And beside a wage?
    No wage of Midu about 1902 was found. The nearest laborer's wage is of rural Beijing in the same year, 470 cash a day by one fuel store's books, in a count its historians say changed after 1860 and at a level they call extraordinarily low (What did an unskilled laborer earn?); the nearest of the same province are Yunnan's official building wages of 1769, 133 years earlier, at Nanning 0.06 tael a day for a craftsman and 0.04 for a laborer (What did carrying stone cost?). This project sets the stele's figures against neither.
