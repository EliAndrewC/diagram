What did a shrine's rites and furnishings cost?

Rokugan's prices are counted in koku and its smaller coins, and the setting's money rules judge a price by how many days of a craftsman's or a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give what the historical record holds for the fees of a shrine's dedication and the price of its small furnishings in Edo-period Japan, each set beside the day wages of its place and time.

The one fee found is what the priestly houses of the Fushimi Inari shrine in Kyoto charged for installing a branch of their god in another shrine, often a small one: a graded fee, of which most buyers chose the cheap grade, 300 hiki before 1864 and 3 bu of gold after. No fee was found for a local monk's or priest's dedication, for a rite purifying the ground, or for depositing remains at a temple, and no price for a shrine's bell and rope, its name plaque or its offering table.

Installing an Inari shrine

  • What were the grades before 1864?
    Six, in a schedule of offerings kept by one of Fushimi's priestly houses, undated, with those of related houses dated, two of them tentatively, to before 1800 and one to before 1823: a great rite of one large gold coin, a main rite of five silver pieces, a middle rite of three, a small rite of 500 hiki, an abbreviated rite of 300 hiki, and 200 hiki for one outside the rites.
  • And from 1864?
    Seven, the same for all of Fushimi's issuers, its two priestly lineages and its temple: 20 ryo, 7 ryo 2 bu, 3 ryo, 2 ryo, 1 ryo 1 bu, 3 bu, and 2 bu for the very abbreviated rite. The two lists match grade for grade, 500 hiki to 1 ryo 1 bu, 300 to 3 bu and 200 to 2 bu, which makes 100 hiki a bu, a quarter of a ryo; that equivalence is this project's reading of the lists, not the paper's.
  • Which grade did people choose?
    The cheap one. Of 46 certificates that show their grade, 28 were the abbreviated rite, and the rest spread over the others: one very great, two great, four main, three middle, six small and two very abbreviated.
  • Where did such a shrine stand?
    Some were small: a receipt of 1821 places one in the back garden of a house plot held in common by a Kyoto street ward.
  • What was the cheap grade in days of pay, about 1800?
    About 50 days of a Kyoto laborer's pay, or about 10 days of a city carpenter's. In 1802-1804 a Kyoto casual laborer earned about 0.92 monme a day, and Osaka's carpenters 4.7 times what Kyoto's unskilled laborers did; at 60 monme to the ryo, 300 hiki was 45 monme. The small rite's 500 hiki was about 80 laborer-days.
  • And in 1864?
    No wage of Kyoto in 1864 was found. The nearest is an Edo carpenter's, 0.125 ryo a day, meals included, from 1853 to 1860, four to eleven years earlier; at that rate the 3-bu grade was 6 carpenter-days and the 20-ryo grade 160. The nearest unskilled wage is Kyoto's of 1802-1804, sixty years earlier.
  • Was this what a local priest was paid?
    No. It is the fee for a branch of Fushimi's god, paid to Fushimi; no fee was found for a local monk's or priest's dedication of a shrine.
