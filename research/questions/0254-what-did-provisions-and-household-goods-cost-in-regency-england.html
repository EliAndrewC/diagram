What did provisions and household goods cost in Regency England?

Rokugan prices its goods by its own customs, and nothing here sets one of them. This is a comparative question: what one English household manual of 1825 says provisions and household goods cost, a yardstick for players who know the shillings of Austen and Dickens.

The Complete Servant, a London household manual of 1825 by two former servants, tells a housekeeper to know the prices of what she buys and to lay in stores when they are cheap, but it prices almost nothing itself. The few prices it gives are of bread by an earlier law, a London herb mixture drunk in place of tea, grain in its tables for converting the old measures, hired horses by the mile, gold and silver by the ounce, and the book itself. A shilling was twelve pence, and a pound twenty shillings.

The prices the book gives

  • What did bread cost?
    The book records an earlier law by which a quartern loaf cost as many pence as the best wheat cost shillings, with a penny more for baking; and when the best wheaten bread sold at 8d., the standard loaf was to sell at 7d. and the household loaf at 6d. A quartern loaf weighed 4 pounds 5 ounces and 8 of the units the book's table heads "dwts."; London bakers, by a later law, sold bread by the pound only. It does not say what bread cost in 1825.
  • Grain by the bushel, in the book's conversions.
    To show what the new imperial bushel did to a price, the book takes oats worth 2s. 8d. a bushel by the old Winchester measure, which became 2s. 9d. by the new; barley or rye at 5s. 4d., which became 5s. 6d.; and wheat or malt at 8s., which became 8s. 3d. It does not say these were the prices of the day.
  • A herb mixture in place of tea, 2 shillings a pound in London.
    The book gives a "medicinal tea" of dried English herbs, to be drunk in place of tea or coffee, and says that in London, where herbs were dearer than in the country, the mixture could be had at 2s. a pound.
  • Hiring a post-chaise and pair cost 1 to 2 shillings a mile.
    Its table of the cost of posting runs from a shilling to two shillings a mile for a post-chaise and a pair of horses; two pairs were charged double and a single horse half.
  • Gold was £4 an ounce and silver 5 shillings.
    An ounce of standard gold, 22 carats fine, was worth £4, and an ounce of silver 5s.
  • The book itself cost 7s. 6d.
    Its title page gives its price as seven shillings and sixpence.

What the book does not price

  • Meat.
    The book tells the cook how to choose beef, veal, mutton, pork and poultry and when each is cheapest, but gives no price for any. Its one figure, beef at 5½d. a pound, is an example of how to read its reckoning tables.
  • Butter.
    No price is given. The dairy maid's penny for each pound of butter is a perquisite, in What did a Regency English household pay its servants?
  • Tea.
    No price is given for tea itself, only for the herb mixture above.
  • Sugar.
    No price is given. Its one figure, sugar at 10d. a pound, is an example of how to use its multiplication table.
  • Beer and ale.
    No price is given. Servants were allowed ale and table beer in kind, and the book's one figure for drink, a gallon of wine "worth 5s." by the old measure, is an example of converting to the new.
  • Candles and fuel.
    No price is given. The book advises laying in candles and soap when their price is likely to rise, and gives coal's measure, 36 bushels or 12 sacks to the chaldron, but not its price.
  • Cloth.
    No price is given for cloth or clothing.
  • Keeping a horse.
    No cost of keeping a horse is given. The book's division of a large income puts horses, carriages and liveries in one share with the servants. That share is in How did a Regency English household divide its income?
