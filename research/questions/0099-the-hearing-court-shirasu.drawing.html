How our maps draw the hearing court (shirasu)

Our maps draw the hearing court of a county magistrate, not one of the Edo town magistrates' courts most accounts describe, as a roofed court against the front of the office hall, below the raised dais where the magistrate sits. The parties kneel on straw mats on its floor, and the clerks sit in the hall above it, never on the court. The office hall's size is at How our maps draw the office hall and its clerks (goyakusho).

  • Our maps draw the hearing court with a roof over it, not as an open yard.
    A magistracy's court had a roof built over it or was a floor inside the building. So the court is drawn with a building's solid outline, and posts along its open side.
  • The posts along the court's open side stand two ken (~12 ft) apart.
    Each post is drawn 1 ft square, to scale. The posts' size and spacing are this project's own figures, a GUESS.
  • What covers the court's floor is a knob: white gravel, or cobbles from the local riverbed.
    Both are attested: white gravel at the magistracies, and river cobbles at the intendant's office at Takayama, whose buildings survive, where white sand was scarce. Each plan takes one.
  • The parties kneel on straw mats on the court's floor.
    At Edo the plaintiffs and defendants sat on straw mats spread on the gravel, and so did the peasants, townsmen and lesser ronin. That is accurate. So every place where a party kneels is drawn with its mat: a bare spot with no mat has no counterpart in the record.
  • How are the mats arranged?
    The accused kneels at the center, a third of the way in from the dais, with the plaintiff behind to one side and the village officials behind to the other, as at Edo, where the town officials, headmen and landlords sat behind the accused. That arrangement is attested only at Edo, so carrying it to a county court is a GUESS.
  • Our maps draw the accused's mat about 6 by 3 ft, and the village officials' mat 12 ft long, for several to kneel on.
    The sizes and the spacing are this project's own figures, a GUESS.
  • The magistrate's dais is the front of the office hall, not a pavilion of its own.
    At Takayama the examination room was one part of the office, which this project reads as one block, so the court lies against the hall where the magistrate sits, and the magistrate hears cases in the same building where the day's paperwork is done. The rooms our maps draw behind the dais are at How our maps draw the office hall and its clerks (goyakusho).
  • The dais is drawn as a single dark block, darker than the hall's floor, so it reads as the place where the magistrate sits.
    That is a map drawing convention. The real magistrate's level was a room: at Edo a tatami room divided into three, with a board veranda about 3 ft wide at its front, as set out at The hearing court (shirasu). Our maps draw neither its mats nor its veranda.
  • Our maps seat the clerks in the hall above the court, not on its floor.
    At Edo the clerk sat with the examining officer in the room between the magistrate's room and the court, and some magistracies set a further upper room apart for the examiners and the scribes. That placing is accurate.
  • Our maps seat the clerks to either side of the magistrate, level with the dais.
    They are not drawn in a room of their own in front of the magistrate's, as at Edo: a deliberate deviation, for simplicity. No page we read gives how large the clerks' place was, so the seats' size is a GUESS.
  • Our county magistracy draws no waiting shed for the parties, and leaves the ground before its hearing court open.
    A waiting shed for summoned parties, the koshikake, is attested, in the sources we read, only at the shogunate's courts in Edo, a city's courts, and the record is silent for an intendant's office (The hearing court (shirasu)), the office our county seat follows. This project does not carry the Edo shed to a county seat: every source we read ties it to the shogunate's courts in the capital, and drawing one would put a city court's building where the record of an intendant's office shows none. So the open ground before the court, as drawn, is a GUESS on that silence.
