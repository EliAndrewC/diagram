How big was a farmhouse's lot in a clustered village?

On our maps each farmhouse of a clustered hamlet stands on its own ground, its yard and outbuildings round it, so every map has to say how much ground a household held. The research below shows the few house lots the historical record measures, in Japan and in China, and what it leaves unmeasured.

A farmhouse's lot is the yashiki of a Japanese land register; in one village's registers it held the house and its outbuildings. Where the record measures such lots, a typical one ran about 90 to 180 tsubo (about 300-600 sq m, or 3,200-6,500 sq ft), and lots of 300 tsubo or more were few. No page we read measures the ground between neighboring lots in a clustered village.

  • In one Musashi village in 1698, the middle house lot was about 157 tsubo.
    The register of the shogunate's share of Hyakken village (now Miyashiro, Saitama), extracted holder by holder in 1698, lists each registered holder's plots with the holder's house lot among them, and totals the house lots at 1 chō 9 tan 11 bu, which is 5,711 tsubo. Counted lot by lot, its 34 house lots run from 42 tsubo to 480: half are under about 157 tsubo (~5,600 sq ft), the middle half run from about 85 to 230, and three are over 300. The count is this project's, from the register's own entries. The register does not say whether the village's houses stood together or scattered.
  • Another village's registers give smaller lots.
    The land-survey registers of Kikoba village give homestead lots smaller than Hyakken's (Threshing and drying yards at farmhouses).
  • In China in the 1920s, a farm's buildings and threshing floor took about 0.06 hectare.
    Buck's survey of farms in seven localities of four provinces, made in 1922-1925, averages 0.06 hectare a farm under buildings and threshing floor (~600 sq m, about 180 tsubo), about 2 per cent of the farm, and finds the farmsteads usually in villages or hamlets. It measures the buildings and the floor, not a walled lot, and it was made after the empire ended.
  • In a surveyed Manchu village, most house lots ran 300-500 square meters.
    A survey of one village in Liaoning, made between 2013 and 2017, measured the lots of its older quarter, laid out before the late 1960s: mostly 30 m or less wide and generally 50 m or less deep, close to one to two, commonly 300-500 sq m (about 90-150 tsubo), and only a few over 1,000 sq m (about 300 tsubo). The lots are measured as they stand today, not from an older record.
  • How far apart did the houses stand?
    The record gives a figure only for the two other forms of village (Clustered and scattered villages). On the Tonami plain, whose farmsteads stand scattered, each in its grove, the farmhouses stand about 100-200 m apart; in the planned row colony of Santome Shinden each house and its farmland took a strip 40 ken (about 73 m) wide along the road (Row villages). No page we read measures how far apart neighboring houses stood in a clustered village.
  • What the record does not hold.
    No page we read gives a farmhouse lot's size in China before the 1920s. Whether a government ever set the size of a commoner's house lot was not searched.
