How our maps draw washing places at the water's edge

Where a stream or ditch passes a settlement's houses, the map draws places to wash clothes and vegetables at the water's edge, and a town gets its water in one of three ways. Both are decided at random for each settlement.

  • A village's washing places are shared, or one at each house - a knob.
    The record shows two forms: a shared one on the channel or stream bank, like the shared kawado on the channels of the town of Gujo Hachiman, and one at each house where a channel runs past the houses, like the landing at nearly every door of the Jiangnan water towns. So the map chooses one form at random for each settlement where water passes its houses. The record gives no shares between the two, so the odds are arbitrary.
  • A shared washing place is a footing of steps down the bank, which we draw about 4 ft wide.
    The record names the footings built for washing but no page we read gives their form or size, so the steps and the width are a GUESS.
  • The map draws one shared washing place for every few households on a bank.
    No page we read counts them, so the number is a GUESS.
  • Where the map sets a washing place.
    A shared one stands on the bank of any stream, ditch or farm channel that runs within about 120 ft of the houses it serves, at the point nearest the middle of those households and on their side of the water; where a lane, a bridge or a building takes that spot it is moved along the bank, up to about 32 ft either way, and a group with no spot left keeps none. A house's own stands at the end of its farm's channel in the dooryard, so that form is drawn only where the farms take their water by channel. No page we read says how far a household walked to wash or where on the bank the footing stood, so the distance and the spot on the bank are a GUESS.
  • A washing place is drawn as a small pale footing with its steps marked across it.
    This is a map drawing convention: footings built for washing are known, but no page we read says what Japan's were built of or whether they were stepped.
  • A town's water supply is another knob: wells alone, channels down the streets, or pipes.
    A town with neither channels nor pipes draws only its wells. A castle town may have channels led through its streets, with washing places along them. Or it may pipe its water to shared and household wells, which the map draws as wells. The record gives no shares among the three, so the odds are arbitrary.

The rule the map follows: where a stream or ditch passes a settlement's houses, the map chooses at random either shared washing places - each a footing of steps down the bank, about 4 ft wide (a guess), one for every few households on that bank (a guess), at the bank point nearest the households it serves, on their side, moved along the bank where something else stands there - or, where a channel runs past the houses, one step at each house on it, where its channel ends. A town is given at random wells alone, or street channels led from its river with washing places along them. Piped water is drawn as wells.
