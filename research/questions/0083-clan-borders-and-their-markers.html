Clan borders and their markers

Under the Tokugawa, in the Edo period, the domains of Japan (the lands each lord, or daimyo, held and governed under the shogun) marked off their territory with agreed, linear borders, and the evidence is their boundary disputes, their boundary markers and their maps. A border could be a line two domains had argued over and then agreed.

One example is the border between the domains of Morioka (the Nanbu clan's) and Sendai (the Date clan's), settled in 1642 after half a century of dispute and marked with a line of more than a hundred earth mounds from the mountains to the Pacific, known in English as the Nanbu-Date border mounds. Separately, the shogunate had every province draw a map of its lands (kuniezu), and its Genroku revision of those maps drew the district boundaries on them.

  • How far had Japan's domains come toward fixed, linear borders in the Edo period?
    A study of the period's boundary disputes, boundary markers and map-making finds the domains well into building a territorial order of demarcated borders, each excluding the other, and frontiers that had been broad, fuzzy zones becoming clearly drawn lines. Its author adds that this was not true everywhere: some areas stayed much less territorial than others.
  • Morioka and Sendai agreed their border in 1642, early in the Edo period, after more than fifty years of dispute.
    The two domains agreed to build more than 100 mounds along 130 km (~426,509 ft) of the line, so that it could not be confused again. The line ran from Mount Komagatake in the Ou Mountains in the west to the Pacific in the east.
  • The Morioka-Sendai mounds grew smaller and closer together over time.
    They were large and far apart at first, then smaller and more densely spaced, as the border changed from a broad frontier to a line.
  • A further series of smaller mounds was built along the line in 1688, forty-six years after the agreement.
    The English Wikipedia article on the mounds says they were built to mark the boundary more closely.
  • Were earth-mound markers like Morioka and Sendai's common along Edo-period borders?
    The English Wikipedia article on the mounds says markers of the kind were common under the Tokugawa shogunate.
  • The Genroku revision of the provincial maps drew the district boundaries clearly.
    The revision's basic policy drew the district boundaries vividly and wrote each village's assessed yield inside its shape. The entry names boundaries between districts and says nothing of borders between domains.
  • Places in dispute were looked into before the Genroku maps were drawn.
    Where a place had been in dispute since the previous survey, or the old maps disagreed, the matter was put to the intendants and lords and examined before the map was made up.
