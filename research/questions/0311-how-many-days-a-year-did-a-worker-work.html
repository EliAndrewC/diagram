How many days a year did a worker work?

Rokugan's money rules turn a day's wage into a year's income, and the setting gives each county a market day about once a week but no calendar of working days. The findings below give what the historical record holds on how many days a year each kind of worker worked, in Japan, with Qing China beside it.

No count of the days a Japanese day laborer, porter or carpenter worked in a year was found. The one count is for farm servants hired by the year: their contracts gave most of them 2 to 4 rest days a month, and an economic historian reckons that in villages of western and central Japan and around Edo, from 1780 to 1840, they worked 310 to 330 days a year. A village also set rest days for everyone in it, for festivals and the ends of planting and harvest: about 20 a year around 1700, and in places 50 to 80 after 1800, by one historian's study as a later writer summarizes it. The 250-day year that modern wage comparisons use is their assumption, not a count from the time.

Servants

  • How many days did a farm servant hired by the year work?
    About 310 to 330, by the economic historian Yuzuru Kumon's estimate. Of the servants' contracts he gathered, 83 state the days of rest, most of them by the month, clustering at 2 to 4 days a month; 2 to 4 a month is 24 to 48 days a year by this project's arithmetic. From these and other contracts he finds farm workers in a village of Harima, in western Japan, in 1780-1820, in one of Kai, in central Japan, in 1800-1840, and around Edo in 1780-1840 working 310 to 330 days a year, eastern Japan perhaps 10 days fewer than western. His paper puts the peasants' working year at 325 days by 1700.
  • Did a farm servant also have the holidays?
    Kumon cannot tell. It is unclear, he writes, whether servants also rested on the national holidays, and his estimates leave them out; if they did, perhaps 3 days at New Year, 3 at Bon and 5 for the five seasonal festivals, 11 days more.
  • Some servants were hired for half of each month.
    Contracts that set the days of work, rather than the days of rest, often specified 15 days a month, with some variation.
  • When did a shop servant go home?
    On the 16th of the first and the seventh months, the days called yabuiri, when the servants of merchant houses in Edo were given leave to go home to their parents or their guarantor, or spent the day at leisure. On the 16th of the seventh month they could go home, or freely go to plays or to temples.
  • Were those a shop servant's only days off?
    Not by one encyclopedia's account, which says servants' days off were not limited to those two; no count of a shop servant's days off in a year was found. Hiring and the half-year terms of servants are in Servants in a samurai household.

A village's rest days (asobi-bi)

A Japanese village set its own days of rest, by its own rules, for the whole village at once. The figures below come from one historian's study of them, Furukawa Sadao's book of 1986 on village rest days, as two later writers summarize it, and the two disagree.

  • How many rest days did a village keep around 1700?
    About 20 a year: in the late 1600s and early 1700s, the breaks after planting and harvest, with New Year, the Little New Year, Bon, the five seasonal festivals and the shrine festivals, came to about 20 days.
  • How many in the Edo period, generally?
    From 20 to under 30, by a paper on one village's leisure that gives no period for the figure; the rest days, it says, began from the village's shrine festivals.
  • How many after 1800?
    Many more in places. From the 1800s villagers won ever more festival days from their village officials, and these reached 30 to 40-odd a year, in places 50 or 60 or more, and at most 80.
  • Who asked for them?
    Live-in servants on term contracts asked for higher wages, more rest days and lighter work, and young men's associations for festival days. From the late 1700s, as ruined farmers became hired servants, extra rest days grew at their demand.
  • A village rest day was not optional.
    Working on the sly on a rest day was not allowed, and village rules fined it.
  • Did a servant have the village's days on top of his own?
    No source read says. A contract's rest days were one servant's and the village's days were everyone's, so this project does not add the two.

Porters, carpenters and day laborers

  • Were post-station porters kept busy?
    No count was found of the days a station's porter or packhorse driver worked, or of porters hired by the job in towns and ports. What was found is about the villages drafted to back up a highway station (sukego): official travelers were most numerous in the busy farming season, so villages that could not spare their farmers sent money instead, and a man's day or two of walking to the station and back was unpaid.
  • How many men did a station draw?
    Three villages drafted for the Shinagawa station, on the Tokaido, together sent over 100 men a day in the Kyoho era (1716-1736). Porters' and horses' fares are in What did hiring a horse or a porter cost?
  • Did the weather stop a carpenter?
    Yes, though how often was not found. A study of Edo around the 1820s notes that the street peddler and the carpenter both worked in trades that depended to some degree on the weather, so they did not necessarily earn their set amount every day. No count of a carpenter's working days, and no figure for his work in winter, was found.
  • How many days did a day laborer find work?
    No count was found. Kumon notes that day laborers were not guaranteed jobs on any given day. The laborer's day wage is in What did an unskilled laborer earn?
  • How did a farm laborer's work run by season?
    No split of a farm laborer's days by season was found; Kumon's 310 to 330 days are a year's total for servants hired by the year.

The comparisons of Qing China's wages assume a working year rather than count one, and the Qing government's own accounts used a longer one.

  • The 250-day year is an assumption.
    Allen and his co-authors assume that a year's work was 250 days, roughly full time allowing for holidays, illness and slack periods; Bassino and Ma's comparison multiplies a day's wage by 250; and Liu notes that the method assumes the same 250-day year for every country.
  • Qing accounts reckoned a year at 360 days.
    Yearly payments in local gazetteers are mostly day rates multiplied by a notional 360 working days, a budgeting practice; in practice a year's labor service was split into three or four terms of 10 days to a season of 90.
  • How many days could a woman spin?
    About 200 a year at most, as two historians, Li Bozhong and Kenneth Pomeranz, each reckon it, by Allen and his co-authors' account: an estimate for the Yangzi delta, not a count.
  • Did the working year change?
    Li Bozhong suggests that the average number of working days may have risen considerably in the first half of the 1800s, as Liu reports him. No count of a Chinese worker's days in a year was read.
