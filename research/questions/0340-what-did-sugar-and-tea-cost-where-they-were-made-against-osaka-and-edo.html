What did sugar and tea cost where they were made, against Osaka and Edo?

In Rokugan the Sparrow's tea, the setting says, can always be sold to raise the money for the food they cannot grow. A tariff of typically about 15% of the declared sales value is charged at the gate of the city where goods are sold, and the setting's price ledger reckons a good sold far from where it was made at its price there plus freight, a 15% tariff for each border and a merchant's margin. The findings below give what the historical record of Edo-period Japan (1603 to 1868) holds for sugar and tea: what each cost where it was made and in Osaka or Edo, in the same year.

For sugar the record gives no pair of money prices, because Satsuma domain did not pay its Amami islanders in money: it took their black sugar for rice at a rate it set, a few tenths of a sho of rice for each kin of sugar. In 1830 an islander gave about 4.2 to 6.4 times as much sugar for a koku of rice as Osaka's market would have asked. For the white sugar of Sanuki only the costs between the island and Osaka were found, and for tea only one end at a time: what the shogunate paid Uji's tea masters, and what everyday tea sold for in Edo in 1842 and at one Edo shop in 1852. Raw silk and iron are in What did raw silk and iron cost where they were made, against the city that used them?, and how tea traveled in How did tea travel, and what did it cost at the end of the road? The sugar, spices, incense woods and medicinal roots that came from abroad through Nagasaki are in What did imported sugar, spices and medicines cost at Nagasaki, against Kyoto and Osaka?

Sugar: Satsuma's black sugar from the Amami islands

  • The islanders were paid in rice, at a rate the domain set.
    Satsuma's own collected laws (reimeikan-satsuma-horei-2) reckon what the domain owed the island of Kikaijima for a winter's sugar, in a year the page does not give: 2,175 koku of rice for 580,000 kin, at 3 go 7 shaku 5 sai (thousandths of a sho) of rice a kin (0.375 sho, about 0.68 liter for 600 g of sugar); 4 go 7 shaku 5 sai a kin for 135,000 kin bought over the quota; and 1 sho 8 go a kin for 5,000 kin of white sugar. A second reckoning for the same island, for the winter of an Ox year, pays 3 go a kin for 1,200,000 kin.
  • How did the rate change?
    The earliest found is of 1747, when the islands' land tax in rice began to be paid in black sugar: 3 go 6 shaku of rice to a kin, a rate for turning tax owed into sugar, not a price paid, by a note of Minowa Yu (minowa-oshima-kibocho-1728). After it, 3 go of rice a kin by an order of 1762, 4 go under the lord Shimazu Narinobu, and, in the account of a plan of 1839 within the reform of the minister Zusho Hirosato that began in 1827, 5.07 kin of sugar for a sho of rice, just under 2 go a kin, as Oyano Eiji (oyanome-2018-zusho-zaisei) gives them. Federico Marcon (marcon-2015-knowledge-of-nature), after a Japanese study by Matsui, has the domain buying from the second half of the 1700s at 0.35 sho of rice (0.63 liter) a kin within each village's quota and 0.72 liter, 0.4 sho, over it; he prints the kin as one of "sugarcane", which is read here as sugar, the domain's laws paying nearly the same rice for a kin of sugar, this project's reading and arithmetic.
  • How did the islands' terms stand against Osaka's?
    By two documents of 1830, as a Kagoshima prefectural booklet (kagoshima-kokuto-leaflet) reports them from a 1983 study, the islanders bought their goods at about 4 to 90 times the Osaka market price, and a koku of rice took about 4.2 to 6.4 times the sugar it would have at Osaka's rate. That is a ratio of what sugar bought in rice at the two ends, not of two money prices; it equals a ratio of sugar prices only if rice cost the same at both, which the booklet does not say. A wiki article in English (samuraiwiki-sugar), citing a book of 2009 that was not read, says of the same reform that the domain bought the islands' sugar at roughly a third of what it would be worth at the Osaka markets, with no figure at either end: a summary at second hand, and a smaller gap than the multiples above and below.
  • What is that in silver?
    One paper allows a reckoning. Beside Zusho's rate, Oyano gives average prices "at the time" of about 0.663 monme of silver for a sho of rice and 1.175 monme for a kin of black sugar, without saying where or in which year, and concludes that the islanders were sold rice at 6.2 times the mainland's price. By its own figures the multiple is about 9: the sugar an islander gave for a sho of rice was worth about 5.96 monme at that price, so a kin fetched the islander about 0.13 monme of rice, this project's arithmetic; the paper does not show how it reaches 6.2.
  • Where does that sugar price come from?
    Probably from Osaka's totals for the 1830s. Kito Hiroshi (kito-2008-kanmi-shakai) puts the sugar of Satsuma reaching Osaka in the Tenpo years (1830 to 1844) at 12 million kin, 7,200 tons, a year, and its sales in 1830 to 1839 at 2,350,000 ryo. If 12 million kin came in each of the ten years, that is about 0.0196 ryo a kin, and at 60 monme to the ryo exactly Oyano's 1.175 monme: so his average is read here as Osaka's sale price over that decade, this project's arithmetic and reading. Kito gives no price himself, and his yearly quantity is a round figure for the era: the yearly average in his table for 1830 to 1832, compiled from earlier studies, is 13.2 million kin, at which the average would be about a tenth lower.
  • And by weight?
    At a kin of 160 monme, 600 g, the kin of Kito's 12 million kin in 7,200 tons, and the 1.35 g or 0.975 g of silver in a monme of the two coinages of those years (How much silver was a monme, a ryo and a bu, coinage by coinage?), Oyano's average is about 1.9 to 2.6 g of silver for a kilogram of black sugar, and what the islander got about 0.2 to 0.3 g, this project's arithmetic. Reckoned through gold instead, Kito's totals come to about 2.0 to 2.7 g a kilogram in Osaka, the same within the play of the coinages.
  • What made up the gap?
    Freight, paid in sugar, was a small part: by a schedule of 1745, about 10 kin in every 100 from Amami Oshima, 11.5 from Kikaijima and 17.5 from Tokunoshima, the page not saying to which port. In Osaka one merchant house had the sale of a million of the seven million kin shipped each year, with a commission of 2 rin a kin, about 1.7% of Oyano's average price. The rest lay with the domain, which bought the sugar on the islands and sold it in Osaka, this project's reading. About 51% of the sugar gathered at Osaka in 1830 to 1832 was Satsuma's, and Satsuma's sugar was eaten mainly in Edo and the northern provinces.
  • How far was it?
    Amami Oshima is about 905 km (~562 mi) from Osaka in a straight line, this project's arithmetic, and the sugar went by sea; no source read gives the days or the route beyond the freight above.

Sugar: Ryukyu, Sanuki and Awa

  • What of Ryukyu's sugar?
    No price at either end was found. The black sugar Satsuma sold in Osaka included Ryukyu's, and in 1807, on the domain's finance office's inquiry into the Ryukyu freight, the freight on sugar delivered to the storehouse office within the Ryukyu-kan at Kagoshima, this project's reading, was given as 23 kin 5 go 7 shaku of sugar in every 100 kin, nearly a quarter.
  • By what hands did Ryukyu's sugar reach Osaka?
    The royal government sent its sugar to the office it kept at Kagoshima, the Ryukyu-kan, whose officials sold it to the Satsuma merchants admitted there; from there Satsuma domain took charge, and the sugar was sold at the domain's warehouse-residence in Osaka by tender among the sugar brokers licensed to bid, as Kurima Yasuo (kurima-ryukyu-sato-ryutsu) reads the older histories; a price he does give is of 1880, after the kingdom's end. For the kingdom's years he gives no price at any of the three places.
  • What did Sanuki's white sugar cost to bring to Osaka?
    Takamatsu domain, in Sanuki on Shikoku, about 140 km (~87 mi) from Osaka across the Inland Sea in a straight line, shipped its white sugar there in casks; about 1858 the freight on casked goods was raised from 2 monme to 2 monme 5 bu a cask, a domain record quoted by Kodama Yoichi (kodama-takamatsu-sato-kawase) says, and the domain took a levy of two in a hundred on sugar sold.
  • How was it sold there?
    As a rule a fifth of each cargo was landed at the domain's sugar office in Osaka and sold by tender to the brokers admitted to the domain's Osaka residence, and the other four-fifths went to the wholesalers who took Sanuki's sugar on consignment, to be sold with the office's winning bid as the standard.
  • Is there a price at both ends?
    No. Kodama values the sugar at a flat 2 monme 3 bu a kin in domain notes for all of 1836 to 1871, with a kin of 230 monme (about 860 g) and a cask of 100 kin on average: his own round figure, not a price of any year or place. Against it the freight of about 1858 would be about 1% of a cask's value, this project's arithmetic. No sale price in Osaka and no dated price in Sanuki was found in the paper, about half of whose pages were only searched on their page images for prices and charges, and nothing was found for the white sugar of Awa.

Tea: one end at a time

  • What was tea worth at Uji?
    The one figure found is what the shogunate paid its tea masters for the leaf of powdered tea: from 1642, 78 monme of silver a kin for the finest grade, 52 for the next and 26 for the third, a kin being 200 monme (750 g, ~1.7 lb), and the payment was not raised again before the shogunate fell, Anada Sayoko (anada-edo-uji-chashi) writes. It was a payment the buyer fixed, not a price made in a market, this project's reading.
  • What did tea sell for in Edo?
    In 1842, when the shogunate ordered prices cut, a ward headman's return listed everyday leaf tea in nineteen rows under eighteen names at 88 to 500 mon of copper for a kin of 200 monme before the cut and 84 to 456 mon after, as Oishi Shinzaburo (oishi-1966-tenpo13-bukka-kakiage) tabulates it. The return does not say where any of the teas was grown, nor whether the price is a shop's; Oishi says of the returns as a whole that they are at times retail prices. Six of its names, Hatsuzakura, Yaezakura, Osozakura, Wakamatsu, Hatsune and Yaegaki, at 180 to 448 mon before the cut, are names this project also reads under the Suruga heading of the handbill of 1852 below: a likeness of names, not a statement by the return of where its teas grew.
  • What did an Edo tea shop ask, by where the tea was grown?
    One handbill was found, of autumn 1852, from the tea merchant Yamamoto Kahei of Nihonbashi in Edo, as a tea shop's column reproduces it from a book on Japanese tea (shinchaen-ocha-nedan-1852). It prints a Kyoto address beside the Edo one and lists teas by name with their prices under several headings, among them powdered tea and the leaf tea of Uji and Shigaraki, each of those at a price a kin; the column reads one line of it, a powdered tea at 27 monme a kin. As far as the small reproduction can be read, this project's reading and no more than that, the leaf teas of Uji and Shigaraki begin at 200 mon a kin and run to 5 monme on the first page of the picture, the list going on to dearer teas on the second; a further list headed Suruga begins at 150 mon, with names legible to 400 mon and one at 4 monme 5 bu, and it too may go on. These are one merchant's prices: no price at Uji or in Suruga stands beside them, and the handbill does not say how heavy its kin was, so they give no ratio and no price by weight.
  • Why are the two not a pair?
    They are different teas at different stages: the shogunate's powdered-tea leaf at a fixed payment, and the town's everyday leaf tea as a ward return lists it. In silver by weight, this project's arithmetic by the coinages of each date, the Uji payment of 1642 was about 104 to 312 g of silver a kilogram, and, the same monme holding less silver by then, about 34 to 101 g in the 1840s; Edo's leaf tea of 1842 was about 1.1 to 6.3 g. No ratio is drawn from them.
  • And Suruga's tea, or a price at both ends for any tea?
    None was found: no source read gives a price of Uji or Suruga tea where it was made beside its price in Edo or Osaka in the same year. How Uji's tea traveled, and the search for Suruga's, are in How did tea travel, and what did it cost at the end of the road?
