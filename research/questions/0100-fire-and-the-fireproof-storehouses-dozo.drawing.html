How our maps draw fire water and the fireproof storehouses (dozō)

Wooden buildings burned, and the defense was water kept standing beside them. So on a plan of one compound and its buildings our maps draw about eight to twelve fire-water tubs, one at each major wooden building and two at the kitchen, and none at the earth-walled storehouses (dozō), which were built not to burn. The tubs are drawn only on these building plans: a town or city map shows its fire defense as a watchtower instead.

An office's records storehouse is drawn as one of those earth-walled storehouses, with a floor of about 450 sq ft at most, no larger than the one records storehouse whose floor was measured.

  • Why put the tubs where the fire risk is?
    Standing water was kept at the wooden buildings, at the entrance and on the roof, and the Forbidden City's vats stood wherever a timber hall was far from water. So the maps give the flammable wooden buildings the tubs, and the kitchen, whose open cooking fire burns all day, the most. No page read ranks the buildings for fire water; the ranking is this project's reasoning.
  • Each tub stands beside a door of its building.
    The water was kept at the entrance, so our maps set each tub just to one side of a door, clear of the doorway, and the kitchen's two one either side of its door, or side by side where one side is taken. How near counts as beside the door is a CONVENTION of this project: within about 10 ft, the distance the second of two tubs side by side needs.
  • How many tubs does a compound get?
    About eight to twelve: one at each major wooden building and two at the kitchen, whose cooking fire burns all day, so the total follows from how many wooden buildings the compound has. The count is this project's reckoning from that placement.
  • Earth-walled storehouses (dozō) get no tub.
    The plastered storehouse was the fire-resistant building, where valuables went in a fire, so a tub there would defend the one building made not to burn.
  • A wooden granary may get one tub.
    Unlike an earth-walled storehouse, a vented wooden granary can burn, so one tub may stand at it to cover its part of the yard.
  • Our maps draw a tub about 2.5 ft across.
    The size is a GUESS: no page read gives a rain tub's size.
  • The tubs are drawn on compound plans only.
    A town or city map draws its buildings as outlines and no fixture of a single building - no house's well, privy, hearth or tub; it keeps even the rain barrels feeding a well off the map, showing only the shared wellhead. A building's fire water is presumed there, not missing. A tub is smaller than the line that would draw it at that scale, so tubs are not scattered onto a settlement map.
  • A walled town or city map shows its fire defense as a watchtower.
    Walled towns and cities carry the communal fire-watch tower by default (How our maps draw fire watch towers and firefighting gear (hinomi yagura)): the tubs fight a fire at the building, the tower spots it for the town.

The records storehouse of an office is one of the earth-walled storehouses, and its size is set by the one such store that was measured.

  • How big is a county office's records storehouse?
    No larger than Takayama's. The book storehouse of the intendant's office (jinya) at Takayama, the one records store measured, has a floor of 41.46 m² (~446 sq ft), and it served an office that governed a whole province, so a county office's was no larger - this project's reading.
  • What shape is a county office's records storehouse?
    About 24 by 18 ft, a GUESS. 24 by 18 ft is 432 sq ft, inside the ceiling that Takayama's measured store sets; no page read gives a records storehouse's length and depth.

The rule the map follows: a county office's records storehouse is an earth-walled kura with a floor of no more than about 450 sq ft - accurate as a ceiling set by the one measured office; its proportions (for instance about 24 by 18 ft) are a guess.
