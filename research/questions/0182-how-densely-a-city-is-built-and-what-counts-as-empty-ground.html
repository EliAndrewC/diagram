How densely a city is built, and what counts as empty ground

A walled Chinese city was often built larger than the city that grew inside it. Its wall was sized by the city's rank in the administration, in the expectation that a higher-ranked city would grow larger, and in many cities a surprisingly large part of the walled ground lay under crops, lakes and ponds. By our estimate the streets took a tenth to a fifth of the rest and the government's compounds only a few percent.

Inside the built-up ground, the commoners of Edo were packed far tighter than its samurai.

  • A walled city's wall was sized by its rank, for the growth a higher-ranked city was expected to see.
    A study of the walled cities of late imperial China finds that the higher a city's administrative rank, the larger the area its planners enclosed, more for the growth they expected of a prefectural capital over a county seat than for defense.
  • A city that never grew was left with large tracts inside its wall that could be farmed.
    Cities built large for their rank that failed to develop important commerce were left with large tracts of land inside the wall that could be given to agriculture.
  • In many cities much of the walled ground lay under crops and water.
    In many cities a surprisingly large proportion of the walled ground was given over to cultivation and to lakes, ponds, streams, canals and springs, and the higher a city's rank, the likelier its open land and water were to be made parks. The study gives no percentage for either.
  • Fields and water inside the wall helped a city last out a siege.
    A city's ability to last out a siege was greatly helped by farming inside its wall and an assured water supply. Holding off floods is given to the walls of many riverine cities, and to earth ramparts outside some north China cities; the purposes the study gives for fields and water inside the wall are lasting out a siege and being cut off from rural disorder, not floods.
  • How much of a walled city was left unbuilt?
    The three accounts found from before the empire fell in 1912 are all of capitals or provincial capitals. Under the Sui and Tang the south of Chang'an, out to the outer wall, had in general no houses and was plowed and planted; Jinan's lake took about a fifth of the ground inside the city's original wall; and a French visitor of 1895 put at least a third of Beijing down as waste garden or wild ground. No page we read gives a county seat's share. Since a county seat was walled for the least expected growth, the study's account would put its share below a provincial capital's fifth rather than above it.

The streets of a county seat were few, and most of its compounds were reached by lanes.

  • A county seat's main streets joined its gates, but in most plans none ran straight from one gate to another.
    At its most regular, two streets joining four gates formed a cross with the drum tower at the crossing, or a street from the south gate met the east-west street in a T, with the yamen to the north. Most plans, though, avoided a street running straight from one gate to another.
  • Pingyao's old city has four main streets, eight lesser streets and seventy-two lanes.
    Together they tie together some 4,000 house compounds and the lives of the old city's 20,000 residents.
  • What share of a walled city went to streets?
    Roughly a tenth to a fifth, this record's estimate: no page we read gives any city's street share as a figure. The one check is arithmetic on a grid whose sizes are known. Heian-kyo's blocks were fixed at about 120 m (~394 ft) a side, with lanes about 12 m (~39 ft) wide between them, so a field of such blocks with a lane on every side gives about 17% of its ground to streets, inside the range.

The government's compounds and drill grounds took the rest of a county seat's ground.

  • We guess that a county seat's government compounds took only a few percent of its ground.
    The study says where the yamen stood and gives no area or share for civic buildings, and no other page we read gives one.
  • The surviving county yamen at Neixiang is built around eighteen courtyards, one behind another.
    The surviving county yamen at Neixiang covers more than 47,000 square meters (~11.6 acres), with 18 courtyards one behind another and more than 280 rooms; the page gives no share of it built over.
  • A county seat's great drill ground could lie outside a gate, and a small one inside the wall.
    Chongming's great drill ground, about 6 acres, lay outside its east gate, and a small one for training troops stood in the southeast corner inside the city; the small one is given no size. Drill grounds themselves are at Drill grounds, archery ranges and riding grounds (jiaochang, yaba, baba).

A Japanese castle town shows how unevenly the built-up ground was packed, by the rank of those who lived on it.

  • In Edo the townsmen lived far more densely than the samurai.
    In Edo the townspeople and commoners lived in a much more densely populated area than the samurai, in a series of gated communities called machi; some of the great lords' residences there took vast grounds. In a castle town the townsmen's area was smaller than the samurai's, and their houses were built along the road without any gaps. None of these gives a figure for the difference.
