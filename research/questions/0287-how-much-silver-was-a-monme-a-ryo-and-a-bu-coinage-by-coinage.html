How much silver was a monme, a ryo and a bu, coinage by coinage?

Rokugan counts its prices in koku and smaller coins, and the setting's money rules set them beside historical prices, which Edo-period Japan counted in silver monme, gold ryo and copper mon. To compare prices across two centuries, each of those units has to be put in one measure. The findings below give how much pure silver a monme held, and what a ryo, a bu and a kanmon of copper mon were worth in it, coinage by coinage, as far as the historical record shows.

A monme was a weight, 3.75 g, and silver passed by weight as chogin, bars of silver alloyed with copper; each recoinage changed how much of that weight was silver. A monme of coin held 3.0 g of pure silver until 1695 and again from 1714, 1.725 g from 1736, 1.35 g from 1820, 0.975 g from 1837, and about half a gram from 1859. The ryo was a gold coin, the koban, with a quarter-ryo coin, the bu, so a ryo's worth in silver is its market rate in monme times the silver in a monme of the day.

  • Old and new silver passed side by side after a recoinage.
    From 1714 to the end of 1722 silver of six finenesses passed together, each by its fineness. The Genbun silver was withdrawn only at the start of 1827, seven years after the Bunsei silver was issued. The old silver was declared withdrawn in 1842, five years after the Tenpo silver was first minted, but the deadline for exchanging it was put off again and again; the Tenpo silver itself passed until the silver units were abolished in 1868, and so beside the Ansei silver issued in 1859.

The 3.11 g of the Kyoto basket

  • Where does 3.11 g a monme come from?
    The authors of the Kyoto basket in What did a Kyoto laborer's year of goods cost? convert every price in monme at 3.11 g of pure silver, and give no basis for the figure. It is 83% of a monme's weight, finer than any chogin issued after 1736; the Genbun silver that passed in their years held 1.725 g.
  • The spreadsheet of their prices assumes 3.75 g.
    The file of their Mitsui prices takes a monme as 3.75 g of pure silver, and says the true content is uncertain and may have been less, perhaps 3 to 3.2 g if prices were reckoned in fairly pure silver.

A ryo and a bu in silver

  • What a ryo fetched in silver, and what that silver weighed.
    The law set a ryo at 50 monme of silver and 4 kanmon of copper in 1609, and at 60 monme and 4 kanmon in 1700. The market differed: early in the period a ryo was about 43 monme, and around 1700 silver stood near 50 monme. From 1720 a study of rice prices gives Osaka's rate nearly year by year, with three years of the 1710s; a chronology of Edo money on a website, copying a 1986 reference book and naming a city, Edo, for only a few years' rates, gives a rate for most years from 1692; from 1787 the Osaka market's daily rates are in What was copper coin worth in silver?. A bu is a quarter of each ryo figure in the table at the foot of this page.
  • The silver coins of the later 1700s and 1800s held less than their face.
    Eight nanryo two-shu silver pieces made a ryo; each weighed 2.7 monme of fine silver, which the article reckons at 21.6 monme of pure silver a ryo (81 g), against 27.6 monme in 60 monme of Genbun silver. At the 97.81% silver its article gives for the coin, a ryo of them held 79 g. In 1773 the shogunate explained the difference by its own reckoning that a ryo equaled 24 monme of pure silver (90 g). The nanryo of 1824 weighed 2 monme at 97.96% by the same table: 59 g a ryo. Four Tenpo one-bu silver pieces of 1837, each 2.3 monme at 98.86%, weighed 9.2 monme a ryo, 34 g of pure silver, against 15.6 monme of silver in a ryo of Tenpo chogin; the Ansei one-bu of 1859, 89.36% silver, held 31 g a ryo.

On one Osaka day of 1766, the nearest to that row in the same question, a ryo was 63.82 monme and a kanmon 15.19 monme: at Genbun's 1.725 g, about 110 g a ryo and 26.2 g a kanmon, 66 mon a monme. Edo's mon a monme is Edo's copper rate of the 1820s, 6,610 mon to the ryo, over Osaka's 63.4 monme to the ryo. Kyoto's own rate in the first month of 1867 was 107.1 monme to the ryo and 8,460 mon, about 79 mon a monme.
  • The 1700s and the 1860s rest on a secondary chronology.
    Where the Osaka study and the chronology both run, from 1720 to 1789, their decade means agree within about 1.5 monme to the ryo. By the end of the period chogin hardly circulated, and the silver monme had become a unit of account in ledgers and shop prices.
