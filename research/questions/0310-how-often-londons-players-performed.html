How often did London's players perform in Shakespeare's time?

Rokugan's troupes keep their own calendar, and nothing here sets one. This is a comparative question: how often the London companies of Shakespeare's time played, a yardstick for anyone at the table who wonders whether the Globe played every night.

London's companies played in the afternoon, once a day, six days a week, and changed the play almost daily from a stock of some two dozen, adding a new one about every fortnight. They stopped for Lent, for some weeks of summer spent touring, and whenever plague deaths ran high. The 1590s figures here come from the diary of Philip Henslowe, with whom the companies played, as the historian E. K. Chambers relays it.

  • The play began in the afternoon.
    A visitor from Basel, Thomas Platter, wrote in 1599 that two and sometimes three comedies were played in London every day at two o'clock, at separate places. An actor's articles with Henslowe of 1614 bound him to be dressed and ready to begin the play at three in the afternoon on every day a play was played. A summons to play at Court in the evening did not always mean a blank afternoon.
  • A company played six days a week, and never on Sundays.
    In 1592 the company led by the actor Edward Alleyn played six days a week for eighteen weeks, missing only Good Friday and two other days. In 1594-1597 the Admiral's men played on every weekday except in Lent, at a season's start or end, and now and then, chiefly in December; there were no plays on Sundays.
  • The bill changed almost every day.
    In those eighteen weeks of 1592 the company gave 105 performances of 23 plays, each for anywhere from one day to fifteen, and seldom played the same play twice in a week; five were new.
  • A new play came about every fortnight.
    In 1594-1597 the Admiral's men brought out fifty-five new plays, at an average of one a fortnight.
  • How many performances a year?
    About 243: the Admiral's men gave 728 performances in the three years 1594-1597, in London seasons covering about 126 weeks, which is about 42 weeks of London playing a year (both yearly figures this project's arithmetic).
  • Lent stopped the playing for five or six weeks.
    The Admiral's men broke for thirty-seven days in Lent 1595 and forty-three in 1596; in 1597 they stopped only eighteen days and then played three days a week until Easter.
  • Summer was a vacation, spent touring.
    In 1595 they broke for fifty-nine days, with visits to Bath and Maidstone; in 1596 the summer vacation lasted ninety-five days, and in 1595-1596 they are noted at Coventry, Bath, Gloucester and Dunwich.
  • Plague closed the theaters.
    On 23 June 1592 the Privy Council forbade all plays until Michaelmas; plague then came in the course of the summer, and the company traveled the provinces from July to December. A draft royal license for Queen Anne's players, early in King James's reign, let them play publicly once plague deaths in London fell to thirty a week.
  • From 1608 the King's men wintered indoors.
    The King's men did not leave the Globe, but took up their winter quarters in the Blackfriars, a roofed theater of the kind until then used only by companies of boys.
