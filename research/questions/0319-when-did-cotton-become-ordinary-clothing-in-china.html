When did cotton become ordinary clothing in China?

Rokugan's tax rules set cotton beside common iron as an ordinary, low-rate good, and the setting's list of regional cash crops does not name it. The findings below give what the historical record holds for China; Japan's is in When did cotton become ordinary clothing in Japan?

Cotton came to China in stages over about a thousand years. Its cloth was long known as tribute from distant peoples, while China itself clothed the population in other fibers. The plant was grown first on the frontiers: at Turfan in the far northwest by the early 500s, in Yunnan under the Tang dynasty, and on the southeast coast by the late 1000s, with Hainan probably earlier. It was common in the lower Yangzi by 1285, and from 1289 the Yuan dynasty's government took it as a yearly levy.

Among the cloths it displaced were ramie and hemp, and hemp had been what most of the population wore before the Song dynasty. A Ming statesman, Qiu Jun, wrote that rich and poor everywhere depended on cotton, yet ramie and kudzu stayed in use for summer, and Guangdong still could not grow enough for itself in the late 1600s.

In what century did cotton clothing become widespread?

Writers of the time state it only in general terms, and no source counts the wearers. The dates by region below are this project's guess from those statements.

  • Across most of China, among commoners, was it the 1400s?
    Qiu Jun's treatise is the earliest general statement read: the seed had spread across the realm, north and south, and rich and poor all depended on it. This record's entry for the treatise dates it to 1487, though no page read for this question states the year. The historian Paul Pelliot, in his own gloss, puts universal use "one century after Polo". The verdict is attested only for these general statements.
  • In the lower Yangzi, was it earlier?
    Probably, but no source read dates its wearing there. The evidence is the summer tax fixed for Jiangnan in 1296, payable in cotton cloth among other goods, and Wang Zhen's statement of 1313 that after the conquest of the south merchants sold it in the north and its wearing grew.
  • In the north, was it by the end of the Ming dynasty?
    This project's guess is the 1500s and early 1600s, with low confidence: in Xu Guangqi's day the north grew cotton and bought its cloth from the south, and its own weaving took off only in the last Ming decades. No source read dates when northern commoners wore it.
  • Was it Guangdong's summer clothing in the late 1600s?
    Apparently not, though Qu Dajun does not say so outright. He has Guangdong's summer cloths as the likes of banana, kudzu and ramie, and sets them against its winter cloth, mostly brought in.
  • By 1637, was it ordinary for all ranks?
    Song Yingxing says cotton cloth against the cold was the same for high and low, grown across the realm, with looms in every ten houses, though that last is a manner of speaking. The attestation is for winter wear. Ramie, by the same author, stayed in hot-weather clothing.

First record of the plant and the cloth

  • Does the Han history's wording mean cotton?
    It does not say so. Of the Ailao of the southwest it lists wool rugs, a cloth it calls bodie, and fine cloth it calls langan. The next sentence has one Ailao cloth spun from 梧桐木華 (wutong mu hua, "wutong tree flower"). Of Hainan under the emperor Wu it says only 布 (bu, "cloth"). A paper by Harriet Zurndorfer, marked by its author not for citation without permission, takes the Ailao passage as good-quality cotton, without saying which word it means, and says official documents recorded cotton cloth in what is now Yunnan around 200 BCE. Pelliot says that with the word po-tieh, for cotton, the Han history draws on a later source.
  • Was langan cloth ramie?
    Qu Dajun says so: in the speech of the Liao tribes it was the word for ramie.
  • Cotton textiles have been found far earlier in the far west.
    Zurndorfer's paper reports cotton from Tarim Basin burials of the first millennium BCE.

Cloth arriving as tribute and trade

  • Under the Han and Tang dynasties cotton came as tribute.
    Qiu Jun says distant peoples offered it, that China did not have the seed, that commoners did not wear it and that officials did not levy it. The Liang emperor Wu's black cotton tent of the early 500s shows cotton at court between those dynasties, though Pelliot does not say it was tribute.
  • Tang writers placed the plant outside China.
    In 736 a commentator wrote that cotton cloth was woven from a plant which did not exist in China, and Pelliot found no Tang text on cotton growing in China proper.

The frontiers: first growing, region by region

  • The northwest: Turfan by the early 500s.
    Pelliot, reading the Liang dynasty's history, has cotton grown around Turfan in the first half of the 500s, and tribute of its cloth from that region alone in the statutes of 713-741. He found no sign of it passing from there into Gansu, Shanxi or Shaanxi at that date.
  • The southwest: Yunnan under the Tang.
    Pelliot has cultivation then limited to Yunnan and Tongking, or at least unrecorded elsewhere, and thinks it had probably already begun on Hainan.
  • Hainan and the south coast by 1178.
    Zhou Qufei records it in three south-coast prefectures (Lei, Hua and Lian) and among the Li of Hainan, used in place of silk and ramie. Pelliot adds that most of Hainan's cotton then was grown by the Li, and that by 1225 the island was the chief source shipped to Fujian.
  • Did Zhou Qufei mean that cotton was plentiful, or that the rich wore it?
    The two characters 富有 (fuyou, "abundant; the rich have") can be read either way. This project reads them as "plentiful", as they plainly mean elsewhere in the same chapter; Pelliot translates "some of the rich".
  • Fujian and Guangdong by the late 1000s.
    A work of 1085 says the local peasants there competed to plant it, and Pelliot takes this and a work of about 1125 as proving it extensive by the end of the 11th century.

The Yangzi valley and the north

  • Did cotton reach the Yangzi by the 1100s?
    That is a modern inference, not a record. Pelliot reasons backward from a gloss of 1285 and from the wars of the 1270s, and concludes that it went back "at least to the first half of the 12th century"; elsewhere he says it was grown in Jiangnan "from the 12th cent." Yet he calls the order of 1289 "the first mention of cotton cultivation in central China".
  • By 1285 it was common in Jiangnan.
    The Yuan commentator Hu Sanxing, a man of the region, wrote that there was much of it there, and described how it was sown, ginned and spun.
  • Was it the late Song or the Yuan that spread it?
    A farming manual with a preface of 1273 is the crux, and the two accounts disagree over whose it was. Zurndorfer's paper calls it issued by the Song state in its waning days; Pelliot says it was prepared by order of Qubilai. Zurndorfer has growing reach the lower Yangzi, Sichuan and Shaanxi by about 1270-1300. Pelliot reads Wang Zhen's introduction in his work of 1313 as showing it had spread to the lower Yangzi and Sichuan before 1276.
  • Wang Zhen, in 1313, has it recent in the east and northwest.
    He calls it lately much sown in the lower Yangzi and in Shaanxi, the first time, Pelliot notes, that northwestern China appears as a cotton region.
  • When did Huang Daopo teach Songjiang to process it?
    The sources give different dates, and one calls the story a legend. Tao Zongyi, late in the Yuan, says only that an old woman of that name came from Yazhou "early in the dynasty" and that Songjiang had already sought its seed from Fujian and Guangdong. Zurndorfer's paper gives her a birth year of 1245 with a question mark and calls the story a "well-known legend". Pelliot stresses that much cotton was already grown in Jiangnan by 1285, before her date in later tradition.

A commercial crop, and the state's part

  • The state took a hand from the 1280s.
    In 1289 cotton offices were set up in five southern regions and the population charged 100,000 pieces a year. In 1296 the Jiangnan summer tax was fixed, payable in cotton cloth, silk and floss.
  • Was cotton taxed under the Yuan at all?
    Qiu Jun says not: it was not yet levied under the Song and Yuan, so their histories' treatises on revenue say nothing of it. The Yuan history's treatise on revenue contradicts him with the summer tax of 1296. Zurndorfer's paper and Pelliot agree that cotton was taken in tax by then.
  • In the south it was already a market crop under the Song.
    Zurndorfer's paper, citing Billy So, has it commercial in Fujian in the early Song, with a yearly quota of 5,000 bolts sent to the court.
  • The Ming ordered it planted.
    At the founding, private land of 5 to 10 mu was to have half a mu each of mulberry, hemp and cotton, doubled above 10 mu. The year 1368 is Pelliot's. In 1376 a bolt of cotton or ramie cloth was accepted for six dou of rice in tax, and hemp cloth for four.
  • Cotton and bast cloth were still taxed side by side about 1500.
    The tax accounts of the Hongzhi reign (1488-1505) list cotton cloth beside ramie and hemp cloth.

What cotton replaced, and how fast

  • Before it, China wore silk, hemp, kudzu and coarse wool.
    Qiu Jun names those four. Hemp, Zurndorfer's paper says of the first through ninth centuries, was worn by most of the population, and dress rules rested on the difference between it and silk.
  • Why did it win?
    Wang Zhen says that it spared the rearing work of silk and the twisting and connecting of threads that hemp and ramie need, and kept out the cold.
  • Ramie and kudzu did not vanish.
    In 1637 ramie still made hot-weather clothes and curtains, and the thread for sewing garments of cloth. In the late 1600s the kudzu of Leizhou was popular across the realm.
  • How fast did it go?
    No source read gives a rate of change.

By region

  • Jiangnan: one district is reported as more than half in cotton.
    A note on an inspection of 1615 in Xu Guangqi's farming compendium says the land at Haishang, which this project reads as the Shanghai district of Songjiang, came to nearly two million mu, more than half of it in cotton, no less than a million mu. Ye Mengzhu, in the late 1600s, says that in his county it was as widely planted as rice.
  • North China grew the cotton and bought the cloth.
    Xu Guangqi has raw cotton shipped south and cloth shipped north. In the years just before he wrote, in an undated comment, Suning county alone was weaving a tenth of what Songjiang did, and Zurndorfer's paper dates Suning's cellar weaving to the last Ming decades.
  • Guangdong kept its own summer cloths.
    Qu Dajun says Guangdong's cotton did not meet the needs of its ten prefectures, so its winter cloth came mostly from Wu and Chu, on the lower and middle Yangzi.

By class

  • Dress under the Ming, as a Songjiang writer recalled it.
    Ye Mengzhu says the grand wore thick wild silk and fine kudzu, that the plain wore fine purple-flower or white cloth in winter, that poor scholars had the white cloth robe as ordinary dress, and that servants and entertainers wore only blue-black. He does not name the fiber of the white cloth, though Pelliot says the word "cloth" had come, by his own day, to mean cotton goods.
  • Foreign visitors described the poor in cotton.
    Staunton's embassy account of the 1790s says cotton was universally worn by the lower orders of both sexes. Robert Fortune in the 1840s calls blue cotton cloth the principal dress of the poorer classes in the north.

Shares of households, acreage and clothing

  • What share of households or of clothing was cotton?
    No premodern source read gives one, for any region or century. The nearest is outside the period: John Lossing Buck's farm survey of the 1920s, where cotton clothing predominated and 23.5 percent of households had clothing from raw material off their own farm.
  • Is there any acreage share?
    Only for one place and one zone. Haishang's is above. Li Bozhong, as a later paper reports him, estimated cotton's share of the fields in the southern Jiangsu cotton zone at 50 percent in the late Ming and about 70 by the mid-Qing.
