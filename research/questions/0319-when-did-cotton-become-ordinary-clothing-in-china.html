When did cotton become ordinary clothing in China?

Rokugan's tax rules set cotton beside common iron as an ordinary, low-rate good, and the setting's list of regional cash crops does not name it. The findings below give what the historical record holds for China; how the crop got there is in How did cotton reach China and spread across it? and Japan's story in When did cotton become ordinary clothing in Japan?

Cotton came to China in stages over about a thousand years. Its cloth was long known as tribute from distant peoples, while China itself clothed the population in other fibers. The plant was grown first on the frontiers: at Turfan in the far northwest by the early 500s, in Yunnan under the Tang dynasty, and on the southeast coast by the late 1000s, with Hainan probably earlier. It was common in the lower Yangzi by 1285, and from 1289 the Yuan dynasty's government took it as a yearly levy.

Among the cloths it displaced were ramie and hemp, and hemp had been what most of the population wore before the Song dynasty. A Ming statesman, Qiu Jun, wrote that rich and poor everywhere depended on cotton, yet ramie stayed in use for summer, kudzu still made a costly fine cloth, and Guangdong still could not grow enough for itself in the late 1600s.

In what century did cotton clothing become widespread?

Writers of the time state it only in general terms, and no source counts the wearers. The dates by region below are this project's guess from those statements.

  • Across most of China, among commoners, was it the 1400s?
    Qiu Jun's treatise is the earliest general statement read: the seed had spread across the realm, north and south, and rich and poor all depended on it. He presented the treatise to the throne in the 23rd year of the Chenghua reign (1487). The historian Paul Pelliot, in his own gloss, puts universal use "one century after Polo". The date rests on these general statements alone.
  • In the lower Yangzi, was it earlier?
    Probably, but no source read dates its wearing there. The evidence is the summer tax fixed for Jiangnan in 1296, payable in cotton cloth among other goods, and Wang Zhen's statement of 1313 that after the conquest of the south merchants sold it in the north and its wearing grew.
  • In the north, was it by the end of the Ming dynasty?
    This project's guess is the 1500s and early 1600s, with low confidence: in Xu Guangqi's day the north grew cotton and bought its cloth from the south, and its own weaving took off only in the last Ming decades. No source read dates when northern commoners wore it.
  • Was it Guangdong's summer clothing in the late 1600s?
    Apparently not, though Qu Dajun does not say so outright. He has Guangdong's summer cloths as the likes of banana, kudzu and ramie, and sets them against its winter cloth, mostly brought in.
  • By 1637, was it ordinary for all ranks?
    Song Yingxing says cotton cloth against the cold was the same for high and low, grown across the realm, with looms in every ten houses, though that last is a manner of speaking. The attestation is for winter wear. Ramie, by the same author, stayed in hot-weather clothing.

A commercial crop, and the state's part

  • The state took a hand from the 1280s.
    In 1289 cotton offices were set up in five southern regions and the population charged 100,000 pieces a year. In 1296 the Jiangnan summer tax was fixed, payable in cotton cloth, silk and floss.
  • Was cotton taxed under the Yuan at all?
    Qiu Jun says not: it was not yet levied under the Song and Yuan, so their histories' treatises on revenue say nothing of it. The Yuan history's treatise on revenue contradicts him with the summer tax of 1296. Zurndorfer's paper and Pelliot agree that cotton was taken in tax by then.
  • In the south it was already a market crop under the Song.
    Zurndorfer's paper, citing Billy So, has it commercial in Fujian in the early Song, with a yearly quota of 5,000 bolts sent to the court. The scholars Liu Pingsheng and Ge Jinfang report the gazetteer of Siming, in its section on maritime trade, listing cotton cloth and floss among the "coarse" goods on ships from Fujian, Guangdong and Hainan. They hold that cotton goods were precious gifts early in the Southern Song dynasty and ordinary wares by its middle and later years, which is their reading and gives no count.
  • The Ming ordered it planted.
    At the founding, private land of 5 to 10 mu was to have half a mu each of mulberry, hemp and cotton, doubled above 10 mu. The year 1368 is Pelliot's. In 1376 a bolt of cotton or ramie cloth was accepted for six dou of rice in tax, and hemp cloth for four.
  • Cotton and bast cloth were still taxed side by side about 1500.
    The tax accounts of the Hongzhi reign (1488-1505) list cotton cloth beside ramie and hemp cloth.
  • How much did the early Ming state take?
    Attested only as a modern suggestion, at second hand. A second paper of Zurndorfer's, marked by its author not for citation, reports modern scholars' view, drawn from the Ming court's records, that government use could have reached 15 million bolts a year before 1400. A bolt in that paper varied from 32 to 41 centimeters wide and from 5.72 to 11.45 meters long, averaging seven meters.

What cotton replaced, and how fast

  • Before it, China wore silk, hemp, kudzu and coarse wool.
    Qiu Jun names those four. Hemp, Zurndorfer's paper says of the first through ninth centuries, was worn by most of the population, and dress rules rested on the difference between it and silk.
  • Why did it win?
    Wang Zhen says that it spared the rearing work of silk and the twisting and connecting of threads that hemp and ramie need, and kept out the cold.
  • Ramie and kudzu did not vanish.
    In 1637 ramie still made hot-weather clothes and curtains, and the thread for sewing garments of cloth. In the late 1600s the kudzu of Leizhou was popular across the realm.
  • How fast did it go?
    No source read gives a rate of change. The historian Guo Weidong says in passing that cotton replaced hemp and silk as China's chief material for clothing and bedding in the Yuan and Ming periods, with no evidence set beside it.

By region

  • Jiangnan: one district is reported as more than half in cotton.
    A note on an inspection of 1615 in Xu Guangqi's farming compendium says the land at Haishang, which this project reads as the Shanghai district of Songjiang, came to nearly two million mu, more than half of it in cotton, more than a million mu. Ye Mengzhu, in the late 1600s, says that in his county it was as widely planted as rice.
  • Did Songjiang clothe the empire by about 1500?
    Zurndorfer's second paper has its weaving fully commercial in the later 1400s, and has local gazetteers claiming by the end of that century that it "clothed the empire". That is a boast reported at second hand and no measure of wearers.
  • How much cloth did Jiangnan weave?
    Modern estimates disagree, and both are at second hand. For the late Ming that paper gives 20 million bolts a year from Yan Zhongping, without naming the region, and as much as 50 million from Li Bozhong, which it elsewhere assigns to Jiangnan; Li has Jiangnan at 100 million by the mid-Qing.
  • North China grew the cotton and bought the cloth.
    Xu Guangqi has raw cotton shipped south and cloth shipped north. In the years just before he wrote, in an undated comment, Suning county alone was weaving a tenth of what Songjiang did, and Zurndorfer's paper dates Suning's summer weaving in underground cellars to the last Ming decades.
  • Guangdong kept its own summer cloths.
    Qu Dajun says Guangdong's cotton did not meet the needs of its ten prefectures, so its winter cloth came mostly from Wu and Chu, on the lower and middle Yangzi.

By class

  • Dress under the Ming, as a Songjiang writer recalled it.
    Ye Mengzhu says that, in informal dress, men from officials down to licentiates who dressed richly wore da rong cloth, pongee and fine kudzu, that those who dressed plainly wore fine purple-flower or white cloth in winter, that poor scholars had the white cloth robe as ordinary dress, and that servants and entertainers wore only blue-black. He does not name the fiber of the white cloth, though Pelliot says of his own time, not Ye Mengzhu's, that the word "cloth" had come to mean cotton goods.
  • Foreign visitors described the poor in cotton.
    Staunton's embassy account of the 1790s says cotton was universally worn by the lower orders of both sexes. Robert Fortune in the 1840s calls blue cotton cloth the principal dress of the poorer classes at Shanghai and the other towns of what he calls the north of China.

Shares of households, acreage and clothing

  • What share of households or of clothing was cotton?
    No premodern source read gives one, for any region or century. The nearest is outside the period: John Lossing Buck's farm survey of the 1920s, where cotton clothing predominated and 23.5 percent of households had clothing from raw material off their own farm.
  • Is there any acreage share?
    Only for one place and one zone. Haishang's is above. Li Bozhong, as a later paper reports him, estimated cotton's share of the fields in the southern Jiangsu cotton zone at 50 percent in the late Ming and about 70 by the mid-Qing.
  • How much did a household use, and how much was sold?
    Attested only for the mid-Qing, at second hand from Zurndorfer's second paper: an average household, in no region the paper names, consumed 8.4 bolts a year (Li Bozhong), and 52.8 percent of all cotton cloth made in China went to market (Xu Dixin and Wu Chengming). Neither says what share of a household's clothing was cotton.
  • How much cotton cloth was there per head about 1750?
    Attested only as one historian's estimate, in a draft its author marked not to be cited without permission. Kenneth Pomeranz gives China 6.2 to 8.3 pounds of cotton cloth per head, probably nearer the low end, and the text beside his table speaks of it as consumption. For the Yangzi Delta without its salt-producing prefectures, about 31 million inhabitants, he gives 14.5 pounds per head of output, the amount consumed locally unknown, so it is no measure of what the delta's commoners wore. The appendix he names for his sources is not in the copy read, the table does not say that the pounds are per year, and neither figure is a share of households or of clothing.

Prices are in what cloth cost, the labor in how long cloth took to make, and cotton's place in a year's budget in a year's necessities in China.
