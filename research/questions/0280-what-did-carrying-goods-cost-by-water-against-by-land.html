What did carrying goods cost by water, against by land?

Rokugan's goods go by road and by river: Fox lumber is shipped downriver, and a price there is judged against a day's wage. The findings below give what the historical record holds for what carriage cost by water and by land, in Qing China first, then in Edo Japan, each set beside the wages of the same place where a source gives them.

Water was far cheaper than land. In the Qing province of Jiangsu, the official rates paid a boat a fiftieth to a twentyfifth of what they paid a porter for the same load and distance; a historian of Qing freight puts water at about a tenth of carriage by pack mule; and the Ming statecraft writer Qiu Jun reckoned that river transport saved three or four tenths of the cost of land carriage and the sea seven or eight tenths. Every Qing rate below is a rate the state paid for its own building or its own grain, not a fare a merchant charged. The land rates by themselves - Japan's post stations, carts and porters in Zhili and Hunan, Yunnan's oxcarts and Gansu's carts - are in What did hiring a horse or a porter cost? and What did carrying stone cost, by cart and by porter?

Qing China: boats and porters in Jiangsu

Jiangsu's regulation on the prices of building materials sets, district by district, what a boat was allowed for bringing stone, bricks and lime, and what porters were allowed for carrying them from the landing to the site. The edition the database uses is undated; a study of 1940, whose title places these regulations in the Qianlong reign (1736-1795), used another copy, printed in 1830.

  • What did a boat cost?
    A boatload of stone came 100 li for 1.2 tael of silver at Kunshan and Changshu, where a boat carried 10,000 jin or a little more. At Zhenyang, Chongming and Jiading, a boat of 10,000 jin of stone was 0.01 tael a li and of bricks or lime 0.006. In five Songjiang counties a boat of 10,000 jin of bricks and lime from 100 li or more away was 0.8 tael; at Wujin, Wuxi and Jiangyin a boat of 50 dan (5,000 jin) of lime from over 100 li was 0.3; at Jingjiang, 200 li away, 1.0 for bricks and 0.5 for lime. That is 0.005 to 0.012 tael to carry 100 jin (about 60 kg, ~130 lb) 100 li (about 58 km, ~36 miles), this project's arithmetic.
  • What did a day's work earn there?
    In each of these districts a laborer was allowed 0.04 tael a man, and a craftsman 0.05 or 0.06 tael a workday. The laborer's rate is written per man; this project reads it as a man's day, as What did carrying stone cost, by cart and by porter? does. So 100 jin carried 100 li by boat cost an eighth to a third of a laborer's day, this project's arithmetic.
  • And the porters?
    0.003 tael for every 100 jin carried a li at Kunshan, Changshu, Wujiang, Zhenyang, Chongming and Jiading, and 0.002 at Wujin. These were mostly short carries from the landing to the site, and where a row gives the distance it prices the whole carry, not the li: 0.003 tael for every 100 jin carried the 1 or 2 li from the river in the Songjiang counties, and 0.005 for the 2 to 4 li at Wuxi, Jiangyin and Jingjiang. At 0.003 a li, a porter's 100 li would have been 7.5 days of a laborer's pay, this project's arithmetic; no row prices a carry that long.
  • How many times dearer was the porter?
    About 25 to 50 times the boat, for the same weight and distance, in the same districts: 25 for Kunshan's stone, 30 for Zhenyang's and 50 for its bricks and lime, and about 33 for Wujin's lime, this project's arithmetic. In Hunan the rates of 1769 in What did hiring a horse or a porter cost? make a porter about 19 times a boat, this project's arithmetic.
  • Some rows are not rates by weight.
    Rafts of timber and bamboo, and in several districts boats of bricks and lime, were allowed a share of the goods' price - 0.05 tael for rafts and 0.03 for boats on every tael of the materials' value - so they cannot be turned into a cost per jin per li; the database's own cells for the rafts, 0.018 to 0.045, differ from the remarks' 0.05.

Qing China: tribute grain by river and by sea

  • What did a river lighter cost near Tianjin?
    1.32 tael for every 100 li, for a boat carrying 100 shi of grain: in 1695, 150 Tianjin lighters were assigned to carry grain on to Jizhou. That is 0.0132 tael a shi for 100 li, this project's arithmetic. No wage of 1695 at Tianjin was found; the nearest in this record is the Zhili regulation of 1769, 74 years later, a Tianjin laborer's 0.09 tael a day and a craftsman's 0.12 (What did a skilled craftsman earn against a laborer?).
  • And a sea junk from Shanghai?
    0.55 tael. When purchased rice was proposed to go north on trial in 1868, its freight was to follow the sand junks' precedent, 0.55 tael, to Zizhulin at Tianjin, with 0.07 tael a shi more for lighters, warehouse rent and porters and 0.03 a shi for insurance. The clause does not repeat "a shi" for the 0.55; this project reads it as per shi, like the charges beside it. The sea route had been reckoned in 1826 at over 4,000 li (about 2,300 km, ~1,430 miles), made in a little over ten days. Putting the two together, 0.55 tael for over 4,000 li is under 0.014 tael a shi for 100 li, this project's arithmetic across 42 years. The nearest wage in this record is again Tianjin's of 1769, 99 years earlier.
  • What did sea transport cost the state in all?
    For Jiangsu's tribute grain, no more than 0.7 tael a shi when it was first set, paid from the canal's funds; a memorial the chapter gives after 1875 says Jiangsu's additions were not far from 1 tael and Zhejiang's had reached 1.
  • Two other rates the chapter gives.
    In 1874 grain Jiangxi bought at Shanghai went to Tianjin by the China Merchants' steamers at 2.7 tael a shi, about five times the junks' 0.55 by this project's arithmetic; and Tianjin's lighters in the frozen season, as the page punctuates it, were paid 8.4 tael for every 100 shi, with no distance given, a rate later raised by 5 tael.

How many times cheaper was water?

  • About ten times cheaper than a pack mule, as a historian estimates it.
    Ruoran Cheng (cheng-2024-transport-cost) writes that in early and mid-Qing China water freight was about a tenth of carriage by mule pack, and that the lower Yangzi kept about 1 to 10 between water and land at the end of the 1600s, 0.125 g of silver a ton-mile by water against 1.254 by land. For a survey of 1890 he gives 1 to 10 up to 1 to 25 between a junk from Jiangsu to Henan by the Grand Canal and the Huai River and land, and as low as 1 to 37.5 in Jiangsu.
  • How did each mode compare, in his estimate?
    His table for the late 1600s, his own conversion to tael of silver for 100 kg carried 100 km: a main river 0.016, a secondary river 0.021, a mule or pack horse on flat land 0.213, a wheeled vehicle on flat land 0.077, a porter on flat land 1.594, and hill country 0.452. Against his laborer's wage for the end of the 1600s, 0.06 tael a day, 100 kg (~220 lb) carried 100 km (~62 miles) cost about a third of a day by a secondary river, one and a quarter days by cart, three and a half by mule and over 26 by porter; a porter was about 76 times a secondary river and a cart nearly 4 times, this project's arithmetic.
  • Was river transport as cheap as the sea?
    No, by the Ming writer Qiu Jun's reckoning: river transport saved three or four tenths of the cost of land carriage and the sea seven or eight, because river boats still had men hauling them; the sea, he added, risked loss by drowning, so that weighing gain against harm the two came out about even. So river transport cost six or seven tenths of land and the sea two or three, this project's arithmetic.
  • The same was known in the Tang.
    Before Liu Yan took charge of grain transport in 764, rice carried overland from Runzhou to Yangzi had cost 19 cash a dou; putting it in sacks on boats cut that by 15 cash, as Qiu Jun quotes the history. So the boat cost about a fifth of the road, this project's arithmetic.

Edo Japan

No Edo-period statement was found of how many times cheaper water was than land. What survives are freight figures by sea, with no land carriage beside them in the same source.

  • What did shipping sake to Edo cost?
    About 60 monme of silver for every 10 horse-loads (da) of sake was the standard freight, in a historian's note that also says it varied and rose toward the end of the Edo period; he cites it in checking a contract of 1855 for a taru ship of Nishinomiya, one of the ships that carried sake from the Osaka region to Edo. The note gives no distance and no year for the standard. The nearest wages in this record are the Edo carpenter's 5 to 5.5 monme of silver a day and a laborer's 300 mon in the 1830s to the 1850s (What did a skilled craftsman earn against a laborer?), so a horse-load's freight was a little over a carpenter's day, this project's arithmetic; the laborer's wage is in copper and is not converted.
  • What did the Hagi domain pay to ship its rice?
    A share of the rice itself: 3.4% for rice shipped to Osaka in 1652; about 4% from its busiest district, both to Osaka and to Hagi, up to 1763; and nearly 6 to 7% within twelve years of a rise in 1764. The share is paid in rice, so no wage prices it. How the share varied with distance is in How far did goods travel before carriage priced them out?
  • What could a pack-horse driver carry?
    A chuma driver of Shinano, a province with no boat routes, usually drove three or four horses and carried about 100 kan a trip, about 375 kg (~830 lb), this project's arithmetic. No chuma freight rate was found; the post stations' fares by the stage are in What did hiring a horse or a porter cost?
