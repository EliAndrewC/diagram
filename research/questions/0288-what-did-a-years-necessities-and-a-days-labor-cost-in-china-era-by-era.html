What did a year's necessities and a day's labor cost in China, era by era?

Rokugan's money rules weigh a price against what a laborer earned and spent in a year. The findings below give, era by era from the Song to the early 1900s, what the historical record holds for the price of a year's necessities in Chinese cities, and a laborer's day wage beside them, each in the money and the measure it was paid in.

No one series runs through China's history as the Mitsui books run through Japan's. Each era below rests on its own record: an official price list, a local writer's notes, a foreign newspaper's list, a modern survey, or a modern reconstruction. Prices are in taels of silver, copper cash or, from the 1900s, dollars; a tael was ten mace, and a shi of grain was ten dou or a hundred sheng. What a tael, a string of cash and a dollar held in silver is in What was a tael, a string of cash and a Chinese dollar worth in silver? No record read prices fish, buckwheat or beans other than soybeans in most eras, and lamp oil only in a reconstruction.

The Song and the Ming

  • What did rice cost under the Song?
    Only rice is priced, and only as a modern historian's averages, reported by another in grams of silver: by decade, 12 to 60 grams of silver a hectoliter from the 960s to the 1110s, 243 to 265 in the 1120s and 1130s, and 30 to 96 from the 1140s to the 1250s. Averaged, about 28 grams a hectoliter in 961-1120 and 47 in 1141-1260. How they were turned into silver is not given, and no Song rate of copper cash to silver and no Song day wage was found.
  • And in the Ming's own reckoning?
    The Ming statutes' table for valuing stolen goods, after a ruling of 1497, puts a shi of rice at 25 strings of the state's paper money and a tael of silver at 80; it is in What did fruit cost?, and gives no other good of the basket.
  • What did rice cost in the late Ming?
    By reign, on the same historian's averages: 0.584 tael a shi in 1522-1566, 0.638 in 1573-1620, 0.927 in 1621-1627 and 1.159 in 1628-1644.
  • What did Beijing's officials pay in 1615?
    The Ministry of Works' handbook of 1615 priced wheat flour at 0.008 tael a jin, glutinous rice at 1 tael a shi, sesame oil at 0.028 a jin, firewood at 0.125-0.35 tael a hundred jin and coarse white cotton cloth at 0.094 a zhang; other books of the time give salt at 0.006-0.007 a jin and coal at 0.1-0.12 a hundred jin. These are the prices the government paid in the capital, not shop prices. No price of ordinary rice is in the list.
  • What was a laborer paid?
    The 1615 handbook paid the ministry's hired craftsmen and laborers from 0.03 to 0.07 tael a day. An estimate for an unskilled building laborer in the Lower Yangzi, not Beijing, is 0.036 tael a day in 1530-1549, 0.040 in 1550-1599 and 0.043 in 1600-1649.

Songjiang, 1670-1690

  • What did the goods cost?
    A writer of Songjiang, by Shanghai, recorded prices year by year, in silver or copper cash. New rice was 0.63 tael a shi in the autumn of 1672, the usual price for some years after; white rice 2 taels in the dear summer of 1680, 0.85 in 1682 and about 0.9 in 1684. Soybeans ran usually a little below rice, a shi of beans worth 8 or 9 dou of rice: 0.6-0.7 tael a shi in 1682, about 1 in 1684. In 1682 wheat was 530 cash a shi, barley 250, and yuanmai, a grain he lists apart from both, 350. In 1684 the finest biao cotton cloth was about 0.2 tael a bolt and the coarse 0.13-0.15. Salt, a coastal salt-making district's, ran about 0.01 tael a jin from 1651, 0.032 in the long rains of 1683 and 0.006-0.007 in 1688; firewood of 14 or 15 jin cost 0.006-0.007 tael in 1688; pork was 0.025 tael a jin in 1673 and 0.05 in 1680.
  • And a day's wage?
    The writer records none, and no laborer's wage of Songjiang in these years was found. The nearest is the Lower Yangzi estimate: 0.055 tael a day in 1650-1699.

Beijing, 1736-1760

  • What did Beijing's officials pay in 1736?
    On the Ministry of Works' list settled in 1736: wheat flour 0.014 tael a jin, glutinous rice 2.52 a shi, bean flour 0.0135 a jin, salt 0.012 a jin, sesame oil 0.044 a jin, firewood 0.162 or 0.3 a hundred jin, coal 0.26 or 0.27 a hundred jin, and coarse white cotton cloth 0.09 a zhang. Again these are the government's prices, and the list has no ordinary rice.
  • What did a laborer earn?
    The same list paid its laborers 75 copper cash a day, which its historian turns into 0.094 tael at the 800 cash a tael a memorial of 1737 reports for the capital. The Zhili regulation of 1769 averages 0.077 tael a day for unskilled construction labor in the Beijing districts (What did a skilled craftsman earn against a laborer?); a modern estimate for a Beijing building laborer of the 1760s is 0.0789 tael.
  • And a modern reconstruction of the whole basket?
    Economic historians priced a basket in Beijing about 1750, in grams of silver: sorghum 0.48 a kg, beans 0.84, meat or fish 2.04, cotton cloth 6.14 (a meter, the table says), lamp oil 3.3, fuel 11.2 a million BTU. Their figures are not records: they carried Beijing's shop prices of the early 1900s back by Zhili's wholesale grain prices, priced rice by the Yangzi delta's rice, meat and oils by wheat flour, and held cloth at one price. Their year-by-year file, in grams of silver, is in the table below.

Suzhou and the Lower Yangzi, 1750-1799

  • What did rice cost?
    A Jiangnan writer recalled rice at ten-odd cash a sheng in the 1720s and 1730s; 35-36 in the insect famine of 1755; then 14-15 cash as the normal price; 56-57 in the drought of 1785; and after that, famine or harvest, 27-28 to 34-35 cash. At 760-840 cash a tael, rates he gives for the early and middle Qianlong years (in the next question), 14-15 cash a sheng is about 1.7 to 2.0 taels a shi; 27-35 cash is about 1.9 to 2.7 taels at the 1,300-1,400 of 1796, and 3.0 to 4.4 at the 800-900 of his youth. He names no other good of the basket.
  • What did a laborer earn?
    The Lower Yangzi estimate is 0.064 tael a day in 1700-1749 and 0.071 in 1750-1799. Another study set Suzhou's laborer at 0.09 tael in 1700, close to the cloth calenderers', strong men paid by the piece, whose day it reckons at 0.0994 tael in 1730 and 0.1144 in 1772. The first estimate's author finds the second's Lower Yangzi wages of the 1700s thirty per cent higher than his own, and its unskilled wages closer to a skilled worker's. The government's regulated wages of 1769-1795 set an unskilled construction worker in Jiangsu at 0.040 tael a day.

  • What did a laborer earn?
    The one series is a rural fuel store's near Beijing: 95 cash a day in 1820 and 99 in 1822, the year's average, with food. Canton, 1835

  • What did the goods cost?
    A list printed in Canton in 1835, in taels: rice up to 3 a picul; wheat flour 0.04 to 0.06 a catty; salted fish 0.05 to 0.16 a catty; salt 0.02 to 0.04 a catty; firewood 0.1 to 0.18 a picul, charcoal 1.0 and coal 0.24. It gives no soybeans, oil or cloth by the length.
  • A room, and a day's wage?
    A house of two rooms, large enough for five, let for $1.50 a month; the poorest lived in hovels or boats at a dollar a month or less. A laborer in the fields or a workshop, a porter or a menial was paid one mace, a tenth of a tael, about fourteen cents, a day.

Beijing, 1900-1919

  • What did the grains cost?
    Beijing's retail prices, by decade, in dollars a hundred catties: flour 5.73 and 5.78; wheat 3.82 and 4.30; millet 4.46 and 4.92; corn flour 3.08 and 3.56; bean flour 5.16 and 5.68; rice 5.76, in 1913-1919 only. A survey of 1918-1919 put the market price of rice at $10 or $12 a hundred catties, twice the series, its grade not said.
  • A room, fuel and a day's wage?
    In a study of 195 families, with incomes of $30 to $269 a year, living some seven miles outside the city, rents averaged $5 to $12 a year, and fuel enough to keep a family from freezing, bought rather than gathered, $6 a year. An unskilled construction worker was paid 19.1 to 27.2 cents a day, food included, averaging 23.4 cents in 1900-1909 and 22.2 in 1910-1919.
