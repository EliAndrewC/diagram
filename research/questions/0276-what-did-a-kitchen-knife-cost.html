What did a kitchen knife cost?

The setting's price list guesses what an ordinary and a fine kitchen knife cost in Rokugan, and weighs a price by how many days of a laborer's or a craftsman's work it would take. The findings below give what the historical record holds: what kitchen knives cost in Edo-period Japan and a cleaver in Qing China, set beside the nearest day wages found.

Two Japanese price lists were found. In 1851 the knife wholesalers of Edo listed their cooking knives at between about 0.7 and 19 monme of silver a knife at the reduced prices, the plain vegetable knife cheapest and the best thin-bladed cooking knife dearest; in 1665 a charge set in Mimasaka province put a smith's kitchen knife at 8 monme. No price was found for a knife by a named maker, and no count of the kitchen knives a smith made in a day. In Beijing a one-jin kitchen cleaver was priced at 0.1 tael of silver in 1736.

Edo's wholesale prices, 1851

  • Edo's tool and blade wholesalers priced their kitchen knives by kind and grade.
    In 1851 the Edo guild of wholesalers in carpenters' tools and forged blades filed a list of its prices cut, dated the ninth month, each item at its old price in black ink and its reduced price in red. These are wholesalers' prices, not what a household paid at the counter. The prices, in monme of silver for ten knives:

  • Two readings of the handwriting stand under that table.
    The list does not print its unit beside each knife. A note "for ten blades" stands beside the first price of the first cooking knife; the nearest other unit notes stand beside the first small knife above it and beside the needles below the knives, so this record reads every knife between as priced by the ten; it is a reading of where the note stands, not a line of the list. The third knife's name is written in a cursive hand that the library's machine reading prints as "two-monme knife"; this record reads it as the deba, the heavy fish knife, a reading of the handwriting that may be wrong.
  • What was a day's pay in Edo in 1851?
    No laborer's wage of Edo dated 1851 was found; a record of the customs of 1837-1853 gives an Edo laborer's 300 mon a day, without a year (What did a skilled craftsman earn against a laborer?). The nearest unskilled wage is Kyoto's of the same year: 3.37 g of silver a day, about 1.08 monme, from the Mitsui house's accounts; a wage study finds Edo's laborers paid at broadly Kyoto's level from 1818. The nearest craftsman's wage is an Edo carpenter's: 4 monme a day for a top hand and 3.75 for an ordinary one in 1854, three years later, with 1.2 monme for meals; a price table gives a flat 7.5 monme a day, meals included, from 1853 to 1860.
  • How many days of pay was a knife?
    At wholesale, a middle vegetable knife was about three-quarters of a Kyoto laborer's day, a middle sashimi knife about four days, and the best thin-bladed knife about 18 days, or about five days of an Edo carpenter's top rate of 1854. This is this record's arithmetic, and a shop's price would be higher.

A provincial charge, 1665

  • A smith's kitchen knife was charged 8 monme in Mimasaka in 1665.
    A register of craftsmen's charges set in the eighth month of 1665, kept in a record of Mimasaka province, names three smiths and lists their work: a sword, tested on a body, 60 monme of silver; a pair of razors 3.2; another knife, large or small, 5.6; a kitchen knife 8 apiece. Smiths other than those three were to agree prices below these, so for every other smith in the province they are ceilings.
  • What was a day's pay then?
    No wage of 1665 was found, and none of Mimasaka or its neighbor Okayama in those years. The nearest are of Kyoto in the 1650s. In the Joo era (1652-1655), the years of its kitchen account of 1653, the Zen temple Myoshinji paid its carpenters 1 monme 8 bu of silver, wage and rice allowance together, about twelve years before the register; the historian who reports it does not write "a day", and this record reads it as a day's pay because the temple's accounts count carpenters' work by the man-day. The temple's account of its bathhouse, built in 1656, paid carpenters about 1.7 to 1.8 monme a man-day, wage, meal money and share together, and day laborers about 0.8, by this project's arithmetic from its totals.
  • And further back?
    Hired day laborers at the works on Osaka Castle were paid 0.8 to 1 monme a day at the quarries in 1620, and in 1621 the rate was fixed at 1 monme in Osaka and 0.5 at Fushimi; in 1628 the castle's day-labor accounts paid 1.2 and 0.8 monme a man for two of their jobs. These are wages of the shogun's great works, about forty years before the register. All these wages and the register's prices fall between the first minting of Keicho silver in 1601 and of Genroku silver in 1695.
  • How many days of pay was the knife?
    About four and a half days of the 1653 carpenter's pay and about ten of the 1656 laborer's, by this project's arithmetic, across a gap of nine to twelve years and from Kyoto to a province.

The noted makers, and the work of a smithy

  • What did a knife by a noted maker cost?
    No price was found for a knife of Sakai (the city of the tobacco-knife guild below), Seki, Takefu or Tosa by name.
  • Sakai's tobacco-knife smiths were a licensed guild.
    In 1730 the Sakai tobacco-knife smiths were allowed to stamp their knives with a guild mark; the guild had 31 shares and paid 30 ryo a year for the license, and levied 1 rin a knife to run its hall. The guild's own rules of 1761 put the levy at 3 rin a knife. The two sources do not say whether the levy rose between the two dates or one of them is wrong.
  • How many knives did a smith make in a day?
    The one figure found is a cap. The Sakai guild's rules of 1761 allowed a smith working at night 21 large, 27 middle or 30 small tobacco knives a day, and any more to be counted as daytime work; the historian who quotes them reads this as a limit on night work, and believes there was a daytime limit too but says it cannot now be known. No count was found for kitchen knives.
  • Takefu's domain taxed sickles and vegetable knives by the hundred thousand.
    Takefu's sickle smiths worked under a guild of bellows-shares, each share the right to work a forge: 72 smithies in 1797, 111 in 1850 and 129 in 1866, with 151 shares from 1853, a share selling for 300 monme of silver in 1814 and 600 in 1842. About 1865-1868 the Fukui domain taxed 807,000 sickles and vegetable knives, a count the prefectural history does not divide by town, and in 1864-1868 the town's sickles and vegetable knives sold outside the domain came to 20,000 ryo. The tax and the sales count sickles and vegetable knives together, so they give no price of a knife and no smith's output in a day.
  • Who did the work besides the smith?
    In Sakai about 1757, a historical geographer's summary of another scholar's work says, the cutlery trade was carried by smiths, grinders and handle-makers, and the smiths also acted as wholesalers. In a petition of 1757 the Sakai tobacco-knife smiths swore to raise no quarrel with their forge helpers, middlemen and provincial wholesalers, trades the city history calls closely tied to them; by the 1850s the polishers and middlemen were in repeated dispute over raised pay, a memorandum of 1855 records. In Mimasaka in 1665 eight polishers were named as a trade apart, with their own charges, but those charges are for swords and polearms only. No handle-maker for kitchen knives was found.
  • Who sharpened a kitchen knife, and for how much?
    The Morisada manko, a record of the 1830s to the 1850s, says a polisher sharpened short swords, kitchen knives, small knives and saw teeth, and was not allowed to polish swords, which were a separate craft's work. It gives no fee, and no sharpener's fee for a kitchen knife was found. The one polishing rate found near the Mimasaka register's years is for swords: in 1662 the Uwajima domain's fixed prices for craft work set the polishing fee at 6 bu a sun of blade for an old blade and 8 for a new one, the page not saying which bu.

A cleaver in China

  • A kitchen cleaver was priced at 0.1 tael in Beijing in 1736.
    The official price list the Ministry of Works settled in 1736 priced a kitchen cleaver weighing one jin at 0.1 tael of silver, and the Qing statute book prices a storehouse knife and a kitchen cleaver of one jin alike at 0.1 tael. The same list paid a laborer 75 copper cash a day and building craftsmen 154 cash. At the 800 cash to the tael that a memorial of 1737 reports for Beijing, the historian's own conversion, the cleaver is about a laborer's day and about half a craftsman's.
  • And late in the Ming?
    About 0.05 tael for a cutting knife, from the Wanli reign's records as the same historian tabulates them, at second hand. The Ming Ministry of Works' handbook of 1615 paid its hired craftsmen and laborers between 0.03 and 0.07 tael a day, the nearest wage found: the historian names no class for either end, and the knife price comes from another book of the same Wanli reign, so the gap is not known closely. So the knife was about two-thirds of a day at the highest rate and about one and two-thirds at the lowest.
