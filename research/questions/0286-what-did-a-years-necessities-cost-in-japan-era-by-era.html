What did a year's necessities cost in Japan, era by era?

Rokugan's money rules weigh a price against what a laborer earned and spent in a year. The findings below give, era by era from the 1620s to the 1860s, what the historical record holds for the prices the records give for a year's necessities in the cities of Edo-period Japan, in the money and the measure each record counts them in.

Four published series price the year's goods in Japanese cities. The account books of the Mitsui merchant house give rice, salt, firewood and lamp oil in Kyoto in 1741-1762 and from 1791, and in Edo and Osaka from 1818, with wheat in some years from the 1790s, charcoal in Edo and Osaka, and a laborer's day wage in Kyoto and Edo. An economic historian's file gives rice in every decade, labeled Osaka's in 1600-1650 and Osaka's from 1701, and recorded prices of soybeans, barley and cloth from the 1720s, lamp oil from the 1740s and edible oil from the 1800s; it names Osaka for its rice and edible oil, and for the other goods names a price history without a city. A study of prices in 1600-1650, which that file names as its Osaka source, prices rice, soybeans, salt, oil and cloth, and a study of rice prices gives Osaka's rice market year by year from 1701. Every price is in silver monme. No series prices fish, beans other than soybeans, buckwheat or millets before 1879, or a room's rent in Kyoto or Osaka. The nearest rent found is Edo's, in What did it cost to rent a room or a house?

How the figures were taken

  • Every figure below is an era's mean, this record's arithmetic.
    Each is the mean of every figure the series holds in the era, each weighted alike, with the count of figures and the lowest and highest. The Mitsui books give a spring and an autumn figure a year; the other three files a figure a year.
  • The Mitsui books count in silver, even in Edo.
    Every Mitsui price is in silver monme, as the house's account books recorded it, in all three cities.
  • Mitsui's "mugi" is read as wheat.
    The word could mean barley or wheat; the file's transcriber reads it as wheat, because wheat is the likelier grain in a city market and, by the weight of a measure of barley, the price would be implausibly cheap beside rice. The Osaka sheet heads the column white wheat; the Edo sheet asks whether it is wheat or barley.
  • Only the recorded prices of the economic historian's file are used.
    The file fills the years and goods with no record by estimates, and marks each estimate; this record leaves every marked figure out. Its wage is a servant's year, not a day's pay, so it is not given here.
  • The coins changed within the eras.
    A monme was a weight of silver coin, and the coin was recoined several times, so a monme of one era held less silver than a monme of another. What each era's monme, ryo and mon held in silver is in How much silver was a monme, a ryo and a bu, coinage by coinage?

1620-1650, and the years of the recoinages, 1690-1739

  • What did the goods cost in 1620-1650?
    Rice 33.5 monme a koku (20.6-59.4, 31 years); soybeans 31.0 a koku (22.2-40, 17 years); salt 1.08 a to, a tenth of a koku (0.85-1.54, 5 years); oil, not said whether for lamps or food, 2.41 a sho (1.7-4.4, 14 years); white cloth 2.86 a tan (2-3.8, 16 years); bleached cotton cloth 10.0 a tan (8-13.5, 3 years). The study prices no wheat or barley, and fuel only by units a year's basket cannot use: pine by the 9 by 9 cm and coal by the sack, which alone it says it cannot convert. No laborer's day wage of these years was found.
  • What did rice cost, 1690-1739?
    By decade, in monme a koku: 1690s 54.2; 1700s 71.2; 1710s 106.5; 1720s 47.9; 1730s 53.8, ten years each. From 1701 these are Osaka's rice market of the rice study, year for year; for the 1690s the file does not say which record it used. Barley, recorded in five years of each of the 1720s and 1730s, averaged 0.19 and 0.38 monme a kg; soybeans are recorded once, in 1725, at 0.36 a kg, and cloth in 1725 and 1726, at 4.2 and 4.3 a tan.
  • The silver coin was recoined again and again inside this era.
    Silver of 64 percent fineness was issued in 1695, baser silver in 1706, 1710 and 1711, silver of 80 percent fineness in 1714, and baser silver once more in 1736; coins of six finenesses passed side by side until the end of 1722. The dear rice of the 1710s falls in the years the basest of them was issued and passed.
  • And in the Mitsui books, 1710-1716?
    In Edo, six figures of 1710-1716: rice 103 monme a koku (69.9-162), salt 41.4 a koku, lamp oil 430 a koku, firewood 3.43 and charcoal 9.87 for 10 kan, a day laborer 1.75. Kyoto's one figure of 1713: rice 151.5, salt 29.3, lamp oil 878, firewood 3.82, a laborer 1.19. Osaka's of 1713: rice 135.9, lamp oil 773, firewood 5.43, charcoal 4.84.

  • Kyoto 1745-1754 is the era of the record's Kyoto basket.
    What did a Kyoto laborer's year of goods cost? gives that basket and its prices as its authors printed them; its salt is in What did salt cost? and its firewood in What did charcoal cost?. What the Mitsui books add here is the rice and lamp oil a koku in the table above.
  • Kyoto's salt from 1791 is doubtful.
    The Kyoto books' salt falls from 15.5-18.5 monme a koku in 1758-1762 to 5.7-7 in 1791-1793, and stays well below Edo's and Osaka's in the same years: about two-fifths of them in 1818-1829, a sixth to three-tenths in 1830-1859. This record reads it as a change of the measure or the grade of salt, not a fall in price, a reading the printed table was not read to confirm; the figures marked * are not used for a price.

  • The file's one cloth figure of the 1860s is left out.
    It is 133 monme a tan in 1867, about seventeen times the 1850s' figures; nothing in the file explains it.
  • Two cloth prices of the 1840s disagree.
    The economic historian's file's cloth, which it heads linen and places in no city, runs 6 to 9 monme a tan in 1830-1844. The Kyoto basket's authors priced its cloth from an Osaka price of the 1840s at 5.83 g of silver a meter, about 19.9 monme a tan of 10.6 m at their 3.11 g a monme, this record's arithmetic, more than twice as dear; which grade of cloth each prices is not said. What did cloth and clothing cost? gives the range of cotton's prices.
