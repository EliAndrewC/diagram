How our maps draw sloping ground and hillsides

Our maps are deliberately not topographic: they draw no contours, no spot heights and no hillshade. They show that the ground slopes by what the slope does instead - a hill drawn as a picture of a hill, terraces on steep ground, and water that runs from the high side of a village to the low - because shaded relief misreads on a map drawn from straight above, and the slope can be read from things a village really had.

  • High ground is drawn as a picture of a hill.
    A hill is a mound drawn as stacked rings, each a shade darker toward the top, with short hachures around its foot and trees scattered over it. It follows the oldest way maps showed relief, a picture of the hill rather than lines of survey. The pictorial mound is a map drawing convention.
  • Terraced paddies are drawn only where the slope is steep.
    There they are long, narrow, curved strips along the contour with a riser between them. A gently tilted village is drawn without terraces, since a real valley floor shows no visible steps, and terraces there would make it read as mountainous. That steep ground alone shows terraces rests on general reading; no source is cited. How the terraces themselves are drawn is at How our maps draw terraced and valley paddies (tanada, yachida).
  • On gentle ground, the water shows which way the land falls.
    The source and the pond sit on the high side, the ditches that share out the water fan in from there, and a drainage ditch leaves the low corner and runs off the map. Wet rice needs water out as well as in, so a drain is a feature the reader expects anyway, and it doubles as the downhill cue. That the water marks the slope, and that a reader expects a drain, both rest on general reading; no source is cited. How the drains are drawn is at How our maps draw field drains (akusuiro).
  • The maps lay no shaded relief across the ground.
    Shaded relief is conventionally lit from the upper left of the sheet, so on a map drawn as if looking straight down it reads as a light source - a sun in the northwest - rather than as rising ground. It fights the straight-down view the rest of the sheet is drawn in, and it misleads. The tones on a hill's own rings belong to the picture of the hill, not to a light falling across the sheet. Leaving out shaded relief is this project's own drawing decision, a map drawing convention.

The rule the map follows: no contour lines, no spot heights and no shaded relief are drawn on any map. High ground is a pictorial mound; the fall of the land is read from the water - source and pond high, ditches fanning downhill, a drainage ditch leaving the low corner off the map - and terraced paddies are drawn only where the slope is steep enough for a reader to expect risers.
