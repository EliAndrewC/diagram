How our maps draw fire watch towers and firefighting gear (hinomi yagura)

Our maps draw a walled town's fire watch as one tower or one watch house with a ladder on its roof, and a city's as a few towers spread among its commoner quarters. A town with no wall, an unwalled seat of government such as the town round an intendant's office, and a village draw none. Nothing else of a town's defense against fire is drawn: the fire-water tubs are too small to show at a town's scale, no page we read puts a fire pond in a street, and firebreaks are left off (How our maps leave out firebreaks (hiyokechi)).

  • Only a walled town or a city draws a fire watch.
    This follows this project's reading that a packed core behind a wall is where a fire grows great and is kept in, and that the open ground of an unwalled town breaks a fire - both GUESSES, since no page we read says either.
  • A village or hamlet draws no fire-watch tower, fire ladder or fire pond.
    No page we read shows a fixed fire apparatus in an Edo-period village, so leaving it off is a GUESS.
  • An unwalled seat draws no fire tower.
    By this project's GUESS its defense against fire lay in the fireproof storehouses of the intendant's compound, the headman's house and the merchants' houses. A small bell frame at the intendant's compound is the one thing that could defensibly be added, and leaving it off is as accurate.
  • A walled town draws one fire watch, as a tower or as a watch house with a ladder and bell - a knob.
    The form is chosen at random for each settlement between the two the history shows: a free-standing fire-watch tower, or a watch house carrying a ladder and bell on its roof. By Edo's ratio of one tower to about ten wards, a town of a few wards falls below one tower, and in Edo a ward without a tower had a ladder fitted on its watch house instead - which is why the watch-house form is one of the two.
  • A provincial city draws at least two fire towers.
    Edo kept many, so each tower on the map stands for a quarter's whole fire watch - a map drawing convention. In practice that is one tower amid each major commoner quarter: three for a city with laborer, servant and merchant quarters to cover.
  • A fire watch stands in a commoner quarter, never in the samurai ward.
    It stands among the laborer and merchant blocks.
  • A walled town's fire tower stands clear of the rampart.
    It stands apart from the guard towers on the rampart itself.
  • A fire tower is sited on an open gap with sightlines, not beside a ward gate as the townsmen's towers stood.
    It is placed last, into a cleared block or an open seam the finished map already has, so adding it moves nothing else. The townsmen's tower stood beside a ward gate; siting it on an open gap instead is a map drawing convention.
  • No two fire towers stand within about 690 ft of each other.
    That is one watch radius, the distance a tower is taken to watch over: its own neighborhood of rooftops. This is this project's reading, a GUESS.
  • Each fire tower stands amid the district it watches.
    A floor on the distance between towers is not enough by itself: two towers a generous gap apart can both stand on one side of the center and leave the city's largest mass of roofs with no tower amid it. So each commoner house inside the walls is assigned to its nearest tower, and each tower stands near the middle of the houses assigned to it: no farther from their center than 0.9 of the district's radius, or one watch radius (about 690 ft) if that is larger.
  • A fire tower keeps at least 15 ft of open ground all round, and the same from every wellhead.
    An open braced tower's footing and ladder stand off its neighbors' eaves, and it must never block the court a quarter draws its water from.
  • A fire tower is drawn as an open braced frame with a red bell.
    The open legs are history: a town's tower was not allowed to have its legs boarded in, and was kept lower than the shogunate's brigade towers. The bracing and the bell's red are a map drawing convention, so that the tower reads apart from the solid guard towers on the rampart: a fire tower stands among the roofs it watches, not on the wall.
  • A fire tower is drawn about 26 ft square, a map drawing convention.
    No page we read gives the size of a tower's base.
  • A town's fire-water tubs are not drawn.
    A corner tub is at most some 3 1/2 ft across by this project's GUESS, since no page we read gives its size. That is about one pixel at a town map's 3 ft to the pixel, so it is left off - a map drawing convention of omission. The watch house is at How our maps draw the night watch and ward gates (kido); a building plan's own tubs are at How our maps draw fire water and the fireproof storehouses (dozō).
  • No fire pond is drawn.
    No page we read describes one in a town street.
