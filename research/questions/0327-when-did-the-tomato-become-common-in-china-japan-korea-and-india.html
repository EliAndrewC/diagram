When did the tomato become common in China, Japan, Korea and India?

Rokugan's notes mention no tomato. The findings below give what the historical record holds for it in China, Japan, Korea and India.

The tomato is an American plant. In all four countries it was written down or drawn long before it was much eaten: in the Wanli reign in China, by 1668 in Japan, in 1614 in Korea and about 1801 in India. On this project's arithmetic, China took about 310 to 380 years to plant it widely, Japan about 220 to 280 years to take it up as food, and Korea about 320 years; India's sources disagree. Other new crops are in when tobacco was first grown, when the sweet potato was first grown and when the white potato was first grown.

China

  • The tomato is first recorded in China in the Wanli reign (1573-1620).
    The modern accounts name the Qunfang pu, a Ming book on plants, under the name 蕃柿 (fan-shi, "foreign persimmon"). That book was not read here. A newspaper article quotes it describing a plant tied up on a frame, "most worth looking at", from which the article concludes the tomato came in as an ornamental.
  • Was it cultivated from the start?
    Only as an ornamental, on these accounts, and no source read gives a first year of planting. It was "only sporadically grown near cities" for a long time.
  • When did China begin to grow it on a large scale? The modern accounts say the twentieth century.
    Three accounts agree on the outline and differ on the date of the turn:
    • Liu Yanchun of the Shanghai Botanical Garden has planting for food begin in the northeast, north and center in the 1930s, and large-area planting as a national vegetable in the 1950s.
    • The historian Li Xinsheng has it spreading in the Republic mainly around big cities with little grown, and its heyday only after 1949.
    • A newspaper writer, Ai Lisi, has it grown even in the late Qing and early Republic only around Beijing, Qingdao, Shanghai and Guangzhou, for Western restaurants.
    No figure for area or tonnage before the 1950s was found.
  • When did commoners begin eating it? Two accounts differ by a generation.
    Li says tomato with egg swept the country only after 1949. Ai Lisi says it was already a popular dish in the 1930s.
  • Do older foreign witnesses contradict the late date?
    They show the plant present, and do not show it common:
    • An unsigned article from Canton in 1835 lists tomatoes among vegetables "to be found in most parts of the country", with no word of how much.
    • The missionary Justus Doolittle, writing of Fuzhou in 1865, reports no large tomato, and a cherry-sized one growing wild and not eaten by the Chinese.
    • F. H. King priced tomatoes in Shanghai's Boone Road market on 6 April 1900 at 5.16 cents a pound, in US money, a little above eggplant at 4.30.

Japan's first dates are later than China's and come from artists' sketches.

Japan

  • The earliest dated record is a painter's sketch of 1668.
    The painter Kano Tan'yu sketched a tomato, under the name 唐なすび (to-nasubi, "Chinese eggplant"), in 1668. Kano Tsunenobu sketched fruit on the branch in 1703, about the size of a modern mini tomato, as おらんだなすび (oranda-nasubi, "Dutch eggplant"). Japanese Wikipedia gives the first written description as the Yamato honzo of 1709, which calls it 唐柿 (to-gaki, "Chinese persimmon").
  • When did it arrive? The modern accounts disagree.
    None gives its evidence:
    • A national farm research agency says the 17th century, from Europe by way of China to Nagasaki.
    • Japanese Wikipedia says the Dutch brought it to Nagasaki "in the early 17th century (around the Kanbun era)". That phrase does not agree with itself, since Kanbun 8 is 1668.
    • A vegetable-market agency says the beginning of the 18th century, which the 1668 sketch contradicts.
  • A "red eggplant" in a farm manual of about 1629 to 1654 is not the tomato.
    The Seiryoki lists 赤茄子 (aka-nasu, "red eggplant") among its eggplants. The same word named the tomato in the Meiji period, so a later reader could mistake the entry.
  • Through the Edo period (to 1868) the tomato was grown to look at.
    Three modern accounts call it ornamental, one also medicinal. No source read gives a first year of planting, though a sketch of fruit on the branch means someone grew it.
  • When did Japan take it up as food? The accounts run from 1887 to after 1945.
    The position that it was no great part of Japanese cooking even when available holds for the Edo period and the first Meiji years. After that the accounts differ:
    • Nine varieties came from the West in 1868, but their smell put eaters off.
    • Japanese Wikipedia says it spread as a vegetable from about 1887.
    • The research agency says the Meiji imports were too sour and did not take, and that only the American Ponderosa of the Showa period, from 1926, began to settle it into the diet.
    • The vegetable-market agency says food demand rose with American varieties in the 20th century.
    • Japanese Wikipedia adds that demand leapt after the Second World War.
    No figure for area or consumption was found for any of these dates.

Korea's first record is the earliest of the four to carry a year, and rests on a modern identification.

Korea

  • The earliest Korean record is usually read as a persimmon-like plant of 1614.
    Yi Su-gwang's encyclopedia of 1614 describes 南蠻柿 (nam-man-si, "southern-barbarian persimmon"), a herb fruiting in autumn and tasting like a persimmon, whose seed an envoy brought back from China. The text does not say tomato. That is the reading of modern writers.
  • Was it cultivated?
    The food historian Ko Young says it was not seriously cultivated, and reports the scholar Yi Gyu-gyeong (born 1788) treating it as a weed easily confused with the chili. No source read gives a first year of planting.
  • Common eating is reported in the 1930s.
    Ko Young says tomato farming got under way in the 20th century with varieties such as Prince of Wales. A newspaper he quotes said in September 1935 that Koreans too had recently come to eat many tomatoes.

India's dates come through one blog, which quotes its witnesses and is the only account read.

India

  • The earliest reliable Indian reference named is a cookbook of 1801.
    A food-history blog says it holds a recipe for "foreign eggplant" soup, wilayati baigan. The blog judges the plant perhaps noticeable by the end of the 18th century.
  • When it was first cultivated is not stated.
    No source read gives a first year of planting in India. The blog's earliest word on cultivation is an 1865 catalog from Bombay.
  • When did it become common? The witnesses the blog quotes disagree.
    None of these works was read here:
    • The botanist William Roxburgh, in 1832, is reported as recording it commonly used throughout the subcontinent.
    • George Watt in 1889 has it still chiefly grown for Europeans, with Bengalis and Burmans putting it in sour curries.
    • Duthie and Fuller, in reports of 1882 to 1893, say it was coming into favor with Indians as food.
    • The blog itself concludes that it took several decades to be widely grown and eaten, and calls it ubiquitous by the late 19th century.
