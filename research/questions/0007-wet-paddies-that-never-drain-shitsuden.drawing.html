How our maps draw wet paddies (shitsuden)

Our maps draw a wet paddy as a rice plot tinted blue-green where the other plots are green. The tint marks the ground, not the season: a wet paddy stays waterlogged even when no rice is growing, so it is a kind of plot, not a moment in one. It goes on the low plots at the foot of the field, along the drain that carries the water away, which we take to be the wettest ground.

  • Why does the tint go on the plots along the field's drain?
    In a traditional terraced paddy the water passes from plot to plot, and no line can be drawn between irrigating and draining, so we take the plots at the foot, on the drain, to be the wettest ground. That siting is our own inference from how the water moves, not something a source states.
  • Which plots wear the tint is a drawing convention, not a survey.
    A polder or terraced field tints every low plot: 22 of 22 on the Enokida map, 40 of 40 on the Tanada map. A comb field gives each plot of its lowest row, the ones whose foot lies on the drain, a 45% chance of the tint, and leaves green any plot of that row that tapers to a point.
  • On a comb map, blue is a sample of the wet ground, not the whole of it.
    The Inashiro map shows 8 blue plots; the plots of its lowest row left green are as wet as the blue ones. Only the random draw, or a plot's pointed shape, left them untinted.
  • The blue shows wetter ground, not deeper water or water seen between the plants.
    No page we read says whether a paddy's water shows between the rice plants until their leaves close over it, so the tint rests on the ground being wetter, not on what a viewer would see.
