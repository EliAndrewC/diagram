What did a shrine's other sanctuaries, halls and finishes cost?

Rokugan's prices are counted in koku and its smaller coins, and the setting's money rules judge a price by how many days of a craftsman's or a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give what the historical record holds for the other forms a small shrine could take, its halls, its roofs and its finishes in Edo-period Japan, with the Shibasaki and Tanashi payments set beside the nearest day wages the record gives.

Most of these were not found priced. Every small sanctuary whose cost was found is of the nagare-zukuri style, and no cost was found for a kasuga-zukuri or shinmei-zukuri sanctuary, a stone hokora, timber by species, red or black finishes, or openwork transoms. What survives is a village headman's diary, which records the rebuilding of the Hachiman shrine of Shibasaki, in the Tama district of Musashi, and notes single payments for its roofs and its worship hall in 1840 and 1841, and one carver's contract for a small sanctuary of 1858.

Other forms of sanctuary

  • What did a kasuga-zukuri or shinmei-zukuri sanctuary cost?
    No cost was found. The small sanctuaries whose building or repair costs were found are all of the nagare-zukuri style (What did a small shrine sanctuary cost to build?).
  • What did a stone hokora cost?
    No cost was found. The nearest stonework priced is a pair of stone lanterns (What did a shrine's stonework cost?).
  • What did a cover-shed cost?
    Only as part of a larger repair: the Mitake shrine's small repair of 1764-1770, about 9.5 ryo, took in the sanctuary, its covering house and a repainting together, and gives no share for the covering house (the sanctuary page). No cost of a cover-shed alone was found.

The worship hall and the offering hall

  • What did a small worship hall cost?
    The one figure found is Shibasaki's of 1840, 11 ryo 2 bu to the carpenter for the building and its joinery, the woodcutters' and sawyers' work included, as one sum with no breakdown (the sanctuary page).
  • What did its doors and screens cost?
    Paid apart, to a joiner of Shimowada. At the end of the first month of 1841 he was given the hall's doors and screens to make as a set, timber included, with 1 ryo paid down; in the fourth month, its twelve doors cut and fitted, the rest of his fee, 2 bu 2 shu, was paid in full. If nothing was paid between the two, the whole came to 1 ryo 2 bu 2 shu; the paper quotes no payment between them and states no whole price.
  • What did an offering hall cost?
    No cost was found.

Timber and roofs

  • What did hinoki, keyaki or sugi timber cost?
    No price was found, by the piece or by volume. At Shibasaki in 1841 cedar and pine fetched from Hachioji were out of stock and very dear, and 1 ryo was sent with two men to buy cedar for the ridge-raising scaffold; that is money carried, not a price. Ten woodcutters' wages and food for the timber cutting of 1840 came to 2 bu and some copper coins, with no count of days.
  • What did a thatched roof take?
    Only its quantity and labor are given. At the end of the eleventh month of 1840, 36 horse-loads of thatch grass were brought from Musashino for the shrine by six levy horses, unpriced. In the first month of 1841, 2 ryo 3 bu for the wages and keep of the helpers on the roofs of both the sanctuary and the worship hall were handed to Shichirozaemon, reckoned on a trimmed thickness of 1 shaku 2 sun (about 36 cm). The paper names neither roof's material; that the grass and the trimmed thickness were for thatch is this project's guess.
  • What did a wooden-shingle roof cost?
    Only one purchase was found. In the ninth month of 1840, the sanctuary's roof finished, the roofer Kyubei was given 3 bu extra and a 2-shu gift on leaving, and eight lots of roof boards cost 2 bu 1 shu and some copper coins. The size of a lot, the roofer's wage and a price by area are not given, and the diary does not call the boards shingles; that they were is this project's guess.
  • What did a copper roof cost?
    Only within a whole repair: about 43 ryo in 1749 for a repair centered on re-roofing a one-bay sanctuary in copper, with no price for the roof alone (the roof page).

Finishes and carving

  • What did vermilion, red ochre or black lacquer cost?
    No price was found.
  • What did relief panels cost?
    3 ryo a panel for the new sanctuary at Shibasaki, paid by a few leading families outside the carpenter's contract; the count of panels is not given (the sanctuary page).
  • What did carved beam ends cost?
    Only within a whole sanctuary's carving. The Tanashi shrine's sanctuary of 1858 is small, its core 4 shaku 3 sun (about 1.3 m) between posts along the ridge and about 1.2 m (3.87 shaku) deep, built wholly of unpainted keyaki; its carving runs over the wall panels, beam-end noses, cambered beams and down to the posts and tie-rails, 147 pieces by the contract and the carving estimate of its building. The carver contracted it all for 135 ryo, a price the shrine calls exceptionally low, the building being paid for by the village headman alone. That is about 0.9 ryo a piece on average, panels and small pieces together.
  • And a large sanctuary's?
    The Kawagoe Hikawa shrine's sanctuary, begun in 1842 and roofed in 1849, wholly of keyaki and carved all over, cost 1,369 ryo by a document of 1847, the roofing apart; its whole cost is not known. The Tanashi shrine says it is said to have cost about 2,000 ryo in all. A sanctuary this large serves only as an upper bound for a small one's carving.
  • What did openwork transoms cost?
    No price was found.

Day wages beside them

  • What was a day's pay at Shibasaki in 1840-1841?
    No wage of the place and years was found. The nearest craftsman's wage read is a carpenter's at Kumehara in 1830, in the Saitama district of the same province, ten years earlier: 2 monme of silver a day and 1 monme more that this project reads as his food allowance. The nearest unskilled wage read is a Kyoto casual laborer's of 1802-1804, about 0.92 monme a day in spring, almost forty years earlier and far to the west.
  • What were the Shibasaki payments in days?
    At 60 monme to the ryo, a rate this project borrows, 1 ryo was 20 days of the Kumehara carpenter, food included, and about 65 days of the Kyoto laborer. So the joiner's 1 ryo down payment was 20 carpenter-days or 65 laborer-days; his last 2 bu 2 shu, about 12 or 41; the roof boards' 2 bu 1 shu, about 11 or 37; the roofer's 3 bu 2 shu of extra and gift, about 18 or 57; and the roofing helpers' 2 ryo 3 bu, 55 or about 180.
  • And the Tanashi carving in 1858?
    The nearest craftsman's wage read is the carpenter's wage of Edo-Tokyo in 1853-1860 by a price table, 0.125 ryo a day, meals included, which the paper reading it takes for an official or agreed rate rather than the market's, at which 135 ryo was 1,080 carpenter-days. No unskilled wage of the 1850s was read; the nearest is the Kyoto laborer's of 1802-1804, about 55 years earlier, at which it was about 8,800 days.
