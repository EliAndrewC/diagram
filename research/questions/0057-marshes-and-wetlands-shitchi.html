Marshes and wetlands (shitchi)

Wet rice began in the marsh: the earliest domesticated rice found in China comes from naturally marshy ground close to rivers. Farmers went on making paddy by draining marsh - behind high dikes in China, and on the low plains of Japan's rivers. Where the draining stopped, the ground stayed wetland, and paddy let go on such ground turns back into it.

Below a village's fields, the wet ground lay where the water came back to the surface. On an alluvial fan, the water that sinks into the gravels springs out again along the fan's toe, so the wet ground there follows the fan rather than the valley. Villages cut thatch and reed from the ground round them; at Edo an inlet was filled to make land for the city, and a Song official proposed a belt of ponds and marsh across China's northern frontier against cavalry. What grows at a marsh's edge is at Reed beds and the marsh's edge (yoshihara), and paddy that stays wet all year at Wet paddies that never drain (shitsuden).

Marsh was the ground wet rice was first made from.

  • In China, a poldered field was land reclaimed from a swamp or a lake behind a high dike.
    Houses stood on the wider parts of the dike, which was usually planted with trees against erosion, and inside it the polder was divided into blocks of fields with drainage channels down the middle. The polder and its dike are described at Polder dikes: what they were made of, how big, and what grew on them.
  • A modern study on the lower Tumen River found that paddy made from marsh goes back to wetland plants when it is abandoned.
    On a lowland plain of the lower Tumen River, where farming turned most of the marsh into paddy, a modern study followed abandoned fields as annual weeds gave way to knotweed and then to tussock sedge, and judged such fields good ground for restoring wetland.

Much of the wet ground below a village's fields lies at the foot of an alluvial fan.

  • The middle of an alluvial fan was poor ground for paddy, because its water ran underground.
    Below the fan's apex, which is steep and small, the river runs underground, so the water table is low. In Japan, the mid-fan was often left as scrub woodland, before the Second World War it became mulberry fields, chiefly in northern Kanto, and after it some became orchards. A government teaching guide to land classification says the same: the mid-fan grows crops that need little water, mulberry and fruit trees.
  • At an alluvial fan's toe, the water that sank into the gravels comes back up as a line of springs.
    The fan's gravels let water through very easily, and the water under the ground comes out as springs at the toe.
  • Villages and paddy have gathered along a fan's spring line since ancient times.
    Water is easy to get there, so settlements and paddy were sited along it.
  • How wide is the wet ground below an alluvial fan?
    As wide as the fan, not as wide as the valley it lies in - this record's reading of where the spring line runs. A fan is laid down in a fan shape with the valley mouth as its apex, and its springs come out along its toe, so the line follows an arc round the fan's own foot. A wetland study says the same from the other side: slope wetlands form at breaks in slope where groundwater comes out, and are often found on the edge of highly permeable fans.
  • Is the water at the toe the water the fields above put in?
    In good part, as this record reads it. On a fan in the Rocky Mountains, the stream seeping into the fan was the main source of its groundwater, and on the Tedori fan in Japan the water standing on the paddies recharges it alongside the river's water. Irrigation engineers know the same relationship: water lost downward from an irrigation scheme raises the water table until the ground waterlogs.

On a river's floodplain, away from the hills, the wet ground lies elsewhere.

  • On a river's floodplain, the wet ground is the backswamp behind the natural levees.
    The slight rises along the channel hold floodwater behind them, where it stands and lays down mud, and a backswamp of fine material forms behind the natural levee. Its edge is set by the levees, again a feature of the ground rather than the width of the valley.
  • In Japan the backswamp was traditionally cleared for paddy.
    It was brought under cultivation and used as paddy, and new fields were made with vigor by draining wetland and reclaiming lakes and ponds.
  • No page we read compares the plants at the wet edge of a diked field with those at the wet foot of an open valley.
    Some low fields sit inside dikes that keep the river out; others lie open at the foot of a valley slope.

At Minuma, the paddies shed their water into a drain at their lowest ground.

  • Japan's farm ministry's modern standard lays a field's interceptor drain along the contours.
    The farm ministry's standard sets the interceptor drain nearly parallel to the contour lines, with a gentle fall, and the collectors that gather its water usually at right angles to them, 200 to 300 m (~656-984 ft) apart. It is the standard for upland fields, not paddy, and says nothing of a fan's toe or of wet ground.
  • When the Minuma reservoir was turned into paddy in the Edo period, the ground along its drain was farmed as wet paddy.
    The supply canals ran along the terrace edge a step above the paddies, and the drain at their lowest ground. Those paddies were poorly drained deep paddies until drainage works made them dry around 1970.
  • Was a reed marsh ever left along a drain before modern times?
    No page we read shows one. The one Edo-period reclamation we read, Minuma's above, farmed the ground beside its drain as wet paddy; that this held elsewhere is this record's reading of one reclaimed marsh, not a rule found.

How much wet ground survived differed between village and city.

  • The wettest paddy never dried out, and could grow no winter crop.
    A wet paddy drains so badly that the water never leaves it and no winter crop can be grown. The deep-mud fukada was called a swamp paddy.
  • Japan's state set about draining its wet paddy from the Meiji period on.
    Such paddy was turned into dry paddy by national drainage works only from the Meiji period onward.
  • A yato valley bottom was easy rice land where it could be drained, and marshy where it could not.
    Spring water was easy to get and floods did little harm, so rice was easily grown once drainage was secured; with little sun and poor drainage it often became marshland.
  • Villages cut their grass and reed as a crop.
    Ground round a settlement was kept as a thatch field, burned every year to keep the forest out and cut for roofing, and reed stems made blinds.
  • Thatch fields, in wetlands among other places, were used in common by the settlement.
    The Japan Thatching Culture Association names reed among the plants cut for thatch, and says the thatch fields, in a settlement, in the hills round it or in wetlands, were used in common as the settlement's common land, its iriai. That a given village's own reed marsh was used that way is this record's interpolation from that general account, as at Reed beds and the marsh's edge (yoshihara); no page we read says it of one village's marsh.
  • Was there wet ground at a town's or a city's edge?
    Little, by this record's reconstruction: a gradient of drainage and land value, from villages that had neither the means nor the reason to drain every wet margin, to towns and cities that commanded corvee-scale engineering and whose outskirts were too valuable to leave wet. No source states the gradient itself.
  • The old city of Suzhou, in China, was threaded with canals that reached out into the countryside.
    The old city had a network of rivers and canals reaching out to the countryside, though no page we read says the fields' ditches emptied into it.
  • At Edo, the Hibiya inlet beside the castle was filled in from 1592.
    The inlet was filled from 1592, and wholly in the early Edo period.
  • The villages that grew a city's vegetables manured them with its night soil.
    The villages that fed a city its greens used its night soil on a large scale. What a town's dry edge carried instead is at Did a town's edge grow vegetables for the town, fed by its night soil? and Why is a town's edge wooded or grassed rather than bare, and with what?
  • Around Osaka, merchants made new fields on the old bed of the Yamato River and the shallows of Osaka Bay.
    The new fields lay on the old bed of the rerouted Yamato River and the shallows of the bay. That the same held around Edo is this record's reading.
  • Where wet ground did survive near a city, what was it?
    Ground with an edge and a purpose, in this record's reading, never a spreading seep below a ditch. A flood basin is land set aside to hold a river's floodwater for a time; a moat, a lotus or fish pond and a deliberate defensive flooding are this record's synthesis.

Wet ground could also be kept, or made, for war.

  • A Northern Song official proposed a belt of ponds, marshes and dikes across the northern frontier, in Hebei, to check the enemy's cavalry.
    He Chengju's memorial would open the Yi River and lead its water east to the sea, over three hundred li east to west, using the ponds and marshes and building dikes to store water for military farm colonies.
  • Bitchu Takamatsu castle stood in low wetland that served as its moat.
    The wetland round the castle formed a natural moat. Besieging it in 1582, Hashiba Hideyoshi ringed it with an embankment to drown it - the "water attack on Takamatsu castle".
  • This record reads flooded paddies round a castle town as a defense, though no page we read says so.
    It reads them as a glacis, open wet ground an attacker had to cross. Wet ground that survives near a fortification is in this reading military ground, not waste.
