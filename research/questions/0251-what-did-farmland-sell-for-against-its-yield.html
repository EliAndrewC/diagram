What did farmland sell for, against what it yielded?

Rokugan's setting notes value a single farm at about 50 koku, and a family's plot leaves its landlord 2.5 koku a year after tax. The findings below give what the historical record holds for what farmland sold for against what it yielded, in Qing China and in Edo Japan.

No page read gives what farmland sold for in years of its net yield, in either country. What survives comes close from two sides. In Qing-era southern Fujian, contracts that sold or pledged the right to a field's rent paid their buyers about 12.6% a year on average, roughly eight years of the rent. In Edo Japan, land passed by pledge rather than by sale, and the lender took the land's yield toward principal and interest; the one valuation from the time in years of net income is for a town lot in central Edo, at twenty years.

In Qing China

In southern Fujian a holder could pledge or sell the rent a field paid, rather than the field, and the contracts let a historian reckon what the buyer earned each year.

  • What did a buyer of a field's rent earn?
    Across 36 contracts from Yongchun county, in southern Fujian, the yearly return averaged about 12.6%. Three paid over 20%, the highest 37.2%; four came near 20%; eleven paid under 10%, five of them between 6.5% and 7.1%. In years of the rent, that is about 8 years on average, and from about 2.7 to 15 years across the range.
  • Was that return after tax?
    In one contract, at least: the seller passed part of the land tax on with the rent he pledged. The study does not say whether the others did.
  • Two worked cases.
    In 1746 a buyer paid 9 taels of silver for 212 jin of rent grain a year, a return of 17.7% once the grain is reckoned as husked rice and valued in copper cash. In 1792 a rent right sold outright, a contract the study elsewhere calls a pledge of land, returned a nominal 7%, reckoned on the assumption that the buyer collected a second rent as large as the one he owed, about 14 years of that rent.
  • Did a field's rent pay as well as lending?
    In this market they were the same thing. The study treats a pledge of rent as a loan, the rent paid as interest, so its mean of 12.6% is a village lending rate, and it overturns the usual belief that village loans ran at 20% or even 30%. At Shicang in southern Zhejiang, by the author's earlier study, the yearly rate on pledges of land fell from the middle of the Jiaqing reign to late in the Guangxu reign, roughly the 1800s: from 30% to 20% where the loan was repaid in cash, and from 20% to 10% where the interest was paid in grain. Another historian, working an example, takes 12% a year as far below the rates usually quoted for village credit, where 36% a year is often used, though such loans carried risks.
  • What did a pledge cost against an outright sale?
    About two-thirds of the price at Shicang, where a pledge and a live sale were the same thing, and usually 60-80% of it for a live sale by several scholars' estimates.

In Songjiang prefecture, near today's Shanghai, a local writer recorded land prices beside their rents through the crises that followed the fall of the Ming.

  • How much did Songjiang land sell for?
    In the middle of the Chongzhen reign, in the 1630s, the best land of Huating and Qingpu counties fetched over ten taels a mu, and Shanghai's best three to five. Early in the Shunzhi reign, the first of the Qing from 1644, rice was dear and buyers competed: land of Huating and Qingpu rented at 1.5-1.6 shi a mu fetched 15-16 taels, and Shanghai land rented at 6-7 dou fetched 3-4.
  • Prices fell below a year's rent when taxes rose.
    Once rice prices eased and the burden of tax and labor service grew, by the early 1660s rice fell to 5-6 qian a shi, the property of a middling household was worth nothing, and the finest land fetched no more than 3 to 5 qian a mu, where the land of these counties was let at over a shi a mu. At those prices land sold for less than one year of its rent.
  • In 1680, one to three years of rent.
    In the spring of 1680, rice being dear, land in the author's county rented at 7 dou a mu rose to 2 taels a mu, and land in Huating and Lou rented at 1.4-1.5 shi to 7-8 taels; the next year rice fell and the rush eased. That summer polished rice sold at 2 taels a shi. At that price the land sold for about 1.4 years of its rent in the author's county and about 2.3-2.9 years in Huating and Lou.
  • What these Songjiang figures leave out.
    They are years of gross rent, before the owner's tax, and the author does not say whether a rent was counted in husked rice or in unhusked grain; a rent in grain was worth less, and the land's price in years of it higher. Each was recorded at a moment of crisis, not in a settled market.
  • How far did land prices move over the dynasty?
    A writer of the Qing records land at 50 to 100 taels a mu in the middle of the Ming, depending on its fertility; one or two taels at the Ming's fall; good land at two or three early in the Qing; four or five under Kangxi; back to the early level under Yongzheng; seven or eight, the best over ten, in his childhood under Qianlong; and over 50 taels fifty years later. He gives no rents, so these prices cannot be set against a yield.

In Edo Japan

The shogunate banned the permanent sale of fields in 1643, and land passed instead by pledge: a farmer sold his fields for a term, or borrowed against them and took them back when he repaid.

  • How was a lender on land paid?
    From the land itself. The lender held the use of the pledged field and applied its yield to principal and interest, so the debt bore no interest of its own.
  • Who paid the land's tax?
    The lender. Making the farmer pay the tax and levies on a pledged field was illegal from 1687. A borrower could stay on as the lender's tenant, working the pledged field while the lender bore its tax. So the lender's return was the field's yield, or its rent, after the tax.
  • What were fields pledged for?
    The printed papers of the Okayasu family of Kumehara, a village of Hyakken district in Saitama county, in Musashi province, include three pledge deeds. In 1700 a landholder of Suka village, witnessed by a kumigashira, a group head, of Kumehara, pledged a holding of paddy, dry field, thicket and house plot of about 1.15 cho for 70 ryo for two years; in 1720 fields of about 4.1 tan went for 24 ryo for two years; and in 1726 about 1.07 tan of lower-grade paddy for 3 ryo for three years. In each, the lender was to pay the tax and levies. That is about 6.1, 5.8 and 2.8 ryo a tan. These are sums lent, not prices, and no deed gives the land's yield, so they cannot be turned into years of yield.
  • What if the borrower could not repay?
    The field could be forfeit. From 1695 the shogunate let the lender keep it if the deed said so; in 1722 it forbade forfeiture and withdrew the ban the next year after large risings over pledged land; and from 1744 it in effect allowed fields to change hands by pledge and forfeiture. The 1726 Kumehara deed has the borrower agree that if the term lapsed unredeemed, the field became the lender's.
  • Local custom said the same.
    A survey of local customs reports that in one district of Echigo land could be mortgaged for up to 10 years but never sold, and that in the villages of two districts of Suruga there was nominally no sale of land: a mortgage was used.
  • Was land priced as so many years of its yield?
    In one of the Edo town office's valuations, a town lot in the central districts let wholly to ground tenants was valued at twenty times its owner's net receipts, the net capitalized at 5%; the valuation stood in for the recorded sale price where that had gone out of date. For farmland, two historians are reported to have shown, from farm records of the Kinai and of Sanuki, that its price equaled the net yield left to the farmer capitalized at the rate of interest; the passage read gives no rate. No page read gives that multiple for Edo-period farmland.

Kinds of land, and the setting's farm

  • Did irrigated land sell for more than dry land?
    No price for either was found. In Kumehara the tax shows the difference: in 1698 upper-grade paddy was taxed at 0.48 koku of rice a tan, on the paddy left after a deduction for flood loss, and middle-grade dry field in money, 90 mon a tan. In China, prices followed a field's fertility, and the rent it bore, which ran higher in Huating and Qingpu than in Shanghai.
  • Did land near a city sell for more?
    No page read says. The one premium recorded in Songjiang, up to half again, was paid for a plot beside one's own land.
  • The setting's farm against these.
    The setting's family plot yields about 15 koku a year; the landlord collects 7.5, pays 5 in tax and keeps 2.5. A single farm has been valued in play at about 50 koku, varying enormously with the domain, a figure the notes give beside the freeholder's own farm, the exception, since most farmers are tenants. Read against the landlord's 2.5 koku, 50 koku is 20 years of net, a 5% return, the same as the central Edo town lot, and over twice the eight years of rent that Fujian's buyers paid on average. The setting notes searched give no rate of interest to set beside it.
