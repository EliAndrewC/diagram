What did a shrine's stonework cost?

Rokugan's prices are counted in koku and its smaller coins, and the setting's money rules judge a price by how many days of a craftsman's or a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give what the historical record holds for the stone pieces of a small shrine in Edo-period Japan, each cost set beside the nearest day wages the record gives.

One stone piece was found priced: a pair of stone lanterns bought for the Hachiman shrine of Shibasaki, in the Tama district of Musashi, in 1841, from a mason of Atsugi. The same headman's diary notes what the shrine's stone base and its entrance curb were to cost and a payment for extra men at the base. No price was found for a small stone torii, a tall lantern, guardian lions or foxes, a water basin, a stele, a five-ring pagoda, a stone coffer or a stairway, or a mason's day wage.

The stonework at Shibasaki, 1840-1841

  • What did a pair of stone lanterns cost?
    4 ryo 3 bu (4.75 ryo). In the fifth month of 1841 the mason Chugoro of Atsugi, in Sagami, came and took the contract for a pair of stone lanterns for the shrine, to be received at Hachioji Shinmachi, for 4 ryo 3 bu, with 2 bu paid down. Their size is not given.
  • What did the entrance stones cost?
    At the same visit the shrine ordered paving stones for its entrance, only the curb on three sides, estimated at 2 bu 2 shu.
  • What did the stone base under the sanctuary cost?
    Only parts of it are given. In the eighth month of 1840 an estimate for the stones of the rounded base was answered, and the Tomoeya, a house the shrine borrowed from, was asked to pay 2 ryo 2 bu 2 shu on it; whether that was the whole price is not said. In 1841 the shrine repaid the Tomoeya 2 kanmon of copper coins it had advanced as a fee for work on the base stones, their piling or their loading; the paper's printing of the diary's word for the work is unclear on the scan.
  • What was a mason paid for a day?
    No day wage of a mason was found. The nearest is this: in the third month of 1841 the masons of Ina village came to fit the base and set the foundation stones, and because the work was heavy the pay of four more men, 1 bu in all, was agreed. The diary names neither their trade nor their days. If each worked one day, each was paid 1 shu, about 3.75 monme of silver; that is this project's reading.

The other stone pieces

  • What did a stone torii cost?
    No price of a small one was found. The one figure found is for a great torii, said to have cost 3,200 ryo in 1708-1709, with no size given: an upper bound only (the torii page).
  • What did one tall lantern cost?
    No price was found.
  • What did guardian lions or foxes, a water basin, a stele, a five-ring pagoda or a stone coffer cost?
    No price was found for any of them.
  • What did a stone stairway cost?
    No price was found; the nearest are the base stones and the entrance curb above.

Day wages beside them

  • What was a day's pay at Shibasaki in 1840-1841?
    No wage of the place and years was found. The nearest craftsman's wage read is a carpenter's at Kumehara in 1830, in the Saitama district of the same province, ten years earlier: 2 monme of silver a man, which this project reads as a day's pay, and 1 monme more that this project reads as his food allowance. The nearest unskilled wage read is a Kyoto casual laborer's of 1802-1804, about 0.92 monme a day in spring, almost forty years earlier and far to the west.
  • What was the stonework in days?
    At 60 monme to the ryo, a rate this project borrows, the lanterns' 4.75 ryo was 95 days of the Kumehara carpenter, food included, or about 310 days of the Kyoto laborer; the base-stone payment of 2 ryo 2 bu 2 shu about 52 or 171; the entrance curb's 2 bu 2 shu about 12 or 41. At the 3.75 monme a man of the extra men above, the lanterns were about 76 man-days.
  • And the 2 kanmon in copper coin?
    No rate of copper coin to silver at Shibasaki was read, so it is left in coin. The nearest wage counted in coin is the carpenter's rate that thirty-nine villages agreed in 1818, kept in the same collection of village papers, twenty-three years earlier: 200 mon, which this project reads as a day's pay, as the rule names no unit. At it, 2 kanmon was 10 carpenter-days. No unskilled wage in coin was found.
