Firebreaks (hiyokechi)

Our maps draw no firebreaks: a firebreak is open ground, and open ground cannot be drawn as a feature without inventing one, so the fire watch towers and fire gear carry the story of fire instead. The research below shows the firebreaks Edo kept, which our maps leave out as a drawing convention, not because towns had none.

A firebreak (hiyokechi) was open ground kept clear so that a fire could not spread across it. The shogunate set such ground aside in Edo after the Meireki fire of 1657, and in the broad sense the word also covers streets kept wide for the same purpose, the hirokoji. There were only about a dozen sites, and because nothing permanent could be built on them, the widened streets at busy places filled with stalls and shows.

  • Widened streets, the hirokoji, counted as firebreaks too.
    Open ground against fire, in the broad sense, included streets made wide for the same purpose, and most of Edo's hirokoji were laid out as fire-clearance open ground to stop a fire spreading.
  • Edo's firebreaks were a few open sites, thirteen by the Kyoho era.
    Five burned-over sites were set aside after the Meireki fire, and more were added, mainly to shield Edo Castle, until there were thirteen.
  • Busy firebreak streets filled with stalls and shows, and many became pleasure quarters.
    No permanent building could stand on a hirokoji, but at busy bridgeheads and before popular temples and shrines they filled with movable stalls, markets, street performers and reed-screened show booths, so that many grew into pleasure quarters.
  • Were firebreaks ever left between single houses?
    None we read describes one. The firebreaks recorded are whole sites set aside after the fire and streets kept wide for the same purpose, and the article on them gives no rule for spacing houses apart, so that they were never gaps between single houses is this project's reading of that silence.
