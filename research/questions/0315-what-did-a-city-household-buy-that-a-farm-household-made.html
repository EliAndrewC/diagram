What did a city household buy that a farm household made?

Rokugan's money rules price a koku by what it buys and by the paid working days it stands for, and neither sees the work a household did for itself. The question here is what a household in a city bought rather than made for itself. The findings below give what the historical record holds on what a city household bought, what each cost and who did the work it did not buy, in Edo above all, with Kyoto, Osaka and Beijing beside it.

In Edo, the shogun's capital in the Edo period (1603-1868), a household could buy its rice already polished, and bought its firewood and seasonings, its baths, much of its clothing secondhand and its sushi at a street stall. What it did for itself, at the tenement's shared well, was the washing, the rinsing of rice and the cooking, and the wives gathered there. No source read counts the days or hours that work took, for a single man or for a family. Why such work was so rarely written down is in Why was women's household work so rarely written down?

What an Edo household bought, item by item

  • Rice could be bought polished.
    In Edo only the rice polishers could sell white rice to the consumer, and from 1744 rice polished in the villages could not be sold in the city; besides the polishers' shops, about 1,100 polished rice in the street in 1727. The polishers' numbers and their work are in Rice hulling and polishing (tsukigomeya).
  • What did a peddler's household spend on food each day?
    A modern paper reports an account of Edo around the Bunsei era (1818-1830): a vegetable peddler, coming home each day, had 200 mon taken from his earnings for rice, 50 mon for miso and soy sauce and 12 to 13 mon for his child's sweets, and kept 100 to 200 mon. He started out on 600 to 700 mon of stock money lent by a boss, with the pole and baskets, repaying it each evening with 2 to 3 mon of interest on every 100, by a magazine's report of the same account.
  • What did a carpenter's family spend in a month?
    By the same account, a carpenter earned 5.4 monme of silver a day, meals included; his month's rent was 10 monme, food for a couple and one child about 30 monme, seasonings and firewood together 58 monme, and with tools, furniture and clothing his month came to about 128 monme. What firewood and charcoal cost by the bundle and the bale is in What did charcoal cost?
  • Cooked food was sold on the street.
    As a magazine reports the Morisada Manko, a piece of sushi cost 8 mon and one of egg omelette 16 mon; the writer adds, citing nothing, that an egg then cost 7 to 20 mon. Where people were about at night, every block had three or four sushi and tempura stalls.
  • Much clothing was bought used.
    Commoners in the Edo period wore mostly cotton, and much of it was secondhand; Edo had used-clothes wholesalers and dealers, and buyers walked the streets to buy clothes up, 220 of them licensed in 1659. No recorded price for a used garment was found; what new cloth cost is in What did cloth and clothing cost?
  • Washing could be sent out, but by whom is not known.
    By the first years of the Genroku era (about 1688) there were washing shops, which washed a garment whole or took out stains; in Edo the washers were said to be mostly women, in Kyoto mostly men. No price, and no account of whose clothes they washed, was found.
  • Was mending bought?
    No source read names a trade that mended a household's clothes, or a price for it.
  • A tenement had no bath, so its people paid at the bathhouse.
    An Edo back tenement had a shared privy in its alley and no bath. The adult fee was about 10 mon until an order of 1841 set it at 8 mon, 6 for a child and 4 for a small child; dearer firewood raised it to 12 mon in 1862-1863, and it rose again to 16 mon in 1865 and 24 mon in 1866, as a magazine reports the Morisada Manko; a scrub of the back was extra. Baths at home are in Baths (furo).
  • A tenement's water was piped to a shared well.
    The tenement's water came from a shared well fed by the Kanda aqueduct, not from groundwater. In many Japanese cities, until modern waterworks began in the first years after 1868, not a few places drew their daily water from channels or bought river water from water sellers. No price for water from a seller was found.

Who did the work a city household did not buy

  • The wives did the washing and the rice at the well.
    The shared well's water was for drinking and also for washing clothes, bathing, rinsing rice and cooking; the well was the tenement's shared place for cooking and washing, so the wives gathered there, and a late-Edo picture book shows a tenement wife rinsing rice.
  • How many days or hours a year did a city household's own work take?
    No source read gives a figure, for a single laborer in a back tenement or for a laborer or artisan with a family. The two dated budgets are both family men's: the peddler paid for a child's sweets, and the carpenter fed a couple and a child.
  • Did a single man work less than a married one?
    One writer says so, citing nothing: married peddlers, who had a wife and children to keep, worked hard every day, while Edo, a city of many single men, had countless carefree peddlers living from day to day; of the peddlers who stayed under a boss, it says, some rested when it rained, and some days because they did not feel like working. The paper behind the budgets says only, from a writer of the same period, that a peddler had to save against days when bad weather kept him from trading.
  • Single samurai in barracks cooked for themselves or ate out.
    Retainers posted alone to their lord's Edo compound for a year had to see to their own meals, each cooking for himself or eating out, and many went out to the public baths though the compound had one; foot soldiers shared rooms the size of a commoner's back-tenement unit and cooked for themselves. These were samurai, not laborers; no like account of single laborers was found.

Kyoto, Osaka and Beijing

Little was found for the other two great Japanese cities, or for China.

  • In Kyoto and Osaka many houses had a bath.
    As the same magazine reports the Morisada Manko, many houses in Kyoto and Osaka had a bath room, and only the tenants of small rented houses used the bathhouses, while in Edo the tenement dwellers used them often. Beyond this and Kyoto's washing shops, nothing on what a Kyoto or Osaka household bought was found.
  • Beijing's water came by water carriers.
    Qing Beijing, from 1644 to 1900, drew salty water from wells and had two systems of water carriers, the state's in the Inner City and private carriers in the Outer City. No price for that water, and nothing on Beijing's used-clothes or washing trades, was read.
