In Rokugan a tariff of typically about 15% of the declared sales value is charged at the gate of the city where goods are sold, and the setting's price ledger reckons a good sold far from where it was made at its price there plus freight, a 15% tariff for each border and a merchant's margin; the Sparrow's tea, the setting says, can always be sold to raise the money for the food they cannot grow. The findings below give what the historical record of Edo-period Japan (1603 to 1868) holds for goods that came from abroad through the port of Nagasaki: what they cost there and in the cities of central Japan.

Until Japan's own white sugar reached the markets of Kyoto and Osaka at the end of the 1700s, white sugar and rock sugar depended on the Chinese and Dutch ships that called at Nagasaki. For that sugar one short article gives a price at both ends in the same year: in 1692, 97 monme of silver for 100 kin at the Dutch post against 140 at retail in Kyoto, 1.44 times. Imported white sugar: Nagasaki against Kyoto and Osaka

  • What did it cost at Nagasaki, against Kyoto, in 1692?
    Yao Keisuke (yao-2011-edo-sato-shokubunka) writes that the import price of white sugar at the Dutch post on Dejima in Nagasaki was 97 monme for 100 kin that year, and that its retail price in Kyoto, by the post's physician Engelbert Kaempfer, was 140 monme. Kyoto's price was thus 1.44 times Dejima's, this project's arithmetic.
  • What made up the gap?
    Of the 43 monme added on the way, the article says, 10.76 monme was the cost of carriage from Nagasaki to the Kyoto and Osaka region. That is a quarter of the gap and about 8% of the Kyoto price, this project's arithmetic; the article does not divide the other three quarters among the merchants who handled the sugar.
  • How far was it, and how did the sugar travel?
    By the received account, which the article reports and then qualifies, the sugar bought at Nagasaki's auctions went by ship to the drug wholesalers of Doshomachi in Osaka, and the sugar brokers of Sakaisuji sent it on to Edo and the rest of the country; a good deal, he adds, never took that road, staying instead in circulation around Nagasaki, by his count more than a tenth of what was imported, or going straight to the neighboring domains of Saga and Fukuoka. Nagasaki is about 565 km (~351 mi) from Osaka and about 600 km (~373 mi) from Kyoto in a straight line, this project's arithmetic. The article gives no days for the voyage.
  • What did the charges of 1697 do to the price?
    When the Nagasaki office that handled the foreign trade was set up in 1697, a charge was laid on imports; on sugar it came to about 200%, the article says, so that the auction price was three times the cost and the retail price in Osaka nearly five times, close to 300 monme. It gives no year for those multiples beyond the office's founding, and it does not repeat the unit of that price, which is read here as the same 100 kin, this project's reading.
  • And in the 1800s?
    Sugar had become a good for speculation: in 1808 the price at Dejima had fallen to 69 monme for 100 kin, while the retail price within Japan could reach 660 monme, the article says, without naming the city. That is up to 9.6 times, this project's arithmetic.
  • What is that in silver by weight?
    At the article's 100 kin of 60 kg and the silver in a monme of each date's coin (How much silver was a monme, a ryo and a bu, coinage by coinage?), white sugar in 1692 was about 4.9 g of silver a kilogram at Dejima and 7.0 g in Kyoto, and the carriage about 0.5 g a kilogram; in 1808, about 2.0 g at Dejima and up to 19 g at retail, this project's arithmetic.
  • How far can these figures be leaned on?
    Not far. The article is a short piece for the general reader, without footnotes: it credits the Kyoto price to Kaempfer and its other prices to no one, naming the Dutch post's account books and the records of Nagasaki only in general, and none of these was read here. Its two prices of 1692 stand at different stages, an import price against a retail price in Kyoto. And the sugar is an import, landed at Nagasaki, not a good made in one region of Japan and sold in another: it stands beside the sugar of Satsuma and Sanuki as a neighbor, not in their place.

Spices, incense woods and medicinal roots

  • Was a price at both ends found for any of them?
    No. One search for each found no price where the good was landed set beside a price in Osaka or Edo in the same year, for pepper or cloves, for aloeswood or sandalwood, or for rhubarb or licorice.
