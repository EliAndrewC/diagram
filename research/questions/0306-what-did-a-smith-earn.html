What did a smith earn?

The setting's notes count smiths among the commoner craftsmen, and its price lists say what their tools and blades sell for, but not what a smith kept of the price. The findings below give what the historical record holds: a tool's price against the iron in it, how many sickles a smithy made in a year and who worked in it, in Edo-period Japan and Qing China, and how a village smith was paid in the twentieth century.

No smith's yearly takings or income was found, in Japan or China. What survives are prices: of a sickle beside the iron it was made of on one Beijing list of 1736, of a hoe from Sakai or Osaka in 1822 and of a village smith's repairs in Echizen or Wakasa in 1841, with iron priced in Japan only in rice. A sickle-making town's petition of 1862 gives its output a year; a village smith worked with his family and one or two helpers, and a Qing smithy hired one or two hands when busy. A tool against its iron

  • A Beijing list of 1736 priced a sickle beside its iron.
    The official price list the Ministry of Works settled in Beijing in 1736 priced a sickle weighing 12 liang (three-quarters of a jin) at 0.07 tael of silver, and on the same list wrought iron at 0.0225 tael a jin (ordinary) and 0.027 (good), steel at 0.085 and pig iron at 0.004; black charcoal at 0.5 to 0.6 tael a hundred jin and white charcoal at 1.1. These are an official list's prices for the capital, not a village smith's.
  • How much of the sickle's price was its iron?
    Three-quarters of a jin of wrought iron came to about 0.017 to 0.020 tael, a quarter to three-tenths of the sickle's price, by this project's arithmetic. That leaves out any steel in its edge, the charcoal and the iron lost in forging, none of whose quantities was found, so the smith's margin cannot be told from it. The list's day wage for craftsmen names the building trades, not smiths.
  • A hoe from the smiths of Sakai and Osaka cost about 5.6 monme in 1822.
    In his book on farm tools, published in Osaka in 1822, the agronomist Okura Nagatsune priced a hoe for loam of the Nishinari district of Settsu at 5 monme 6 bu of silver (a bu here being a tenth of a monme), and at 5 monme 5 bu in the price table at its end, which gives the prices at which the farm-tool smiths of Sakai and around Osaka made them, a historical geographer reports.
  • What was iron worth in Japan?
    The one series found prices iron in rice, not silver: a kan of iron was worth 0.29 to of rice (a to being a tenth of a koku) in Osaka in 1812, 0.43 in Edo in 1826, 0.52 in Osaka in 1843 and 0.68 in Edo in 1844, in a historian's table that a metallurgist reprints and corrects for its rows before 1620. At the Mitsui house's Osaka rice price of the spring of 1822, 68.8 monme a koku, the 1812 and 1826 ratios make a kan of iron about 2 to 3 monme of silver, by this project's arithmetic across the two sources and across those years.
  • How much of the hoe's price was its iron?
    A prefectural library's leaflet on Okura's book gives the weights of two kinds of hoe it pictures from the book: a fork-shaped Bitchu hoe of 700 to 800 me, twice the 350 me of a flat hoe, a me being about 3.75 g. If a hoe of 700 to 800 me were all iron, its iron would be worth about 1.4 to 2.4 monme, a quarter to two-fifths of the Sakai hoe's 5.6, by this project's arithmetic. It is a rough bound: the leaflet does not say how much of either weight is iron, the traditional hoes that open Okura's list of hoes had a wooden body with only an iron part fitted to it, and the Sakai hoe's own weight was not found.   • What did a village smith charge in Echizen or Wakasa in 1841?
    A sealed agreement on the prices of smiths' work, of 1841, set the village smiths' 11 to 15 monme of silver for making the point of a hoe, 4 monme for the blade-edge of a three-pronged hoe, 3 monme for the handle of a five-sho pot and 0.15 monme for resharpening a weeding sickle, the prefectural history reports. A hoe's point at 11 to 15 monme is two to three times the whole Sakai hoe of 1822; whether the province, the nineteen years or what the work covered explains the gap was not found, and this record does not reconcile them.
  • Where the smith came to the farm, the farmer supplied the charcoal.
    In an early farm book, the Seiryoki, finished about 1661-1681 by one agrarian historian's reading, a household's fourth man cut charcoal for the smith and helped him make the farm tools; the same historian, Furushima Toshio, read this as the travelling smith forging at the farm with the household's charcoal, the farmer himself swinging the sledge, a historical geographer reports.

How much a smithy made

  • How many sickles did a house of Takefu make in a year?
    In 1862 the smiths' guild of Takefu (then Fuchu), a sickle-making town of Echizen, told the production office set up in the town in 1859 it could deliver at least 2,000 lots a year, which the prefectural history estimates at about 570,000 sickles. The town had 111 smithies in 1850 and 129 in 1866, so this is some 4,400 to 5,100 sickles or more a smithy a year, by this project's arithmetic: a house's output, not one smith's, and a specialist sickle town's, not a village smith's.
  • How much did a village smith make in a year?
    No count was found. A household record of 1823 in Hida says a visiting smith lodged with it some forty days, seven of them working for the house itself.

Who worked with a smith

  • A country smith worked with his family and one or two helpers.
    In 1834 a smith from Hida asked a Mino village to let him set up as a smith there, bringing his wife and children and one or two helpers; in 1840 another, aged 57, asked to move to a village with his wife and four sons aged 21 to 29 and a daughter of 16 to work as smiths. In 1857 the town of Takayama in Hida had 53 smiths working away from home.
  • A Qing smithy hired one or two hands when it was busy.
    From criminal case reports of the late 1700s and early 1800s, a historian finds a Shanghai county smith who worked mostly to order, making iron spindles for cotton spinning and farm tools, and a Henan smith who hired a helper whenever work was busy; small masters of every trade hired few hands, usually one or two and at most two or three.

What a smith took home

  • What were a smith's takings and income in a year?
    Neither a smith's gross takings nor his net income in a year was found for Edo-period Japan or Qing China.
  • Was a village smith paid in rice?
    In the twentieth century, yes. In a Niigata survey of 1941 the smith went round the farms from mid-November collecting worn hoes and their rent in rice, called smith's rice, and re-tipped them over the New Year; in a Kumamoto village studied in 1935-1936 a smith hired from the next village worked two weeks each spring and was usually paid in rice. No record of a smith paid this way before 1868 was found.
  • What did a carpenter earn in a year?
    The one yearly account of a craftsman found is an Edo carpenter's, at third hand: a book's reckoning, which takes the years 1804-1830 as its standard, gives him 5.4 monme of silver a day, meals included, for 294 days of a 354-day year, 1,587.6 monme in all, against a household's spending of 1,534, leaving 53.6 over, a blog reports from it.
