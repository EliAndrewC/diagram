How our maps draw the townsmen's office and guild halls (machi-kaisho, huiguan)

A town map draws no ward office (machi-kaisho), the office a town's commoners ran for themselves, and no guild hall (huiguan). An office in every ward is recorded for Sakai and the other self-governing towns, Kyoto and Osaka, not for a magistrate's seat like our county town. Where a headman keeps the town's papers, the office is their own house on the main street, drawn as a house.

  • An office in every ward is recorded for the self-governing towns, Kyoto and Osaka.
    Those are the towns that kept one in every ward, and a county town is none of them.
  • Our county town is drawn like the castle towns that set up no ward office.
    A county town is its magistrate's seat. In some castle towns the commoners' officials worked at the town magistrate's office and no ward office was set up. This project takes those castle towns as a county town's nearest counterpart, so a town map draws no ward office.
  • The headman's house serves as the town's office, on the main street.
    In Edo the ward headman's house served as the office. Setting it on the main street is this project's choice, after Edo's three town elders, whose residences doubled as their office on corner lots facing Honcho street. It is drawn as a house, with nothing to mark it as an office.

The guild hall comes from China rather than Japan: in Ming and Qing China a huiguan was the hall of its members, people from one home region or merchants of one trade.

  • No guild hall is drawn.
    No page we read places a huiguan in a county seat, so our county town has none.
