How our maps draw the samurai quarter and count its households (bukechi)

A city's samurai quarter is sized to the city's samurai, about a tenth of its population, and holds only samurai households, their servants and government ground. About two thirds of the city's samurai households live inside its wall: most as single houses of every rank in the samurai quarter, a few in the governor's compound. The rest live on walled country estates outside it.

  • The samurai quarter is one district of a mostly commoner city - a deliberate deviation.
    A historical castle town's samurai ground was often half of it or more, but this setting's provincial city is only about a tenth samurai.
  • At least two thirds of the samurai households a city's declared population implies are drawn as samurai houses.
    A token cluster of a couple of dozen houses would not hold them. This floor is on the houses drawn, calibrated against the maps already drawn, not a finding; the share of households living inside the wall is the GUESS below.
  • The houses vary by rank: at least three large senior houses among a clear majority of small junior ones.
    A castle town's plots were granted by status and stipend, so a quarter of houses all one size is not a castle town's. Both counts are calibrated against the maps already drawn. How big each rank's lot is drawn is at How our maps draw a samurai's lot and house (bukeyashiki)
  • The large houses are spread through the rows, not grouped by the castle.
    A castle town's upper retainers had their houses grouped by the castle, but here the large houses are interleaved through the rows so they front the streets prominently. The interleaving is calibrated against the maps already drawn.
  • Samurai houses line their streets: at least a third of them stand within about 270 ft of a lane.
    The deeper lots sit behind them. The third and the 270 ft are calibrated against the maps already drawn.
  • A samurai house inside the wall is enclosed as its rank calls for, a senior house by an earthen wall and a long-house gate.
    A provincial castle town's samurai plots were enclosed by earthen walls, board fences or hedges, and our maps enclose each lot by its holder's rank, as How our maps draw a samurai's lot and house (bukeyashiki) sets out. How the quarter as a whole is fenced and gated is at How our maps draw city wards and their gates (machi, kido).

How many of the households live inside the wall is a guess, but the history points one way.

  • How many of a city's samurai households live inside its wall?
    About two thirds, with the rest on country estates outside it. The two thirds is a GUESS: no page we read gives a share. On the evidence the true figure leans higher, since the history puts a castle town's samurai inside its enclosure where it had one, as at Funai and Hagi, and its country samurai out in the villages. The space budget built on it is at How our maps size a city's wall to its population
  • Did the low-ranking samurai housed at a castle town's edge live inside its wall?
    A castle town's lower ranks, such as foot soldiers, lived at its edge, and in this setting ashigaru are peasants, but its other low-ranking samurai are samurai still. Whether their quarters stood inside an enclosure or beyond it the sources do not settle - Hagi's outer enclosure took in its lower retainers' residences, while the general rule put the foot soldiers' group residences on the outskirts. Our maps count them inside the wall, a GUESS.
  • A samurai household drawn outside the wall is a country estate among the fields, not a quarter against the wall.
    In this setting a samurai family keeps at most a country estate near some farmland, as the country samurai of history had residences scattered through the farming villages. So our maps draw no free-standing samurai house beyond the wall: an unwalled house there would be undefended and belong to no estate.

Where a city fences its samurai quarter off as a ward of its own, the ward keeps commoners out, as a castle town kept samurai and townsmen apart.

  • The samurai ward holds only its own residents.
    Samurai houses large and small, their live-in servants and government ground stand inside the fence; laborers, merchants, shops, inns and burakumin do not.
  • A monks' house may stand inside the ward, with its temple.
    A temple may stand inside the ward - the warrior fortune's temple beside the garrison quarter is the case - and its monks' house belongs with it.
  • The ward is laid out before the commoner quarters.
    So no commoner household is ever placed inside it: one that would have fallen inside the fence is housed elsewhere in the city instead.
  • A fenced samurai ward is filled out with more samurai households, not with commoners' row houses.
    Each comes with lodging for its own live-in servants, who make up much of the added population, so the samurai stay within their share of the city's population.
