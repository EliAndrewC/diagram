What did raw silk and iron cost where they were made, against the city that used them?

In Rokugan a tariff of typically about 15% of the declared sales value is charged at the gate of the city where goods are sold, and the setting's price ledger reckons a good sold far from where it was made at its price there plus freight, a 15% tariff for each border and a merchant's margin; the Sparrow's tea, the setting says, can always be sold to raise the money for the food they cannot grow. The findings below give what the historical record of Edo-period Japan (1603 to 1868) holds for two such goods, raw silk and iron: what each cost where it was made and in the city that used it, in the same year.

Kyoto's weavers paid more for raw silk than it cost in the districts that reeled it, but not much more: in most years from 1818 to 1849 about a fifth to two-fifths more than in the Fukushima district, and from 1830 to 1849 about a twentieth to a quarter more than at Maebashi, itself a market town. For iron one ironworks' own reckoning survives, from Harima province in 1736 to 1741: bar iron expected to sell in Osaka for about 15% more than it cost to make, the carriage taking most of the difference. No source read gives the road the silk took, what its carriage cost, or a price of Izumo's iron in Osaka. Sugar and tea are in What did sugar and tea cost where they were made, against Osaka and Edo?, what carriage cost by water and by land in What did carrying goods cost by water, against by land?, and two other goods with a price at both ends of their road, safflower and rice, in What did safflower and rice cost where they were grown, against Kyoto and Osaka?

Raw silk: the districts against Kyoto

  • What did raw silk cost at Maebashi, in Joshu, against Kyoto?
    In 1830 a kan of raw silk (3.75 kg, ~8 lb) was 5.986 ryo of gold in Kyoto and 5.256 at Maebashi; in 1836, 6.028 and 5.844; in 1849, 7.333 and 6.889. So read two series from a 1983 study of Edo-period prices by Yamazaki Ryuzo, as a doctoral thesis by Wulan Qiqige (wulan-2014-sanshi-kinuorimono) reprints them. In the thirteen years that have both prices, 1830 to 1839, 1841, 1848 and 1849, Kyoto's was 1.01 to 1.28 times Maebashi's, and in ten of them 1.02 to 1.15 times, this project's arithmetic.
  • Maebashi was a market, not a farm gate.
    The thesis calls Maebashi the central market of a producing district rather than a consuming city, and notes, after another historian, that its thread is said to have been a fine one and dearer than other thread, for its ease of transport among other reasons. So the gap measured from Maebashi may be narrower than a district's own, this project's reading.
  • What did it cost in the Fukushima district against Kyoto?
    The same study gives Kyoto's price as an index of the price in the Shindatsu district, around Fukushima, set at 100, for twenty-three years between 1818 and 1849: 132 in 1818, 143 at its highest in 1826, 120 in 1831, 98 at its lowest in 1841 and 121 in 1849. The thesis sums it up as 130 or more until 1830 and 120 to 125 after, though its own table has 120 to 129 in four of the earlier years and 126, 128 and 98 in three of the later. Kyoto was thus about 1.2 to 1.4 times the district's price in every year but 1841.
  • What was the district's own price?
    The thesis reprints the Shindatsu prices too, by five places of the district, with no unit stated: 4.237 in 1818, the year Kyoto stood at 5.611 ryo a kan. Since 5.611 over 4.237 is the index's 132, the table is read here as ryo a kan like Kyoto's; and the index was not taken from one place's column throughout, this project's reading.
  • What is that in silver by weight?
    By the silver in a ryo of each coinage (How much silver was a monme, a ryo and a bu, coinage by coinage?), about 109 g in 1818 and 83 g in 1830 to 1836, a kilogram of raw silk was about 163 g of silver in Kyoto against 123 g in the Fukushima district in 1818; 133 g in Kyoto against 116 g at Maebashi in 1830; and 133 g against 129 g in 1836, this project's arithmetic.
  • How far apart were they, and how did the silk travel?
    Maebashi is about 335 km (~208 mi) from Kyoto and Fukushima about 520 km (~323 mi), in a straight line between the modern cities, this project's arithmetic; the roads were longer. The thesis gives no road, no days on the way, no cost of carriage and no account of what made up the gap, and no other source cited here does.
  • And the Shinshu districts?
    No price of Shinshu's raw silk set against Kyoto's in the same year was found. The thesis says only that the Shindatsu district and the other districts stood at about the same level.

Iron: an ironworks against Osaka

The one document found that sets both ends side by side comes from the papers of a domain in Harima province, not from Izumo or Iwami.

  • What did bar iron cost to make at a Harima works, against its sale in Osaka?
    A method book for running an ironworks, dated to the Genbun years (1736 to 1741) and kept among the papers of Mikazuki domain in Harima, reckons a model year. Its split bar iron comes to 72.74 monme of silver a pack-horse load as made at the works, and its long bar to 60 monme, as Tosa Masahiko and Kano Ayuko transcribe it (tosa-kano-2024-genbun-tetsuzan). It expects 2,218 loads of the split iron to sell in Osaka for 186 kan 93 monme and 292 loads of the long bar for 19 kan 972.8 monme: 83.9 and 68.4 monme a load, 1.15 and 1.14 times the cost at the works, this project's arithmetic.
  • These are an operator's estimates, not prices paid.
    The figure at the works is what the iron cost to make, wages, charcoal and rice reckoned in; no iron changed hands there. The Osaka figure is the sale the writer expected. The pig iron that went to the forges is reckoned at 31.6 monme a load.
  • What made up the gap?
    Carriage, mostly. The book allows about 7 monme a load for pack-horse hire and sundries from the works to Osaka, 17 kan 570 monme on the year's 2,510 loads, and of that 4.5 monme for the first leg, to Yamasaki. After costs of 178 kan 849.1 monme and the carriage, 9 kan 646.7 monme was left of a sale of 206 kan 65.8 monme. So carriage was about a twelfth of the Osaka price and the remainder under a twentieth, this project's arithmetic.
  • What is that in silver by weight?
    The book does not give the weight of its load; its own totals make it 32 kan, about 120 kg (~265 lb). At the 1.725 g of silver in a monme of the coinage of 1736 (How much silver was a monme, a ryo and a bu, coinage by coinage?), split bar iron was about 1.05 g of silver a kilogram at the works and 1.21 g in Osaka, long bar 0.86 g and 0.98 g, and pig iron 0.45 g at the works, this project's arithmetic.
  • How far was it?
    The pages read do not name the works. Mikazuki, the domain's seat, is about 100 km (~62 mi) from Osaka in a straight line, this project's arithmetic.
  • What did Iwami's and Izumo's iron fetch?
    Only the producers' end was found. A petition of 1830 put the pig iron of the shogunate's lands in Iwami at about 30,000 loads worth about 1,200 kan of silver, a third of it sold to Osaka, a third to Kyushu and a third to the northeast and the Sea of Japan coast, as Sagara Eisuke (sagara-2009-iwami-tatara) reports it from another historian's reading. In Izumo the Sakurai house of Tagi sold, on average in 1831 to 1835, 4,534 loads of pig iron a year for about 170 kan 753 monme, and its forges made 1,048 loads of bar iron reckoned at about 80 kan 280 monme; where it was sold is not said (sagara-2009-tagi-sakuraike). That is about 40 monme a load for Iwami's pig iron, and about 37.7 for Izumo's pig iron and 76.6 for its bar, this project's arithmetic. No Osaka price of those years was found, nor the weight of these loads, so they give no ratio and no price by weight.
