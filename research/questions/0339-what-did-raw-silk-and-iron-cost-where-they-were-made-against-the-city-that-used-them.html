What did raw silk and iron cost where they were made, against the city that used them?

In Rokugan a tariff of typically about 15% of the declared sales value is charged at the gate of the city where goods are sold, and the setting's price ledger reckons a good sold far from where it was made at its price there plus freight, a 15% tariff for each border and a merchant's margin; the Sparrow's tea, the setting says, can always be sold to raise the money for the food they cannot grow. The findings below give what the historical record of Edo-period Japan (1603 to 1868) holds for two such goods, raw silk and iron: what each cost where it was made and in the city that used it, in the same year.

Kyoto's weavers paid more for raw silk than it cost in the districts that reeled it, but not much more: in most years from 1818 to 1849 about a fifth to two-fifths more than in the Fukushima district, and from 1830 to 1849 about a twentieth to a quarter more than at Maebashi, itself a market town. For iron one reckoning for an ironworks survives, in a method book of Harima province written in the Genbun years (1736 to 1741), which sets it down undated as the method established from of old: bar iron expected to sell in Osaka for about 15% more than it cost to make, the carriage taking most of the difference. The thread went up to Kyoto with contract couriers and was paid for through them, by bills or by the money they brought back. No source read gives the road it took, a rate for its carriage, or a price of Izumo's iron in Osaka. Sugar and tea are in What did sugar and tea cost where they were made, against Osaka and Edo?, what carriage cost by water and by land in What did carrying goods cost by water, against by land?, and two other goods with a price at both ends of their road, safflower and rice, in What did safflower and rice cost where they were grown, against Kyoto and Osaka?

Raw silk: the districts against Kyoto

  • What did raw silk cost at Maebashi, in Joshu, against Kyoto?
    In 1830 a kan of raw silk (3.75 kg, ~8 lb) was 5.986 ryo of gold in Kyoto and 5.256 at Maebashi; in 1836, 6.028 and 5.844; in 1849, 7.333 and 6.889. So read two series from a 1983 study of Edo-period prices by Yamazaki Ryuzo, as a doctoral thesis by Wulan Qiqige (wulan-2014-sanshi-kinuorimono) reprints them. In the thirteen years that have both prices, 1830 to 1839, 1841, 1848 and 1849, Kyoto's was 1.01 to 1.28 times Maebashi's, and in ten of them 1.02 to 1.15 times, this project's arithmetic.
  • Maebashi was a market, not a farm gate.
    The thesis calls Maebashi the central market of a producing district rather than a consuming city, and notes, after another historian, that its thread is said to have been a fine one and dearer than other thread, for its ease of transport among other reasons. So the gap measured from Maebashi may be narrower than a district's own, this project's reading.
  • What did it cost in the Fukushima district against Kyoto?
    The same study gives Kyoto's price as an index of the price in the Shindatsu district, around Fukushima, set at 100, for twenty-three years between 1818 and 1849: 132 in 1818, 143 at its highest in 1826, 120 in 1831, 98 at its lowest in 1841 and 121 in 1849. The thesis sums it up as 130 or more until 1830 and 120 to 125 after, though its own table has 120 to 129 in four of the earlier years and 126, 128 and 98 in three of the later. Kyoto was thus about 1.2 to 1.4 times the district's price in every year but 1841.
  • What was the district's own price?
    The thesis reprints the Shindatsu prices too, by five places of the district, with no unit stated: 4.237 in 1818, the year Kyoto stood at 5.611 ryo a kan. Since 5.611 over 4.237 is the index's 132, the table is read here as ryo a kan like Kyoto's; and the index was not taken from one place's column throughout, this project's reading.
  • What is that in silver by weight?
    By the silver in a ryo of each coinage (How much silver was a monme, a ryo and a bu, coinage by coinage?), about 109 g in 1818 and 83 g in 1830 to 1836, a kilogram of raw silk was about 163 g of silver in Kyoto against 123 g in the Fukushima district in 1818; 133 g in Kyoto against 116 g at Maebashi in 1830; and 133 g against 129 g in 1836, this project's arithmetic.
  • How far apart were they, and how did the silk travel?
    Maebashi is about 335 km (~208 mi) from Kyoto and Fukushima about 520 km (~323 mi), in a straight line between the modern cities, this project's arithmetic; the roads were longer.
  • Who carried it, and how was it paid for?
    Courier houses, against bills. In 1789 fixed courier houses of Kyoto and Edo, with a courier of Fujioka in Joshu whom the prefecture's commentary takes for the Kyoto house's branch, bound themselves to carry the thread, silk goods and money of a silk merchant of Tomioka, a document published by Gunma Prefecture shows (gunma-monjokan-hikyaku-ukeoi-1789); the merchant shipped to the silk wholesalers of Kyoto, Edo and Nagoya. By a deposit note of 1819, in Makijima Takashi's study of two such houses (makijima-2013-hikyaku-kinyu), a courier house advanced money to a country buying agent, carried the thread and cloth he bought to Kyoto, Osaka and Edo, and handed him the price when it came back; by a bill of 1831 a shipper drew on his Kyoto buyer and was paid at once; and the freight to Kyoto and Osaka was settled twice a year. A thread buyer of Fukushima used the same house's bills.
  • What did the carriage cost?
    Only a branch's totals were found. The Fujioka branch of one courier house, at its stock-taking of the seventh month of 1839, had collected 51 ryo 1 bu 2 shu and 3 kan 350 mon in freight on goods going up to Kyoto, and laid out 16 ryo 2 bu on goods sent to Kyoto by way of Edo. Neither source gives a rate for a bale or a load, the road, the days on the way, or what the Kyoto wholesalers took, and no other source read does.
  • And the Shinano districts?
    No price of Shinano's raw silk set against Kyoto's in the same year was found. The thesis says only that the Shindatsu district and the other districts stood at about the same level.

Iron: an ironworks against Osaka

The one document found that sets both ends side by side comes from a domain's papers and bears on the ironmaking of Harima province, not of Izumo or Iwami.

  • What did bar iron cost to make at a Harima works, against its sale in Osaka?
    A method book for running an ironworks, dated to the Genbun years (1736 to 1741) and kept among the papers of Mikazuki domain, reckons a model year. The reckoning carries no year. It is of the "six-blow" working, which the book's editors place in the part that sets out the method established from of old; the one dated part is the estimate for the newer "four-blow" working, drawn up in 1739, which the editors take to show roughly when the book was written. So the figures below are no later than the Genbun years, and may be older. Its split bar iron comes to 72.74 monme of silver a pack-horse load as made at the works, and its long bar to 60 monme, as Tosa Masahiko and Kano Ayuko transcribe it (tosa-kano-2024-genbun-tetsuzan). It expects 2,218 loads of the split iron to sell in Osaka for 186 kan 93 monme and 292 loads of the long bar for 19 kan 972.8 monme: 83.9 and 68.4 monme a load, 1.15 and 1.14 times the cost at the works, this project's arithmetic.
  • These are an operator's estimates, not prices paid.
    The figure at the works is what the iron cost to make; no iron changed hands there. The Osaka figure is the sale the writer expected. The pig iron that went to the forges is reckoned at 31.6 monme a load.
  • What made up the gap?
    Carriage, mostly. The book allows about 7 monme a load for pack-horse hire and sundries from the works to Osaka, 17 kan 570 monme on the year's 2,510 loads, and of that 4.5 monme for the first leg, to Yamasaki. After costs of 178 kan 849.1 monme and the carriage, 9 kan 646.7 monme was left of a sale of 206 kan 65.8 monme. So carriage was about a twelfth of the Osaka price and the remainder under a twentieth, this project's arithmetic. That remainder is the gain on the works' own bar iron alone. The book goes on to three more gains: 4 kan 700 monme on the 470 koku of the year's rice that were sold, 7 kan within 30 kan of goods sold, and 1 kan 690 monme on 130 loads of iron bought up within the works. It sums the four as a year's profit of over 23 kan, which is the figure the editors' table of contents gives for the item.
  • What is that in silver by weight?
    The pages of the book read here do not state the weight of its load; its own totals make it 32 kan, about 120 kg (~265 lb), and the editors' table of its contents gives a load of pig iron as the same 32 kan. Which silver its monme were is not said, and the undated reckoning leaves two coinages open (How much silver was a monme, a ryo and a bu, coinage by coinage?). At the 1.725 g of silver in a monme of the coinage of 1736, the coin of the years the book was written, split bar iron was about 1.05 g of silver a kilogram at the works and 1.21 g in Osaka, long bar 0.86 g and 0.98 g, and pig iron 0.45 g at the works. At the 3.0 g of the coinage of 1714 to 1736, if the figures were set down before the recoinage, they come to 1.82 g and 2.10 g, 1.50 g and 1.71 g, and 0.79 g, all this project's arithmetic; the debased coins of 1695 to 1714, should the reckoning be older still, are not reckoned. The ratios between the works and Osaka, and the shares of carriage and remainder, hold whichever coin it was.
  • How far was it?
    The model year names no works. Its editors suppose that the domain had the book from the Chigusa house, which held the ironworks of Shiso district, and two of its later items reckon work done at the Ichinotani works in the Chigusa valley, now Chikusa in Shiso city. Chikusa is about 114 km (~71 mi) from Osaka in a straight line, and Mikazuki, the seat of the domain that kept the copy, about 100 km (~62 mi), this project's arithmetic. Of the road the book gives only its first leg, by pack horse to Yamasaki; the editors' commentary adds nothing on the way from there to Osaka, on boats or on days.
  • What did Iwami's and Izumo's iron fetch?
    Only the producers' end was found. A petition of 1830 put the pig iron of the shogunate's lands in Iwami at about 30,000 loads worth about 1,200 kan of silver, a third of it sold to Osaka, a third to Kyushu and a third to the northeast and the Sea of Japan coast, as Sagara Eisuke (sagara-2009-iwami-tatara) reports it from another historian's reading. In Izumo the Sakurai house of Tagi sold, on average in 1831 to 1835, 4,534 loads of pig iron a year for about 170 kan 753 monme, and its forges made 1,048 loads of bar iron reckoned at about 80 kan 280 monme; where it was sold is not said (sagara-2009-tagi-sakuraike). Those were hard years for the house: famine ran through the country from 1833 to 1836, and Sagara presumes that iron sold poorly with it, so its averages are those of a slump. That is about 40 monme a load for Iwami's pig iron, and about 37.7 for Izumo's pig iron and 76.6 for its bar, this project's arithmetic. No Osaka price of those years was found, nor the weight of these loads, so they give no ratio and no price by weight.
  • Who set the Osaka price of Izumo's iron in the 1780s?
    The shogunate, for seven years. From 1780 it kept an iron office in Osaka: the iron of every province was to be shipped there, and the office decided the price at which it was bought, Sagara writes in a study of Matsue domain's ironmasters (sagara-2012-matsue-tatara). The ironmasters' price, in a phrase the study quotes, had been lowered by degrees since the office; it was withdrawn in 1787, and they were left deep in debt to the Osaka wholesalers. The study gives no price of those years at either end.
