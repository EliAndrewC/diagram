The town's street front: shop widths, how close the houses stood, stories and roofs (machinami)

A town's street front was a row of merchants' houses, each built across the full width of its lot and running long toward the back. How much street a house held measured its owner's wealth: in Kyoto, from about 10 ft for a small trader to 65 ft or more for a great merchant house. In Qixian, a Chinese county seat that was a home of the Shanxi merchants, a shop ran wider, some 40 to 50 ft, with courtyards behind it. How a town sorted its lots back from the road is at How a town is zoned: shops on the street, housing behind, and what one merchant's house held from its shop front to the back of its lot at Merchants' townhouses (machiya).

Whether the houses touched turned on the traffic. On a busy highway they stood eave to eave across their lots, some with small fire walls on the roof between neighbors; on a side road or in the mountains they stood apart, behind front yards, built much like the farmhouses around them. Western Japan roofed its town houses in tile and eastern Japan in board weighted with stones, and a town that was half a village kept its thatch. Many Japanese town houses had only a low storage loft at the street front, kept low at first by official bans, though in time full second stories spread. Qixian's street front stood two stories high under gray tile.

  • In Kyoto, the width of a townsman's house on the street measured their wealth.
    Kyoto's townsmen fell into three bands: those with large houses of ten ken or more of frontage (~65 ft), the ordinary shopkeeper with four or five (~26-33 ft), and the small trader with one and a half to two (~10-13 ft), or renting. The division had been growing since the middle of the Edo period.
  • Edo laid its townsmen's quarters out in lots five or six ken wide and twenty deep.
    Each block was planned with an open space at its middle, and the ground facing the street, twenty ken (~130 ft) deep, was cut into strip lots of five to six ken of frontage (~33-39 ft).
  • In Edo, the shop at the front of a city lot was kept to about five ken deep, with rented tenements behind it.
    The arrangement, a front shop about five ken (~33 ft) deep and back tenements behind it, was quite standardized.
  • Edo's great stores bought up the lots beside them.
    Many merged neighboring lots into one wide frontage: some had 36 ken (more than 200 ft), and some had 15 ken (~90-98 ft) with ground that ran back across the town boundary.
  • How wide was a merchant's frontage in a small Japanese town?
    A post town's lots on both sides of the road were planned strips of nearly the same depth, differing in their frontage, but no page we read gives a small town's frontages in feet, or how many of its merchants were small traders, ordinary shopkeepers or great houses.

A Chinese county seat's shops followed another plan, a street building with courtyards behind it.

  • How wide was a shop in a Chinese county seat?
    In the old county town of Qixian, one of the homes of the Shanxi merchants, the shops on the old streets mostly followed the northern plan of one courtyard or several behind the street. A shop's frontage was generally five bays, each 2.5 to 3 m (~8-10 ft), about 41 to 49 ft in all, and the shops ran deep: the Juquantang pharmacy is more than 9 m (~30 ft) deep. That is one town of wealthy trading houses; no page we read gives the spread of shop sizes across Chinese county towns.

Back in Japan, the houses along a town's street touched or stood apart. The city's parallel, its commoners' continuous street walls, is at Urban commoners built in continuous street walls and Machiya row density: a commercial street is a continuous built edge.

  • A machiya was built to the full width of its lot.
    It came to fill the whole frontage of its lot, with a floor plan long toward the back.
  • On a busy highway, a post town's houses stood eave to eave.
    In post towns on roads with heavy traffic, such as the Tokaido and the Nakasendo, the main house stood on the road across the full frontage of its lot, and by the late Edo period inns planned like a big city's machiya lined the road eave to eave.
  • Some houses that touched raised small fire walls on the roof between them.
    Some Edo-period machiya raised an udatsu, a small roofed wall, on the roof at the boundary with the neighbor, to stop a fire spreading from next door. The post town of Unno on the Hokkoku road still shows them on its roofs.
  • On side roads and in the mountains, a post town's inns often stood apart, behind front yards.
    There, inns whose plans differed little from the thatched farmhouses around them often took a front yard on the road side and stood with a gap between them and the next house.
  • No page we read counts how many post towns stood eave to eave and how many stood apart.
    The pages on post towns and on single stations describe both forms, but none counts the towns that built each.

Chinese towns built close.

  • In China, houses stood close, and in the Huizhou style fire gables rose between them.
    Because traditional houses were mostly of wood, and to save land, the lanes between houses were very narrow, and a gable wall rising well above the roof was put between one house and the next to stop fire spreading. This horse-head wall is a hallmark of the Huizhou style.
  • Did a Chinese county seat's shops share their walls?
    In the old county seat of Qixian, which still keeps the building style of its region's workshops and dwellings since the Ming and Qing, the shops on both sides of the cross-shaped main street stand close-set as fish scales, and by 1920 the town had 214 trading houses. Neither this page nor the one on fire gables says in so many words that a county seat's shops shared their walls.

Seen from the street, a town's houses differed in how high they rose and what their roofs were made of.

  • A machiya's second story began as a low half-story at the street front, kept low by the bans of the lords' domains (han).
    Many machiya had a low second story used for storage. At first only the street side had one, built with its eaves lowered, and it was low because the domains restricted it by prohibitions.
  • In time the machiya's second story grew to the full size of the ground floor.
    In time the upper floor came to nearly equal the lower in area and served as a guest room.
  • In Kyoto the half-story stayed usual, except for the great merchants.
    Through the whole Edo period the second floor of a Kyoto machiya developed little except in large merchant houses.
  • A busy post town's inns had two stories and board roofs by the late Edo period.
    On the Tokaido and the Nakasendo, two-story, board-roofed inns lined the road.
  • Seki, on the Tokaido, still mixes two-story, half-story and one-story houses along its street.
    Its machiya are diverse, in the plastered style and the style with exposed posts.

Roofs differed by region, by traffic and by date.

  • Western Japan roofed its town houses in tile, eastern Japan in board weighted with stones.
    In the west the roof was tiled and the outer wall plastered right over the posts; in the east the roof was board, with deep eaves and a light, gently pitched roof weighted with stones.
  • Why did some town houses turn their gable end to the street?
    A machiya usually ran its roof ridge along the street (hirairi), but some turned the triangular gable end to it (tsumairi). That form is thought to be, in western Japan, a survival of the thatched-roof house, and in eastern Japan a way of dealing with snow.
  • The Kiso valley's traditional roof was board weighted with stones.
    The post town of Narai reproduces it in part, at its public water spots and elsewhere.
  • A town that was half a village roofed in thatch.
    Ouchi, a "half farming, half station" stop on the Aizu western highway, still lines its old road with thatched houses in orderly rows.
  • Even Edo was roofed mostly in shingle and board in its early years.
    In the early Edo period shingle and board roofs were still the majority.
  • Edo's fires turned its townsmen toward tile roofs and plastered walls, which the shogunate recommended in 1720.
    After the great Meireki fire of 1657, Edo first tried spreading earth over thatch and shingle roofs, which did not last. In 1720 the shogunate recommended tile roofs, which it had for a time forbidden to townsmen, and walls plastered thick like a storehouse's.
  • In late Edo-period Edo, the plastered storehouse front spread to the main house itself.
    The house itself was plastered in thick earth walls like a storehouse, with earth doors at its openings.
  • Kyoto set the merchant's storehouse at the back of the lot, never on the street.
    It was the opposite of Edo's plastered street-front shops.
  • No page we read gives a fire law for a small town's roofs.
    The fire rules we found were made for Edo.

Chinese street fronts rose higher, under tile.

  • In the Chinese county seat of Qixian, the street front stood two stories high, under gray tile.
    There the building on the street was mostly two stories, the shop or workshop below and a storeroom above, under the small gray tile of its old houses.
  • In South China, a street shop rose two or three stories, the family above the shop.
    This shop-house, common in South China and Southeast Asia, used its ground floor as a shop and the floors above as dwellings.
