How our maps draw the ground outside a city gate (guanxiang)

Every main-road gate of a city on our maps has a gate market outside it: at least six shops within about 1,560 ft of the gate, strung along the approach road, the outermost running off the edge of the map. A city may also draw a small tile works just beyond its built ground, on water, and draws no lime kiln near its wall.

  • Every main-road gate gets a gate market, not only the busiest.
    The study of Chinese walled cities finds a suburb outside at least one gate of nearly every city, and the largest in the direction of the main traffic. That each main gate earns one, at a scale that differs from gate to gate, is this project's choice, going beyond the study's floor of one gate.
  • In the drawn cities the southern gate, on the wider approach road, has the bigger market.
    The wider southern approach carries the bigger market, and the northern gate a row of stalls.
  • A river city's gate on the water has its wharf in place of a market.
    The whole wharf stands outside that gate, where a road gate has its market.
  • How many buildings does a gate market hold?
    Ten to forty at a busy gate, a GUESS the maps were calibrated against. Two counts stand above it. One is forty-eight horse inns outside a capital's gate on its trunk road north, one trade alone. The other is 124 houses in the entrance ward of Odawara, a Japanese castle town. A provincial capital's strip sits sensibly below both, which is where a thinned strip belongs.
  • The drawn strip is about as long as the one street of its kind we found measured.
    Nanguanxiang, a street district in the market town of Xiashi, in Haining, runs about 350 m (~1,148 ft). The drawn strip, within about 1,560 ft of the gate, is of the same order of length.
  • At least six shops stand within about 1,560 ft of each main gate.
    The floor is set below a busy gate's ten to forty because the drawn strip is thinned: about as long as a real gate street, it holds fewer buildings.
  • The outermost shops run off the edge of the map.
    The cut says the suburb continues past what the map shows - a drawing convention.
  • Each gate market has a lodging house.
    It stands in the strip outside the gate, among the shops; this is this project's choice. Who stayed over on market day is at The market-day flophouse - who actually stays over.
  • A city also draws a lodging house inside its walls.
    The gate strips do not carry all of a city's lodging; this one inside is this project's choice.
  • A gate kept only for sorties has no market.
    A purely military sally gate, opening onto empty field with no road, carries no market, no lodging house and no caravanserais. No city on our maps has one yet.

No city map draws kilns outside its wall yet; when one does, these are the rules. The evidence for them comes from Edo, in Japan.

  • A city may draw a small tile works just beyond its built ground, on water.
    It is drawn as a cluster of kiln houses rather than one shop, as at Imado, on the Sumida river, where tile firing was the specialty. No page we read gives how many kilns a provincial city kept; the small size we draw is a GUESS.
  • A city draws no lime kiln in its near hinterland.
    Edo's castle lime was burned by kiln owners in outlying villages and carried in. This rests on Edo alone; that a provincial city's lime also came from away is a GUESS by extension.
