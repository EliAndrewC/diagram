How our maps draw the swept ground around shrines and graves

Our maps claim no swept ground. Before 1868 the record shows the grave itself weeded, and no page we read attests a cleared band around a graveyard or a swept ground around a shrine's hall or its arches; what is attested of those is custom recorded in modern times, so the maps leave it out. The scrub of the commons and the reeds of a marsh are kept off only the ground a shrine or a grave plot itself occupies, and a shrine is marked by its grove, its approach (sando) and its gateway arches (torii).

  • The scrub around a shrine stops at the edge of its precinct, with no swept collar beyond it.
    The scrub keeps off the precinct's own ground, its buildings and its approach. How far the precinct's open ground reaches past the hall is a GUESS.
  • An arch clears only its own footing.
    No swept ground is drawn under or around a shrine's arches.
  • A grave plot keeps the scrub off its own ground, and no wider.
    The graves themselves are clear and the scrub runs up to the plot's edge; no cleared band is drawn around it. Other features are placed without regard to the plot's cleared ground.

A shrine's precinct is open ground reaching past its hall, and the edge of that open ground is drawn ragged.

  • The cleared ground's edge is drawn ragged because use made the clearing, not a survey.
    Open ground around a shrine is made by use - feet, the traffic of the approach, whatever tending it got - spreading outward from the feature, and its edge falls wherever that use peters out into the scrub; nobody surveys it. That is this project's reasoning, since no page we read says how far round a shrine or a grave the clearing reached or where it stopped. Ruled edges belong to what is surveyed or built - a wall, a paddy bund, a house plot - which is the same reasoning that gives the perimeter dike and the common burial ground their shapes.
  • The ragged edge of the cleared ground is notched inward, never lobed outward.
    The irregular edge is cut into the rectangle the ground would otherwise have: shallow notches, deeper along the sides than at the corners. Outward lobes were tried and rejected, for two reasons. Cleared ground is a claim of upkeep, so drawing it irregular should mean the upkeep falls short of the ideal, never that it annexes ground it does not reach. And a lobe can newly cover scrub or marsh that is already there, as a graveyard's lobe can reach a marsh at a slope's foot. Notches only make every patch a part of the rectangle, so nothing else on the map moves.
  • The ragged edge changes only the drawing, not where anything else is placed.
    The ground a shrine or a grave plot keeps clear of other features stays a plain rectangle, and the same feature always draws the same ragged edge.
