How did tea travel, and what did it cost at the end of the road?

In Rokugan tea is carried far from the hills where it grows, and a cup costs more the farther the leaf has gone. The findings below give what the historical record holds instead: how tea was packed and carried on three Chinese roads and one Japanese one, what the carrying cost, and what tea sold for at each end.

Tea for Tibet went up from Ya-chou in Sichuan on men's backs, about 150 miles in 17 or 18 days, and the porter was paid two-tenths of a tael a package, a tenth of the best tea's price at Ya-chou, where it was packed. Beyond Ta-chien-lu, the trading town on the Tibetan border, the cheaper bricks sold for two and a half to four times as much at Lhasa. Tea for Russia crossed the Gobi by camel and ox cart, about 800 miles, a camel taking 40 to 50 days, for about 2.5 taels per 100 catties, about a tenth of what one account says the chests cost. The Ming state traded tea for horses at rates it set itself. In Japan the shogun's tea came from Uji to Edo in a grand procession of jars, but no source read gives what it weighed or cost. China: tea on men's backs, Ya-chou to Ta-chien-lu

  • How far, and how long?
    About 150 miles (~240 km), walked in 17 or 18 days, by the porters William Woodville Rockhill (rockhill-1891-land-of-the-lamas) met on the road in 1889, carrying tea from Ya-chou and the towns near it up to Ta-chien-lu.
  • How was it packed?
    At the Ya-chou packing house Rockhill visited, which made the better grades, the leaves were steamed, rammed into long bamboo baskets in a wooden mold and dried, each package nominally 18 catties; the package, the pao, weighed 18 to 23 pounds (~8-10 kg). At Ta-chien-lu each pao was split into four bricks, nine bricks were sewn into a rawhide case, and two cases made a yak's load, Rockhill writes; his own footnote counts twelve bricks to the case, three packets of four, and puts the case at 45 pounds (~20 kg) on average.
  • What did a porter carry?
    Nine packages on average, 190 to 200 pounds (~86-91 kg), and some men 17 packages, over 300 pounds; women often seven or eight, and children of six or seven a package or a package and a half. Nearly all were peasants, who carried when there was nothing to do on their small farms, or when the farms were so small their women could work them.
  • What was he paid?
    Two mace, two-tenths of a tael of silver, a package, whatever it weighed, for the whole road from Ya-chou to Ta-chien-lu; they lived on maize cakes and vegetables. For an average load that is 1.8 taels a trip, about 0.1 tael for each day of the loaded walk, this project's arithmetic; the walk home is not counted, and Rockhill does not say what it took or paid.
  • And against the tea?
    Rockhill gives the best brick tea's price as 2 taels a pao at Ya-chou, though, he says, none of the brick tea could be bought there or anywhere east of Ta-chien-lu, so 2 taels is read here as the packing houses' price. Carrying a pao of it to Ta-chien-lu cost a tenth of that, this project's arithmetic. At Ta-chien-lu in 1889 a brick of the third grade, the one traded as money, sold for 0.24 tael, at Rockhill's rate of 0.32 tael to the rupee. The two prices are for different grades and units, so they make no pair.
  • How much went up?
    Ten to thirteen million pounds a year reached Ta-chien-lu for the Tibetan market, and on one stretch of about 15 miles Rockhill counted 481 porters carrying tea.
  • What did a laborer earn there?
    No day wage of the 1880s was found for Sichuan. The nearest unskilled wage in this record is from the account books of a fuel store in rural Beijing, converted by its historians to 0.024 tael a day in 1880, nine years earlier and far to the north, with food given besides; the nearest craftsman's is Zhili's official 0.12 to 0.15 tael of 1769, 120 years earlier (What did an unskilled laborer earn?, What did a skilled craftsman earn against a laborer?).

China: from Ta-chien-lu to Lhasa

  • What did a brick cost along the road?
    As Father Desgodins, a missionary on the border, priced the grades in 1883, quoted by Rockhill: the third grade, a brick of 2.5 pounds, sold for 10 annas at Ta-chien-lu, 1 rupee at Bat'ang, 1.5 to 2 rupees off the high road and 2 to 2.5 rupees at Lhasa; the fifth, nearly 5 pounds of mostly prunings, for 12 annas 6 pies, 1 rupee, 1.5 to 2 and 2 to 2.5; and the first, the best, 5.5 pounds, at "R. 1.4" at Ta-chien-lu and 3 to 4 rupees at Lhasa. At 16 annas to the rupee, the third grade cost 3.2 to 4 times as much at Lhasa as at Ta-chien-lu, and the fifth 2.6 to 3.2 times, this project's arithmetic.
  • Tea as money.
    The third-grade brick was the traders' money: a sword cost three bricks, a horse twenty packets of four, and workmen and servants were paid in bricks, Desgodins wrote. Where tea was weighed instead, the fifth grade was the medium.
  • And to the north?
    Tea from Ch'iung-chou, packed in packages of 50 catties, sold in the Tsaidam for about 10 taels of silver a package, Rockhill writes; he gives no price at its source.

China: the Ming state's tea for horses

  • How far did the tea go?
    The Ming set up tea-and-horse bureaus on the western frontier, and the tea-trading land ran over 5,000 li, from the Sichuan border to the Tibetan lands, in the words of the official history (mingshi-80-chafa). That is the extent of the trade, not one haul.
  • What did a horse cost in tea?
    At first 1,800 jin of tea a horse, which proved too much; under the founding emperor, before 1397, it was set at 120 jin for a top horse, 70 for a middling one and 50 for a colt. In 1490 a censor proposed raising 400,000 jin of tea to trade for 4,000 horses, about 100 jin a horse, and it was adopted. These are the state's rates, not a market's.
  • How was it licensed and packed?
    At first a merchant's license covered 100 jin of tea for a fee of 200 cash; later the fee was set at 1,000 cash for the same 100 jin. The Tibetans could not judge weights and bargained by the basket, the bi, so in 1515 a censor set 1,000 jin to 330 baskets, about 3 jin a basket, this project's arithmetic.
  • What was it worth in silver?
    Between 1467 and 1490, in a famine, the state commuted 100 jin of tea at 0.5 tael of silver. No Ming laborer's wage is in this record; the nearest are craftsmen's 0.03 to 0.06 tael a day in a Ming military costing that names no place or year (What did arrows cost?), against which 100 jin at that rate was 8 to 17 craftsman-days, this project's arithmetic.

China: by camel to Kyakhta

  • How was it carried?
    On camels or in one-ox carts. A camel carried no more than 300 catties (400 pounds, ~180 kg) and took 40 to 50 days for the 804 miles (~1,290 km) from Kalgan to Kiakhta on the Russian border, Harry Parkes (parkes-1854-kyakhta-caravan) reported in 1854. The encyclopedia gives the whole road in stages: Kyakhta to Urga, 170 miles, where carts could be used; Urga to Kalgan across the Gobi by camel caravan, with ox carts for heavy loads, 625 miles in about 30 days, though it says elsewhere that the crossing took at least 30 to 40; and Kalgan to Beijing, 100 miles in about four days by mule.
  • What did it cost to carry?
    From 1.5 to 3.5 taels for 100 catties for the whole crossing, by the scarcity of beasts, and most usually 2.5 taels. That is about 7.5 taels for a camel's 300 catties at the usual rate, and about 0.3 tael per 100 catties per 100 miles, against about 0.7 on the Ya-chou porters' backs, this project's arithmetic.
  • And against the tea?
    One account put the chests at 50 catties and 18 dollars each, which Parkes reckons nearly 25 taels a picul of 100 catties; others gave chests of 40, 60 and 90 catties. The bricks for Russia weighed about 2.75 pounds on average, some 3 to 4, and cost 16 to 22 pence each. So the usual freight was about a tenth of the chests' cost, this project's arithmetic. Parkes gives no price in money at Kiakhta: money was banned there, and commissioners of both sides set the price of each import and of the tea to be given for it in barter, with brick tea the unit of value. Among the Mongols the only real currency was very coarse brick tea, a traveler of 1863 found.
  • What did a laborer earn there?
    No wage at Kalgan or on the road was found. The nearest unskilled wage is the rural Beijing fuel store's, 94 copper cash a day in 1850, about 100 miles from Kalgan; the nearest craftsman's is Beijing's official list of 1736, 154 cash against a laborer's 75, over a century earlier (What did an unskilled laborer earn?, What did a skilled craftsman earn against a laborer?).

Japan: the shogun's tea from Uji

  • Who carried it?
    The buyer: the Uji tea masters grew the tea and filled the jars, and carrying them was the buyer's affair; the shogunate made its carriage a show of power, the tea-jar procession.
  • How did it travel?
    From 1633 to 1866 the envoys left Edo about the fourth month and were back about midsummer, and at its largest the procession passed 1,000 people. The route changed over the years, the encyclopedia says, but more than 100 empty jars went out by the Tokaido, were filled at Uji with the finest tea, and came back toward Edo by the Nakasendo and the Koshu road; until about 1700 they were then stored in Kai province and reached Edo only after the summer. A road handout dates the change: the return went by the Nakasendo until 1737, while the jars were stored in Kai, and by the Tokaido from the next year, sometimes leaving it to avoid the Shichiri and Imagiri ferries, because the jars disliked damp. In 1867, with no procession, the jars went to Edo as plain baggage handed from station to station by relay porters.
  • What did it weigh and cost?
    No source read gives the tea's weight, the cost of carrying it or its price.
