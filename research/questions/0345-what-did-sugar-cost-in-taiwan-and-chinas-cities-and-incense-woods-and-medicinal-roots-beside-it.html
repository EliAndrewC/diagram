In Rokugan a tariff of typically about 15% of the declared sales value is charged at the gate of the city where goods are sold, and the setting's price ledger reckons a good sold far from where it was made at its price there plus freight, a 15% tariff for each border and a merchant's margin. The findings below give what the historical record of Qing China holds for sugar, and for spices, incense woods and medicinal roots: what each cost where it was made, what it cost in the city or port that bought it, and how seldom the two were found together.

For sugar no price where it was made was found beside a Chinese city's price of the same year. There is a producer's price in Taiwan with the freight to Suzhou about 1723, city prices alone in Shanghai and Beijing, and one cargo with both ends, bought in Taiwan and sold in Japan in 1871 for 1.77 times its price. Sugar: Taiwan, Guangdong and the cities

  • What did sugar cost in Taiwan, and to ship to Suzhou?
    About 1723 the official Huang Shujing (huang-shujing-taihai-shichalu) wrote in his notes on Taiwan that its three counties made some 600,000 baskets of cane sugar a year, of 170 or 180 jin each; black sugar was worth 0.8 or 0.9 tael for 100 jin and white 1.3 or 1.4, and the freight to Suzhou was a little over 0.2 tael a basket. At 37 grams of silver to the tael and 100 jin taken as a picul of 60.5 kg, that is about 0.5 and 0.8 grams of silver a kg, and freight of about a twelfth of white sugar's price and a seventh to an eighth of black's, this project's arithmetic. No Suzhou price of those years was found, so no ratio is drawn.
  • How did it travel?
    By sea junk. Buyers contracted for the sugar before it was made and loaded it at once, but the ships had to gather into convoys, which took several tens of days, and put in at Xiamen for the customs, each ship passing two escorts and eight inspections, none without a fee; so, Huang writes, freight was dear and sugar cheap. By Xiamen the passage is about 1,070 km in two straight lines, which makes the freight about 0.7 gram of silver for 100 kg carried 100 km, this project's arithmetic.
  • What did sugar cost in the cities?
    One end only. In Ye Mengzhu's Shanghai county the best white sugar was 3 or 4 fen (hundredths of a tael) a jin of old and again under the Kangxi emperor (reigned from 1662), then 5 or 6 fen, and fell to 3 and then 2 in a winter the text dates by a reign year that is 1681 and a cyclical year that is 1683, with yellow and black sugar about 1 fen. Cane sugar came from Jiangxi and Lingnan, the far south: when the Guangdong troops rebelled in 1676 and the road through Ji'an in Jiangxi was cut, the price jumped, and cane was then planted in his own county, whose sugar stayed cheaper than the traded kind though never of the best white. At Peking in 1821 white sugar candy was 130 copper cash a jin and brown 120. Shanghai's white sugar at its price of about 1680 is about four times Taiwan's of about 1723, this project's arithmetic, but the two are some forty years apart and are not a pair; no Guangdong or Fujian price was found beside a city's.
  • One cargo with both ends: Takow to Yokohama, 1871.
    The trading house Jardine Matheson bought 5,256 piculs of brown sugar at Takow (now Kaohsiung) in southern Taiwan in July 1871 at $2.43 a picul and sold it at Yokohama three months later at $4.30, 1.77 times the buying price, as the economic historian Ku Hui-wen (ku-2022-taiwan-sugar) reckons from the house's own invoice and sales account. At 26.6 grams of silver to the dollar that is about 1.1 and 1.9 grams of silver a kg, over about 2,350 km of sea in a straight line, this project's arithmetic. The buyer is a Japanese port, so it says nothing of a Chinese city's price.
  • Where did that cargo's gap go?
    Before it sailed the sugar stood at $3.06 a picul: storage, packing, boats and porters had added about $0.30, and export duty, commission and insurance $0.32. At Yokohama 4,937 piculs were left, 6% less by this project's arithmetic; the charges there were $3,441.61, of which the ship's freight was $2,160.72 and the import duty $522.39; and the account closed $1,706.08 ahead on sales of $21,237.62, a gross margin of 8%. Freight was so about $0.44 on each picul sold, under a quarter of the $1.87 between the two prices, this project's arithmetic. The house's fourteen such accounts of 1870 to 1880 averaged a gross margin of 5%, some of them losses.

Spices, incense woods and medicinal roots

  • No pair of prices was found for any of them.
    The nearest are city prices alone. For incense woods, Shanghai's sandalwood was no more than 0.2 tael a jin in the winter of 1679, after 2 or 3 taels early in the Shunzhi reign (1644 to 1661), and Beijing's list of 1736 has white sandalwood at 0.48 tael a jin and aloeswood at 4 taels. For medicinal roots, a jin of the drug yujin (a turmeric-like root, by this project's gloss) was worth no more than 0.8 tael in Shanghai in 1686. For spices no pair was found.
