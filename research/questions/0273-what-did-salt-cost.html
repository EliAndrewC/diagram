What did salt cost?

Every household in Rokugan buys salt, and the setting judges a price by how many days of a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give what salt cost in the villages of Edo-period Japan and under the salt monopoly of Qing China, each beside the nearest wages found.

In Kashimo, an upland village of Mino, a household bought salt from peddlers at the door for about 17 to 20 mon a sho from 1763 to 1772 and about 31 to 33 in 1773 and 1774, the year it paid a hired farm hand 60 mon a day. In Qing China salt cost a copper cash or two a jin at the Huai saltworks, and the state fixed its price in the Changlu district at about a hundredth of a tael a jin; on Beijing's official list of 1736 a laborer's day bought about 8 jin of salt and a craftsman's about 16.

Japan

  • What did salt cost in a mountain village?
    The household of Hikoshichi in Kashimo bought its salt each year, mostly in the first and second months, from salt sellers who came to the village. By its diary, as the booklet that tables it gives the purchases, it paid 100 mon for 6 sho in 1763, 5 sho in 1765, 5 sho 5 go in 1768 and 5 sho 6 go in 1772; in 1773 the price jumped to 3 to 3.2 sho for 100 mon, and stayed there in 1774. That is about 17 to 20 mon a sho from 1763 to 1772 and 31 to 33 in 1773-1774, by this project's arithmetic.
  • How was it bought?
    By bargaining at the door: on one day of 1772 the household's second son and a seller from Kutami settled on 5 sho 6 go for 100 mon, and the seller was paid 551 mon for 3 to 1 sho (31 sho; a to was ten sho); before noon another seller came to the house and sold 2 to 1 sho (21 sho) at 5 sho 7 go for 100 mon. When the price rose in 1773, the diary says it had become far too dear.
  • And beside a laborer's day?
    In 1774 the same household paid a man who worked its autumn fields for 27 days a wage reckoned at 60 mon a day. At that year's price a day's wage bought about 1.9 sho of salt, by this project's arithmetic; at the price of 1763, eleven years earlier, it would have bought 3.6.
  • And beside a craftsman's?
    The diary as the booklet gives it names no craftsman's day wage. The nearest found in mon is a village carpenter's, 200 mon agreed by thirty-nine villages in 1818 (read here as a day's, since their rule names no unit), more than forty years later and in another province (What did a small shrine sanctuary cost to build? gives the place).
  • What did a village dealer pay, and charge?
    In 1842 the village of Otsukawa, in the Chita district, reported its prices to the authorities in answer to an order to lower them. For salt in bales of 9 sho, it bought at 45½ bales for a ryo of gold and sold at 18 mon a sho. That is about 410 sho for a ryo bought, by this project's arithmetic. The part of the list the museum shows gives no rate between the ryo and the mon, so the dealer's margin is not computed.
  • And beside the wages of 1842?
    The museum shows only the list's food prices, and no wage among them. The nearest unskilled wage is a farm day laborer's of the same year in Nagata, a village now in the city of Oamishirasato, set at 132 mon by the shogunate's official list (What did sake cost, by the shop and by the cup?); at Otsukawa's price it would buy about 7 sho, by this project's arithmetic, though the wage and the price are from different villages. The nearest craftsman's is the village carpenter's 200 mon of 1818, read as a day's, twenty-four years earlier.
  • Did salt cost more inland?
    One account says so: 5 or 6 mon a sho on the Inland Sea, about 10 carried to the Tokai and Edo, about 15 on the Sanriku coast, doubling within two ri inland and tripling within three, 120 mon in Yonezawa and 300 at most in Ichinoseki. It is a blog citing a published history of salt, with no year for any price, and it says only that the prices are said to have been so.
  • What did salt cost in Edo, Ako salt itself, or a bale at the Edo wholesalers?
    No price was found for any of them.

China: the price at the saltworks and the official price

  • What did salt cost where it was made?
    A cash or two a jin at the Huai saltworks. Tao Shu, put in charge of the Huai salt district, arguing about 1830 against taxing salt at the saltworks of the Huai district, said salt there was worth only 1 or 2 cash a jin. His rules for selling Huaibei salt on tickets set 400 jin to a yin, at 0.64 tael and 1.88 with the fees; the 0.64 was the salt itself, reckoned at the current price in cash; that is 0.0016 tael a jin, or 0.0047 with the fees, by this project's arithmetic.
  • What did the state fix it at?
    For the Changlu district, from 1688, 0.0104 to 0.0126 tael a jin. A salt censor wrote in 1728 that under Kangxi, when a tael bought 1,400 to 1,500 cash, a jin of salt was 16 cash; now a tael bought 2,000 cash and the price had stayed the same, some selling as low as 13 or 14. In 1800 the Jiaqing emperor, refusing to raise the price, said a person spent only 1 or 2 cash a day on salt.
  • What did salt cost in Beijing, beside a day's wage?
    The official price list of 1736 set salt at 0.012 tael a jin, in the same compilation that set a laborer's day at 75 cash and a craftsman's at 154, which the list's historian converts to about 0.094 and 0.19 tael. A laborer's day bought about 8 jin of salt and a craftsman's about 16, by this project's arithmetic. The same historian sets beside it a Ming price of 0.006 to 0.007 tael a jin, taken from two Ming books, the Wanli kuaiji lu and the Wanshu zaji.
  • What did it sell for near the sea?
    In his home prefecture by the sea, whose counties he names as Hua, Lou, Qing and Shanghai (Songjiang, by this record's identification), Ye Mengzhu recorded salt at up to 0.1 tael a jin in the spring of 1651 and 0.06 to 0.07 after the fourth month; about 0.01 tael after that; 0.032 in the spring of 1683, when all salt was official salt brought from the prefectural and county towns and private salt had vanished; and no more than 0.006 or 0.007 in 1688. He gives no wage.

China: how far the price rose on the way

  • What did a yin cost by the time it reached its market?
    Before Tao Shu's reform, the cost of a yin of Huaibei salt reached more than 10 taels by the time it had been shipped to its selling district. This is the merchants' cost, not a consumer's price, and the study does not say that the yin of the old system weighed the same 400 jin, so no markup is computed from it.
  • And to the consumer?
    A historian of smuggled salt puts official salt at a few cash a jin at the saltworks and 30 to 50, even 60 to 70, cash in the consumer's hands, and quotes the historian Xu Hong that salt worth no more than a cash a jin at the saltworks cost 60 or 70 cash a jin when shipped to its selling district. Neither figure carries a year.
  • What did a visitor find in 1909?
    F. H. King, traveling in China in 1909, was told that salt usually cost 1½ to 4 cash a catty to make and sold at retail for three-quarters of a cent to 3 cents a pound, which he reckoned at twelve to fifteen times the cost; he names no place for either figure. In Sichuan he gives a cost of 13 to 14 cash a catty to make, with 9 cash of tax, and no retail price. The wages he gives for the Tianjin region are 50 cents a day in Mexican dollars for a master mechanic and 18 for a journeyman; since he does not say whose cents his retail price is in, salt is not set against them.
