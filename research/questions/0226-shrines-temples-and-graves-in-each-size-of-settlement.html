Shrines, temples and graves in each size of settlement

In this setting, the clergy a settlement keeps follows its size. Every village district has a country monk, every county town at least one preceptor and every provincial city at least one provincial abbot, all of them overseen by the Ministry of Rites; the preceptors serve in town monasteries, the abbots in the cities' temples. A village's shrine, a town's monasteries and a city's temples follow from that ladder.

Historical Japanese and Chinese settlements kept more than this ladder gives, and varied more from place to place.

  • Did a hamlet keep a shrine of its own?
    Modern counts give many small communities a shrine of their own, but those counts reflect a wealthier, even post-industrial society. A hamlet kept no shrine of its own, only a household shrine in the odd house (a hamlet's shrine).
  • Villages kept no Buddhist temple, one, or several.
    When the Tokugawa state made every household register with a temple, not every village had one that could take parishioners, and many households chose a temple by nearness. Some villages kept one or more of their own (a village's temple).
  • The Ming statutes ordered an altar to the gods of soil and grain for every hundred rural households.
    The statute, as a modern historian quotes it, set up one altar within every hundred households in the countryside.
  • A county town kept a shrine of its own for its district.
    Japan's rule of 1871 fixed about one district shrine, ranked above the village shrines, for each household-register district of some twenty villages (Shrines in towns and cities).
  • A city kept a principal shrine.
    Edo kept the Sanno shrine for the whole town, the peak of its shrines (Shrines in towns and cities).
  • A Chinese county seat kept the state cult's altars and its City God temple.
    Under the Qing, wherever a local government office stood there was a City God temple (the state cult).

The dead, too, lay and burned in grounds that followed the place they came from.

  • A hamlet's dead could lie at its own edge.
    In one Wakasa hamlet the burial ground stood at the hamlet's edge, while the graves its families visited stood in the temples inside it (where a village buries its dead).
  • A village could keep a graveyard of its own.
    Japanese has a name for a graveyard that a village's inhabitants held in common and managed themselves (where a village buries its dead).
  • Where the Jodo sect spread, a settlement often burned its own dead.
    In regions of the Jodo sect, cremation grounds that commoners set up themselves, as a settlement's shared facility, stand out (cremation grounds).
  • The dead were burned away from the houses.
    They were burned in open country away from where people lived (cremation grounds).
  • Did a village keep a mound for gathered bones?
    No page we read puts one in a village before modern times.

The smallest shrines were not kept by rank at all; they stood at particular places, whatever the size of the settlement.

  • The roadside guardian deity (dosojin) was enshrined at boundaries.
    It stood at a village's entrance and exit, a mountain pass, a crossroads and the foot of a bridge, most often as a stone monument or a stone image.
  • China's counterpart to the dosojin was the earth god.
    A count from the gazetteers of the Yangzi delta found about one earth-god temple to every ten-odd lowland villages, a figure the historian reporting it doubts as too low (Wayside shrines and stone figures (dosojin, jizo)).
  • A small shrine could stand near a well.
    At Mikurahana a dragon-god shrine, a shrine to a water deity rather than to the way, stands near a named well, and the people of Amasegawa keep a day of their own for its festival.
