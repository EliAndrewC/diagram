Staff rowhouses and barracks (nagaya)

The junior officials of a shogunate intendant's office lived in the huts and rowhouses (nagaya) that were their official quarters, and a lord's foot soldiers and lower retainers lived in rowhouses too. A rowhouse was a long, narrow building of one story, divided along its length into small dwellings, each an earth-floored entry and a raised plank floor. The rowhouses that were measured run about 80 to 145 ft long and 12 to 24 ft deep. The historian Mitamura Engyo (mitamura-nagaya-tcpip) traced the form to the soldiers' lodging huts at the foot of the Warring States mountain castles.

Where an office's staff lived, on its grounds or in a town or district of their own, is at Where an office's staff lived.

Five rowhouses of warriors and officials survive or are measured: Takayama's storehouse keepers' rowhouse, two rows of foot soldiers' dwellings, and the gate ranges of an intendant's office and a lord's seat, whose gateway ran through the rowhouse itself (The main gate and its gatekeepers (nagaya-mon)). The plans of an office and of a lord's seat give the size of a dwelling in their rowhouses.

  • The gate rowhouses of the intendant's office at Omori run 33 m (~108 ft) at a width of about 4 m (~13 ft).
    The office governed the Iwami silver mine and some 150 villages around it, and its front gate survives with the gate rowhouses attached on its left and right.
  • The rowhouse gate of the Kaibara jin'ya is 13.5 ken long by 2 ken deep (~81 by 12 ft).
    Kaibara was the seat of a lord of 20,000 koku. The text of the site's designation as a national historic site gives these measurements. The city's own description gives it as about 13 ken long.
  • The Kaibara rowhouse gate was divided into rooms on either side of its gateway.
    The national designation text gives it three rooms of eight tatami mats (about 12 ft square) on one side, and one such room and an earth-floored room on the other. The city's own description names them: a horse-viewing room and a gun store on the side of the three rooms, and a guardroom and the earth-floored room on the other. How many of the three rooms the gun store took is not recorded.
  • The Shibata domain's foot soldiers' row is 43.6 m (~143 ft) long and 7.3 m (~24 ft) deep.
    The national record of cultural properties dates it to 1842, late in the Edo period, and says the domain's lower-ranking warriors lived in it, eight households joined in one building.
  • Who lived in the Shibata domain's foot soldiers' row?
    The national record names the building a foot soldiers' rowhouse and its residents lower-ranking warriors. A local history page says they were not foot soldiers but retainers of lower status still: by post, servants of the gate-guard, flag-bearer and other companies, and rope men.
  • In the Obata domain's foot soldiers' row, each dwelling is 2 ken wide and 3.5 ken deep (~12 by 21 ft).
    Each is one living room, an entrance and a kitchen, the smallest unit of dwelling. A row held ten of them, and two such rows stood, east and west.
  • At the Sakai town magistracy, a doshin's dwelling was 2 ken across the beams by 3 ken along the ridge (~12 by 18 ft), one to a man.
    The magistracy's plans draw its doshin in a rowhouse of uniform dwellings, each that size.
  • At the Sayama domain's jin'ya, the dwellings of its middle and lower retainers' rowhouses were all 2.5 ken (~15 ft) deep, and 4 to 6 ken (~24 to 36 ft) long by rank.
    The domain's plans give 6 ken to the pages (chukosho), 5.5 to those ranked with the pages (chukosho-nami), 5 to the escort company and 4 to the foot samurai (kachi); Uematsu Kiyoshi (sayama-jinya-uematsu) counts the pages a middle rank and the escort company and foot samurai lower.
  • Takayama's restored storehouse keepers' rowhouse has a floor area of 342.83 m² (~3,690 sq ft).
    A Gifu prefecture document lists its floor area with the date of its restoration, March 1992.
  • How big was a staff rowhouse?
    On these measurements, 12 to 24 ft deep and 80 to 145 ft long, built as a row of bays about 12 to 18 ft wide: the Obata dwelling's 2 ken, the Sakai doshin's 3, and the Shibata row's length shared among its eight households, on this project's arithmetic. A retainer of higher rank had more: Sayama's dwellings ran 4 to 6 ken (~24 to 36 ft) along the row.
  • What was a staff rowhouse built of?
    A country jin'ya's staff rows could be thatched. A table of the Sayama domain's dwellings gives its rowhouses a roof thatched with wheat straw, and a lean-to that the modern author reads as pantile, a roof of interlocking clay tiles; the table's year is not given. The Obata foot soldiers' row, as it stands today after its repairs, is timber and of one story, with a hipped roof (sloping on all four sides) of thatch now covered in copper sheet, and earth walls on its main part. The Shibata row of 1842 has a hipped thatched roof. A gate rowhouse could be tiled: the Kaibara jin'ya's has a hip-and-gable roof of pantiles. Of the four rows' pages, only Obata's gives its walls, earth walls in their present state; none gives the walls or the frame of an intendant's office's staff row, and the Chinese pages we read are silent on what a yamen's clerks' and runners' quarters were built of.

Inside, a warriors' rowhouse was divided into dwellings, though the servants below the foot soldiers shared large rooms.

  • The historian Mitamura Engyo gives a warrior's rowhouse a standard dwelling of 9 shaku by 2 ken (~9 by 12 ft).
    A page quoting his collected works has him call it the middle rowhouse, where a lord's men below full samurai rank lived, down to the foot soldiers: one ken at the entrance an earth floor for taking off shoes, the other a raised plank floor.
  • Mitamura traced the rowhouse dwelling to the soldiers' lodging huts at the foot of the Warring States mountain castles.
    That origin is his own reading. He cites a war tale, the Dobora Gunki, on the servants' room at Okuwa castle: a rowhouse dwelling so short that a spear hung inside it had its butt sticking outside.
  • Three retainers on duty in Edo late in the Edo period shared one dwelling of their domain's rowhouse.
    In 1860 Sakai Hanshiro, a retainer of the Wakayama domain, lived in the duty rowhouse of his domain's Edo residence at Akasaka with his uncle and a colleague, the two juniors cooking by turns.
  • The chugen, servants below the foot soldiers, lived together in large rooms.
    The attendants below the foot soldiers were organized in groups under a head, and in their lord's residence lived communally in large rooms such as the chugen room.
  • A samurai's gate range held a room for the chugen beside its gateway.
    In a samurai's own residence the gate range held rooms such as the gatekeeper's room and the chugen room, in its parts on either side of the gateway, and they lodged retainers and servants.
  • How big were a lord's common rooms, and how many men shared one?
    No page we read says.
  • How many men shared one dwelling of a staff rowhouse?
    No page we read says how many unmarried men usually did. The pages count a row's dwellings as households, and the Obata domain's plan of its grounds writes twenty names in all on the rowhouse's site, where two rows of ten dwellings stood - one name to a dwelling, on this project's arithmetic, which fits a single man to a dwelling as well as a family. The Edo duty rowhouse of 1860 held three men to one, and the Sakai magistracy's plans give each doshin a dwelling of his own.
  • Did a rowhouse before modern times have beds or bunks?
    No page we read puts a bed, a bunk or a sleeping shelf in a premodern rowhouse or servants' room.
  • When did beds come into a Japanese barracks?
    The earliest barracks we found with beds is a modern army's: an infantry barracks built at Nagoya in 1873, whose squad rooms are shown today furnished with wooden beds. It stood at Nagoya until it was taken down in 1963, and its page does not say when the beds were put in, so 1873 dates the building, not the beds. When beds first came into a Japanese barracks we did not find.
