Did the Tokugawa restrictions on foreign trade stop new crops reaching Japan?

Rokugan's history has gaijin merchants admitted as far as the limits of port cities, and then forbidden to enter on pain of death. The findings below give what the historical record holds for new crops under Japan's own restrictions on foreign trade.

Japan's shogunate sharply cut its foreign contacts in edicts of the 1630s, but the dated record of new plants does not stop there. Plants are recorded for two centuries afterward on Chinese and Dutch ships and from Ryukyu to Satsuma, and in the 1720s from Korea through Tsushima. No source read says the edicts stopped them. Whether the edicts slowed them is another matter, and the one dated list read cannot say. The crops that came before the edicts are told in when tobacco was first grown, when the sweet potato was first grown and when the white potato was first grown.

The restrictions

  • The reference works date the restrictions differently.
    The one page read gathers several dictionaries, whose entries give:
    • A series of edicts from 1633 to 1639.
    • A policy running from 1639 to the arrival of Perry in 1853.
    • A policy established in 1641, when the Dutch were moved to Dejima at Nagasaki.
  • Some foreign contact stayed open.
    One entry says only Chinese and Dutch trading visits and trade with Koreans through Tsushima went on. Another adds diplomatic relations with Ryukyu.

The dates below are from a scholar's table of plants reaching Japan, unless another source is named. Its dates are often the first time a plant is noticed, not the day it landed.

Crops recorded before the edicts

  • The American crops in this list were recorded in Japan before the edicts.
    In order:
    • The white potato in 1576, which the table doubts as too early, and 1598 on another account.
    • Pumpkin seed in 1579, and maize in the same year on a Portuguese ship to Nagasaki.
    • Tobacco and chili in 1592-95, by one tradition, and tobacco seed imported and grown at Nagasaki in 1599.
    • The sweet potato, in the table's row for 1611, brought home by Satsuma men who had been on campaign in Ryukyu.

After the edicts, the same table keeps naming plants, though it centers on garden plants.

Plants recorded after the edicts

  • China and the Dutch kept sending plants.
    The named cases are:
    • Hardy begonia from China to Nagasaki in 1641, spreading from 1644-47.
    • A living betel palm on a Dutch ship in 1720, and a bramble on a Chinese ship in 1721.
    • Thirty coconuts on a Dutch ship in 1725, at the shogunate's order. They were planted in the Izu islands and Suruga, and they died.
  • Korean ginseng came through Tsushima on the shogunate's request.
    Tsushima domain sent three live roots in 1721 and, by 1728, six consignments of 35 roots and 60 fruits in all. Cultivation was mastered by the end of the Kyoho era.
  • Ryukyu also supplied Satsuma.
    The Ryukyu wax tree came from Ryukyu to Satsuma around the Enpo era, 1673-80, and later spread across Kyushu.
  • The tomato and the peanut are the American food plants named here that are first dated after the edicts.
    The tomato was sketched in 1668 and 1703, and its route is disputed in when the tomato became common. A work of 1675 says the peanut had "recently" come, with no route.
  • Cabbage is said to have come first as an ornamental, and as food only later.
    An agriculture ministry page says the Dutch brought it to Nagasaki in the Edo period, with no year, and that at this time it did not settle as a vegetable and was grown as a flowerbed ornamental. The same page dates edible cabbage to the mid-19th century. The table's only cabbage date is 1860, in seed brought home by the embassy to America.
  • The shogunate itself sought plants in the first half of the 1700s.
    Under the shogun Yoshimune's policy on medicines, a variety of medicinal plants were obtained from China and Korea.
  • New arrivals surged in the 1800s, before the ports opened.
    The table's author says those from Ryukyu and south China stand out until about 1829 and that Dutch ships' cargoes seem to be many after that. After the ports opened in 1859, American and European garden plants came in a flood.

What the edicts did

  • Did the edicts stop new plants reaching Japan? No source read says so.
    Dated plants continue after the edicts on the Chinese and Dutch ships and from Ryukyu to Satsuma, and in the 1720s through Tsushima, which are the routes the reference entries leave open.
  • Did they slow new plants down?
    The sources read cannot say. The table centers on garden ornamentals, with some vegetables and fruit. Many of its dates are first notices, so a count of its rows would measure notice, not arrivals, and none is given here.
