Did English traveling players gather their money at the door or pass a hat?

Rokugan's actors and storytellers are paid by its own customs, and nothing here sets one of them. This is a comparative question: how English players of Shakespeare's time took their audience's money, a yardstick for anyone at the table who has heard that English players passed a hat.

English players took their money at the door. A touring company playing in an inn yard kept a man at the gate who took the money as people came in, and London's theaters charged a penny at the door and more at each further door. No source of the period we read says a hat was passed before the play's end.

  • At an inn yard, the money was taken at the gate while the play went on.
    On 15 June 1583 the Queen's Men, a company only a month old, played in the yard of the Red Lion inn at Norwich. While the players were playing, a man named Winsdon tried to come in at the gate without paying until he was inside, and fought with the gatekeeper; in the struggle he spilt the money out of the gatekeeper's hand, and Bentley, who played the Duke, hearing of the fight at the gate, went off the stage. That the gatekeeper was still taking money after the play had begun is this page's reading of the two depositions together; neither says so in words.
  • London's theaters took a penny at each door.
    Thomas Platter wrote in his diary in 1599 that a playgoer paid one penny to stand, a second penny at a further door to sit, and a third at another door for a cushion in the best place. Collectors called gatherers are thought to have stood at the theater's entrances with sealed money boxes, smashed to get the takings out, and Ben Jonson in 1614 wrote of a playgoer who would "drop but sixpence at the door".
  • What went round during the play was food and drink.
    Platter says that in the pauses food and drink were carried round among the audience, for each to buy at his own cost.
  • Was a hat passed before the end of the play?
    No source of the period we read says so. The nearest is a historian's view of 1912, W. J. Lawrence's, that "preliminary payment at the door and secondary 'gathering' in the galleries" were habits the playhouses kept from the inn yards; it is his reading, not a document of the time, and it does not say when in the play the gathering came. Platter's further pennies, paid at further doors, are the period's own account of a second payment.

The penny beside a day's wage

  • A penny was a sixth of a skilled man's usual day, a third of a thresher's.
    A university teaching page on Shakespeare's England gives a skilled journeyman in the city up to a shilling a day, more usually half that, with his food and drink; thatchers and hedgers about sixpence a day; threshers and reapers half that; and the actors of Philip Henslowe's company a shilling a day. It gives the same penny for standing in the pit, the level ground before the stage, two pence for the galleries, and twelve pence to the shilling. The shares are this page's arithmetic. The teaching page names no source for its wages and dates them only to Shakespeare's time, and its thatchers and threshers are farm workers, not Londoners.
