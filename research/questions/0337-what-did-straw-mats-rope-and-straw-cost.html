What did straw mats, rope and straw cost?

The setting's notes name rope, sandals and mats among the things a household buys and give no price for a straw mat, a bundle of rope or a load of straw. The findings below give what the historical record holds on their prices in Edo-period Japan; they were gathered as the nearest neighbors of the empty charcoal bale.

A straw mat cost 29 mon in Edo's relief-shed account of 1806 and 25 mon in a Mino village early in 1766. In Edo's price returns of 1842 Kazusa mats were 0.31 to 0.43 monme of silver each, a third to a half of a day laborer's pay that year. No price was found for an empty rice bale or for straw sandals. Rice bales, straw mats, rope and sandals

  • Empty rice bales: traded, with no price found.
    The order of 1859 for Edo castle (Were empty bales bought and sold?) names empty rice bales and leaves their price to private agreement. No price for an empty bale stated to be a rice bale was found.
  • A straw mat cost 29 mon in Edo in 1806, and 25 mon in a Mino village early in 1766.
    The relief-shed account of 1806 entered 1,150 straw mats at "29 mon each", beside the empty bales at 12. At Kashimo village in Mino a household bought 8 mats for 200 mon in the last days of the lunar year Meiwa 2, early in 1766, 25 mon each by this project's arithmetic; the same household paid a hired farm hand 60 mon a day in 1774, eight years later (What did salt cost?), so a mat was about four-tenths of that day.
  • Rope cost 67 mon a bundle in Edo in 1806.
    The same account entered 370 bundles of rope, for tying the sheds, at "67 mon a bundle"; it gives no length or weight for a bundle. The Hanno contractor's bundles of 1763 (Who made charcoal bales, and of what) are a different measure at about 3 mon.
  • What did straw sandals cost?
    No price, and no count of a day's output, was found.
  • How was a tax-rice bale tied?
    The register of Seki village in Musashi, which its printed edition heads 1720, says its castle rice went 3 to and 7 sho to the bale (37 sho), the bale tied in five places with standing rope at ten and its mouths closed in the way the register calls cha-kagari, and went to the granary at Asakusa by packhorse. It gives no price for the bale and no time to make one.

Edo's mats, matting and straw in the price returns of 1842

What Edo's price returns of 1842 are, and their charcoal, is at Edo's charcoal by the bale, 1842.

  • Kazusa mats cost 3 to 4.3 monme of silver the ten.
    The mat return lists full-size mats of Kazusa under five brands at 4.34, 4.07, 3.81, 3.5 and 3.25 monme of silver for ten, and mid-size mats at 3.08; it enters no reduced price. A mat was 0.31 to 0.43 monme, by this project's arithmetic.
  • How much of a day's pay was a mat?
    The Mitsui house's Edo books paid a day laborer 0.88 and 0.91 monme of silver in 1842 (Edo's charcoal by the bale, 1842). By that wage a Kazusa mat was about a third to a half of a day, by this project's arithmetic.
  • Two dealers priced old mats beside the new season's.
    Two mat dealers of Isecho returned what they had sold mats for in 1840 and in 1841, the mats of the new season and "old" mats brand by brand: in 1840 the new full-size mats at 5.85 down to 4.5 monme the ten and the old at 4.9 down to 3.9; in 1841 the new at 6 down to 4.65 and the old at 5 down to 4.1. Brand for brand the old sold at 83 to 92 percent of the new, by this project's arithmetic.
  • Were the old mats used mats?
    The returns do not say. They head the two kinds "new and old mats", call the new "newly arrived" and list the old under the same brands as the new; whether the old had been used or were earlier stock is not stated. They are therefore not offered here as a price for used straw goods.
  • Straw sold by the ten bundles, and thin matting by the ten sheets.
    The straw and matting return prices straw from the Ayase river side of Musashi at 240, 140 and 80 mon the ten bundles for top, middle and low, cut to 224, 132 and 72, and straw from the Tama river side at 164, 100 and 48 mon, cut to 153, 90 and 45. Thin matting for tatami was 372, 348 and 248 mon the ten sheets, cut to 348, 324 and 232, and a second kind of matting 212, 184 and 164 mon, cut to 200, 172 and 156. That is about 5 to 24 mon a bundle of straw and 16 to 37 mon a sheet of matting before the cut, by this project's arithmetic; the return gives no size or weight for a bundle. With these in copper and the day laborer's pay in silver, no count of days is given.
