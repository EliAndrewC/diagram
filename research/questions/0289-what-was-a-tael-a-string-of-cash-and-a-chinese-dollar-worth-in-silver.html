What was a tael, a string of cash and a Chinese dollar worth in silver?

Rokugan's money rules turn a price into silver. The findings below give what the historical record holds for what China's moneys, the tael of silver, the string of copper cash and the silver dollar, were worth in silver from the Song dynasty to the early 1900s: what a tael weighed, how many cash it bought, and how many taels a dollar fetched.

China paid in two metals at once: silver by weight, counted in taels, and copper coin, counted in cash, with European and American silver coins circulating beside them, mainly on the south and southeast coasts, from the 1700s. What a tael weighed is known within about 2%; how fine its silver was, and how many cash a tael bought, varied by place and year.

The tael and the dollar

  • How much silver was a tael?
    About 37 grams. One study of Qing wages takes 37 grams, the main units ranging from 36.54 to 37.58. A wage historian gives the treasury's kuping tael as 36.9 grams in the 1500s and 37.3 after the mid-1600s, the tael for tribute grain paid in money a little lighter and that of transit taxes and customs a little heavier. The Song-dynasty tael is taken as 37.68 grams.
  • How pure was it?
    No figure was found. Silver was paid as ingots and pieces whose weight and purity varied between government offices and local authorities, and the tael itself varied by region and trade. A tael's roughly 37 grams is its weight, and how much of it was pure silver is not known here.
  • And a dollar?
    About 0.72 tael, or 26.6 grams of silver at 37 grams a tael: the rate the data files of Beijing's wages and prices of the 1900s state. In Canton in 1835 a mace, a tenth of a tael, was about fourteen cents, so a dollar about 0.71 tael; the same journal counts the candareens paid for a catty as equal to the cents paid for a pound, which at 1⅓ pounds a catty makes a dollar about 0.75 tael, this project's arithmetic. In Beijing in 1918-1919 the exchange shops gave 136 to 140 copper coins for a dollar, and the old cash were seldom used.

The string of cash

  • How many cash made a string?
    A string, a tiao or chuan, was usually of 900 to 1,000 cash, but in some regions as few as 150; in wide regions, a witness of 1889 says, quoted from a book of 1927, one cash counted as two in sums above 20. The "capital cash" of Beijing, northern Zhili and Shandong passed at two to one standard cash. In rural Beijing a tiao was 500 cash before 1860 and 100 after. In the Beijing price file of the 1900s, a tiao is 100 cash or ten coppers.
  • What was the official rate?
    In the late 1500s about 800 standard cash to the tael in Beijing, whose mints cast heavier coin, and about 1,200 in the Lower Yangzi. The Qing fixed 1,000 cash to the tael from the Shunzhi reign, though the real rate moved often and differed widely by time and place.

  • Which cash are these?
    Not always the same. The Ming rates are mostly the court's own, set for its payments and taxes; the Wanli coin's is what the official coin had come to pass for, and the one market rate for private coin is for debased cash, at 3,000 to 7,000 to the tael. The rural Beijing rates count a string as 1,000, but the study that prints them reads the local cash as worth half the official count.
  • A rate of 1728 that does not fit.
    A salt censor of the Changlu salt district wrote that under Kangxi a tael bought 1,400 or 1,500 standard cash and in 1728 2,000. That is well above the rates of Songjiang in 1678-1690 and of Beijing in 1737. It may be counted in the north's cash, two to one standard coin, which would make it about 700-750 and 1,000: a GUESS of this project's, not a reading any source gives.
  • Is there a market rate decade by decade?
    Not in any copy that could be read. The series historians cite are in print only.
