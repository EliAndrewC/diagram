What did tea, raw silk and sugar cost in China where they were made, against the city that bought them?

In Rokugan the Sparrow's tea, the setting says, can always be sold to raise the money for the food they cannot grow. A tariff of typically about 15% of the declared sales value is charged at the gate of the city where goods are sold, and the setting's price ledger reckons a good sold far from where it was made at its price there plus freight, a 15% tariff for each border and a merchant's margin. The findings below give what the historical record of Qing China (1644 to 1912) holds for tea and raw silk: what each cost where it was made and in the city or port that bought it, in the same year.

Only tea has both ends in one account. Black tea from Fujian's Wuyi hills reached the export merchants at Canton at about 1.7 times its cost in the hills in the 1820s, and sold at Shanghai in the 1840s for about 2.75 times what the farmer was said to get, export duty included. For raw silk no price where it was made was found beside a Chinese city's price of the same year: it has two ports' prices in 1757. Sugar, with spices, incense woods and medicinal roots, is in What did sugar cost in Taiwan and China's cities, and incense woods and medicinal roots beside it? The same tea's cost from the hills to Canton and London over the Company's years is in What did a famous good cost far from where it was made?, brick tea in How did tea travel, and what did it cost at the end of the road?, and what the tael and the dollar were in silver in What was a tael, a string of cash and a Chinese dollar worth in silver?

Tea: from the Wuyi hills to Canton and Shanghai

  • What did one season's Canton contract allow for the tea in the hills?
    Samuel Ball (ball-1848-tea-cultivation), the East India Company's inspector of teas at Canton, prints the terms on which the Canton merchants "were said" to have contracted with the tea-men of the hills in the season 1824-6, and takes two of the prices apart. A picul delivered at Canton for 20.5 taels, with no money advanced, left 11.91 taels for the tea before packing; one at 18.5, with 4,200 taels advanced to the tea-man, left 10.83. The Canton price is 1.72 and 1.71 times the tea's, and the two ends are about 7.3 and 12.5 grams of silver a kg, or 6.6 and 11.3, this project's arithmetic at 37 grams of silver to the tael and 60.5 kg to the picul. The hill figure is what was left of the contract price after the charges, not a price seen paid in the hills.
  • What made up the gap?
    In the first reckoning, of the 8.59 taels between hills and Canton: chests and packing 1.31 taels; interest on the tea's cost for ten months 2.40; transport and duties to Canton 3.92; six months' interest on that 0.36; and the tea-factors' profit 0.60. Carriage and duties were so about 46% of the gap, interest 32%, packing 15% and the factor 7%, this project's arithmetic. Here Ball calls the 3.92 "transport and duties"; in his chapter on the roads it is the carriage alone. The road, by river and porter over two passes in six weeks to two months, is in What did a famous good cost far from where it was made?; in a straight line the hills are about 700 km from Canton.
  • Do the table's lines follow from their own terms?
    Not all. Interest at 18% a year for ten months on a picul costing 14 taels is 2.10 taels, where the table prints 2.40, and the footnote's two interest lines come to 1.05 and 0.42 where it prints 1.00 and 0.48, this project's arithmetic; both columns add to their printed totals. Ball himself warns, before his worked examples of such accounts, that there "may be some little discrepancy in them, which I may not now be able to reconcile".
  • What did tea bought in the hills come to?
    Ball works two purchases of a "chop", a lot of several hundred chests: one of 1,000 chests bought at 10 to 16 taels a picul came, after the trade's allowances, ten months' interest and packing, to about 10.3 taels a picul, and one of 600 chests bought at 16 to 21 taels to 12.09; twelve more averaged 12.1, with 1.1 for chests. He gives none of them a year.
  • What did a farmer get, against the price at Shanghai?
    The plant collector Robert Fortune (fortune-1852-tea-countries), who traveled the road from the Wuyi hills himself, writes that the growers sold "good common Congou" at about 80 cash a catty, which he makes 4 taels a picul, and that it sold at Shanghai for about 12 taels in 1846, 11 in 1847, 9 to 10 in 1848 and 11 in July 1849, export duty included. At 11 taels Shanghai's price was 2.75 times the farmer's, and 2.1 times with the duty taken off; the ends are about 2.4 and 6.7 grams of silver a kg, this project's arithmetic. Fine teas he was told sold at 160 cash a catty, 8 taels a picul, "a sum probably higher than that which is actually paid", against a Shanghai average of about 22, again 2.75 times.
  • What made up Fortune's gap?
    For the common tea, a picul: chest and packing 0.847 tael; the wholesale dealer's inspection and extra firing 1; carriage 1.359; the Hangzhou custom-house 0.037; and export duty at Shanghai 2.530, which with the tea make 9.773 taels. So common tea barely covered its cost, and at the lower of its two prices of 1848 did not; on fine tea, laid down for about 14 taels, he reckons a clear profit of 8 taels a picul, and of 4 in 1848. Of the 7 taels between farm and an 11-tael sale, the duty was 36%, carriage 19%, the dealer 14%, packing 12% and what was left 18%, this project's arithmetic.
  • How far, how long, and by what?
    Fortune's table: from Chong'an county town at the foot of the hills to Hekou by land, 280 li in 6 days; to Yushan by water, 180 li in 4; to Changshan by land, 100 li in 3; to Hangzhou by river, 800 li in 6; to Shanghai by canal, 500 li in 5: 1,860 li, and 28 days with the changes of boat and bad weather. He takes that for 620 miles at three li to the mile but thinks 400 miles (~645 km) nearer the truth. Porters and boats cost 1,740 cash a chest, of which the two land legs, a fifth of the li, took 1,200. His 1.359 taels a picul is about 13 grams of silver for 100 kg carried 100 km at 400 miles, or 8 at 620, this project's arithmetic.
  • How far can Fortune's figures be leaned on?
    He says himself that he cannot speak for the costs with the confidence he has in his distances, having them with the help of a Shanghai merchant; the route is one he supposes the tea to take; the farm price has no year; and in 1846 and 1847, when Shanghai's trade was mostly barter through Canton brokers, the dealer from the hills probably got "something considerably less" than the prices show.
  • And tea in the cities that drank it?
    Only the city's end was found, so no ratio is drawn. Beijing's official price list of 1736 has tea leaf at 0.058 tael a jin and Songluo tea at 0.2. In Ye Mengzhu's Shanghai county, when he wrote in the Kangxi reign (his chapter's dated prices run to 1690), the best of the Huizhou tea that went under the name Songluo cost no more than 0.2 or 0.3 tael a jin; Huizhou is in Anhui. At Peking in 1821 ordinary black tea was 1 tael a jin, which is eight times Ball's hill cost for Congou a few years later, a tea of unknown grade at a shop against a wholesale lot, this project's arithmetic. No price in the Fujian or Anhui hills of those years was found.

Raw silk: the export silk at two ports

  • Was silk cheaper at the port nearer to where it was made?
    Not in 1757, the one year with both. The Company sent ships to Ningbo from 1755 because it was "nearer to the producing districts of all the best silk"; there in 1757 it paid 225 to 250 taels a picul for raw silk, and 260 to 270 was asked at the season's close. At Canton that January it had contracted for 250 piculs "of the 12 Skain sort" at 187 taels, and by September the Dutch had contracted for about 100 at 230. Ningbo's price was so 1.2 to 1.3 times the Company's Canton contract and about level with the Dutch one: about 138 to 153 grams of silver a kg against 114 and 141, this project's arithmetic. The Company's records do not name the silk's district; taking it as Huzhou, Jiangnan's chief silk prefecture by this project's identification, Ningbo is about 180 km away and Canton about 1,100 km, in straight lines. The contracts differ in month and perhaps in grade, and both are ports selling to foreign ships.
  • What did the Company pay at Canton before that?
    In November 1755 195 taels a picul for 400 piculs, and 190 for the rest in December. The same years' Bohea tea was 12 taels a picul at Canton in a first contract of 1755, and 11.1 on average over that season, and generally 10 at Ningbo in 1757.
  • And in the north?
    Beijing's official list of 1736 prices raw silk at 0.9 tael a jin, about 55 grams of silver a kg and under half the Company's Canton price of twenty years on; the list names no grade or place of origin, so it is not set against the ports as a ratio. No price in Huzhou or Suzhou was found beside a northern or a Canton price of its year, nor what the carriage north cost.
