How our maps draw pawnshops (shichiya)

A county seat on our maps keeps three or four pawnbrokers, and a city more, in proportion. Most are ordinary shop-houses that also take pledges, and what shows on the map is the store where the pledges are kept. Every city keeps at least one by the rule at How our maps draw shops and trades in towns and villages.

  • How many pawnbrokers would a county seat keep?
    Hachijo, a crossroads village of 856 inhabitants, had three, and at that rate a county seat of about 1,200 has three or four.
  • Most pawnbrokers are drawn as ordinary shop-houses.
    Pawnbroking was often a sideline to another trade, so the shop itself is a shop like any other; only the pledge store sets it apart.

Where the pledges are kept is a knob, chosen at random for each settlement between the two forms the record knows: the Japanese pawnbroker's storehouses behind the shop, and the Chinese pawnshop's walled building on the street.

  • A Japanese pawnbroker's pledges are drawn in one to three earthen storehouses in a walled court behind the shop.
    Keeping pledges called for large, solid storehouses. The count of one to three and the walled court are a GUESS: no page we read gives either.
  • A storehouse is drawn with the ordinary storehouse glyph.
    A drawing convention: a pawnshop adds more of an ordinary storehouse rather than a new kind of building.
  • A Chinese pawnshop is drawn as a high-walled building of two stories on the street, its pledge store in one of its courts.
    The Qing pawnshop of Qinxiang stood two stories on its old street, walled 26 ft high at the front and 16 ft on its other sides, about 59 by 66 ft. Yaowan's, on its West Street, kept its pledge store in the front court of a four-court compound. That the building is larger and its court smaller than the Japanese form's is a GUESS.
  • The size of the pledge store's court, in either form, is our own guess.
    The size is a GUESS: no page we read gives the size of a pledge store or its court.
