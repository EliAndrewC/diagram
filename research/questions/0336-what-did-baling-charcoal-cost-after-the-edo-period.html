What did baling charcoal cost after the Edo period?

The setting's price list prices charcoal by the bale and says nothing of the bale itself. The findings below give what the historical record holds on the cost of baling charcoal, and on empty bales, in Japan after the Edo period ended in 1868, when accounts and government surveys first set that cost beside the charcoal's price; none of it is an Edo-period price.

Every figure here is later than 1868; what the Edo period itself left on the empty bale is at What did an empty charcoal bale cost, and who wove it? In 1870 an empty bale of unstated kind cost 1 monme of silver in the accounts of the Tamagawa aqueduct's works office at Hamura, half of one laborer's pay in the same account. Baling was 6.5 percent of the price of white charcoal in the forestry bureau's figures for 1914, and about a tenth of the cost of making charcoal in its figures for 1932.

An empty bale in the Tamagawa aqueduct's accounts, 1870

  • In 1870 an empty bale cost 1 monme of silver in the Hamura works office's account, half of one laborer's pay.
    The works office at Hamura kept a file dated Meiji 3 (1870) on the floods of the seventh and ninth months of that year. Its account entered, for flood defense of the bank in Fussa village, 18 empty bales at 18 monme, "1 monme a bale"; the entry before it closes with 316 laborers at 632 monme, "2 monme a man". A later entry has 90 empty bales at 90 monme, at the same rate. A bale was half of one laborer's pay, by this project's arithmetic.
  • The same account prices mats and kaya rope.
    Beside the 90 bales it entered 95 mats at 156.75 monme, "1 monme 6 bu 5 rin each", and 35 bundles of kaya rope at 15.75 monme, "4 bu 5 rin a bundle".
  • Is that an Edo-period price?
    No. The file is dated two years after the Edo period ended, and the account does not say what the bales had held or whether they were new. It is a dated figure for an empty bale of unstated kind; the Edo accounts of 1802 to 1808 are at An empty bale's price.

Baling as a share of the charcoal's price and cost

  • The nearest figure is of 1914: baling was about 6.5 percent of the price.
    The forestry bureau's survey put the standard cost of baling white charcoal in Japan's home territory, which the survey counts without Hokkaido, in 1914 at 8.38 yen for 100 bales of 10 kan, in a total cost of 115.67 yen and beside a standard settlement price of 128.31 yen: 6.5 percent of the price, by this project's arithmetic. At Hikawa in the Tama hills it was 20 yen in a price of 200, a tenth. The figure is the whole cost of baling, not divided into bale, rope and work, and it is half a century after the Edo period.
  • In the five prefectures after Tokyo in 1914 it ran from about 3 to 10 percent.
    The five rows after Tokyo's in the same table have baling at 8.47 yen in a settlement price of 160 (Kanagawa), 5 in 150 (Niigata), 3 in 90 (Toyama), 6 in 100 (Ishikawa) and 12 in 120 (Fukui). Those are 5.3, 3.3, 3.3, 6 and 10 percent, by this project's arithmetic.
  • In 1932 baling was about a tenth of the cost of making charcoal.
    The forestry bureau's compilation of the prefectures' reports on charcoal burning in 1932 gives baling, for charcoal from natural forest, as 10.0 to 12.8 percent of the cost of white charcoal according to the wood, 10.7 on average, and 9.8 to 13.2 percent of the cost of black, 11.4 on average. In money the average was 1.984 yen in 100 kan of white charcoal that cost 18.461 yen to make and settled at market for 19.971, and 1.811 yen in black that cost 15.867 and settled for 17.081. That is 9.9 and 10.6 percent of the price, by this project's arithmetic; the percentages of cost are the bureau's own. The baling is again one figure, not divided into bale, rope and work.
  • At an Aomori village in 1939, baling was 10 sen of the 45 sen a bale cost to make.
    A field study of Sarube village gives the cost of a bale of charcoal there in 1939 as 45.0 sen, of which the wood was 18.8, the baling 10.0 and the carriage 7.0; a Tokyo-market survey printed beside it has baling at 12 percent of cost. The study supposes a bale to sell at 1 yen, of which the baling would be a tenth, by this project's arithmetic. It gives no weight for the bale.
  • Was the baling bought or made at home?
    At Sarube it had been largely bought: the study says charcoal there was mostly burned by poor farmers as winter by-work, and that when the village was given rope-making machines, baling that had depended largely on purchase moved to home production. It does not say whether that baling was for charcoal, rice or both. The surveys of 1914 and 1932 do not say.
  • Packing and sundries together took 10 to 20 percent of a burner's income, in a figure from Ishikawa's forestry history.
    A university column reports this from the history of forestry in Ishikawa prefecture, with nearly 40 percent going to the forest's owner for the wood; it gives no year and does not separate packing from sundries.

Tokyo's dealers in empty rice bales, about 1915

  • Wholesalers sent buyers round Tokyo for empty rice bales.
    A handbook of small trades printed in 1915 reckons that Tokyo's three million inhabitants ate about ten thousand bales of rice a day and so turned out over ten thousand empty bales, which had their further uses; wholesalers each sent out two or three buyers to collect them across the city. A good buyer could buy about 200 bales a day, a hired buyer making a quarter in profit and one working alone three-tenths, 1 yen to 1 yen 50 sen a day. The handbook gives no price for a bale, and these are rice bales, not charcoal bales.
