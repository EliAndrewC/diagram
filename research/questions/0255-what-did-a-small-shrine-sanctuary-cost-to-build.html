What did a small shrine sanctuary cost to build?

Rokugan's prices are counted in koku and its smaller coins, and the setting's money rules judge a price by how many days of a craftsman's or a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give what the historical record holds for building and repairing a small village shrine's sanctuary in Edo-period Japan, each cost set beside the nearest day wages the record gives for its place and time.

No account read gives the carpenter-days of building a new small sanctuary. What survives, from the Tama district of Musashi, is a carpenter's winning bid of about 30 ryo, most likely for the new one-bay sanctuary finished in 1841, and the costs and labor of repairs to two one-bay sanctuaries from 1749 to 1805. Carpenters and sawyers repairing a sanctuary in the district were paid between 1 and about 1.7 monme of silver a day in 1749, and an unskilled laborer's wage in the district was not found.

The new sanctuary at Shibasaki, 1841

The Hachiman shrine of Shibasaki village, in the Tama district, lost its sanctuary to fire in 1824. Its new one was a one-bay sanctuary in the nagare-zukuri style, its bay 6 shaku (about 1.8 m), with a karahafu gable over the eaves; timber cutting began in 1827 and it was finished in 1841.

  • What did the carpenter bid?
    About 30 ryo. A carpenter of Hachioji bid over 80 ryo and others over 50 and 60, and a carpenter of Haijima won the work at over 30 ryo. The paper does not say which of the shrine's buildings the bid covered; the worship hall's carpentry was agreed with the same carpenter separately, so the bid was most likely for the sanctuary.
  • What was paid apart from the bid?
    The carving of the sanctuary's wall panels, 3 ryo a panel, was not in the carpenter's contract and was paid by a few leading families. The village headman also bought the timber gathered at Hachioji and arranged its carriage, once out of stock and very dear, and paid the many villagers who worked as laborers, hired or obliged; that the timber and the labor were outside the bid too is this project's reading.
  • What did the worship hall cost?
    11 ryo 2 bu (11.5 ryo) to the same carpenter for raising it and its joinery, the woodcutters' and sawyers' work included, as one sum.
  • What did the village raise?
    Over 120 ryo: over 30 from selling the fire-killed cedar and cypress, over 16 from the parishioners' daily savings, over 20 given by the raftsmen of the Tama and Aki rivers over six years, 40 from the village's households and 17 or 18 from leading believers.
  • What was a day's pay in the district then?
    No wage of Shibasaki in 1841 was found. The nearest craftsman's wage read is Kumehara's in 1830, in the Saitama district of the same province: a carpenter 2 monme of silver a day, with a further 1 monme a day that this project reads as his food allowance. The nearest unskilled wage read is Kyoto's of 1802-1804, about 0.92 monme a day in spring, almost forty years earlier and far to the west.
  • How many days of work was 30 ryo?
    About 600 to 1,100 craftsman-days, if the whole bid went in wages, or about 2,000 days of a Kyoto laborer's pay. At 60 monme to the ryo, the rate the 1749 Tama accounts imply and a price table gives for the 1850s, 30 ryo was about 1,800 monme; at 3 monme a day, wage and food together, it bought 600 carpenter-days, and at Tama's top rate of 1749, about 1,080. That the bid was nearly all wages is this project's reading, from the carving being outside the contract and the headman's paying for timber and labor himself.

Repairs to small sanctuaries, 1749-1805

Two sanctuaries of the Ome area, in the same district, were repaired again and again, and the headman's papers keep their costs.

  • How much work went into a repair?
    The Kasuga shrine's sanctuary, at Nogami, was repaired in 1749 with 187 man-days of carpenters and sawyers and 24 man-days of roofers from Edo, about 211 in all. The repair, the author infers, centered on re-roofing in copper; its cost and its shares are on the roof's page, What did a shrine roof and its finish cost?
  • What were those craftsmen paid?
    Carpenters and sawyers were paid at 9, 10 or 15 men to 1 bu of gold, the best of them 1.67 monme of silver a day, so 1 to 1.67 monme; the Edo roofers were paid more, 3 monme a day, the freight between Edo and Ome apart. No unskilled wage of the district was found; the nearest is Kyoto's of 1745-1754, about 0.91 monme a day (What did an unskilled laborer earn?).
  • What did a later repair of the same sanctuary cost?
    About 61.7 ryo in 1805, when, the author infers, its front was reworked and painted; the carpenters' and painters' wages were 44.2% of it and materials 16.2%. No wage of the district for 1805 was found; the nearest are Kumehara's carpenter at 200 mon a day in 1818, thirteen years later, and Kyoto's laborer at about 0.92 monme, the spring rate of 1802-1804.
  • What did repairing a sanctuary of the asked size cost?
    About 9.5 ryo. The Mitake shrine's sanctuary at Shinmachi is a small one-bay nagare-zukuri, its bay a little over 4.2 shaku (about 1.3 m), built in 1689; its repair of 1764-1770, small work on the sanctuary with its covering house and a repainting, cost about 9 ryo 2 bu, two-thirds of it (66.3%) craftsmen's wages and their rice, 29.1% materials. The paper gives no cost for its building in 1689. The nearest wages are Tama's of 1749, fifteen to twenty-one years earlier, and Kyoto's laborer of 1745-1754.

Day wages near the Tama district

  • What did the craftsmen of a Musashi village earn in 1818?
    Thirty-nine villages around Kumehara agreed their craftsmen's day wages in 1818: a carpenter 200 mon, a sawyer 180, a carter 180. It also sets three graded day rates, 116, 100 and 80 mon, whose trade the transcription leaves blank; no wage is named for an unskilled laborer.
  • What did a small timber structure take in 1830?
    Kumehara's estimate for rebuilding its notice board prints 50 carpenter-days at 2 monme of silver each, but its sums, 40 monme for the wages and 20 monme of allowance at 1 monme a head, fit 20 days. The estimate gives as its reason that a great fire in Edo had made timber and all goods dear in the countryside too.
