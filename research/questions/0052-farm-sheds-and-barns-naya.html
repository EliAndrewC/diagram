Farm sheds and barns (naya)

A naya was a farm's shed: a storage building, mostly built apart from the house, that held the crop and the farm tools. The two measured sheds from about the end of the Edo period, which closed in 1868, are about 18 and 27 ft long; the registered barns that survive from after 1868 run to about 38 ft. Sheds stood on no fixed side of the plot. How many a farm had is at The farmstead and what stood on it (yashiki), and how a shed compares with a storehouse at Farm storehouses (kura).

  • A shed held the crop and the farm tools.
    A naya is chiefly a storage shed built as a separate building, and on a farm it stores the crops and the implements. The reconstructed late-Edo headman-class farm in Chiba keeps its farm tools in one.
  • How big was the work shed from Hannan, near Osaka, built in the last decades before 1868?
    About 18 by 12 ft, perhaps somewhat more. Dated to 1830-1867, it measures 3 by 2 ken, is gabled and tiled, covers 28 sq m (~301 sq ft), and is divided into two rooms, one of them earth-floored. If its ken is 6 shaku, about 1.82 m (~6 ft), the frame is about 18 by 12 ft and 20 sq m (~214 sq ft), half again as long as it is deep; that is this project's arithmetic. The register's 28 sq m is more than that frame, and the page does not say what makes up the difference, so the shed may have been somewhat larger than 18 ft.
  • A shed from Nerima, in Tokyo, is about 27 by 15 ft.
    It measures 8.17 m (~27 ft) along its ridge by 4.54 m (~15 ft) across, is hip-roofed, and was first thatched with straw. Its roof frame and its uneven posts date it to the end of the Edo period or the Meiji years.
  • How big was an ordinary Edo farm's shed?
    The two sheds above, from Hannan near Osaka (about 18 ft long) and Nerima in Tokyo (about 27 ft), are the only measured sheds found from the end of the Edo period or just after; beyond them no page we read gives a size.
  • How big were the barns built after 1868?
    Mostly larger than the two earlier sheds. Three barns registered as Tangible Cultural Properties give sizes. The plainest, of the 1910s or 1920s near Osaka, is 6.9 m (~23 ft) by 3.9 m (~13 ft), earth-floored inside and simply built. A Meiji barn of another household near Osaka is 11.7 m (~38 ft) by 4.9 m (~16 ft). A 1907 barn of a farm in Kagawa, on the island of Shikoku, covers 99 sq m (~1,066 sq ft). The two whose length is given are about 1.8 and 2.4 times as long as they are deep.
  • The three registered barns built after 1868 were tiled and earth-walled like a storehouse.
    All three are gabled and tiled, and walled in earth like a storehouse.
  • Who owned the barns built after 1868?
    Households of the late nineteenth and early twentieth century; the Kagawa barn is part of the compound of an old family that prospered by milling rice and barley.
  • Where on the plot did a shed stand?
    On no fixed side, in the four that say. The Hannan work shed, built before 1868, stands in the farm work area on the west side of its plot. Of the three barns built after 1868, one stands to the southeast of its main house, one between the detached room and the rice storehouse, and one at the east end of its plot, facing west.
