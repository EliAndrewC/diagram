How do economic historians carry a basket of goods into another time's money?

Rokugan's money rules tell players what a koku would be worth today by pricing a laborer's year of goods then and now. Historians who build such baskets of goods mostly use them for something else, and the research below shows what they compute with them, how they handle prices that moved apart, and how far rice serves as a yardstick.

A basket of goods is a fixed list of a year's food, cloth, fuel and shelter. In the studies read here, economic historians divide a wage by its cost in the same time and place, or price it in each place's own prices as a poverty line, so what they report is a ratio or a share of the population, not what a coin would buy today. Where two places bought different goods, they price both baskets in both places and take the geometric mean of the two answers. Institutions that do put an old sum into today's money give several answers side by side, each named for what it is measured against.

What the baskets compute

  • A welfare ratio is a year's wages divided by the cost of a family's basket.
    In a study of Chinese and European wages from 1738, a full-time year's earnings divided by the cost of a family's bare-bones year answers whether a man working full time could keep a family at that level; these ratios are called welfare ratios.
  • The Japanese wage comparisons are the same kind of ratio.
    Bassino and Ma measure Kyoto's wages from 1741 as the day wage over the cost of one adult's basket for a year, which they liken to the welfare ratio of an earlier study by Allen, a household's year of wages over three times the cost of a basket for one adult. Their basket and its Kyoto prices are in What did a Kyoto laborer's year of goods cost?
  • A basic-needs poverty line is priced in each place's own prices.
    Allen's poverty line fixes what a person needs and prices it in the local prices of each country, so that the same needs everywhere, not an exchange rate, make countries and years comparable.
  • The long-run count of the very poor since 1820 is built the same way.
    Moatsos sets a poverty line for every year and country separately and counts the share of the population unable to meet basic needs, minimal nutrition and heated shelter among them, at the prices of locally available goods at the time; his method follows Allen's.

Which index: two baskets and their mean

  • Where two places bought different goods, each basket is priced in both, and the two results are averaged.
    Comparing London and Beijing about 1750, the European and the Beijing baskets each give a ratio of the cost of living, and the final step is their geometric average, the index economists call a Fisher index. The authors call imposing modern theory on eighteenth-century behavior this way a debatable procedure.
  • In that comparison the choice of formula made little difference.
    The European basket gave a ratio of 1.12 and the bare-bones basket 1.17, and their geometric average was close to both; the authors tried many formulas and weights and found their conclusions about relative real wages did not depend in any important way on the choice.
  • The gap between the two results is read as the uncertainty itself.
    In the modern comparisons of countries' price levels, the ratio of one basket's result to the other's is one standard measure of how wide the margin of choice is, the gap that the geometric mean resolves.
  • How wide can that gap get?
    Between the United States and Tajikistan or Kyrgyzstan in 2005, one basket's result was 9.6 and 5.1 times the other's, a spread at which, the authors say, one may doubt whether the data support the comparison at all.
  • Over two centuries, ordinary price indexes miss some goods' changes far more than others'.
    Nordhaus finds that the usual indexes vastly overstate how much the price of light rose over two centuries. He sorts consumption by how much it changed since about 1800: home food and most clothing changed little and the indexes likely miss little; housing changed a great deal while staying recognizable; heating and lighting changed so completely that the indexes do not try to capture it.

One figure or several

  • MeasuringWorth gives several modern values for one old price, each against a different yardstick.
    Its method applies several measures of a later year to an amount of an earlier one, and which answer is best depends on what is being valued and against what: the cost of a fixed basket, a wage, or the size of the economy. Its example, a loaf of bread at 7.7 cents in 1931, comes out between 92 cents and $15.20.
  • The Bank of Japan's currency museum gives one ryo as about ¥63,000 by rice and about ¥345,000 by a carpenter's wage.
    It prices a koku of rice, which it takes as a ryo's worth in the 1700s, at the government's figure for polished rice at the end of 2016, though the Edo price was for brown rice, and the 23 carpenters a ryo once hired for a day at a carpenter's day wage of 2016, and says the answer differs greatly by the good chosen. The wage answer is about 5.5 times the rice answer.
  • Even by rice alone the answer moves with the period.
    The same sheet puts a ryo by rice at about ¥100,000 early in the Edo period, about ¥40,000 to ¥60,000 in the middle and later years, and about ¥4,000 to ¥10,000 at its end.

Which papers put a basket into today's money

  • Allen prices poverty budgets in US dollars two ways, to show the problem.
    Pricing a set of national poverty budgets at US shop prices, and again at Indian prices turned into dollars at the exchange rate for buying power, gives a dollar cost about half as large the second way, and the budgets rank differently; he takes it as the reason to make countries comparable by nutritional requirements rather than index numbers. These are modern budgets, not historical ones.

Rice as the yardstick

  • Japan's national library calls converting through the price of rice a convenient method, not an exact one.
    The National Diet Library's research guide says conversion between eras is not easy and no exact value can be had, but that one convenient method takes the rice price as the standard.
  • Farm prices swung with the harvest.
    The Bank of Japan's sheet warns that farm prices in the Edo period were strongly moved by good and bad harvests. Allen, comparing European wages, averaged them over periods of 50 years to deal with their volatility.
  • Rice could fall against other goods.
    In the Kyoho era of 1716-1736, while a recoinage that shrank the money in circulation brought prices down generally, rice fell further than other goods, on the strength of rising farm output.
  • Wages counted in grain can rank places wrongly.
    Wages counted in grain made central and eastern Europe look better off than north-western Europe; a full basket's cost removed that result, since town workers bought bread rather than grain and bought other goods besides.
  • The Japanese wage studies count wages both against a price index and in rice.
    Saito's real-wage index for Kyoto's unskilled laborers deflates the Mitsui house's wages by Shinbo's consumer price index, built from the same books' prices. Bassino and Ma set beside it the same wages counted in kilograms of rice, which tracked it fairly well, since rice weighed heavily in that index. Their own measure is the wage over a whole basket's cost.

Housing in a basic-needs line

  • Allen's poverty line allows 3 m² (~32 sq ft) of housing a person.
    He calls it extreme, often illegal, overcrowding by the standards of rich countries, and sets it beside crowding of 2.3 to 3.6 m² (~25-39 sq ft) a person in Bombay, Ahmedabad, Shanghai and Nairobi.
  • Rent in the US, the UK and France was on the order of $25 a m² a month in his 2011 prices.
    He sought market rents in the poorest districts of large cities; in New York they were for illegal, substandard flats, at about the government's fair market rent, and 3 m² cost $1.76 a day. That is $300 a m² (~$28 a sq ft) a year at $25 a month, and $214 a m² (~$20 a sq ft) a year at $1.76 a day.
  • His 2016 draft had costed housing as a share of the budget instead.
    There, since the international price survey's rents were too incomplete, housing was set at 5% of the budgeted food and other spending.
