Where the ditch leaves the brook: the intake and its weir (toshuko and seki)

A hamlet's irrigation ditch began at an intake on the bank of its brook. Japan's word for the works there, toshuko, first named the whole series that took water from a river - the weir, the intake mouth, the settling basin and the division works. The brook was tapped, not swallowed: in old times a weir mostly closed only half the river, and it was the dug canal, below the intake, that divided the water among the fields.

Where a brook ran high enough, the intake was nothing more than an opening in its bank. Where its level fell, something had to be placed in the stream to hold it back and raise it, and that was the weir (seki).

  • The head works were a series of structures: weir, intake mouth, settling basin and division works.
    The word toshuko first named that whole series - with the intake mouth a culvert where it crossed an embankment - and only later came to mean the weir alone. The modern term still lists an intake weir, an intake mouth and a settling basin among its parts, and a general irrigation manual gives three much like them: an offtake at the side of the river, a regulating structure across all or part of it, and a way to flush out sediment.
  • The water was divided among the fields on the dug canal, below the intake.
    Water taken by a diversion dam enters a main canal, is tapped by secondary canals at division works on it, and passes on into lesser canals. Every fork we read of is on a dug canal below its intake, none on the stream itself.
  • The brook runs on below the intake.
    In old Japan the closures across a river were mostly half-river closures. A weir may be built to let water over its crest at all times, and Japanese has a name for that kind, the washing weir (araizeki). The Ishi-ibi works of 1615 at Saga took their water at a sluice and returned the surplus to the main river. A well-made intake takes only the cleaner upper part of the flow and sends the lower part on down the river, and when the river runs high, water flows over the weir.
  • Even China's two great diversions took only a stated share of their rivers.
    Dujiangyan sends six parts of the Min River into its irrigation channel and four into the flood channel in spring, and the reverse in flood. Lingqu returns seven parts to the Xiang River and passes three into its canal.
  • Did a village's ditch ever take a whole brook?
    A diversion weir is defined as a low dam or wall across a stream to divert "part or all" of its water into a canal, and in an Edo-period drought all the water flowing out of a mountain catchment was taken for farming, each district's return flow used in full by the district below. But no source we read shows a village's ditch taking a whole brook.

Where on its bank the intake stood was chosen for the water it could take there.

  • A Japanese engineering lecture puts the intake on one bank, the side of the land it serves.
    It makes the one-bank intake the rule on torrents and steep rivers, and the principle on gentle ones.
  • A Japanese engineering lecture sets the intake where the current's deepest line runs against the bank.
    A natural intake, one with no weir, needs the thalweg against the bank, deep and steady water, and a river always above the level the canal needs; where a weir holds the level, a point where the thalweg touches the bank is enough. The mouth usually sits beside the sand sluice.
  • A modern irrigation manual puts an offtake in a straight reach, or else on the outside of a bend.
    Failing a straight reach, it takes the outside of a bend, where silt does not settle, though that bank erodes and may need protecting. It lets the offtake leave at an angle where the water is clean, and asks for a right angle or a scour sluice where it carries silt. A Japanese torrent-river intake of 1974, sited just below the middle of a bend's concave bank where a dam downstream held the river's level, took in little sediment.
  • A Japanese treatise of 1902 put the head works where the river leaves the mountains.
    The land-improvement treatise advised: "It is advisable to place the head works generally where the river issues from the mountains."

From the intake mouth, the head race ran down to the first fork.

  • What did a village's intake mouth look like?
    What the mouth of a village's head race was lined or shut with is not recorded.
  • The heads of great canals were closed with gates of timber or stone, or with boards and timbers that could be lifted out.
    On the great canals a gate sat on the intake mouth to open and close the canal and regulate the flow. Early gates were timber, later rebuilt in stone. At the Mulan weir at Putian, begun in 1064, the water held back was regulated "by adding or removing gate boards" set in cut grooves. At the Hamura intake on the Tama River, timbers laid across the weir let the water in, and were taken away in flood so that the gate would not be destroyed.
  • How far did the head race run before it forked?
    No source we read gives a distance. A 1987 survey of Japanese irrigation by the geographer Tabayashi says only that the small mountain canals run "for short distances"; the third-century paddies at Toro, 7.5 hectares (~19 acres), were watered by a canal "more than 370 meters long" (~1,214 ft).
  • Did a village dig a settling basin at the head of its race?
    In modern practice a settling basin follows immediately after the intake mouth, at the start of the canal, reached through an intake court that narrows the flow smoothly from the mouth into the canal. Whether a village dug one is not recorded.

Whether a weir was built at the intake at all was decided by the brook's water.

  • Where the water stood high, the intake was only an opening in the bank.
    "If a river's flow is ample and its level always high, water can easily be taken just by providing an entrance for it." A modern irrigation manual likewise finds a simple offtake enough where a river keeps its level all year.
  • Where the level fell, a weir was built to raise it.
    Where or when the level drops too low to draw from, something must be placed in the stream to hold it back. When a natural intake's river falls, a weir keeps the intake's level up, and some places set up a temporary weir for the intake season alone.
  • China, too, divides intakes into two kinds, with a dam and without.
    "Intake head works are of two types, with a dam and without": the first builds an overflow or diversion dam in the channel, the second opens a mouth on the concave side of a bend in the river. A whole region could take one form throughout: the Ningxia district on the Yellow River drew all its water without dams.
  • How was the head of the ancient Chinese Zhengguo canal built?
    Scholars read it three ways, spanning both kinds of intake, with a dam and without: as a large earth-and-stone dam, as stone-filled crib baskets or a low weir, or as no dam at all and a diversion off the river's bend.
  • A fourteenth-century Chinese farm treatise describes a palisade across a brook.
    Wang Zhen, writing in 1313, says that where a brook's banks are deep and the fields lie high, "upstream on the brook one makes a palisade to check the water, causing it to issue out at the side and run down to irrigate".
  • How many small intakes had a weir?
    No source we read counts them. A Japanese history of head works says intake weirs were hardly built at all in old times: the intake mouth was sought where water could be taken naturally, and a canal cut from it.
