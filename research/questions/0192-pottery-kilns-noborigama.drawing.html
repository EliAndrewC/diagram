How our maps draw pottery kilns (noborigama)

A kiln works on our maps, where a city's everyday pots and roof tiles are fired, stands outside the city's walls: a chambered climbing kiln on a tamped work ground about 140 by 120 ft, with a throwing and drying shed, a clay pit, a fuel stack, its own well, and the two or three cottages of the households that work it. It keeps 60 ft of clear ground between its kiln and anyone else's house.

  • The works is captioned as a kiln works, not a tile kiln.
    Tile and everyday pots were fired at the same works, kilns for both built side by side from the Nara period on, and in this project's reading a town fired more of its clay into bowls, pots and jars than into roof tiles, since few houses had tile roofs before the end of the Edo period. So one works is drawn, not a tile kiln and a pottery kiln, and a map that knows what its works fires names it in the caption.
  • A map draws one kiln works, with two or three cottages.
    A real kiln district could hold dozens of households and many kilns - Imado had about fifty households at the end of the Edo period, and medieval Tokoname's kiln sites number in the thousands. One works says where the city's pots and tiles come from, and a second identical works tells a reader nothing more. This is a deliberate departure from the history.
  • The potters live at the works.
    A firing is stoked round the clock for days, so nobody walks back to town at dusk in the middle of one, and a kiln quarter was a quarter of potters' households: Imado's held about fifty at the end of the Edo period.
  • The works' cottages are counted as the works' own, not among the town's houses.
    This is bookkeeping, not a claim about the setting. The town's rules for its houses - how far each is from a public well, which ward it counts in, how far it stands from the outcaste quarter - are written about its own houses, and a works' cottages outside the walls would be judged by rules that were never about them. The one place they count as houses is the works' own well.
  • Only the firing is kept out of the city.
    Edo's fire law restricted fire inside a crowded timber town, down to the hours a fire might burn and a ban on making fireworks within it, so the kiln stands outside the walls. A crockery dealer or a potter's shopfront inside them is an ordinary shop in the shop rows: in this project's reading the ware is made at the works outside and sold by a dealer inside, as charcoal is burned where the wood grows and tallied where it is sold.
  • A works is sited for its clay, fuel and fire, not by caste.
    In this project's reading potters were not hinin, whose offices were begging, cleaning and executioner's work, so the kiln's siting turns on clay, fuel and fire, not on the caste rules that set a tanning yard apart.
  • The kiln is drawn as a chambered climbing kiln, not a mound.
    A low mound is a charcoal kiln's shape, so a kiln drawn as a mound reads as a charcoal kiln; a climbing kiln is a row of chambers up a slope, firebox at the bottom and chimney at the top, and its chambered shape says the works wants rising ground. The kiln is turned so that it climbs its slope.
  • No slope is drawn behind the kiln.
    A band of slope drawn behind it reads, at a city map's scale, as a stray tab off the kiln's shoulder rather than as ground, and the chambered shape already says it.
  • The map's kiln, 46 ft long, is shorter than any kiln we found measured.
    The smallest is 16.6 m (~54 ft) long, and the Motoyashiki kiln's, the oldest climbing kiln in Gifu, is 24 m (~79 ft) long and about 2.2 m (~7 ft) wide. One works' kiln should run about 55 to 80 ft up its slope and about 7 ft wide, its chambers growing larger as they climb. The shared kilns of 100 m (~328 ft) and more served several kiln houses at once, so they are not the measure of one works. The shorter kiln is a deliberate departure from the history.
  • The works' shed, clay pit and cottages are sized by GUESS.
    A 32 by 18 ft throwing and drying shed, a 30 by 24 ft clay pit and 28 by 18 ft cottages, on a 140 by 120 ft ground, pitched against the other large works a map draws (the charcoal yard's 88 by 58 ft, the brewery's 96 ft hall). No page we read sizes a kiln works' sheds, pit or houses.

The 60 ft of clear ground round the kiln is this project's figure, not a finding: no page we read says how far a real kiln stood from the houses round it.

  • The kiln keeps the 60 ft our maps give an attended fire, like the refining forge's.
    A firing is a very large fire, but an attended one, stoked round the clock until the ware is done, so it takes the distance our maps give the refining forge's worked fire, 60 ft. It is not the 120 ft of a nuisance carried on the air, which guards against smell rather than fire. The distances are listed at How our maps draw shops and trades in towns and villages.
  • The 60 ft is measured from the kiln itself, not from the edge of the works' ground.
    The gap keeps a firing away from the town, not from the households whose trade it is: the works' own cottages and workshop stand close by the kiln, as the Motoyashiki kiln's workshop stood right beside it.
