Village fuel woods and their coppice (satoyama)

A Japanese village's fuel wood was common land, held by the village and cut under its own rules for firewood and charcoal, fodder and grass, and the fallen leaves that fertilized its paddies. It stood beyond the fields from the houses, on the nearest and lowest hill ground round the settlement - the satoyama. Its trees were cut for firewood and charcoal every few decades, and its leaves and undergrowth were raked up and carried off, so it was a light, open wood with herbs on its floor, not brush.

Where one village's wood met the next was not at first clear, and when villages went to law over it, the ruling fixed the boundary as a line drawn on a map, a line that bent.

  • A village's wood stood beyond its fields, on the nearest hill ground.
    A Japanese reference dictionary describes the model village as its houses at the center, its fields around them, and hill and wild land beyond. In the Yoshino mountains the hills are ranked from the nearest and lowest outward, and the nearest - the satoyama - is the fields and mixed woodland on the slopes around the settlement. An English account calls the satoyama the border zone between the foothills and the flat farmland.
  • On the flat Musashino upland too, the wood lay beyond the fields from the houses.
    A forester's account of 1910 describes groves round the houses, then the fields, then the konara woodland on the outer edge.
  • A village's commons were of two kinds: forest, and open grass and riverbank land.
    The forest was cut for firewood, charcoal wood, timber and leaf litter, the grass and riverbank land for fodder and roof thatch. In one district studied, firewood, forage and grass for the household were the main products.
  • Each village governed its wood by rules of its own.
    These commons were iriai land, customary common property. Most of their institutions were set up in the Edo period, and the villagers laid down who might take what, when and how much. In the Yamaguni district, each of 11 villages has its own institutions for its common forest, held by an association of the village's residents.
  • When did a village's boundary with its neighbors become clear?
    Only slowly, it is thought: not at first, but as the self-governing villages of the late middle ages developed.
  • In hill forest, boundaries stayed vague.
    A local history of mountain disputes says that boundaries in the forest were vague above all, because the ground was broken, the valleys steep and the view shut in among the mountains.
  • A boundary fought over at law was fixed as a line drawn on a map.
    A ruling of 1649, early in the Edo period, drew the boundary in vermilion, the land north of the line going to one village and south of it to the other. A ruling map of 1612 marks a boundary with drawn rocks as well as a line.
  • A ruled boundary line could bend.
    On the ruling map of 1612 the officials' seals were set along the line, chosen to some extent at its ends and bends.
  • A wood's boundary may have followed ridges, streams and paths, but that is our guess.
    No page we read on village commons or their boundary disputes says so; it is a GUESS.
  • Was a hillside wood ever laid out as a rectangle?
    No page we read describes a coppice lot laid out as a surveyed rectangle.

One kind of village did lay out its woods in straight lines: the new villages settled on the dry Musashino upland in the later seventeenth century. Kichijoji was settled by households who had lost their homes and fields in the Edo fire of 1657, and Santome was all but finished in 1696.

  • In the new villages of the Musashino upland, a household's wood was a straight-sided strip behind its fields.
    At Santome each strip ran back from the road as house lot, then fields, then flat-land woodland. At Kichijoji some households were granted strips more than 1,000 m (~3,281 ft) long.
  • Kichijoji had no paddy at all.
    The soil was poor, so all of its farmland was dry fields, with no wet rice.

Inside the wood, the work of the village kept the floor open.

  • How often was the coppice cut?
    Every few decades, the accounts differing on how many years. The Japanese Wikipedia article on satoyama says a broadleaf wood was cut every 10 to 20 years, leaving the roots to sprout again, for firewood and charcoal. The English one says every 15 to 20 years, the farmers cutting the trees for firewood and charcoal, which kept the wood from growing into dense, dark evergreen forest. A review of satoyama gives every 20 to 40 years for its secondary coppice forests, and names the deciduous oaks cut for fuel wood and charcoal as Quercus serrata and Quercus acutissima (in Japanese, konara and kunugi). So the accounts span 10 to 40 years between cuttings.
  • Leaves and undergrowth were gathered from the wood for the fields and the livestock.
    In the Edo period young and fallen leaves were gathered from community forests as fertilizer for the paddies, and forest litter and compost for the paddies were gathered from the woods and the grasslands. Fallen leaves and undergrowth also went to the fields as green manure and to cattle and pigs as fodder.
  • The cutting and raking let the sun reach the floor, so herbs and wildflowers grew there.
    Gathering the fallen leaves and cutting the wood regularly let sunlight reach the forest floor, which many spring and summer wildflowers depend on.
  • The shrubs were cut as well as the trees.
    A review of satoyama lists the coppice's management as small clear-cuts from time to time, raking up the litter, and cutting the shrubs.
  • A coppice left uncared for grew poorer, with fewer kinds of herb on its floor.
    A managed coppice stand held 42 plant species against 23 in a stand left uncared for for 50 years, and 13 kinds of herb on its floor against the neglected stand's 3. That a lived-in village's wood never grew a shrub layer is our reading of this contrast, a GUESS.
  • What stood at a wood's edge?
    A German account divides an ideal, fully grown forest edge, from the outside in, into a herb fringe, a shrub belt and a forest mantle of trees whose crowns reach low on the field side. An English account says only that the trees at a woodland edge often differ from those inside, with hedge plants, brambles and low-growing plants. On a worked wood the shrubs were among what was cut, and the undergrowth was carried off, so, on this page's reading, the grass fringe is what remains and thins out under the first crowns - a GUESS.

The village woods did not last.

  • The village woods faded when oil and chemical fertilizer came in.
    Satoyama have been disappearing with the shift from charcoal and firewood to oil, and from compost to chemical fertilizer.
