What did sake cost, by the shop and by the cup?

Rokugan's samurai and commoners drink sake bought by the measure or poured by the cup, and the setting judges a price by how many days of a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give what sake cost in Edo-period Japan at a shop, at a tavern and in a village, and what Chinese farm households paid for bought wine, each set beside the nearest wage found.

In Edo in the Bunsei era (1818-1829) a shopping guide priced its headline brands of sake at 260 to 332 mon a sho and its cheapest at 180, and its aged sake in silver, and a tavern poured good sake at 20 to 24 mon a go and cheap local sake at 8 to 10. A townsman born in 1810 remembered the best sake at 164 mon a sho in Osaka and 200 to 248 in Edo in his childhood, and saw it reach about 350 to 400 from the Tenpo era (1830-1844). In the village of Nagata in 1842 the shogunate's official prices put local sake at 100 mon a sho, against a man's day wage of 132 mon in the same list. No source gives a shop's and a tavern's price for the same sake, so the markup from one to the other was not found.

Sake bought by the measure

  • What did a shop in Edo charge?
    A printed shopping guide of the Bunsei era (1818-1829) listed the sake sellers with each brand's price for a sho: Daikoku 332 mon, Hotei 300, Akenotsuru 260, and the cheap Takasago 180. Sake aged nine years cost 10 monme of silver a sho. A food guide of the same years put most clear sake at 3 monme of silver, without saying for what measure.
  • And by the go?
    A sho is ten go, so the brands priced in mon came to about 18 to 33 mon a go.
  • How did the price move over a lifetime?
    Kitagawa Morisada, born in Osaka in 1810, wrote that in his childhood the best sake sold in Osaka cost 164 mon a sho, and at the same time Edo's 200 to 248 mon; from about the Tenpo era (1830-1844) it became, as printed, "三百五十八十" mon, which the record reads as 350 to 380, or 400. Elsewhere he says that thirty years before he wrote, good morohaku sake in Edo was about 250 mon a sho, and was now 350 to 400. His childhood is about 1815-1825, and since he wrote in 1837-1853 his "thirty years ago" falls in about the 1810s and 1820s too: both datings are the record's reading. His Edo figures of those years, 200 to 250 mon, sit just below the Bunsei shopping guide's 260 to 332, and his 350 to 400 from Tenpo above it.
  • And a sake shop's own price list?
    Morisada reproduces an undated handbill of a sake shop whose sake, he says, runs from 42 to 132 mon, and leaves its date for later study. The handbill is in cursive; the record reads its two lists, of new and of aged sake, as priced by the sho, but its grades and their prices were not transcribed.

Sake by the go, at a tavern or a soba shop

  • What did a tavern in Edo charge?
    By a record of the Bunsei era, a go of good sake shipped down to Edo cost 20 to 24 mon and a go of cheap local sake 8 to 10, and most snacks served with it 8 mon each; two or three go with two or three dishes came to about 100 mon. The page that gives these figures names no record.
  • And at a soba shop?
    An Edo soba shop's price board, drawn in a record of the customs of its time from 1830 to 1853, when it was more or less finished, lists a go of good sake at 40 mon, beside plain soba at 16 (the board is below).
  • Was there a price for a single cup?
    Only one, at one remove: a study of drinking quoted on a popular page says that in the 1750s the Toshimaya, a sake shop by the Kamakura quay in Edo, sold a cup of sake with grilled tofu for 2 mon, and that teahouses in Asakusa sold sake at 28 to 32 mon, without a measure.
  • How much dearer was the tavern than the shop?
    No source gives both prices for the same sake, place and years. The tavern's 20 to 24 mon a go overlaps the shop's 18 to 33, but the two come from different sources and different grades, so no ratio is computed from them.

The soba shop's board

  • What else did an Edo soba shop sell, and for how much?
    Its board, as Morisada draws it: a large steamer of soba 48 mon; plain soba 16; udon in thick sauce 16; arare, soba topped with clam adductors, 24; tempura soba 32; hanamaki, with toasted laver, 24; shippoku 24; egg-drop 32; and a go of good sake 40. The board is in cursive, and the dishes' names are the record's reading of it; the prices are plain.
  • And in Kyoto and Osaka?
    A paper on the shop wall, which Edo's shops had too, priced udon and soba at 16 mon each, shippoku and anpei 24, an egg dish 32 and odamaki 36, the names again the record's reading of the cursive. The 16-mon bowl came on a coarse flat dish.
  • Did the price of a bowl change?
    The 16 mon held until the Keio era; "nihachi", two times eight, meant that price. In the Keio era (1865-1868), as all prices soared, Edo's soba sellers petitioned the authorities and raised it to 20 mon, then soon to 24, and the dishes after it rose in proportion; Kyoto and Osaka, he supposes, followed.
  • How many soba shops were there?
    In 1860 the soba shops inside Edo met over the high price of soba: 3,763 of them, not counting, he supposes, the night sellers.

In the countryside

  • What did sake cost in a village?
    In 1842, after surveying wages and prices, the shogunate ordered each group of villages to buy and sell at official prices. The list it gave the group that took in Nagata, a village now in the city of Oamishirasato, set local sake at 100 mon a sho and sake from Itami at 180. That is 10 and 18 mon a go. These were prices set by order, not prices found in the market.
  • Who sold it?
    In Nagata, six of the sixteen households with a trade on the side kept a tavern, and the taverns of the time mostly sold local sake of their own brewing.
  • And beside the wage?
    The same list set a farm day laborer's wage at 132 mon for a man and 100 for a woman. A sho of local sake was about three-quarters of a man's day, a sho of Itami sake about one and a third days, and a go of local sake about a thirteenth of a day. This is the only source found that gives a sake price and an unskilled wage in the same document.

The day's wage beside the Edo prices

  • What did an unskilled laborer earn in Edo?
    No dated unskilled day wage in copper mon was found for Edo in these years. A popular page gives two figures for no year, naming no source for either: a laborer's day of "about 200 mon", and 320 to 540 mon a day for carpenters, plasterers and navvies together. For the Bunsei era it gives a carpenter, a craftsman, 3 to 5 monme of silver a day.
  • What is the nearest unskilled wage?
    The Kyoto day laborer's wage in the Mitsui house's accounts, in grams of silver: 2.74 to 4.17 g in 1818-1829, the years of the shop and tavern prices. From 1818, Edo's laborers were paid at about the same level as Kyoto's (What did an unskilled laborer earn?).
  • What did a laborer and a craftsman earn in 1837-1853?
    The Morisada manko gives both: in Edo a tobi, a laborer of the building sites, 300 mon a day and a carpenter about 5 or 5.5 monme of silver; in Kyoto and Osaka a building site's helper 280 mon, and in Osaka a carpenter 4 monme 3 bu. By this project's arithmetic, a sho of Edo's best sake at 350 to 400 mon was about 1.2 to 1.3 of a tobi's days; a go of good sake at a soba shop, 40 mon, about an eighth of his day; and a 16-mon bowl of soba about a nineteenth, or about an eighteenth of a Kyoto or Osaka helper's day.
  • And beside the childhood prices of about 1815-1825?
    No unskilled wage of those years was found for Edo or Osaka. The nearest is the Kyoto day laborer's, 2.71 to 4.17 g of silver in 1815-1825. For a craftsman, an Edo carpenter of the Bunsei era (1818-1830) earned 5.4 monme of silver a day, meals included; none of those years was found for Osaka; the nearest is Morisada's Osaka carpenter, given for 1837-1853, some 12 to 38 years later.
  • And beside the Keio soba of 20 and 24 mon?
    No wage of 1865-1868 was found for Edo. The nearest unskilled wage is the Kyoto day laborer's, 4.11 to 4.26 g of silver in 1865-1867, and the nearest craftsman's Morisada's Edo carpenter, given for his writing years, 12 to 31 years earlier.
  • Why are the Kyoto laborer's grams not set beside the mon?
    The ratios above set prices in mon beside wages in mon, so they need no rate between the coins. The Kyoto laborer's wage is in silver, and this question does not convert it; the market rates of copper to silver are in What was copper coin worth in silver?

China

  • How much dearer was bought wine than home-brewed?
    Twice as dear. Writing in Tongxiang county in 1658, a farmer-scholar says that every household of Suzhou, Huzhou, Shaoxing and Jinhua brewed its own, for offerings, guests and those who work the fields, and that wine bought cost twice what it cost to brew, "and that is still a cheap price".
  • What did spirit cost, retail and in bulk?
    In the farm manual of a Mr. Shen of Huzhou, written in the last years of the Ming, spirit distilled from wine lees fetched 2 fen of silver a jin bought retail and 1.6 fen bought in bulk, so retail was a quarter dearer than bulk.
  • And the wine a farmer bought for his hands?
    The same manual gives an old rule that the field hands' wine was bought at a shop, thirty ladles for a dou of rice, and calls it watery and thin, a profit only to the shop. It does not say how much a ladle held, only that 12 liang (three-quarters of a jin) of wine brewed at home would stand in for one ladle, with twice the flavor and strength. Its year-hired hand was paid 5 taels of silver a year besides his rice (How much rice was a koku?), and his employer reckoned 1.2 taels a year for his firewood and wine.
  • What did a Qing tavern charge by the pot or the bowl?
    No price was found.
