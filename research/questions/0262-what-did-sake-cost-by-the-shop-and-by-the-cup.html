What did sake cost, by the shop and by the cup?

Rokugan's samurai and commoners drink sake bought by the measure or poured by the cup, and the setting judges a price by how many days of a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give what sake cost in Edo-period Japan at a shop, at a tavern and in a village, and what Chinese farm households paid for bought wine, each set beside the nearest wage found.

In Edo in the Bunsei era (1818-1829) a shopping guide priced its headline brands of sake at 260 to 332 mon a sho and its cheapest at 180, and its aged sake in silver, and a tavern poured good sake at 20 to 24 mon a go and cheap local sake at 8 to 10. In the village of Nagata in 1842 the shogunate's official prices put local sake at 100 mon a sho, against a man's day wage of 132 mon in the same list. No source gives a shop's and a tavern's price for the same sake, so the markup from one to the other was not found.

Sake bought by the measure

  • What did a shop in Edo charge?
    A printed shopping guide of the Bunsei era (1818-1829) listed the sake sellers with each brand's price for a sho: Daikoku 332 mon, Hotei 300, Akenotsuru 260, and the cheap Takasago 180. Sake aged nine years cost 10 monme of silver a sho. A food guide of the same years put most clear sake at 3 monme of silver, without saying for what measure.
  • And by the go?
    A sho is ten go, so the brands priced in mon came to about 18 to 33 mon a go.

Sake by the go, at a tavern or a soba shop

  • What did a tavern in Edo charge?
    By a record of the Bunsei era, a go of good sake shipped down to Edo cost 20 to 24 mon and a go of cheap local sake 8 to 10, and most snacks served with it 8 mon each; two or three go with two or three dishes came to about 100 mon. The page that gives these figures names no record.
  • And at a soba shop?
    A soba shop's menu, drawn in a record of the customs of its time from 1830 to its completion in 1853, lists a go of good sake at 40 mon, beside plain soba at 16.
  • Was there a price for a single cup?
    Only one, at one remove: a study of drinking quoted on a popular page says that in the 1750s the Toshimaya, a sake shop by the Kamakura quay in Edo, sold a cup of sake with grilled tofu for 2 mon, and that teahouses in Asakusa sold sake at 28 to 32 mon, without a measure.
  • How much dearer was the tavern than the shop?
    No source gives both prices for the same sake, place and years. The tavern's 20 to 24 mon a go overlaps the shop's 18 to 33, but the two come from different sources and different grades, so no ratio is computed from them.

In the countryside

  • What did sake cost in a village?
    In 1842, after surveying wages and prices, the shogunate ordered each group of villages to buy and sell at official prices. The list it gave the group that took in Nagata, a village now in the city of Oamishirasato, set local sake at 100 mon a sho and sake from Itami at 180. That is 10 and 18 mon a go. These were prices set by order, not prices found in the market.
  • Who sold it?
    In Nagata, six of the sixteen households with a trade on the side kept a tavern, and the taverns of the time mostly sold local sake of their own brewing.
  • And beside the wage?
    The same list set a farm day laborer's wage at 132 mon for a man and 100 for a woman. A sho of local sake was about three-quarters of a man's day, a sho of Itami sake about one and a third days, and a go of local sake about a thirteenth of a day. This is the only source found that gives a sake price and an unskilled wage in the same document.

The day's wage beside the Edo prices

  • What did an unskilled laborer earn in Edo?
    No dated unskilled day wage in copper mon was found for Edo in these years. A popular page gives two figures for no year, naming no source for either: a laborer's day of "about 200 mon", and 320 to 540 mon a day for carpenters, plasterers and navvies together. For the Bunsei era it gives a carpenter, a craftsman, 3 to 5 monme of silver a day.
  • What is the nearest unskilled wage?
    The Kyoto day laborer's wage in the Mitsui house's accounts, in grams of silver: 2.74 to 4.17 g in 1818-1829, the years of the shop and tavern prices, and 2.80 to 3.61 g in 1830-1853, the years of the soba shop. From 1818, Edo's laborers were paid at about the same level as Kyoto's (What did an unskilled laborer earn?).
  • Why are the two not set in one unit?
    The prices are in copper mon and the wages in silver, and this record does not yet give the rate between the two coins, so neither is converted.

China

  • How much dearer was bought wine than home-brewed?
    Twice as dear. Writing in Tongxiang county in 1658, a farmer-scholar says that every household of Suzhou, Huzhou, Shaoxing and Jinhua brewed its own, for offerings, guests and those who work the fields, and that wine bought cost twice what it cost to brew, "and that is still a cheap price".
  • What did spirit cost, retail and in bulk?
    In the farm manual of a Mr. Shen of Huzhou, written in the last years of the Ming, spirit distilled from wine lees fetched 2 fen of silver a jin bought retail and 1.6 fen bought in bulk, so retail was a quarter dearer than bulk.
  • And the wine a farmer bought for his hands?
    The same manual gives an old rule that the field hands' wine was bought at a shop, thirty ladles for a dou of rice, and calls it watery and thin, a profit only to the shop. It does not say how much a ladle held, only that 12 liang (three-quarters of a jin) of wine brewed at home would stand in for one ladle, with twice the flavor and strength. Its year-hired hand was paid 5 taels of silver a year besides his rice (How much rice was a koku?), and his employer reckoned 1.2 taels a year for his firewood and wine.
  • What did a Qing tavern charge by the pot or the bowl?
    No price was found.
