Ancestral halls in south-China villages (citang)

An ancestral hall was where a lineage enshrined and made offerings to its ancestors, and where it also met and feasted. It was the symbol of its village, and in most villages of the Pearl River delta the building that led the village's layout: the halls stood at the very front, facing the village pond across open ground, with the houses behind them. Commoners were widely able to set up halls only from the sixteenth century, and they survive mostly in the south.

The common hall was three bays wide and three halls deep, the halls one behind another on one axis, or two halls deep for a small and less wealthy lineage.

  • Most halls stood at the front of a Pearl River delta village, the houses behind them.
    The villages of the Pearl River delta were laid out in a plan of parallel lanes facing a river channel or a pond, and the ancestral halls and the other public spaces stand mainly at the front of the village, with the houses behind them.
  • Villages had one hall at their center, a row of halls across their front, or no hall at all.
    One historian of these villages sorts them, by period, into three layouts: a village centered on one hall, a village led by a row of halls across its front, and a single-row village of flatter social structure with no hall at all.
  • Most halls faced the village pond, their doors to the water.
    At Songtang, as in most Cantonese villages, the halls stand at the very front of the village next to the pond, facing it with their doors to the water. These ponds have a section of their own: Half-moon ponds in south-China villages (banyuechi).
  • A visitor came in from the water, across the drying floor, into the lanes.
    A visitor walking into such a village passes from the water to the drying floor, the heping, and then into the lanes.
  • Most halls had water and a plaza before them.
    A study of the halls of Panyu finds water and a plaza at the very front of most halls, for fengshui and for drainage, fire and fish.
  • A Hakka hall-and-wing house also put a pond and a drying floor before its gate.
    In most Hakka hall-and-wing houses a half-moon pond fronts the house, the drying floor lies between pond and house, and the main gate stands straight ahead on the house's axis. So in both the delta village and the Hakka house the pond faces the hall, or the house and its gate, across open ground.
  • Commoners could build halls widely only from the Jiajing reign of the Ming (1522-1566).
    The Chinese Wikipedia article on ancestral halls dates to that reign the time when commoners were first widely able to set up halls in their homes.
  • The south keeps more old halls than the north.
    The same article names Fujian, Guangdong, Anhui, Zhejiang, Jiangxi, Guangxi and Hunan as keeping more ancestral halls than the north.
  • Guangzhou alone keeps about 1,300 old halls.
    The city government counts about 1,300 old ancestral halls, great and small, hidden in the streets and lanes of Guangzhou.
  • Three bays wide and three halls deep was the common form.
    In Guangzhou prefecture most small lineage and branch halls were three bays wide, and a single axis of three halls is a form with many examples, spread very widely across the prefecture, used by a lineage's founding-ancestor halls, branch halls and sub-branch halls alike. The halls built in the Cantonese region's second great period of hall building, after Guangzhou became the one port open to foreign trade, were mostly small and middling halls of that form.
  • Halls five bays wide were rare.
    About twenty are known in all of Guangzhou prefecture.
  • A small, less wealthy lineage's hall was mostly two halls deep.
    Three halls deep was the first and commonest choice of most halls of higher standing and larger size, but the branch halls, family schools and shrines to the living of lineages with few members and no great wealth were mostly two halls deep.
  • How big was a hall on the ground?
    The pages we read measure one depth: the rear buildings of several halls at Langtou, in Huadu in Guangzhou prefecture, are 8.5 to 9 m (~28-30 ft) deep. No page we read gives a common village hall's whole width and length on the ground.
