Timber yards and log booms (kiba)

Timber reached Japan's cities by water, and the trade settled where good water transport met the people who bought it. The quarters where timber merchants lived together were called kiba, timber yards, and beside them retail timber dealers were found in every region.

Logs came down a timber river loose, were caught at a rope stretched across the water and made up into rafts, and at the end of the river were held in slack water: rafts moored along a bank, or ponds dug off the river. A floating fence of logs round a pen of water - the log boom - we found on record in East Asia only from modern times.

  • Timber merchants gathered where water transport met their customers.
    They gathered at the Horikawa, a stream through Kyoto, from the twelfth century, and timber guilds stood at the Horikawa, at Kizu and at Kamakura's harbor from the thirteenth.
  • How wide a water did a timber quarter need?
    Not a great river. The Horikawa of Heian Kyoto is said to have been about 12.1 m (~40 ft) wide, and able to serve as a log pond.
  • A retail timber dealer kept sawyers and raftsmen, and the builders took their work from him.
    Retail dealers were found in every region, the dictionary entry on timber merchants says. A dealer kept sawyers to cut the large and small sizes his customers wanted, and raftsmen to move the timber he had bought in, and master carpenters and builders took their work from the dealers.
  • Did a town without a river have a timber dealer?
    In this project's reading, yes. The dictionary puts retail dealers in every region, which we read as taking in towns without a river, though the dealers it describes kept raftsmen and so worked on the water.
  • No page we read gives the size of a dealer's yard.
    The pages on timber dealers, timber-merchant wards and timber yards give no yard area for a retail dealer.

Above the timber quarters lay the timber rivers themselves, where the logs were cut and sent down.

  • On the Kiso River, loose logs ran the swift upper river, and rafts the lower.
    Floating separate logs was dangerous work, and rafting needs a wider stream. On the Kiso above Nishikori the rapids meant the logs were floated loose, and at Nishikori they were made up into rafts and carried downstream.
  • A catch rope (tsunaba) stopped the loose logs where rafting began.
    A tsunaba was a river harbor on the middle course of a timber river, where a strong holding rope was stretched across the water to stop the floated timber. The one at Nishikori lay where the Kiso leaves its gorge.
  • The catch rope at Nishikori, on the Kiso River, is on record from the fifteenth century.
    A pass of 1422 - a lord's written permit for goods to go through a toll barrier - sends a hundred rafts of timber "from Kawakami to the tsunaba", Kawakami being, in the dictionary's reading, the barrier at Nishikori.
  • The Kiso rafts went on to Nagoya and to Kuwana.
    Three men rode each raft, and they carried the timber to the Shiratori storage yard in Nagoya and to Kuwana on Ise Bay.

At the end of the river, Nagoya and Edo held their timber in ponds dug off the water.

  • Nagoya kept its timber in a pond beside the Horikawa canal.
    Tradition says a great pond was dug at Shiratori, beside Nagoya's Horikawa (not Kyoto's stream of that name), about 1610. The Kiso rafts went out to sea and up the Horikawa to it, and it was enlarged from 1651 until, by the end of the Edo period, it covered 23,900 tsubo (about 20 acres).
  • Edo's timber trade moved across the river for fear of fire.
    It settled in 1703 at Kiba, in what is now Koto ward, a district of canals where logs floated in ponds until they were sawn and sold, moved there over the fire risk in the older parts of the city. How the merchants were first gathered after the fire of 1641 is at Shops and trades in towns and villages.
  • Each lot in Edo's timber district at Kiba was a pond ringed by dry ground.
    On the 1858 map of Edo the lots look like a row of square islands. At the center of each, a modern guide explains, was a large pond that stored the timber under water, the dry ground lay only round the lot's edge, and the pond was joined to the canals by which, it is said, rafted timber came in and went out.
  • Did storing timber under water also guard it from fire?
    In this project's reading, yes: the trade was moved to Kiba for fear of fire, and there the timber lay in ponds. The guide to Kiba gives fire risk as the reason for the move, not as the purpose of the ponds.

In China, the timber markets on the rivers kept their rafts moored along the bank.

  • On the Qingshui river, in China, a Qing dynasty rule said where rafts might moor.
    At Guazhi a rule of 1797 carved on a boulder on the bank kept the water above a boundary marker for the upland sellers to moor their timber, and forbade the downstream buyers to moor their rafts there.
  • Nanjing's timber rafts moored along a calm stretch of the Yangtze's banks.
    Shangxinhe, a town about 5 km (~16,404 ft) outside Nanjing's Shuixi gate, grew on one of three channels cut from the Yangtze to the Qinhuai river in the Ming dynasty's Hongwu reign. Where the Jiajiang meets the Yangtze the water ran calm, sheltered by Jiangxinzhou island, so the rafts moored mostly along the banks there, and the banks grew into a timber market that became the town of Shangxinhe, said at its height to be "always sufficient in all four seasons".

The log boom - a fence of floating logs anchored round a pen of water - was built on its greatest scale on American rivers in the nineteenth century.

  • Did East Asia use a floating log fence before modern times?
    None was found. The one Japanese account found of a boom of floats strung on a rope, run from the far bank at a sharp angle to draw the logs to the near bank, rests on modern works (of 1976 to 2020). It takes the former imperial Kiso forest as its example, with a rope of vine and steel cable, and gives the form no date of its own beyond the river floating of timber that lasted into the early 1950s. No earlier East Asian boom was found.
  • How large could a boom be?
    The Susquehanna Boom at Williamsport, Pennsylvania, the largest we found, ran seven miles, its line of cribs crossing the river diagonally from bank to bank. It was strung from 352 cribs, man-made islands of stone and timber 22 ft high, and its 450 acres of enclosed river held up to 300 million board feet, nearly a million logs. How boats got past its crossing line the page does not say.
  • On the St. Croix, the boom works ran beside a navigation channel kept for boats.
    The works filled miles of river with piers, pilings and floating log fences, making log channels and holding pens beside a navigation channel the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers maintained from 1883. An act made the works illegal unless the Corps approved them, and logs still spilled from the logging channel into the navigation right-of-way.
  • A hinged sheer boom let the St. Croix works open and close.
    The sheer boom, a more solid hinged boom, swung open or closed between islands, so the works could expand or contract with the volume of logs.
  • The St. Croix boom company answered for its log jams.
    By 1888 Wisconsin had ruled the Boom Company negligent for every jam caused by backups at its sorting gap, the opening in the boom works where the logs were sorted.
  • The American booms were anchored to fixed ground.
    The Susquehanna's hung from stone-filled cribs and the St. Croix's from piers and pilings. The Hudson's Big Boom was of hewn timbers bolted four together raftwise, chained end to end over friction rollers, each end chained to a heavy abutment.
