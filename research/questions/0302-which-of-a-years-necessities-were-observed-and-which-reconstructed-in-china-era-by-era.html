Which of a year's necessities were observed and which reconstructed, era by era, in China?

Rokugan's money rules weigh a price against a year's necessities in eight eras of China, from Beijing about 1500 to Beijing in the 1910s. Not every good in those eras rests on a price someone recorded in that city in those years. The findings below say, era by era and good by good, which prices were recorded and by whom, which were rebuilt by modern historians, and which the record holds but the era does not use.

Which goods each era prices, and from which source, is this project's choice. The sources, and every price but those given below, are in What did a year's necessities and a day's labor cost in China, era by era?; what a tael and a string of cash held in silver is in What was a tael, a string of cash and a Chinese dollar worth in silver? The Japanese eras are in Which of a year's necessities were observed and which reconstructed, era by era, in Japan?

  • How is the table read?

"Recon." is a modern reconstruction, Allen and his colleagues' Beijing prices, carried back from the early 1900s (how it was done is in the question on a year's necessities in China named above). What lies behind the labels

  • Beijing's shop prices in 1821, by a Russian visitor.
    George Timkowski, of the Russian mission that traveled to Peking in 1820-1821, printed a list of the city's prices in 1821, in copper cash, reckoning the tael at 1,100 cash. By the kin, the Chinese jin: carp and other white fish 50 to 60 cash, alive 100 to 130, frozen sturgeon 60 to 400; salt 20; the best wheat flour 35 to 40; oil, its kind not said, 120. Ordinary coal 4½, the best 6, charcoal 13 to 16, and wood, sold by weight, 6; a common workman 130, the line not saying by the day. At his 1,100 cash a tael, salt is about 0.018 tael a jin, white fish 0.045 to 0.055, ordinary coal 0.41 tael and wood 0.55 a hundred jin, this project's arithmetic. He says himself the rate varied, and describes a little string of 500 cash beside the great string of 1,000 without saying which his prices count. He names no shop or season, and of these goods grades only the flour and the coal.
  • Cotton cloth by the piece, in 1821.
    The list prices cottons by the piece, measured in arshins: a piece of 9 arshins, undressed, as it came from the loom, 1⅓; dressed, 7 arshins, ⅗ to 7/10; ordinary Nankin, the best, 20 to 24 arshins, 1 1/10 to 1⅗; a cloth half an arshin broad, 15 arshins, 1 3/10; white cotton, 13 arshins, 1½. The column prints no unit; that these are taels is this record's reading, from the silk prices in taels just above and the raw cotton in cash just below. The arshin, the Russian measure, was 28 inches, 71.12 cm, so the half-arshin cloth is about 10.7 m long and 36 cm wide, about 0.12 tael a meter, and the others run from about 0.06 (the longest Nankin) to 0.21 a meter, this project's arithmetic.
  • A small house for 1,000 cash a month.
    One of the small government houses by the Russian church was let to a married Manchu of the emperor's guard for a thousand cash a month, and he was bound to guard the church as well, so the rent may sit below the market. At 1,100 cash a tael, about 0.91 tael a month, this project's arithmetic.
  • Fish in Beijing in 1602, at second hand.
    A historian of Ming prices, summing up what a fen of silver bought under the Wanli emperor from the records of Hai Rui, Shen Bang and others, puts meat at 2 fen a jin and fresh or salted fish at about the same, but reports a Jesuit's letter of 1602 that in Beijing 1 fen bought a jin of trout. A fen is a hundredth of a tael. Which city the meat and fish of his summary come from is not said.
  • Cloth and rice in Hong Liangji's grandfather's time and in his own.
    Hong Liangji, a scholar of Changzhou, wrote his Yiyan ("Opinions") in 1793, while he held office in Guizhou, as his chronology records. In it he wrote that he had heard that fifty years before, in his grandfather's and father's time, rice cost no more than 6 or 7 cash a sheng and cloth no more than 30 or 40 a zhang, and that they now cost 30 or 40 and 100 or 200. At the 760-840 cash a tael of the early Qianlong years and the 1,300-1,400 of 1796 in the tael question above, the cloth is about 0.036 to 0.053 tael a zhang in the 1740s and 0.071 to 0.154 in the 1790s, this project's arithmetic. They are an essayist's round figures, the older by hearsay, with no grade of cloth. The essay names no place for either, and does not say where or in what year he saw the present prices: he had gone to Beijing in 1790 and held office there, and was in Guizhou from late 1792. This record reads the older prices as his family's, in Changzhou, a reading.
  • Beijing about 1500 has rice alone.
    Its one price is the rice of the Ming statutes' table of 1497, in What did fruit cost?; no price of beans, fish, oil, cloth, salt, fuel or rent in Beijing about 1500 was found.
  • What is still missing.
    Whole soybeans are priced only in Songjiang, and Beijing's own lists price only bean flour; no fish price was found for Songjiang or Suzhou, nor for Beijing in 1736-1760 (but for the reconstruction's meat or fish) or in 1900-1919; and no rent for Songjiang, Suzhou or Beijing before 1727.
  • Form and market, where they differ from Kyoto 1745's.
    What each good of the Kyoto basket was, and at which market, is in What did a Kyoto laborer's year of goods cost? China's differ. The 1615 and 1736 Beijing prices are officials' prices, not shop prices; the reconstruction's rice is old rice priced from the Yangzi delta, and Beijing's staple grain in it is millet and sorghum; its beans and the official lists' are by weight or as flour, not whole beans by volume. The fish is fresh or frozen carp and sturgeon in 1821, salted in Canton, trout in 1602; the oil is sesame in the official lists and not named in 1821; the fuel is coal, charcoal and firewood by weight; the cloth is by the zhang, the bolt or the piece, at widths mostly not given. Each is in the question on a year's necessities in China, above, or on this page, with its source.
