Why did one new crop spread quickly and another slowly in China and Japan?

Rokugan's setting notes name tobacco among its cash crops and do not mention maize, the sweet potato, the tomato or the other American food crops searched for. The findings below give what historians say decided how fast such crops spread.

Li Xinsheng, writing in the magazine Dushu, says many things decided the spread of a new crop, among them whether it helped production and fitted a farming system, whether it was easily cooked and accepted into a diet, and whether it found cultural resonance. His research article and other accounts add soil and climate, rent and tax, markets, government orders, and disaster. Nearly every account read is about China and American plants, and they disagree on whether the Chinese took these crops up quickly or slowly.

When each crop was first recorded is told under tobacco, the sweet potato, the white potato and the tomato. Whether Japan's trade restrictions stopped new plants is told under the restrictions.

How fast the crops spread in China

  • Did the Chinese take up American crops quickly or slowly? The historians disagree.
    • Alfred Crosby, footnoting the historian Ping-ti Ho, says no large Old World group was quicker, and that the sweet potato was "adopted rapidly".
    • Li's research article says maize and the sweet potato came early but took effect late, with their fastest promotion in the southern mountains from the mid-1700s to the mid-1800s.
    • Li's essay for general readers says both: American crops "soon" spread over China, and then asks why they spread so slowly.
    • Li Hua, on one province, calls the sweet potato's spread in Shandong rapid, and quotes a Qing writer exclaiming at how quickly cotton and the sweet potato traveled.
  • Li criticizes the reading of Ho, not Ho himself.
    Li says Ho never claimed American crops decided Qing population growth, and that later writers inflated his work. Ho, as Crosby quotes him, does say these crops contributed most to the increase in food output over two centuries, and puts American plants at about a fifth of national food production, in a passage whose nearest date is 1937.
  • How large were these crops in the mid-1800s?
    Li's own estimate puts maize at 2.75% of sown area and the sweet potato at 0.67%.
  • Where the two sides agree is on the regions.
    Crosby has maize the main food crop in large parts of the southwestern uplands by the late 1700s and early 1800s, and northern farmers not growing it in quantity until the 1800s. Li too puts the mountains first and has the north later still, in the last decades of the Qing and in the Republican era.
  • A first record does not date a crop's spread.
    Li says the date a crop was introduced has no necessary connection with how much of it was grown, and that a crop often had to be introduced several times before it took root. He also says gazetteer phrases such as "found everywhere" do not show a crop's importance.
  • Was Shandong a fast case or a slow one? Li Xinsheng and Li Hua differ.
    Li Xinsheng says the sweet potato never made the list of Shandong's food crops, though it came before Jiangxi, where it was second largest by the late Qing, and says only that it developed somewhat in Shandong. Li Hua says it reached the whole province gradually around the Opium War, then became the main food of its working population, in some counties about two-thirds of the rural staple. He adds that the spread was uneven, with a few counties showing no record of it into the early Republican era.

The fast crops and the slow ones in China

  • Tobacco was among the fastest, as a cash crop.
    Li says its speed shows the natural advantage of a cash crop. A ban of 1632 and later ones failed because of its profits.
  • The pumpkin was the fast food crop.
    Before the 1600s it had reached some scale in every province but the northeast, Taiwan, Xinjiang, Qinghai and Tibet, Li says, because it kept well and yielded much.
  • The peanut was very slow until a new variety and a machine arrived.
    Li gives its arrival on the southeast coast at the end of the Wanli era as an inference, and has it reaching the north only after 1800, because the small variety yielded little, wooden presses were inefficient and demand was low. A large American variety came to Shandong in 1862, and Western oil presses then made it pay. Crosby, footnoting Ho, has peanuts near Shanghai much earlier, while men who had stormed Tenochtitlán with Cortés still lived.
  • The white potato stayed minor, for reasons of arrival, heat and disease.
    Li gives its late arrival, its dislike of heat in China's crowded warm regions, and two troubles of the plant, degeneration and late blight, which he calls the greatest limit.
  • The chili found its market in one region.
    In the southeast it drew little notice, Li says, because the region had no taste for strong spice. The southwest took it up partly from a belief that it drove out damp, partly as a replacement for scarce salt, and partly because it helped rice go down.

The reasons historians give for China

  • A new crop had to fit the rotation already in the fields.
    Li says the sweet potato struggled in the north because its timing clashed with wheat, while maize fitted better, which is one reason maize later did well there.
  • How much did these crops really yield? Li Xinsheng and Chen Shiyuan differ widely.
    Li gives a yield per unit of land of about 190 jin of maize, and about 250 jin of sweet potato counted as grain, against over 300 for rice, from Republican-era data he assumes held for the Qing. Chen Shiyuan, who promoted the crop, claimed 5,000 to over 10,000 jin of fresh sweet potato a mu. Li Hua repeats this, and Li Xinsheng calls it impossible.
  • Taste and cooking held maize and the sweet potato back.
    Li says they did not fit eating habits, were hard to make into dishes and were often recorded as unpleasant to eat. In the old farming regions maize was long grown only by the handful, to amuse children. Crosby calls the sweet potato the food of the poorest classes, the name "sweet potato eater" being an insult in pre-communist China.
  • Farmers preferred a safe harvest to a large one.
    Li says farmers planted several autumn grains to spread risk, and valued a stable yield above a high one.
  • Soil and climate set the limits.
    The accounts name:
    • Maize, on Li's account, stood drought worse than millet and cold worse than buckwheat, feared waterlogging and did not suit the coastal plains. American crops, he says, though they landed on the coast, did well first in the mountains.
    • The sweet potato, Crosby says, did not compete with rice and did well on soils not used before.
    • Li Hua says it asked less of the soil than other crops did, and reports estimates, from no named source, of about 20% of Shandong's arable land as sandy and 15% as saline.
  • The tax system gave these crops no place, on Li's account.
    Li says Qing taxes were paid in coin or grain, that American crops fetched too little to raise the coin and were not accepted as grain, for tax or for rent. The copy of the article that was read names no statute. He adds that newly cleared hill land could be untaxed or lightly taxed, and that there the crops were in demand, for the growers' own use.
  • Landlords could forbid them.
    Under share rent (rent paid as part of the harvest), Li says, landlords would not let tenants plant such low crops, so the more developed tenancy was on the plains, the smaller the crops' influence.
  • Did cash crops help the new food crops or hinder them? Both are argued.
    Li says migrants cleared the hills for crops such as indigo, ramie and sugarcane, that as these took the good fields and grain grew dearer maize and the sweet potato met the migrants' need for food, and that the two still faced competition from cash crops. Li Hua says cash crops took so much Shandong farmland that the working population accepted the sweet potato more readily.
  • Could the government decide what was planted? The accounts differ.
    • Li Xinsheng says officials urged the sweet potato hard but that it failed to enter the northern farming system and was mostly dropped once the disaster had passed, that bans on maize had little effect, and that planting was the farmers' own choice.
    • Crosby says official edicts urged the sweet potato as it spread into nearly every suitable corner of China.
    • Li Hua dates Shandong's general spread from a provincial order of about 1752. None of the 18 gazetteers he read from before the order mentions the crop, and 39 of the 45 after it do.
  • Disasters helped the sweet potato in Shandong.
    Li Hua calls frequent disasters a factor in its rapid acceptance.
  • Which came first, the new crops or the growing population?
    Ho, as Crosby quotes him, says the American crops made continued population growth possible. Li says the reverse: population pressure decided food production. He takes this from a general theory of farming by the scholar Boserup (COMPARATIVE: not drawn from these countries). Li Hua counts farmland per head in Shandong falling from 7.6 mu to 3.41 between 1753 and 1812, but his population doubles between 1753 and 1767, a jump he does not explain.

China and India compared

  • Did land tenure make a difference in how fast China and India took up the new crops?
    A blog's summary of the historian Sucheta Mazumdar's chapter, which was not read, says so. In China inheritable tenancy gave farmers a reason to stay and get more from the land. The new crops became staples because they gave more calories per acre, resisted drought and pests, took less labor and grew on poor soil. India, the summary says, had more arable land and relatively low population growth between 1600 and 1850, and American crops started flourishing there in the 1800s and 1900s, under colonial rule.

For Japan the sources read are few, and they report causes only in passing.

What the sources read say for Japan

  • Is there a historian's account for Japan of why one crop spread faster than another?
    None was read.
  • Crosby names climate and taste.
    He says Japan is too far north for manioc, a tropical root, and that its inhabitants never came to like maize. They disliked the white potato at first, and it grew well in the colder areas. He says it arrived no later than a planting of 1615 that the sweet potato page reads as the sweet potato.
  • Both tie potatoes to famine.
    Crosby says floods and famines of the 1680s showed the white potato made good cattle feed and stood cold, and calls the sweet potato nearly unfailing famine insurance, naming 1832, 1844, 1872 and 1896. A leaflet from the agriculture ministry's Kyushu office says that in the three great famines of the Edo period the crop was presumably established in Kagoshima, and that no one there is said to have starved.
  • The shogunate tried it as a famine food.
    The leaflet says the scholar Aoki Konyo urged the shogun Yoshimune to adopt it, which led to trial plots, and that he brought it to Edo in 1734. It also reports a theory that a domain official, not the fisherman Maeda Riemon, spread it under Maeda's name.
  • Did poor land help the sweet potato in Japan?
    The leaflet says it came to be widely grown in Kagoshima, which is poor in soil and water, but does not give that as the reason. After giving the 1950 harvests, Crosby thinks it unlikely that any other available food could match the two potatoes' yields where they are planted.
  • Did tax, markets or rivalry with rice set the pace in Japan?
    No page read says.
