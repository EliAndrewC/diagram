How much rice was a koku?

In Rokugan a koku is a gold coin, and also the rice that feeds a person for a year, said to be about 40 gallons; the setting's prices and wages are counted in it. The findings below show what the historical Japanese koku held, as a volume and as a weight of rice, and set the setting's figures beside them.

The koku was a measure of volume, not of weight. From 1669, when the shogunate made one measure official across Japan, a koku held about 180 liters (~48 US gallons), and a koku of brown rice, hulled but not polished, weighed about 150 kg (~331 lb) by modern official reckoning. A koku was taken to be what one adult ate in a year, but a samurai's stipend allowed a person 1.8 koku of brown rice a year.

  • A koku was ten to, a hundred shō or a thousand gō.
    These are the smaller measures of the same system: rice was counted in koku in bulk, in shō in a shop and in gō at a meal.
  • From 1669 a koku was about 180 liters (~48 US gallons).
    In 1669 the shogunate ordered the newer Kyoto measure to be the one official measure, and the koku has had the same capacity since; when Japan fixed the shō against the meter, after 1886, it came to about 1.8039 liters, so a koku is about 180.39 liters.
  • Was the koku the same size before 1669?
    About 4% smaller. The newer measure appeared about the 1620s; the Kyoto measure of the late sixteenth century before it held 62,500 cubic bu to the shō, against the newer one's 64,827. The older shō is therefore about 96.4% of the newer, which puts its koku at about 174 liters (~46 US gallons).
  • Did every domain use the same koku?
    Not at once. Some large, old domains kept their own inherited measures and refused the shogunate's order, though, since their trade ran through Kyoto and Osaka, they came over time to make measures that matched the Kyoto one. No page read gives the capacity of a domain's own measure, so how far a domain's koku differed is not known here. Bales of rice certainly differed: the shogunate's bale held 3 to 5 shō, Kaga domain's 5 to.
  • How much did a koku of brown rice weigh?
    About 150 kg (~331 lb), by the official reckoning of the late 1940s. In 1950 the government said it counted 150 kg of brown rice as a koku, a figure derived from its inspection standard of at least 400 monme to the shō of third-grade brown rice; 400 monme is 1.5 kg, about 0.83 kg to the liter (~6.9 lb to the US gallon). A ministry table of 1949 gave a little less, 146.3 kg. These are twentieth-century rice and twentieth-century grading, so they describe the Edo koku only by analogy.
  • Does a measured liter of brown rice bear that out?
    Yes. The ministry's machine measurements of the 2023 crop found brown rice averaging 827 grams to the liter, which comes to about 149 kg (~329 lb) for a koku of 180.39 liters. This is modern rice, dried and hulled by machine.
  • The koku that rated land was a koku of brown rice.
    The assessed yield by which land, domains and stipends were rated was normally reckoned in the volume of brown rice.
  • How much polished rice did a koku of brown rice make?
    Somewhere between about 88% and 91% of it, by volume. One account has a koku of brown rice yielding 0.91 koku of polished rice, and polished rice weighing about the same as brown for its volume, 150 grams to the gō; at that rate a koku of brown rice makes about 136 kg (~301 lb) of polished rice. Another describes 4 to of a samurai's rice from his fief milling down to about 3 to 5 shō, a loss nearer 12%.
  • How much did a koku of unhulled rice weigh?
    No page read gives the weight of a koku-volume of unhulled rice (paddy), or how much paddy made a koku of brown rice. One clue points to paddy being much lighter than brown rice for its volume: a bale of the Heian period, five to of its day, or two of today's, is put at about 30 kg as pounded rice and about 20 kg as rice still in the husk. At that rate a koku-volume of paddy would weigh about 100 kg (~220 lb); this is a guess of ours from one footnote's figures.
  • A koku was taken to be one adult's rice for a year.
    The convention rests on a gō of rice a meal and three a day, which makes a koku about 333 days' eating. It was a reckoning, not a measured diet.
  • How much rice did a stipend allow a person?
    About 1.8 koku of brown rice a year. A samurai's stipend was counted in rations of 5 gō of brown rice a day, 1 to 5 shō a month and 1 koku 8 to a year; the assessed yield's own article says an adult man was issued this much in practice. An Edo merchant family's house rules are reported to give the same 1.8 koku as what a man needed in a year.

The Chinese measure written with the same character, the shi or dan, was a different and smaller one, and it changed over time.

  • The Chinese shi began as a weight and became a volume.
    In early China the shi was a weight of 120 jin, about 31 kg in the Han; in the Song it was fixed as a volume of 10 dou.
  • How large was the shi?
    About 59.44 liters (~15.7 US gallons) in the Tang, and 100 liters in China's present market measures, where the dan of the same sound is a weight of 50 kg. No page read gives the shi's volume under the Qing.
  • How much rice did a hired hand in south China eat?
    A farm manual of the 1640s and 1650s from Zhejiang, in the Lower Yangzi, reckons a hired man's rice at 1 sheng 5 ge a day averaged over the year, and a woman's at half that. The manual does not give the sheng's size, so this cannot be turned into koku here.

The setting's own figures for the koku sit beside these findings as follows.

  • Is a koku "about 40 gallons"?
    Only in imperial gallons. The setting's notes put a koku, the rice that feeds a person for a year, at about 40 gallons. The historical koku of 180 liters is about 40 imperial gallons or 48 US gallons; read as US gallons, 40 gallons is about 151 liters, some 16% short of the koku. The setting's notes elsewhere put a koku at about 180 liters, about 48 US gallons, which matches.
  • Do the setting's weights per koku match?
    Its 150 kg of brown rice matches the modern official reckoning above. Its 136 kg of polished rice matches what a koku of brown rice yields when milled, not the weight of a koku-volume of polished rice, which one account puts at about the same as brown. Its 187 kg of unhulled paddy is not given by any page read. Its yield reckoning's 0.021 koku to the gallon is one forty-eighth, a US gallon of a 180-liter koku. The one 138 kg figure read is for a koku-volume of polished barley in Korea.
  • Did a person eat one koku a year, or more?
    The setting's notes say both that a koku feeds a person for a year and that a person needs about 2 koku a year at the least, and elsewhere that 3.33 shi of rice feeds a person for a year. The historical convention was a koku a year, and the samurai's ration 1.8 koku of brown rice; no page read gives 3.33 shi or 1.5 koku as a person's yearly rice.
