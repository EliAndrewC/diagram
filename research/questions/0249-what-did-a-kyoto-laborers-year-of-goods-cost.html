What did a Kyoto laborer's year of goods cost?

Rokugan's prices are counted in koku and its smaller coins, and to say whether a price is large or small the setting's money rules measure it against what a laborer earned and spent. The findings below give the one historical reckoning of a Japanese laborer's year of goods that those rules lean on, as its authors printed it.

Two economic historians, Jean-Pascal Bassino and Debin Ma, reconstructed in 2005 a year's food, cloth and lamp oil for one adult in Japan, and priced it in Kyoto in 1745-1754, in grams of silver, mainly from the account books of the Mitsui merchant house. The year's goods cost 326 g of silver (~10.5 troy oz), against an unskilled laborer's wage of 2.82 g a day.

  • The basket is a modern reconstruction, not a recorded budget.
    Its authors built it to give an adult 1,940 calories and 80 g of protein a day (the paper writes "an annual per capita requirement", though 1,940 calories is a day's food and it compares its baskets' "daily calories") with set amounts of linen and lamp oil, from records of staple food in the early 1700s and late 1800s and from surveys of other foods in the early 1900s. Its soybeans stand for miso, soy sauce, tofu and natto, so their amount is more than the 1920s surveys report.
  • There are two Japanese baskets, A for an average income and B for bare subsistence.
    Basket A, with more rice, soybeans and fish, is meant for the average income group; basket B, with more barley, less rice and soybeans and no fish, for subsistence. The paper prices its comparisons with basket A throughout.

  • The basket's quantities and prices multiply out to its printed total.
    Basket A's nine quantities times their Kyoto prices come to 325.816 g, which the paper prints as 326, and each good's share of that sum is the share printed.
  • Does the beans price follow from the ratio the paper gives?
    Not exactly. The paper gives beans a price ratio to rice of 0.63, and rice a price of 1.49 g a kg; 1.49 times 0.63 is 0.94, against the 0.99 printed in its table. The paper prices beans by the liter and rice by the kilogram, and does not say on what basis the ratio was taken. It prices fish the same way, from its ratio to rice in early Meiji, but does not give that ratio.
  • What did basket B cost?
    About 236 g of silver at the same Kyoto prices. The paper's footnote gives 236 and 200 g for the 1750 and 1884 benchmarks, under the name of basket A, with real wages of 0.012 and 0.023 "higher than the 0.09 and 0.012" from basket A; but basket A cost 326 g, and its real wage in its own table is 0.009, so the footnote's 236 g is basket B's cost and its 0.09 is a misprint for 0.009. Its 0.012 for 1884 is not basket A's real wage either: the paper's 1884 wage of 4.5 g against a basket of 277 g makes 0.016. Basket B's quantities times the Kyoto prices come to 235.876 g. That is about 28% cheaper than basket A, where the paper's text says roughly 40%.

What was bought, good by good, and where its price was taken

The paper says what form most of its goods took, and where most of its prices came from. Only its rice and lamp-oil prices were recorded in Kyoto in 1745-1754; its soybeans and edible oil borrow those two prices, its cloth is priced from Osaka in the 1840s, and the rest are worked back from prices of the 1870s and 1880s.

  • Rice was white rice.
    The paper sets its soybeans against "the price of white rice in Kyoto", and the Mitsui house's Kyoto table, as transcribed, prices white rice by the koku.
  • Soybeans were the beans themselves, standing for what was made from them.
    Priced by the kilogram, they stand for miso, soy sauce, tofu and natto. Their price is set equal to Kyoto's rice: the Osaka soybean prices recorded from 1757 ran a little above it, but the soybeans traded in Osaka were "of above average quality".
  • The other beans are not named.
    The paper names them only as "Beans (excluding soybeans)", or in its price table "Beans (other than soybeans)", measured in liters, and does not say which bean.
  • Barley, wheat, buckwheat and the millets were priced as grain.
    Their price is worked back from early Meiji ratios to rice. Those ratios are, by this record's reading, the ones in the paper's prices of 1882-1886, which it built from the ratio of farm-gate prices of barley, naked barley and wheat to unhusked rice in 1874-1901, multiplied by the retail rice price of 1882-1886; "buckwheat and others" are buckwheat, foxtail millet, proso millet, barnyard millet and sorghum, priced the same way. So they are priced as grain, by farm-gate ratios to unhusked rice, not as flour or noodles, by this record's reading of the method, and no price of 1745 at any market lies behind them.
  • Fish was salmon and cod.
    Its 1745-1754 price is worked back from its early Meiji ratio to rice, and the paper built its fish price of 1882-1886 from the wholesale price of salmon and cod against the wholesale price of rice in 1882-1886, multiplied by the retail price of rice; the paper does not say whether the fish was fresh, salted or dried. What fish a laborer in Kyoto or Osaka ate in the 1740s was not found. The nearest account is a popular history blog's retelling of an Edo diary of 1860, kept by a samurai on low pay who ate much as Edo's commoners did: it says he seems to have relied on sardines and salmon, and that horse mackerel was an expensive fish then. It is one man, a century later, in another city.
  • Both oils were rapeseed oil.
    Lamp oil was rapeseed oil, and its price stands for edible oil too: in Japan rapeseed oil was used both for lighting and for cooking, "neither a cheap nor a low-quality item", and sesame oil "was more a condiment than an edible oil".
  • The linen was cotton cloth.
    The paper prices it as "cotton linen", from Osaka prices of 1840-1849 in a book of 1963 by Miyamoto, on the ground that raw cotton cost about the same then as in the 1740s. That book was not read, so the weave, the width and the market at which its price was taken are not known here; what a tan of cotton was and what it cost elsewhere are in What did cloth and clothing cost?
  • Were the Kyoto prices retail?
    So they are labeled. The rice and lamp-oil prices come from the Mitsui merchant house's books, which the paper calls "Mitsui Bunko retail prices in Kyoto"; the spreadsheet that transcribes them heads the table "Retail prices and daily wages of unskilled workers in Kyoto" and names its kind of transaction as "Account books". The printed table's own Japanese title was not read. They are prices a great merchant house entered in its own books; whether a laborer paid the same by the sho in a shop is not said.
  • The Osaka soybean prices were market quotations.
    The paper's Osaka soybeans come from the Mitsui volume's Table 3, "from 1757". A library's reference answer lists that volume's tables: its Table 3 is the "Table of the market prices of the main goods in Osaka from the first month of Horeki 7 (1757) to An'ei 2 (1773)", and its Tables 1 and 2 are tables of Osaka market prices too. That the paper's Table 3 is this one is this record's reading, from the same volume, number and first year; the answer does not list the goods the table holds.
  • Miso and soy sauce were priced in the same Kyoto books.
    The Mitsui Kyoto table prices miso by 10 kan and soy sauce by the koku in every spring and autumn of 1745-1754, and the paper does not use them. Miso averaged 15.43 monme for 10 kan (between 12.2 and 18.54) and soy sauce 91.06 monme a koku (between 82.63 and 98.22): about 1.28 g of silver a kilogram of miso and 1.57 g a liter of soy sauce at the paper's 3.11 g of silver a monme, beside the soybeans' 1.49 g a kilogram, by this project's arithmetic.
  • How much did a shop add?
    No markup from merchant to shop or from shop to household was found for Kyoto or Osaka in the mid-1700s, for any good. Two narrower cases were. The Mitsui house's Edo cotton store, which mainly bought and sold cotton goods, made a profit rate on the Ise cotton it bought of 2.6% in the Kansei era (1789-1801), 3.9% in the early Bunsei years and 2.1% in the late Tenpo years, against 4.4, 4.2 and 3.5% for Osaka cotton; a senior clerk's note of 1798 gives 4 to 5% on Ise striped cotton and 11 to 12% on a Gifu weave. The study does not call the store a retail shop, but it says cotton was a good expected to bring in cash from small-profit, high-volume shop-front sales to common people, and it names the Edo store among those actively growing cash sales of cotton. A village salt dealer's margin of 1842 is in What did salt cost?
  • Salt and firewood, and a room.
    The basket holds no salt, no firewood or charcoal and no rent; what salt and firewood the Mitsui Kyoto books priced, and at which market, is in What did salt cost? and What did charcoal cost?, and the rents found in What did it cost to rent a room or a house?
