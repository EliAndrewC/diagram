What did a Kyoto laborer's year of goods cost?

Rokugan's prices are counted in koku and its smaller coins, and to say whether a price is large or small the setting's money rules measure it against what a laborer earned and spent. The findings below give the one historical reckoning of a Japanese laborer's year of goods that those rules lean on, as its authors printed it.

Two economic historians, Jean-Pascal Bassino and Debin Ma, reconstructed in 2005 a year's food, cloth and lamp oil for one adult in Japan, and priced it in Kyoto in 1745-1754, in grams of silver, from the account books of the Mitsui merchant house. The year's goods cost 326 g of silver (~10.5 troy oz), against an unskilled laborer's wage of 2.82 g a day.

  • The basket is a modern reconstruction, not a recorded budget.
    Its authors built it to give an adult 1,940 calories and 80 g of protein a day with set amounts of linen and lamp oil, from records of staple food in the early 1700s and late 1800s and from surveys of other foods in the early 1900s. Its soybeans stand for miso, soy sauce, tofu and natto, so their amount is more than the 1920s surveys report.
  • There are two Japanese baskets, A for an average income and B for bare subsistence.
    Basket A, with more rice, soybeans and fish, is meant for the average income group; basket B, with more barley, less rice and soybeans and no fish, for subsistence. The paper prices its comparisons with basket A throughout.

  • The basket's quantities and prices multiply out to its printed total.
    Basket A's nine quantities times their Kyoto prices come to 325.816 g, which the paper prints as 326, and each good's share of that sum is the share printed.
  • Only the rice and lamp-oil prices were recorded in Kyoto in those years.
    The rice and lamp-oil prices are Kyoto retail prices from the Mitsui records. The soybean price is set equal to rice, because the Osaka soybean prices recorded from 1757 ran a little above it. Beans, barley and wheat, and buckwheat are priced from their ratio to rice in the early Meiji period, after 1868; the cloth from Osaka prices of the 1840s, on the ground that raw cotton cost about the same then as in the 1740s; and edible oil at the lamp oil's price, since both were rapeseed oil.
  • What kind of beans and cloth?
    The paper names the beans only as "beans (excluding soybeans)", measured in liters, and does not say which bean. Its linen is priced as cotton linen.
  • Does the beans price follow from the ratio the paper gives?
    Not exactly. The paper gives beans a price ratio to rice of 0.63, and rice a price of 1.49 g a kg; 1.49 times 0.63 is 0.94, against the 0.99 printed in its table. The paper does not say how the fish price was derived for these years.
  • What did basket B cost?
    About 236 g of silver at the same Kyoto prices. The paper's footnote gives 236 and 200 g for the 1750 and 1884 benchmarks, under the name of basket A, with real wages of 0.012 and 0.023 "higher than the 0.09 and 0.012" from basket A; but basket A cost 326 g, and its real wage in its own table is 0.009, so the footnote's 236 g is basket B's cost and its 0.09 is a misprint for 0.009. Basket B's quantities times the Kyoto prices come to 235.876 g. That is about 28% cheaper than basket A, where the paper's text says roughly 40%.
