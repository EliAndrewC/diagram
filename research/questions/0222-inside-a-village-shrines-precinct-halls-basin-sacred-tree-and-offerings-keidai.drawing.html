How our maps draw a village shrine's precinct: halls, basin, sacred tree and offerings (keidai)

At average wealth, a village shrine on our maps is its one-roof hall - the villagers' worship hall and the country monk's dwelling under a single roof - with the small sanctuary behind it, the torii over its approach, a sacred tree and a stone basin, all in a clearing inside the shrine's wood, and nothing else. What a richer parish gave its shrine, and a stage, a sumo ring or a bell, are knobs.

  • Our maps draw the sanctuary about 6 ft square, one bay wide, at the back of the precinct.
    It is drawn at the size of the one village sanctuary we found measured, behind the hall on the approach's axis, where a sanctuary stood, at the rear of the shrine and on the worship hall's axis (accurate). That the approach runs on the same axis is a map drawing convention: neither source names the approach.
  • Our maps draw the villagers' worship hall 20 to 35 ft on a side.
    The band runs from a thatched hall of 1778 in Saitama, 6.54 m (~21 ft) across, to a shrine's worship hall of 1790 in Ehime, 10.62 m (~35 ft) (accurate as a band). A side hall on a temple's approach at Chikusei, 3.83 m (~13 ft), is not a hall where villagers gathered, so it does not set the small end.
  • Our maps put the worship hall and the country monk's dwelling under one roof, a building of 2,100 to 3,600 sq ft.
    The band rests on one attested example, a city temple's, not a village's, and the only one found (accurate as a band). The example, and the dwelling where it stands apart, are at the monk's dwelling; where the building stands in the shrine's wood is at How our maps site and size a village shrine.
  • A village shrine is bigger than a farmhouse, because it contains one.
    The hall alone is farmhouse-sized or smaller, and the country monk's dwelling is a farmhouse in form (the monk's dwelling); under one roof the two make a building of two to three farmhouses.
  • The first torii stands where the approach enters the shrine's ground.
    The others follow it along the approach, spaced as How our maps draw shrine gateways and the approach to the hall (torii, sando) sets out.
  • No fence, wall or hedge surrounds the precinct.
    The reasons are at Shrine woods and fences (chinju no mori, tamagaki). The precinct itself is the shrine's wood, its area inside the registers' band (How our maps site and size a village shrine).
  • A sacred tree is drawn by default, beside the approach.
    The roped tree is accurate in kind; how common one was at a village shrine is a GUESS, since no source counts them.
  • A plain stone basin for rinsing the hands and mouth stands beside the approach at the innermost torii.
    That one stood beside the approach is accurate, and a parish gave its shrine one before modern times. It is drawn with no pavilion over it; neither the open basin nor the roofed one is attested at a village shrine before 1868, so its roof is a GUESS.
  • The shrine's dedication is named at the sanctuary.
    The kami the shrine serves is named beside its sanctuary; the hall itself carries no label.
  • A kitchen garden and a privy stand at the country monk's dwelling, and fire-water at the wooden buildings.
    This is a GUESS, from the farmhouse the dwelling is drawn as: no page we read names them at a village shrine.
  • A belfry is a knob, not drawn unless it is turned on.
    At small temples a main hall and a belfry alone are the commonest arrangement, but no page says whether a village shrine kept by a country monk had its bell, so the bell is a knob.

Some things a parish gave its shrine are drawn only at a richer parish.

  • Guardian dogs, lanterns and strength stones are on a wealth knob, and none is drawn at average wealth.
    They are gifts a parish gave its shrine as it grew richer. The strength stones stand beside the approach.
  • A Bishamon shrine's guardian pair is drawn as tigers.
    That is accurate at named temples, and its reach to a village is a GUESS.
  • No fence is drawn round the sanctuary, however rich the parish.
    At a village shrine such a fence is attested only in modern times (Shrine woods and fences (chinju no mori, tamagaki)).

Other buildings a shrine might have had are knobs, or are left off the maps.

  • A farmers' stage and a sumo ring are knobs, absent by default.
    Farmers often built their stages on shrine ground, and sumo was offered at shrines across the country, but the maps leave both off unless they are turned on.
  • Subsidiary shrines, an office, a votive-plaque hall and a portable-shrine store are not drawn.
    No page we read places them at a village shrine.
  • A sanctuary with no worship hall, under a covering house, is not drawn.
    A covering house over a small sanctuary is recorded, but our maps house the country monk under the worship hall's roof, which a sanctuary with no worship hall does not allow (How our maps draw a village shrine's monk, the monk's dwelling (kuri), its records and its land).
  • Chinese villages' opera stages, built facing the temple, are not drawn.
    A Chinese temple's stage for plays offered to its god was often built facing the temple; the shrines our maps draw follow the Japanese village shrine, whose stage is the farmers' stage.

A magistrate's compound keeps a shrine of its own, drawn on the building plans (Every administrative compound keeps a shrine). Where it serves more than one kami, how they are housed follows the three forms the record found.

  • Which form a compound shrine takes is chosen at random for each map.
    The pages we read describe three and rank none, so the maps choose among them at random:
    • Altars side by side across the hall, one to a bay, the main kami's in the middle or on the right as you face it, the order a household altar shelf of three shrines is set in.
    • A single altar for the main kami, the others kept behind it and not drawn apart, as on a household's one-shrine shelf; in a hall this form is a GUESS.
    • A second small shrine of its own inside the compound.
  • Altars side by side in one undivided hall are drawn as the first form, and that is a GUESS.
    What the pages attest is the two-bay hall and small halls joined under one roof, not separate altars inside one undivided hall.
