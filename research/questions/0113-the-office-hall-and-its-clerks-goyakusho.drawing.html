How our maps draw the office hall and its clerks (goyakusho)

Our maps draw a county magistrate's office hall as one long building in the office's outer court, sized between two offices whose buildings were measured, with a floor of a few thousand square feet. The clerks' room is a room inside it, beside the day office, where the magistrate's own samurai staff do their daily business. The clerks have no building of their own at the office, to work or to live in.

  • How big is the office hall on our maps?
    Between about 1,600 and 6,750 sq ft. That is always well below the 8,100 sq ft of the Takayama intendant's office, and, except at its low end, above the about 2,850 sq ft of a small branch office's whole central building. The ceiling is accurate, calibrated against the two measured offices; the floor, below the smaller of them, is a GUESS.
  • How long and deep is the office hall on our maps?
    80 to 150 ft long and 20 to 45 ft deep. The smallest and largest halls multiply out to the floor above (80 by 20 ft is 1,600 sq ft; 150 by 45 ft is 6,750 sq ft), but the proportions are a GUESS: no page we read gives an intendant's or a county office's hall a length and depth. The drawn depth is deeper than the one hall measured both ways, a domain's council hall of about 12 by 63 ft.
  • Behind the magistrate's dais, our maps draw a day office and an official study.
    The dais is the front of the office hall, above the hearing court (How our maps draw the hearing court (shirasu)). A dais standing alone would leave the magistrate and the samurai staff nowhere to do their daily paperwork, and, on this project's reading, the private study in the residence stands on the household side of the line between office and home, the wrong place for official business. So the dais is drawn as the front of a deeper hall with those two rooms behind it. That is this project's reading of what the block must hold, a GUESS: no page we read places those two rooms behind the court.
  • Our maps put the clerks' room inside the office hall, behind the dais band and beside the day office.
    Beside the day office it is as at the Takayama Jin'ya, where the workroom of the officials hired mostly from the district was partitioned off beside the room where the shogunate's own officials worked. Behind the dais it is a GUESS for a county office, bounded by Edo's town magistracies. There, the rooms for suits and examinations, the saikyosho and the court among them, were gathered in one quarter of the magistracy, and the plan of the North Town Magistracy of 1841 that the page links to shows, directly behind the saikyosho, which faces the court, a room whose name (調之間, read here shirabe no ma) makes it a working room for the examining officials; no page we read states its use. No plan or account we read of an intendant's office says what lay behind its dais, and in one Chinese county office, as a former county secretary recalled it, the second hall stood behind the great hall.
  • Along the office hall's back row our plans draw, from west to east, the armory, the writing room, the binding room, the clerks' room, the day office and the official study - the writing and binding rooms' places and the study's a GUESS.
    So the clerks' room adjoins the day office, as the workroom of the district's officials adjoined the officials' room at Takayama. At Takayama a writing room and a binding room were named, the binding room's papers going to a ledger storehouse beside it, but beyond the binding room's lying next to the writing room, no page we read says where either stood against the office hall's other rooms, gives either a size, or gives a small office either room before 1868; a writing room at a small office appears only in a rebuild of 1993, first in from the entrance and beside the court room. So the writing and binding rooms' seats between the armory and the clerks' room, and the official study's seat at the back row's east end, east of the day office, are each this project's choice, a GUESS on the record's silence.
  • Our maps give the clerks no building of their own to work in.
    No page we read gives an office's clerks a building of their own to work in, so a separate clerks' building would be a GUESS; they work in their room in the office hall.
  • Our maps house the clerks in town, not at the office.
    An intendant's staff did have housing inside the compound, apart from the office hall: huts and row houses for its tetsuki and tedai. This setting's clerks are heimen scribes hired from the county town, who live at home in town and come in each day, so our maps draw no housing for them at the office. That is a deliberate deviation from the Japanese record.
