How our maps draw polders (weitian, waju)

Every polder on our maps is enclosed by its dike all the way round, and water crosses the dike only at gated sluices: an inlet at the polder's head and an outfall at its tail, which this project reads as its high and low ends. In the one polder whose count we found, a waju, the dike had many openings, drains outnumbering intakes. How many a polder on our maps gets is a calibrated liberty set by its size, and a village polder gets two, enough, in this project's reading (a GUESS), for a small polder.

The rule the map follows, for where the polder and its village sit:

  • Both polders in the pool are drawn against the landward shore.
    The wild pond that feeds the polder stands to its northwest, reed marsh lies along its low south side where the outfall drains, and its dry landward shore lies to the east.
  • Both polders carry a reed fringe along their west flank.
    So the map shows that the west dike holds water back, as every flank of a polder but the landward one does.
  • The reed strip is drawn about 280 ft deep.
    That depth is a GUESS: no page we read gives the width of a belt of reeds outside a polder's dike. The nearest record is Shen Kuo's, of the Wanchun polder, a great official polder rebuilt in 1061: its rebuilders reckoned on ground that sloped gently up for a hundred paces, with a hundred rows of willows along the dike and reeds planted below them, so that the water bit a hundred paces off and never met the dike. The hundred paces measure that whole foreshore, willows and reeds together, not the reeds alone. At the five or six feet to a pace recorded for the Ming dynasty (Fish fry and nursery ponds (yumiao)) they come to some 500 to 600 ft, by this project's arithmetic, so the drawn strip is about half of that foreshore.
  • The village is drawn on the dry ground just off the dike, to the east.
    Houses on a polder's raised margin, which was also its dike, are recorded. Where a polder was built against a shore, with dry ground along its landward side, this project's reading, a GUESS, is that its village stood on that dry ground a few steps off the dike instead.
  • A village is drawn hard against its own polder.
    In this project's reading, a GUESS, a polder's community kept its own dike, so its houses press up to its fields.
  • A polder's village keeps its shelter belt on the windward side its polder leaves dry.
    The wind decides which side a belt stands on, and it stands on one or two windward sides (How our maps draw the shelter belt: its sides, its trees, and why it runs off the edge of the map). On the pool's polders, with the polder to the west and the northwest wind, the belt takes the north end of the houses, and none stands between the houses and the dike, which the village stays a few steps off. On the Nobi plain's ring-dike polders most groves stand on the northwest side of the homestead, and every one-sided grove seen in 2018 stands to the north-to-west of its plot; in the row villages by the ring dike, the aerial photographs of 1949 show each of four such groves as part of one grove running through its whole row, but the study never says which side of a row faces the dike. No page we read puts a belt between the houses and a dike, so leaving none there is a GUESS.
  • Dike-top housing is a knob no map uses yet.
    A polder with water on every flank and no shore to build on would seat its farmhouses in single file on the dike crest, this project's drawing of the houses on the margin that are recorded. The generator can draw it, but no settlement in the pool uses it.
  • A hamlet is drawn with a polder only when its map asks for one.
    Of the field forms a hamlet's fields can take, only the valley fan is dealt at random, though the polder (Polders: fields diked against the fluctuating water (weitian, waju)) and the dike-pond block (Dike-ponds: fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes (sangji yutang)) are attested too; neither is yet among the forms dealt, so both are a known gap.
  • Where a map takes it, each dike-top house stands on a widened stretch of the crest.
    The band bulges round each house instead of the house overhanging the water. The Lake Tai village's raised margin was wider where it held the houses (Polder dikes), but no page we read gives the ground a house took on a dike, so the widened stretch, the house and about 14 ft by 10 ft more, is a GUESS.
  • No dike-top house stands over a sluice notch.
    A house is kept back from each gap where water crosses the dike, about 34 ft on a hamlet map and 68 ft on a village map, so the notch stays open. That a house kept off the notch is a GUESS: no page we read says where on a dike the houses stood relative to its sluices.
  • No yard or garden is drawn up on the crest.
    The crest serves each house as its dooryard and its lane, and the gardens lie down on the plots beside the dike. The Lake Tai villagers had only very small gardens beside their houses and grew vegetables under the mulberry (The dike-pond hamlet), but no page we read says where on a dike a house's yard or garden lay, so drawing none on the crest is a GUESS.
  • Enokida's village falls inside the household range guessed for a small polder.
    Its 16 households sit inside the ten to twenty households this project guesses, a GUESS, for a small polder.

The rule the map follows, for the water inside:

  • A ring canal is drawn round the inside foot of the dike, on the field side.
    From outside in, a map draws the wild water, the dike, the ring canal, the field ditches and the paddies, and no canal rings the dike on its outside: only the feeder, from the wild water to the inlet sluice, and the outfall's short run below its sluice cross the ground outside it. Channels inside the dike are recorded; a single ring at the dike's foot is not, so its place is a drawing convention.
  • The ring canal is drawn straight along the dike's inside foot, square at the corners.
    It keeps to the dike and turns where the dike turns. Its line is a drawing convention: no source read names a canal at a dike's inside foot at all. What is recorded lies elsewhere. Jia Dan, writing of the Suzhou country in 1070, has the ancients cut lengthwise and crosswise channels laid out like a chessboard, so that the water ran outside and the fields were made inside; and in a Lake Tai village studied in 1936 the big drainage trench lay in the lowest part of the dish-shaped polder, whose center was lower than its margin.
  • On a dike-pond block the ring canal is drawn no narrower than the block's creeks.
    The creeks between the ponds open on the ring canal, and a channel is never drawn wider than the one it joins (How our maps draw channel widths). The creeks' own width, about 6 to 8 ft, is itself a GUESS (How our maps draw dike-ponds (sangji yutang)), and this bound is a GUESS: no source read names a canal at a dike's inside foot, so none gives it a width. A rice polder's ring canal keeps the usual widths.
  • A creek between ponds stops where it meets the ring canal.
    Its end is drawn to the ring canal's middle line and no farther, never across it onto the dike's foot. That is a GUESS, a fallback of this project's drawing: no page read says how a channel between ponds met a canal at the dike.
  • The feeder is drawn straight from the wild pond to the inlet sluice.
    Its course is a GUESS: it is drawn as a reach of the wild water itself, by the shortest line to the inlet. No page we read gives the course of a channel feeding a polder's inlet. What is recorded outside a dike is the bounding channels Jia Dan says the ancients cut round the Suzhou polders, lengthwise and crosswise, with the water running outside and the fields inside.
  • The other canals inside a polder are drawn in long, nearly straight reaches that waver gently, with rounded corners.
    On this project's reasoning a dug canal wavers with the ground and with repair, and the finer field ditches are drawn crookeder still. The ring canal is the exception: it holds to the dike's foot and does not waver. The line is a drawing convention.
  • The horita is not drawn yet.
    It would suit a waterlogged polder, in place of ordinary paddy (Polders: fields diked against the fluctuating water (weitian, waju)): long raised strips of rice with water channels between, recorded for Japanese polders from the eighteenth century.
  • The chain pump is not drawn.
    Nothing we read says where it stood on a polder or how many a polder had (Polders: fields diked against the fluctuating water (weitian, waju)).

On our maps a rice polder and a dike-pond block lay out their insides differently: the rice polder on a regular grid of straight canals, the dike-pond block as a mosaic, its lattice of creeks bent out of line so that its four-sided ponds skew to uneven shapes, or on about one hamlet in three as a chessboard of ponds.

  • A rice polder is drawn on a surveyed grid.
    The lower Yangtze polders were laid out on a lattice of channels, so the pool's rice polder keeps the grid.
  • A dike-pond block is drawn as a mosaic or as a chessboard of ponds, rolled for each hamlet.
    The grid is recorded for the canals. For the ponds two layouts are recorded: a mosaic, in a modern essay on the delta's past, and a chessboard, in Qu Dajun's account of Jiujiang in 1678, its dikes the black lines and its ponds the squares. Today the chessboard is also the view from the air by Lake Tai, and in one Pearl-delta village a modern plan would make the ponds regular. With two layouts recorded, each dike-pond hamlet rolls its layout: the mosaic, the form the essay gives the delta's past, on about two hamlets in three, the chessboard on the rest. The odds are a GUESS.
  • The mosaic bends the lattice but keeps every pond four-sided.
    The interior lattice is pushed out of line by a smooth drift that fades to nothing at the fixed outer edge. Neighboring crossing points move together, so the lines stay continuous but bend, the channels between the ponds meander as creeks do, and the parcels skew to trapezoids while each pond stays four-sided, as the record has most of them.
  • A channel is drawn along every pond's edge.
    That is a GUESS. Between ponds the channels are the creeks that boats used (Dike-ponds: fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes (sangji yutang)), but Qu Dajun's chessboard of 1678 has dikes for its lines, not water, and no source read says a creek ran on every side of a pond; whether two ponds shared one bank is not recorded. The maps draw a channel on every side, so each pond stands in a ring of bank of its own.
  • How strongly the lattice bends is a calibrated liberty.
    It was set on the Kuwabata map, which is drawn with it.
  • A mosaic that would pinch a creek is bent less.
    Where the full bend would leave a corridor between two ponds narrower than the creek drawn in it, that block is bent less, the bend eased a twentieth at a time until every corridor holds its creek, so no creek is laid over a bank and no pond is drawn smaller to make room. A block with no corridor pinched keeps the whole bend, as Kuwabata's does. That is a GUESS, a fallback of this project's drawing.
