How did a Regency English household divide its income?

Rokugan's households divide their income by the setting's own customs, and nothing here sets them. This is a comparative question: how one English household manual of 1825 told a gentleman to divide his income, a yardstick for players who know the households of Austen and Dickens.

The Complete Servant, a London household manual of 1825 by two former servants, gives a gentleman a rule "in round numbers" for dividing a large income: a third to the household's provisions and consumption, a quarter to servants, horses and carriages, a quarter to clothes, schooling and extras, an eighth to rent, taxes and repairs, and the rest held in reserve. It then lists the servants a family usually kept at each income, from a widow on £100 a year to a household of £4,000 to £5,000. What each of those servants was paid is a question of its own, What did a Regency English household pay its servants?

The five shares of a large income

  • A third went to the household's consumption.
    33 percent, or one third, "for Household Expenses, including Provisions and all other Articles of Household Consumption".
  • A quarter went to servants and the stable.
    25 percent, or one fourth, "for Servants and Equipage including Horses, Carriages, and Liveries".
  • A quarter went to clothes, children and extras.
    25 percent, or one fourth, "for Clothes, Education of Children, Medical Assistance, Pocket, Private, and Extra Expenses; including Entertainments, &c."
  • An eighth went to the house.
    12½ percent, or one eighth, "for Rent, Taxes, and Repairs of House and Furniture".
  • The rest was a reserve.
    4½ percent "as a Reserve for Contingencies". The five shares make 100 percent. The reserve, the book says, could go to the children's fortunes or to the unforeseen.

  • Smaller incomes were divided by heads of the family.
    A family with children spent from a quarter to a third of its expense on each of the two parents, and about a tenth or a twelfth on each child.
  • How little could a family live on?
    The book holds that £150 to £200 a year will keep a man and his wife, two or three children and a servant girl, and, in words it puts in quotation marks, that beyond it they need spend nothing more, whatever their income.
  • A great house spent well under its income.
    The country gentleman whose servants the book lists had a net income of £16,000 to £18,000 a year and spent no more than £7,000.
