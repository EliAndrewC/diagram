Inns (hatago and carters' inns)

A Japanese inn on the highway (hatago) was a townhouse with rooms to let, its guest rooms upstairs or at the back. A large one at the post town of Akasaka ran to some 54 ft of frontage and some 137 ft back from the street. The commonest Chinese inn under the Ming dynasty was something else: the plain "mule-and-horse" inn of the open country, where carters stopped and often cooked for themselves.

How many inns a post town kept, and the frontages of its large, middling and small ones, are at Travelers' inns and cheap lodging houses (hatago, dian, kichin-yado). Lords and officials lodged elsewhere: Lodgings for lords and officials on the road (honjin).

  • A post town's inns were built on the same plan as its townhouses.
    Past the big street door the earth floor ran to the back, beside it the board-floored shop and kitchen, and several guest rooms were fitted out upstairs or at the back.
  • On the late Edo-period Tokaido, a projecting lattice on an inn's upper story marked it as an inn.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on the merchant's townhouse calls the lattice on the road-side upper floor a mark of the inn.
  • At Akasaka on the Tokaido an inn held an earth floor, rooms, a kitchen, a bath and a privy.
    It was made of an earth floor, a board-floored room, rooms, formal rooms, the kitchen quarters, a bath and a privy.
  • At Akasaka an inn was usually two stories where it faced the road.
    The city of Toyokawa's page on the old post town says two stories was usual on the highway front.
  • One of the large inns at the post town of Akasaka, the Ohashiya, was about 54 ft wide and 137 ft deep.
    The city of Toyokawa's page gives about 9 ken of frontage and 23 of depth.

A survey of China's old inns describes two kinds of inn under the Ming dynasty, and the commoner was the carters' inn of the open country. Later accounts describe northern road inns: the inn yards a traveler found in north China in 1863, and the cart inn of Northeast China, a name from the late Qing dynasty and the early Republic of China that followed it in 1912.

  • The Ming had inns that were an eating house in front and lodgings behind.
    A survey of China's old inns calls them "drink in front, lodgers behind", eating and lodging in one.
  • The commonest Ming inn was the plain "mule-and-horse great inn" of the carters.
    Such inns stood mostly in the open country, and their guests were mostly laborers driving carts, who often cooked for themselves, so the service was simple and the price cheap.
  • A traveler in north China in 1863 found horses and mules kept in the yards of two inns.
    At the inn at Nankow (Nankou) the courtyard was already full of travelers' gear, with horses, mules and donkeys loose all over it. At another inn the travelers' horses and mules stood tied up in the courtyard.
  • In 1863 the inn at Nankou had a dormitory where a traveler's party slept.
    Amid the yells of fellow travelers waking from nightmares, the party retreated to its dormitory.
  • When did Northeast China's cart inn arise?
    The Chinese Wikipedia article on the cart inn says it appeared at the end of the Qing dynasty, though the word's first use cannot be fixed more closely than the late Qing and the early Republic of China (from 1912).
  • A cart inn's rooms held heated platforms sleeping about twenty each.
    Against the cold its rooms had heated platforms (kang) along the north and south walls, each a common bed for about twenty. A self-published account of the Northeast's cart inns gives more than twenty.
  • Where did a cart inn stand?
    One account, self-published and unsourced, which covers the late Qing and the Republic of China together without telling them apart, puts cart inns mostly beside a main road, on high ground near water.
  • What did a cart inn's yard hold?
    The same account describes a thatched, mud-walled yard fenced with tall stakes, holding mud-brick lodging houses and always a large stable, with bundles of hay piled in the corners.
  • Which parts of the carters' inn are recorded before China's last dynasty, the Qing, fell in 1912?
    The one account of the cart inn's yard mixes the late Qing with the Republic that followed it, so what rests on that account alone may be from the twentieth century. The carters' inn is Ming by name, the yard full of animals is seen in 1863, and the cart inn is dated from the end of the Qing. The large stable as a building and the hay in the corners rest on the later account alone.
  • No page we read gives a Chinese inn's size.
    Neither the survey of China's old inns, the accounts of the cart inn, the Chinese Wikipedia article on inns nor forum answers give a frontage, depth or area for one.
