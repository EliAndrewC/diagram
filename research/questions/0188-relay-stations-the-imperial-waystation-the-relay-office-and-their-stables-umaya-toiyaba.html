Relay stations: the Imperial waystation, the relay office and their stables (umaya, toiyaba)

A relay station kept horses and men along a road, and passed travelers and their loads on from one station to the next. In this setting it is the Imperial waystation: a complex of a main hall, barracks and stables inside courtyard walls, with 15 to 30 staff and about 8 to 10 horses, one every ten miles or so.

History has three relay stations to set beside it. The relay station of Japan's ancient highways, the ekika, kept 5 to 20 horses, and the first one confirmed by excavation was a walled compound some 260 ft a side. On the Edo highways the relay was run from an office in a post town's street, the toiyaba, with men and horses drawn from the townsfolk. In China, the post station at Jimingyi was a walled town of its own.

  • The setting's waystation has 15 to 20 permanent staff, and a busy trunk-road station 25 to 30.
    The busier stations count a smith among their staff, for shoeing the horses and keeping the weapons.
  • A waystation keeps 8 to 10 horses on average.
    More where its courier traffic is heavier.
  • A waystation's buildings are a main hall, barracks, stables, gates and courtyard walls.
    So the setting's notes list them.
  • Waystations stand about ten miles apart.
    The spacing is irregular and varies with the route.

The setting's notes do not say how much ground a waystation covers, or where it stands against a town. Of the three histories, the ancient relay station kept horses in the same numbers as the waystation.

  • An ancient relay station kept 20, 10 or 5 horses, by the class of its road.
    The rule was 20 horses at a station on a great road, 10 on a middle road and 5 on a small road, more or fewer as the station's ground required.
  • On the Sanyodo there was in principle one relay station every 16 km (~52,493 ft), about ten miles.
    Each kept 20 horses on hand.
  • The first ancient relay station confirmed by excavation, at Koinumaru, was a walled compound about 80 m (~262 ft) a side.
    Its rammed-earth wall enclosed seven or more tile-roofed buildings on stone footings. A walled compound of several buildings is the same kind of complex the setting's notes list for the waystation.
  • The buildings of the excavated ancient relay stations had tiled roofs, white walls and red-lacquered pillars, rare for their time.
    So the Hyogo prefectural archaeology museum describes the remains at Koinumaru and at a second site, Ochiji.
  • Not every ancient relay station was a compound of its own.
    A good many were run by the district office, or kept in the station master's own house.

In the Edo period, the relay ran from the post town itself.

  • The toiyaba was a post station's office for relaying men and horses.
    A Japanese dictionary defines it so.
  • Where a relay office stood in the street differed by station.
    One in the middle of the street, one at each end, or three along it.
  • A relay office was run by the station's headman and a small staff.
    The headman's deputies assisted, a clerk logged every man and horse in and out each day, and dispatchers assigned the loads to the station's men and horses and to those of the sukego villages.
  • A relay office was sometimes a part of the headman's own house, and sometimes a building of its own.
    A station with two headmen had two offices.
  • A post station usually had one or two relay offices.
    At Okazaki the porters and the horses were relayed from separate offices.
  • A station's relay men and horses were requisitioned from its own residents.
    From 1601, when the shogunate first fixed the Tokaido's numbers, the men and horses were requisitioned from the station's residents.
  • The shogunate fixed how many men and horses a station kept: in the end, 100 of each on the Tokaido.
    In 1601 it made each Tokaido station keep 36 post horses. The rule became 100 men and 100 horses a station on the Tokaido, 50 and 50 on the Nakasendo and 25 and 25 on the other highways, the Tokaido's hundred from 1638.
  • How many draft animals did a caravan bring to a city's gate?
    Many dozens, oxen and packhorses, with their guards, porters and drivers - a GUESS: no page we read counts the animals of one train or names oxen among them. The nearest documented order of magnitude is what a Japanese post station had to keep standing for the road's traffic: each Tokaido station was first made to keep thirty-six horses, and from 1638 a hundred post horses and a hundred porters.
  • When a post station's horses ran short, the villages around it made up the rest.
    When a station's hundred horses were not enough, the neighboring villages, the sukego villages, supplied men and horses, from 1694 at two men and two horses for every hundred koku of assessed yield, and the ring of villages, at first two or three ri around the station, was widened past ten.
  • When a station's own men and horses fell short, its relay office could be crowded like a battlefield.
    The men and horses of the sukego villages were gathered at it, sometimes hundreds of horses and thousands of men.
  • Where did an Edo post station's relay horses stand?
    No page we read describes a stable at the relay office, nor a yard where a hundred horses stood waiting. Since the horses were requisitioned from the residents, this project reads them as kept in the householders' own stables rather than in one place - a GUESS.
  • A castle town kept the relay duty in a quarter of its own, near the castle's main gate.
    The tenma-cho bore the relay horses and the labor that went with them for the lord, and was placed, as a rule, close to the castle's main gate.
  • In time, few of the tenma-cho's houses handled the horses.
    The relay work narrowed to houses such as the ward headman's, where the relay office stood, and many of the quarter's landowners and householders paid their share in money.

Of China's post stations, we read about one, Jimingyi, and it is a much larger thing.

  • The Chinese post station at Jimingyi was a walled town about 2,330 m (~7,644 ft) around.
    Its wall held the station master's office, the station granary, the garrison commander's office, the official lodging court, the horse compound and other buildings. The lodging court is at Lodgings for lords and officials on the road (honjin).
  • At its height in the Qing, Jimingyi held a garrison of more than 300 and 82 post horses.
    That is ten times the staff of the setting's busiest waystations.
  • Jimingyi kept its post horses together, in one horse compound on its back street.
    The compound stood on the street joining the east and west gates along the north, beside its temples.

Of the stables themselves, the only one from before modern times that we found both dated and measured is a castle lord's, and no other like it survives. A warrior's own stable is at Stables (umaya).

  • Hikone castle's stable, built about 1688-1703 in the Edo period, is two single-story wings joined in an L.
    One wing is 25.2 m (~83 ft) long and the other 31.1 m (~102 ft), each 5.9 m (~19 ft) deep.
  • How many horses did Hikone's stable hold?
    Nineteen or 21: the two sources count differently. The national heritage record gives it fittings for nineteen horses. The city counts 21 places to stand and tie a horse, and a small room at the east end for managing them.
  • How wide was a stall at Hikone?
    About 8 to 9 ft. The two wings run about 50 m (~164 ft) once their shared corner is counted once; among 21 places that is about 2.4 m (~8 ft) a horse, among nineteen about 2.7 m (~9 ft), and less again once the managing room is taken out. The division is this project's, not the sources', and it assumes the stalls fill the wings in one row.

If an Edo post station's relay horses stood in their keepers' own stables, as we guess, they stood in stables like a farmhouse's.

  • A farmhouse kept its horse in a corner of the house, or in a stable standing apart.
    A modern Japanese encyclopedia divides farm stables into the inner stable, a corner of the house's earth-floored work room, and the outer stable, standing on its own. It names no period for the houses it describes.
  • A farm stable's entrance was closed with removable wooden bars.
    Its other sides were plank walls.
  • A farm stable usually had a ceiling, with a servant's room or a storeroom above.
    So the same encyclopedia says.
  • How big was a farm stable?
    Wide where winters are long, as in Tohoku, and narrow in the Kinki region around Kyoto and Osaka, about 3 m (~10 ft) square.
  • An outer stable usually had a privy and a shed built on with it.
    So the same encyclopedia says.
  • No page we read measures the stable of an inn or a relay station.
    The Chinese post station's horse compound, too, is named without a size. The wagon inn's long stable with its single trough is at Travelers' inns and cheap lodging houses (hatago, dian, kichin-yado).
  • No page we read describes the ground of a stable yard.
    Neither an inn's nor a relay station's yard is described. The yard where wagon trains stood and animals were watered is at Stable yards and watering troughs.
