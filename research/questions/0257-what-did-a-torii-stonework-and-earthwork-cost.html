What did a torii, stonework and earthwork cost?

Rokugan's prices are counted in koku and its smaller coins, and the setting's money rules judge a price by how many days of a craftsman's or a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give what the historical record holds for sweeping, moving earth, hauling stone and building a great stone torii in Edo-period Japan, and for hedges and stones in Qing China, each cost set beside the nearest day wages found.

No price was found for a plain wooden torii, for stone fence posts or stepping stones, for a mason's day, or for raising a burial mound. What survives is the earthwork and stone carrying of the rebuilding of Osaka Castle in the 1620s, priced beside the laborers' wages of the same ledgers, the load a pack horse carried, what a Kawachi village paid its sweepers and the river works of 1704 paid for earth, and one great stone torii, said to have cost 3,200 ryo.

Earthwork and its wages, Osaka 1620-1628

Much of the castle's work was let out to contractors who hired day laborers, and the account book of 1628 settled what each job had cost.

  • What did moving earth cost?
    7 to 18 monme of silver a tsubo of earth. The ledger's single jobs paid 7 to 10: 9 for one job of removing earth, 10, at a fixed rate, for lowering and leveling the ground of town lots at Fudanotsuji, and 7, at a rate set by the contractors' bids, for moat earth removed and left in front of Hamaya. Its biggest earth line, the digging of the southern outer moat, more than 44,000 tsubo, paid about 17.9, at a rate reached by something like an auction; but that line takes in the foundation trench under the moat's low water-ledge walls, charged by the length of wall, and the one share of it priced as moat earth alone, one daimyo's laborers' digging, is rated at 14.4 in the table, though its sum, 13,340.93 monme for 766.72 tsubo, comes to about 17.4. The paper does not say how big its tsubo of earth was.
  • What was a laborer paid in the same ledger?
    On two of its lines paid by the man, 0.8 to 1.2 monme a man: 1.2 for dredging a drain again, for 994 men, and 0.8 for setting well curbs, for 280; the table does not say how long a man's work was. Taking a man as a day's work, a tsubo of earth at 10 monme cost as much as 8 to 12 laborers' days.
  • And a craftsman?
    The ledger's one carpenters' line pays five carpenters 6.5 monme for pulling down a house, 1.3 monme each if each worked a day. No mason's wage was found.
  • How many men did it take to carry rubble?
    23 men a tsubo, each carrying 16 loads in a carrying net, for the 96 or so tsubo of rubble the Kuroda daimyo house's laborers carried to their stretch of wall in 1620; the paper gives no distance.
  • Was hauling stone paid more than other work?
    Yes. In 1620 the Kuroda house paid 1.3 monme a man for bringing stone out of the hills and 1.5 for hauling it on the sledge, while the Nakagawa house reckoned a day laborer in Osaka at 1 monme, and day laborers at the quarries were paid 0.8 to 1 monme a day that year. In 1621 the day wage was set at 1 monme in Osaka, and at 0.5 at Fushimi for pulling down stone walls.

Carrying stone

  • How much stone did a pack horse carry?
    Two blocks of Oya stone, each about 15 by 30 by 91 cm (half a shaku by 1 by 3 shaku), made one horse-load, the same load as two bales of rice; from before the Edo period until Meiji, stone went by horse. In the age of hand quarrying, a block of as much as 150 kg (~330 lb) was carried out of the quarry on one man's back.
  • What did carrying stone cost a ri?
    No rate was found for stone in Japan, by porter, by horse or by cart. The nearest are the post stations' fares by the stage for a porter's 5 kan (about 19 kg) and a pack horse's load on the highways, with the official Qing rates for porters and carts (What did hiring a horse or a porter cost?), and the Qing rates for stone by oxcart and cart (What did carrying stone cost, by cart and by porter?).

A torii

  • What did a stone torii cost?
    The one figure found is for a great one: the Hatogamine Hachiman shrine at Iida, in today's Nagano prefecture, says its great stone torii, begun in 1708 and finished in 1709 of stone cut on the bank of the Matsukawa, is said to have cost 3,200 ryo of gold. It gives no size. It is an upper bound, not a price for a small torii.
  • What was a day's pay then?
    No wage of Iida in 1708 was found. The nearest found are a Tama carpenter's or sawyer's 1 to 1.67 monme in 1749 and a Kyoto laborer's 0.91 monme in 1745-1754, forty years later and in other provinces. At those rates the torii's 3,200 ryo was about 115,000 carpenter-days or 210,000 laborer-days.
  • What did a wooden torii cost?
    No price was found.

Sweeping, leveling and the shrine's grounds

The one village account found for this work is Ota's, a village of Kawachi, from the re-routing of the Yamato River: what the part of the village held by the shogunate paid laborers while the channel was dug in 1704.

  • What did sweeping a path cost?
    It paid 60 monme of silver for 40 laborers to sweep the house and its grounds for the river magistrates' visit; 10.5 for 7 laborers, at 1.5 monme a man, to sweep the road for two lords passing on an inspection; and 30.6 for 17 road sweepers and house porters together. The 40 were paid 1.5 a man too, this project's arithmetic. Another line pays 60 monme for 30 laborers carrying sand and sweeping in the village, about 2 a man, the two tasks in one sum. The first three are sweeping for a visit; none of it is laying a path.
  • What was a laborer paid there?
    The works' two estimates set a laborer's wage at 1.5 monme of silver a man a day, and in the 5th month a village could not hire hands to reap its wheat at 200 mon a day with three meals, since the river works offered better terms.
  • And a craftsman?
    The study gives no craftsman's wage. The nearest found are the Osaka carpenters of 1628 above, 1.3 monme each if each worked a day, 76 years earlier and in the next province, and the Tama carpenters' 1 to 1.67 monme of 1749 above, 45 years later in Musashi.
  • What did leveling ground cost?
    The estimate of the works' costs that three magistrates sent the shogunate while the digging went on set the laborers' wage at 5.98 monme a tsubo of the works, and 5 for the Honda house's section; at the earlier estimates' 1.5 monme a day, a tsubo was about 4 laborers' days, this project's arithmetic. The Kishiwada domain's 23 cho of the channel, 84,689 tsubo dug and 35,798 embanked, came to 806,113.59 monme of wages, about 6.7 monme a tsubo. The study does not say how big its tsubo was. This is a river works; no figure was found for clearing around a great tree, leveling a small site or setting a rock seat, and the nearest are this and the Osaka earthwork above.
  • What did a sakaki hedge take?
    No Japanese figure was found. In China, a building manual of 1795 from Yangzhou reckons one strong laborer to every 4 zhang, about 12.8 m (~42 ft), of palisade, bamboo fence, willow railing round flower beds, or trench; it does not say for how long, and gives no wage. The nearest Qing wage found is Beijing's official list of 1736, 154 copper cash a day for a carpenter or mason and 75 for a laborer, 59 years earlier and in another province (What did a skilled craftsman earn against a laborer?).
  • What did setting a standing stone take?
    No figure was found for a shrine's stone. The nearest are the 1620 Osaka hauling wages above, 1.3 monme a man for bringing stone out of the hills, and the 150 kg block carried on one man's back. The same Qing manual reckons two strong laborers to every zhang for lifting a stone bridge's stones into place.
  • What did a white-gravel court, a gravel path or a ring of river stones cost?
    No price or labor was found. The Qing manual measures laid pebbles, like flagstones, by area, in zhang and chi, but gives no labor for them.
  • What did a himorogi, a moss court or a dipping place at a stream cost?
    No price or labor was found.
