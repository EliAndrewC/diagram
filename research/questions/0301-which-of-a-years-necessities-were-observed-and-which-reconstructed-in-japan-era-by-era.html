Which of a year's necessities were observed and which reconstructed, era by era, in Japan?

Rokugan's money rules weigh a price against a year's necessities in each of fifteen eras of Japan, from Osaka in the 1620s to Tokyo in the 1880s. Not every good in those eras rests on a price someone recorded in that city in those years. The findings below say, era by era and good by good, which prices were observed, which were taken from another city or another era, and which were worked out from the price of rice.

Which goods each era prices, and from which series, is this project's choice. The series are those of What did a year's necessities cost in Japan, era by era?: the account books of the Mitsui merchant house in Kyoto, Edo and Osaka; an economic historian's yearly file, whose goods mostly name no city; a study of prices in 1620-1650; and, for 1882-1886, the Tokyo retail prices Bassino and Ma took for their basket.

Era by era

  • How is the table read?
    "Obs." is a price observed in the era's city and years; a city's name alone is a price observed there in the era's years, not in the era's own city. "Chained" is a price carried from its 1745-1754 level by the rise and fall of a series with no city, or another city's. "Ratio" is a price worked out from rice by a ratio to rice of the years named. "Held" is a price the record holds that the era does not use. A dash is a good no price was found for. The source follows: M the Mitsui books, Ku the historian's file, Ki the 1620-1650 study, LTES the Tokyo retail prices.

What lies behind the labels

  • Kyoto 1745-1754's grains, other beans and fish are Tokyo 1882-1886's, set on Kyoto's rice.
    Bassino and Ma had no price of 1745-1754 for fish, the grains or cloth, and worked the grains, beans and fish out from their ratios to rice in early Meiji (after 1868). Their prices of 1882-1886 for the same goods are themselves Tokyo's retail rice multiplied by ratios: the grains' by farm-gate prices against unhusked rice in 1874-1901, the fish's by the wholesale price of salmon and cod against rice in 1882-1886. With rice at 1.49 and 1.5 g of silver a kilogram in the two eras, they print barley and wheat at 0.76 and 0.77, buckwheat and other grains at 0.61 and 0.62, fish at 2.18 and 2.19 a kilogram, and other beans at 0.99 and 1.01 a liter. Each stands at the same share of rice in both eras, about 0.51, 0.41 and 1.46 for the grains, buckwheat and fish, this project's arithmetic: one relation counted twice, not two observations. The historian's file prices fish the same way, 1.46 times its rice in every year it covers, and buckwheat at a fixed share of rice.
  • Whose soybeans?
    Bassino and Ma set Kyoto's soybeans of 1745-1754 equal to rice: the Osaka soybeans recorded from 1757 ran a little above it, but were of above-average quality. The historian's file records soybeans in 1725, 1757-1758, 1767, 1771-1772, 1781-1793, 1795-1796 and 1802-1871 and names no city for them. The Mitsui volume it cites holds tables of Osaka's market prices for 1725-1726, 1757-1773 and 1802-1873; that its soybeans of those years are Osaka's market quotations is this record's reading, from the years alone, and for 1781-1796 no table was found. A chained Kyoto or Edo soybean is that series' rise and fall carried from a 1745 level that is Kyoto's rice.
  • Whose cloth?
    The historian's file heads its cloth "linen", by the tan, and names no city; it is recorded in 1725-1726, 1757-1759, 1766-1768, 1771-1773, 1802-1851 and 1867, years that again fall mostly within the Osaka market tables, by this record's reading. Only Tokyo's cloth of 1882-1886 is observed in the era's own city.
  • Kyoto's salt and charcoal by the bale.
    From 1791 the Mitsui Kyoto books price salt and hard charcoal by the bale; what that makes a koku of salt is in What did a year's necessities cost in Japan, era by era? The transcription the record reads has no Kyoto charcoal column.
  • Where are the rents from?
    None is Kyoto's. The record holds rents by the tsubo on an Edo street in the Kansei era (1789-1801) and 1840, the Mitsui house's Osaka rents by the mat from 1814 and in 1846 and 1852, an Osaka going rate of 1832, and an Edo carpenter's rent of the Bunsei era: all in What did it cost to rent a room or a house?, with the popular page whose Edo tenement rents the 1745 and 1818 eras borrow.
  • Tokyo 1882-1886 has no salt, fuel or rent.
    Its rice, soybeans, other beans, edible oil, lamp oil (kerosene) and linen are Tokyo's retail prices. The nearest salt and fuel found are a yearly table's of 1887, which names no city: salt 1.19 yen a koku and charcoal 0.29 for 10 kan, beside soybeans at 4.07 a koku and cotton cloth at 0.31 a tan; it holds no firewood and no rent. At Bassino and Ma's 25 g of silver a yen, about 30 g a koku of salt and 7.3 g for 10 kan of charcoal, this project's arithmetic; a year late and of no named city, they are the nearest, not the era's own.
  • No fish price was recorded for any Edo-period era.
    Every fish price the record holds before 1882 is rice multiplied by a ratio of the Meiji period, and no recorded price of fish in a Japanese city before then was found.
  • Form and market, where they differ from 1745's.
    What each good of 1745-1754 was, and at which market its price was taken, is in What did a Kyoto laborer's year of goods cost? The Mitsui books' Edo and Osaka prices carry the same "retail" heading as Kyoto's, as account-book entries; the historian's file calls all its prices retail; Tokyo's of 1882-1886 are retail, but for the grains, priced from farm-gate ratios, and the fish, from a wholesale one. No series read says the grade of its salt, the kind of its firewood or the weave of its cloth.
