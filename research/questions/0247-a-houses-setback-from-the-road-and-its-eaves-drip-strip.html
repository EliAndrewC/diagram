A house's setback from the road and its eaves-drip strip (dishui)

Rain ran off a house's roof at its eaves and fell in a line along the foot of its walls. In north China the strip of ground it fell on, the dishui or "drip", belonged to the house, and custom kept it a few feet wide at most. In Japan a village house was set back from the edge of its plot so that its rain fell on its own land, in one district about 3 ft back from a road, while a town house might stand right up to the street.

  • In north China a house kept a strip of its own ground at the foot of its wall for the drip from its eaves.
    A 2021 study of Qing-period deeds and customs in north China finds that the drip strip was left when a house was built and belonged, with the courtyard, to the house's owner.
  • How wide was the drip strip?
    Two or three chi in one county, less than one in another. In Jiaxiang county, in Shandong, whoever built a house had to leave 2 or 3 chi of drip ground; in Pingyao county, in Shanxi, unless a deed gave a measure, the drip ground was 6 or 8 cun from the foot of the wall. Both figures come from surveys of local custom made before 1949, whose reliability the study itself calls disputed.
  • A deed could bound a plot by the drip itself.
    A deed of 1868 giving a building site in a Shandong village to build a temple bounds it on the east by the drip of a courtyard wall and on the north by the drip, and a Shanghai house deed of 1883 bounds its west side by the drip.
  • Where a deed met a street, the street itself was the edge.
    A house deed of 1809 from the town of Baoding runs on the north to the official street, and the Shanghai deed of 1883 runs on the east to the main street; neither names a strip between the plot and the street. Both are town deeds; none of the village deeds the study quotes meets a road.

Japan's records of local custom say the same of the village house, and give one figure.

  • In one Japanese district a village house stood about 3 ft back from a road.
    In Ono district, in Mino province, a house in a town needed an official's permit, but a house in a village had to stand back about 3 ft from the boundary of a road or of cultivated land, a rule called "shade-withdrawal" (kage-hiki), in an edition of local customs published in 1892.
  • Elsewhere a village house was set back so that its rain fell on its own land.
    In Sano district, in Totomi province, houses in towns could stand close together, but in villages a house was built far enough from the boundary to shed its rain on its own land; districts of Mino province held that a house must not shed its rain on the adjoining premises.
  • In a Japanese town a house could stand right up to the highway.
    In Yamato district, in Chikugo province, houses in towns could stand close up to the highway line.
  • No page we read says how far a farmhouse's eaves reached out over its drip strip.
    Neither for China nor for Japan.
