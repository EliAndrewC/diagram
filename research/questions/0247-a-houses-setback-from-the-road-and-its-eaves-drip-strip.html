A house's setback from the road and its eaves-drip strip (dishui)

In north China the strip of ground at the foot of a house's wall where the rain from its eaves fell, the dishui or "drip", belonged to the house, and custom kept it a few feet wide at most. In parts of Japan a village house was set back from the edge of its plot so that its rain fell on its own land, and in one district it stood about 3 ft back from a road, while in one district's towns a house might stand right up to the highway.

  • In north China a house kept a strip of its own ground at the foot of its wall for the drip from its eaves.
    A 2021 study of Qing-period deeds and customs in north China finds that the drip strip was left when a house was built and belonged, with the courtyard, to the house's owner.
  • How wide was the drip strip?
    Two or three chi in one county, less than one in another. In Jiaxiang county, in Shandong, whoever built a house had to leave 2 or 3 chi of drip ground, a custom the survey says was not that county's alone; in Pingyao county, in Shanxi, unless a deed gave a measure, the drip ground was 6 or 8 cun from the foot of the wall. Both figures come from surveys of local custom made before 1949, whose reliability the study itself calls disputed. A Baoding house deed of 1884 gives the same order of width: a drip of 3 chi behind a house, onto which the rain was not to spill over into the front courtyard.
  • How far did a village house's eaves reach out?
    About 2 chi, on the one account found. A Shanxi writer, recalling the old deeds of his home village of Hongyadi in the Taihang mountains, says a tiled house shed its water from both slopes, and that the back eaves had a building measure: an overhang of 2.2 chi, and not a cun more, or a boundary dispute with the neighbor followed.
  • A deed could bound a plot by the drip itself.
    A deed of 1868 giving a building site in a Shandong village to a temple bounds it on the east by the drip of a courtyard wall and on the north by the drip, and a Shanghai house deed of 1883 bounds its west side by the drip.
  • In the deeds we read that met a road, the road itself was the edge.
    A house deed of 1809 from Baoding, sold through the city's brokerage, runs on the north to the official street; the Shanghai deed of 1883, for a shop with an upper story, runs on the east to the main street; a shop deed of 1825 from Taichung, in Taiwan, runs on the east to the cart road; and a house deed of 1692 from Huizhou runs on the east to a cross path out to the main road. None of them names a strip between the plot and the road.

Japan's records of local custom, district by district, speak of the village house's setback.

  • In one Japanese district a village house stood about 3 ft back from a road.
    In Ono district, in Mino province, a house in a town needed a petition to the feudal official, but a house in a village had to stand back about 3 ft from the boundary of a road or of cultivated land, a rule called "shade-withdrawal" (kage-hiki), in an edition of local customs published in 1892.
  • Elsewhere a village house was set back so that its rain fell on its own land.
    In Sano district, in Totomi province, houses in towns could stand close together, but in villages a house was built far enough from the boundary to shed its rain on its own land; districts of Mino province held that a house must not shed its rain on the adjoining premises.
  • In a Japanese town a house could stand right up to the highway.
    In Yamato district, in Chikugo province, houses in towns could stand close up to the highway line.
  • No page we read says how far a Japanese farmhouse's eaves reached out over its drip strip.
