How our maps draw the shelter belt: its sides, its trees, and why it runs off the edge of the map

On a map whose farmhouses stand together, a shelter belt stands on the windward side of the houses to shelter them from the cold winter wind - on the north and west, unless the map declares a wind of its own - on one side or two, never all the way round. Its planting is one of two forms, rolled for each map: rows of conifers with lesser broadleaf trees among them, or a mixed broadleaf wood. Along the side it holds, the planting is continuous; where it meets the edge of the map, it runs on past it.

Where a belt stands follows the wind and the houses it shelters.

  • The belt stands on one or two windward sides and is never carried round the settlement.
    That is the form the record finds: the Sendai groves on the north or the west, and the separate patches of grove around southern Chinese villages. The belt's open sides are not gaps and need no explanation.
  • The full ring is not rolled for a village's belt.
    The Izumo plain's grove ringed the whole house before 1868 and later shrank to a hook over the north and west, but that ring is one region's farmhouse form. So the maps give it only to a farmstead's own grove, whose sides are rolled for each settlement (How our maps draw the groves around farmhouses).
  • Belts on the north and west tell a reader which way the winter wind blows.
    The winter wind over Japan blows from the northwest, so a reader who sees belts on the north and west of most settlements is told a real fact about the region's wind; a ring rolled at random would scramble it.
  • Every map takes the northwest wind unless it declares a local one.
    Cold air drains downhill, so a place with a cold wind of its own - off its own high ground, say - records it, and every other place takes the northwest. No map drawn so far records one, so every belt stands on the north and west of its houses, or on one of those two sides, and the settlement is laid out with its back to it.
  • The hamlet maps that draw a village belt each give it one side or two.
    Measured as the arc each belt's clumps span round its own houses: Inashiro 79 degrees, Kuwabata 65 and Sawada 38; Kashikawa and Mizuguchi, whose farms keep their own groves, draw no village belt. None wraps its settlement. They draw as hooks or as straight strips.
  • Each cluster of farmhouses carries its own band along its windward edge.
    The band hugs the cluster, as a farmhouse grove stands on the sides of the house the wind and snow come from, and a town with more than one cluster draws a band for each, merging into the corner woods where they meet.
  • Woods at a settlement's corners are not its belt.
    Masses of woods at the corners alone are decoration, not a wall against the wind.
  • A band reaches out from the farmhouse rows into open ground.
    Its clumps of trees are refused wherever they would fall on a house, a yard or a garden, so a band drawn only over the rows thins to a scatter.
  • At least one substantial stand of the belt lies near a farmhouse: within 150 ft on a hamlet's or a town's map, 300 ft on a village's.
    A stand here is a dozen clumps of trees or more. On the maps this rule was calibrated against, the belt stands between about 37 and 262 ft from the nearest farmhouse, depending on the kind of settlement. The reach and the stand's size are a calibration against those maps.
  • A town nestled against a forest needs no belt of its own.
    The real wood beside it gives the same shelter by other means.
  • The share of the windward side under trees cannot tell a good belt from a bad one.
    Measured on our maps, it ranks them backwards: a narrow belt hugging its cluster of houses covers 4 to 18 per cent of the windward arc, while layouts with woods only at the corners, which should fail, cover 29 to 45. So the rule asks instead for a stand near a farmhouse.
  • A belt that runs nearly north-south can still face the northwest wind as squarely as the maps it was checked against.
    The textbook belt lies across the wind, so a nearly north-south belt on a northwest wind looks as though it faces the wrong way. Measured, it does not. Its face to the wind, the clumps' extent across the wind over their extent along it, is 1.46, against 0.90 to 1.33 for the maps this rule was calibrated against; the principal axis of its clumps is 31 degrees off the across-wind line, the second-best in the set, where every one of those maps runs 33 to 49 degrees off.
  • The wind decides which side a belt lies on, not the bearing it runs at.
    The across-the-wind rule is modern, and the old belts are described by their side, so the maps draw the band that hugs a cluster and set no bearing rule: a belt whose only fault is its compass angle is not at fault.
  • How large is a map's belt?
    Its size follows from where it stands, a band behind the cluster it shelters, so the three hamlet maps that draw one draw theirs at 0.55 to 1.64 ha (~1.4-4.1 acres), inside the range of village woods measured at Groves around a southern Chinese village, on both sides of its median. The one village belt whose width the record found, Tarama's, was 10 to 15 m (~33-49 ft) wide in 2019.

What a belt is planted with follows the two forms the record found. How large each tree's crown is drawn is at How our maps stock woods and size crowns.

  • The belt is drawn in one of two forms, rolled for each map.
    Either conifer-led - darker, taller crowns set in rows along the windward side, with lesser broadleaf crowns among them, the form of the Japanese farmhouse grove, led by cedar in three of the four regions surveyed and planted in rows at two of the four regions surveyed (the research section) - or a mixed broadleaf wood, an irregular wood of rounded crowns like the woods around it, the form of the Chinese village grove. Neither is a line of one kind of tree.
  • The two forms are equally likely.
    That is a GUESS: no page says which was commoner.
  • Is the conifer-led form right for a whole village?
    Drawing it at a village's scale is an interpolation: every survey of it is of a single farmstead's grove.
  • The conifer is the commonest crown in a conifer-led belt.
    That is a GUESS for a village: one Tonami hamlet's farmhouse groves were nearly half cedar, but no page we read gives a village belt's share.
  • The conifer rows are spaced so a reader can make them out.
    That spacing is a GUESS, chosen so the rows can be read.
  • The Izumo plain's clipped pine hedge, grown as a wall round a single farmhouse, is not drawn for a village.
    It is the nearest thing to a planting of a single kind of tree the record finds, and it belonged to one house, not to a village.

Within the side it holds, a belt is kept whole, and a lane must still get through it.

  • Along the side it holds, the belt's planting is continuous.
    A gap funnels the wind, and no old record shows a bare stretch inside a planted run, so a bare run wider than about 30 ft, measured across the wind, counts as a hole and is filled. The 30 ft is a map drawing convention: no source gives a width for an opening in a belt.
  • Where a lane crosses the belt, the planting resumes on both sides of it.
    That crossing is a GUESS: no old record says how a lane passed through a belt, and the old entrances the record found stood on a grove's open side or came in along a stream (the research section).
  • The maps do not draw a modern windbreak manual's crossing: a lane cut through the belt at an angle, set back from its end.
    That is a modern extension service's design rule, and nothing earlier.

A belt near the edge of the map runs off it, and the maps are judged only on what a reader can see.

  • A belt that runs off the edge of the map is sound, and the bare ground beyond is not a hole.
    A reader already understands that a feature meeting the frame continues past it, as every road, field and watercourse on these maps does. So a large area feature near the frame - a shelter belt, a grazing ground, a wood, a forest - crosses the frame rather than sitting wholly inside it, so the reader sees that it goes on. Nothing beyond the frame is drawn, and nothing beyond it is counted against the map.
  • A belt is checked for holes only where a reader can look.
    The belt's recorded outline runs off the page as freely as its clumps do. On one map the outline spans 747 ft along its own across-wind axis while the frame holds only the middle 537 ft, and the planting covers 596 ft, overrunning the visible stretch at both ends; on another, the outline is 917 ft, the frame 741 ft and the planting 787 ft. On both, the planting reaches both ends of the stretch of belt a reader can see. So holes are looked for only inside the page, the same rule the planting follows: a clump whose crown crosses the page edge is kept, and one with nothing on the page is dropped.
  • The planting is not extended to the ends of the outline.
    Planting the end stretches like any gap inside the belt would add one clump on one map, at the page edge.
  • A clump just off the page still counts toward the stretch of belt beside it.
    The page is cropped; the planting is not. If a clump counted only where its crown shows on the page, one hamlet would show a 60 ft bare stretch on its bottom edge. That ground is a homestead: a farmhouse, two threshing yards, two kitchen gardens and a persimmon stand lie within 90 ft of it, and the maps keep the belt off a yard's and a garden's sun (How our maps keep yards and gardens in the sun). The trees in that gap are the dooryard copse, which is what a copse among the houses is for.
  • Nothing keeps a copse in that gap at the page's edge.
    The bare stretch at the page edge is filled by the copse that happens to stand there, and no rule holds the copse in place. For one stretch at one page edge, the maps accept that risk.
  • A break in the planting that a reader can see is still a hole.
    A wellhead seated inside a belt, or a lane crossing it, removes the clumps around it, and that is where the rule against holes earns its keep, so it holds there as written.
