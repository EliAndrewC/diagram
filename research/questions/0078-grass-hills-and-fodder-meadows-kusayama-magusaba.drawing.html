How our maps draw the open hills past the grove

Past a village's fields, and behind its back grove, our maps draw the village's commons, its shared grass ground: open hill ground of coarse grass, low brush and a few scraggly pines, running off the edge of the map. A village is said to have needed grass ground more than ten times the area of its fields, too large for one map, so what the map shows is its near edge, where the grass ground begins as the fields end.

  • A village's meadow is not a plot among its fields.
    At more than ten times the fields it cannot fit on a hamlet or village map, so the map draws only its nearest part: open grass, or grass with thin low pine, on the hill ground past the fields, meeting the edge of the map. How far past the edge of the map the ground runs is a GUESS.
  • Which form a village's grass ground takes depends on the land the map shows.
    Three forms are attested, so the form is a knob: the grass hill where the map has hills, the floodplain where it has a river flat, and now and then, added at random, a small grass plot beside one paddy.
  • Why is the hill past the grove drawn as open scrub, not more forest?
    The hill past a village's protected wood ran from grass and brush through red pine to bare ground, and the map draws the middle of that range - open scrub with a few scraggly pines, neither forest nor bare earth. Where on that range a given map sits is calibrated liberty, not a finding. More forest out there would undo the point of the protected grove, which was green because it was spared where the ground beyond it was not. Drawn open and sparse, the scrub also reads at a glance as different ground from the dense, dark, closed canopy of the grove.
  • Why is the commons drawn beyond the grove, not between the grove and the houses?
    A fengshui grove stood immediately behind and upslope from its village, so our maps order the ground away from the houses as village, then back grove, then fuel-and-fodder commons, then whatever lies past the map: the commons stands on the high side, further out than the grove. That order is this project's reading of where the grove stood.
  • Do rough grazing, pasture and woodland patches keep to the village, grove, commons order?
    The hill country around a village was a mosaic of woods, fields and grassland, so these stand on any dry marginal ground, though never on the flooded rice fields. That the order binds the fuel commons alone is a GUESS.
  • The commons is one small block of grass and brush, repeated, with no outline.
    The grass and the brush are one small block of tufts and dots repeated across the ground the commons covers, the way a map's legend shows a kind of ground, and the pines are drawn one by one, because a tree is a thing a reader can count. The repeated block stops at the commons' edge and leaves bare whatever its ground does not include: the lanes and their verges, the fields' margins, the houses' swept ground. A filled four-sided patch would read as a literal rhombus lying on the hillside, so there is no solid fill and no outline. The repeated block, its edge and the absent outline are map drawing conventions.
  • The scrub band runs under the grove's edge.
    It is a ragged strip along the grove's back, of a width that varies at random, drawn under the grove so the canopy laps its inner edge and wood and scrub meet with no bare seam between them - a map drawing convention.
  • The scrub runs as one band around the settlement.
    The dry margins are filled as one continuous band around the settlement rather than as separate corner patches. That in a valley every edge of the map is marginal land of the same village, grove, commons sequence is a GUESS.

The rule the map follows (the grass ground): on a map with hill ground beyond its fields, the first ground past the fields on the hill side is drawn as open grass or grass with scattered low pine, and it meets the edge of the map; on a map with a river flat and no hills, the grass ground is the floodplain beside the river. A small grass plot tied to one paddy, where a map has one, is one grass patch of about the paddy's own size on the slope above it. The plot's size and how often it is rolled are a GUESS.

The rule the map follows (the commons): the fuel-and-fodder commons is drawn beyond the back grove on the high side - village, grove, commons, off-map - and never between the grove and the houses. Rough grazing, pasture and woodland patches are not held to that order and clothe any dry marginal ground. No commons of any kind is drawn on the flooded rice fields, and the dry margins are filled as one continuous band around the settlement.
