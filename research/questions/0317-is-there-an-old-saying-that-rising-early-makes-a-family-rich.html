Is there an old saying that rising early makes a family rich?

Rokugan's money rules will quote, as a Rokugani saying, that no one who rises before dawn 360 days a year fails to make the family rich. The findings below give whether China or Japan had such a saying, and where its English wording comes from.

The saying in its full form, that no one who can rise before dawn 360 days a year fails to make his family rich, was found in no Chinese or Japanese source; its English wording is traced to Malcolm Gladwell's book Outliers, which gives it as a Chinese proverb. Its two halves are old. That rising early adds a day's work every three mornings was in a poem by 1213, and a Chinese farming almanac begun in 1314 puts it beside the sayings that diligence is a lifetime's plan and that wealth comes only by bitter effort.

  • Where does the English wording come from?
    A 2009 blog post on Outliers reports that Gladwell, arguing that the hard work of keeping rice paddies has tended to make hard work more central to Chinese culture than to many Western cultures, points to this Chinese proverb: "No one who can rise before dawn 360 days a year fails to make his family rich." No Chinese original for it was found.
  • Rising early three mornings makes one day's work.
    Lou Yue (1137-1213), a Song writer, begins a joking poem on his midday nap with the line: "Rising early three mornings makes one day's work; / but old and greedy for sleep, it is not the same for me. / Once, by chance, I got up at the fifth watch [the last night watch before dawn], / and then slept again until noon."
  • A farming almanac puts early rising, diligence and wealth together.
    Lu Mingshan began his month-by-month farming almanac, the Nongsang yishi cuoyao, in 1314, and the imperial catalog's editors take the date of 1330 signed at its end to mark a second printing. It closes with "plain words in common speech" for those who work at farming: a household's plan lies in harmony; a lifetime's plan lies in diligence, and "if you want wealth and honor in life, you must put in bitter effort," as people always say; a year's plan lies in spring; a day's plan lies in the hour before dawn, and rising early three mornings makes one day's work; in keeping a household, diligence and care come first.
  • Was it a farmers' saying?
    In part. Its nearest premodern form is in that almanac, addressed to those who farm, but its line on rising early was a learned poet's joke a century before, and a seventeenth-century family's maxims told the household to rise at first light too.
  • A household rule: rise at first light, but seek no riches.
    The family maxims of Zhu Yongchun, born in 1617 at Kunshan, begin: rise at first light, sprinkle and sweep the courtyard, keep inside and out clean; at dusk rest, and bar the gates and check them yourself. Far from promising wealth, they go on to tell the household to keep itself frugally, and not to seek fine houses or good fields.
  • Japan: a ruler's order to farmers to rise early.
    The Keian edict tells farmers to rise early and cut grass in the morning, work the fields by day, twist rope and weave rice bales in the evening, and do whatever work the time calls for without slackening, in the transcription the Gunma Prefectural Archives publishes. That article promises no wealth. The document bears the date 1649, but scholars now doubt the shogunate issued it: it became widely known from a domain's woodblock print of 1830, and one historian, Yamamoto Eiji, traces it to a rule for farmers of about 1697; another of its articles tells the wife to work hard at the loom, and husband and wife to work into the night. It is an order from above, not a saying. A farm household's year of work is in What work did a farm household do that was never paid?
