How our maps draw pigs and ducks at a dike-pond: sties on the pond dikes

On a dike-pond hamlet our maps draw pig sties, each a small shed with a railed pen, standing on the bank of a fish pond on the side nearest the houses. The pig was the dike-pond district's animal, and a livestock pen on a fish-pond bank, its dung swept into the water by hand, is in a farming compendium of 1639, so the sty stands at the water. Nothing is drawn to carry its waste in: the only shed built to drain into a pond that we found is in modern manuals.

  • A quarter to a half of a hamlet's households keep a sty.
    The share is chosen at random for each hamlet, from its seed. The band is a GUESS: no page we read gives the share of households that kept a pig.
  • Each sty takes a fish pond of its own, the ponds nearest the houses first.
    A pond holds at most one sty, and the nursery ponds of a hamlet that raises fry take none (How our maps draw fish fry and nursery ponds (yumiao)). Both are this project's choices, a GUESS at how a household's sty and its pond went together.
  • A sty stands on the half of its pond's bank that faces the houses.
    It takes the place nearest the houses that has room, and never one farther from them than the middle of the pond, so that it reads as a household's and not as standing on the far side of the water. That bound is this project's choice.
  • No sty stands more than 320 ft from a farmhouse.
    A household with no pond within that reach keeps none. The 320 ft is a GUESS at how far a household would walk to its pigs.
  • The bank a sty stands on is drawn 23 ft wide, a width not taken from a modern manual.
    A modern manual makes a dike carrying a pigsty 5 to 10 m (~16-33 ft) wide; the bank as drawn falls inside that band, but the band is not a rule our maps follow, being modern, and no width for a dike from before modern times (before the Qing dynasty fell in 1912) was found to follow instead. How the banks are drawn is at How our maps draw dike-ponds (sangji yutang).
  • A sty stands on the bank, not out by the canal at the edge of the pond's plot.
    Each pond's plot is edged by a canal, and its bank runs between that canal and the water. A sty's place is set in from the edge of the plot by half the bank's width, so it stands on the bank itself.
  • Each sty is drawn with a privy built over it.
    A pig toilet is an outhouse mounted over a pigsty, once common in rural China, where one character means both "pigsty" and "privy", and Chinese grave models of the Han dynasty show the latrine customarily built above the pigsty. So the shed of each sty carries a small privy over its closed end, marked with the dark circle every privy on our maps carries. The custom was common, not universal, and no count of the sties that carried a privy was found, so drawing one on every sty is a GUESS. The farmsteads keep their own privies as well, where How our maps place farm privies seats them: a sty on a pond bank is not drawn as any one farmstead's, so no house can be shown giving up its privy for the one over the sty, a GUESS.
  • No sluice is drawn in a pond's own bank, so none decides where a sty stands.
    A pond's own sluice would be "a protected opening in the pond dike" closed with boards; the sluices our maps draw are the polder's own, in its outer dike, and why is at How our maps draw dike-ponds (sangji yutang). No page we read says a sty was kept away from a sluice, or set beside one, to keep the water clean.
  • No foul patch of water is drawn beside a sty.
    A modern manual warns of stale water beside the livestock and cures it by spreading the manure about the pond. A round of manure-spreading is not something a map shows, and a tinted patch would draw a condition we found described only in that modern manual.
  • No duck pen is drawn.
    The delta's ducks were herded in the rice fields, and the fenced dry run on a pond dike is a modern manual's form, so this project, which draws no form found only in modern sources, draws none.
  • A sty at a fish pond is drawn on a dike-pond hamlet only.
    A pig penned at a fish pond is a Chinese form: Japan, outside Okinawa, seems to have kept its pigs loose in the forest, and no page we read links a Japanese pig to a pond. An ordinary rice village's pigs are at How our maps draw pigs and ducks in south-China rice villages
