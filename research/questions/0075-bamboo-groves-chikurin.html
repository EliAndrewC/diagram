Bamboo groves (chikurin)

Bamboo grew across Japan's lowland countryside wherever the winters were not too hard, and farming households used it for baskets, fans, tools, building and food. It does not grow as single stems scattered through a wood: a stand of bamboo is a thicket of its own, spreading by shoots from an underground rhizome, its stems all clones of one plant, in most cases nearly one species, and shading out almost everything else. Madake, the common timber bamboo, can grow a culm about 20 m (~66 ft) tall and at most about 10 cm (~4 in) across.

A farming settlement kept its bamboo in two places. On the farmstead it grew in the household's own grove of trees, low under the tall trees, where it stopped the wind beneath their branches, in a grove kept on the side the wind came from, and on some farms beside the storehouses or, at houses by rivers and on flood-prone ground, where its roots held the soil. Apart from the houses, bamboo stood in thickets of its own, the take-yabu; the villages of one district near Kyoto managed their own bamboo groves, and before 1868, in the Edo period, settlements near Kyoto were painted ringed by bamboo. The farmstead's grove as a whole is at Groves of trees around farmhouses (yashikirin).

  • Moso and madake stop where the winters are hard.
    A survey of moso and madake across northern Japan found that their range there depends mainly on temperature, and found no stands at sites whose mean yearly minimum fell below -16.8°C (~2°F), in line with earlier work that moso cannot stand about -18 to -20°C (~0 to -4°F).
  • How far north could moso and madake grow?
    Not much past central Honshu and the northern coasts, on the one projection we read. The same survey, projecting a climate warmer than today's, still keeps them south of 41 degrees north, on the plains and hills of central Honshu and the coasts of northern Honshu. That is a projection for a warmer climate, not a survey of where bamboo grew.
  • How cold could madake take?
    Growers disagree. One gives a minimum of -15°C (~5°F), another -20 to -23°C (~-4 to -9°F), and the second says colder weather will likely damage its leaves and canes. No page we read says the new shoots take frost, so the most that can be said is that bamboo is frost-tender at the margin of its range.
  • How common was bamboo at a lowland farming hamlet?
    Common, by this project's reading: one account gives every homestead on the Tonami plain a bamboo stand (below), and no page we read counts it anywhere else. The countryside of farming villages, the satoyama, was a mosaic of woods, paddies, dry fields, grassland, streams and ponds, and as late as the 1960s it was used for, among much else, giant bamboo forests. So a lowland paddy hamlet in a temperate province could have bamboo, and a cold upland one may have none: the frost limit is sourced, and how common bamboo was below it is an inference.
  • Bamboo had many uses on a farm.
    Madake's long fibers made it the cane for baskets and fans, its sheaths wrapped food and, in one variety, covered some geta, and it made shakuhachi flutes. On the Tonami plain the shoots were eaten and the culms went into building, river-bank works and barrel hoops, bamboo was an important material for everyday life, and with cedar and zelkova it was used both for building new houses and for everyday tools.

On the farmstead itself, the clearest accounts come from the Tonami plain, where more than 7,000 farmhouses each stand inside a grove of their own, the kainyo, and from the igune groves of the Sendai plain.

  • On the Tonami plain, bamboo was one of the plants of a farmstead's grove.
    The Tonami plain's research institute describes the kainyo as built round cedar with many other trees, and says there used to be many bamboo stands in it - madake, moso, hachiku and yadake - though few are left today. A comparison of the regions' groves lists bamboo among the Tonami grove's lesser plants, after cedar.
  • On the Tonami plain, bamboo grew in the grove from the west round to the north of the house.
    The same institute found cedar, hackberry and alder often mixed with bamboo from the west round to the north, the tall cedar, oak and zelkova from the south round to the west, and little grove on the east, where the house fronts. It says the grove held off the strong south wind of early spring and the cold seasonal wind of winter.
  • Did a Tonami farm's bamboo also stand on the south side, with the storehouses?
    One account says so. Besides the institute's west-to-north side, the Japanese Wikipedia article on homestead groves puts Tonami's storehouses, barns, fruit trees and bamboo on the south side of the house.
  • Was the Tonami bamboo's west-to-north side the one the wind came from?
    In part, by this project's reading. The institute does not say which of its two winds that side met. The Japanese Wikipedia article on homestead groves puts the Tonami grove's tall windbreak trees on the west and south, against wind and snow, so there the bamboo's side and the windbreak's share only the west. Elsewhere many igune stand on the north and west of the house, against the wind and the snow, and at Isawa in Iwate the grove shelters the house from the northwest wind of winter, so the Tonami bamboo side is read as windward from those groves, not stated.
  • On the Sendai plain, bamboo filled the low part of a farmstead's grove, against the wind.
    Sendai City's plan for replanting the igune describes the traditional grove as a body of tall evergreens such as cedar and black pine, with bamboo filling the bare lower part of the trees, where there are no branches, to stop the wind there, and a few fruit trees. The Tonami institute says moso and other bamboos are very good against strong wind.
  • By rivers and in flood-prone ground, bamboo was planted for its roots.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on homestead groves says that at houses near rivers and where floods are frequent, alder and bamboo, which spreads its roots, are planted to keep the soil from washing away.
  • On the Izumo plain, pine and bamboo are said to have bound the bank a house was raised on.
    The clipped pine grove of the plain, the tsuijimatsu, is said to have begun when households raised their plots against floods behind an earth bank and planted pine and bamboo, which stand up to water, to firm it.
  • Behind the house is recorded too.
    A newspaper history of the Fuyu persimmon names one persimmon, in the middle of the Meiji period, after 1868, standing in the bamboo grove behind a house in Gifu, and the Chinese farm book Bu nongshu puts bamboo and trees behind the house where the site is broad. The book is at Kitchen gardens beside farmhouses (yashikibatake).
  • On the Musashino upland, one farm grew its bamboo along its south side, where the wind was already broken.
    A survey of the upland's homestead groves found a farm at Ogawa with a madake grove all along its south side: across two roads to the north stood a row of houses with groves, so, its owners said, no bamboo was needed on that side as a windbreak, and basket makers used to come to buy the bamboo. The village is at Row villages (resson).
  • Which side of the house did a farm's bamboo take?
    More than one. The sources give at least five seats: with the storehouses, at the wet edge, on the wind side in the grove, behind the house, and on the south side where a neighbor's grove already broke the wind. No page we read gives a share for each.
  • Did every farmstead keep bamboo?
    On the Tonami plain, yes, by one account: the plain's research institute, describing the plain's homestead groves in 1996, says a homestead always had a bamboo stand in its grounds, kept for use, of madake, moso, hachiku, yadake and medake. Another of the institute's pages says those stands, once many, are hardly seen today. No page we read gives a share of farmsteads keeping bamboo anywhere else.
  • How much of a farm's grove was bamboo?
    No page we read says. None gives a share of a grove's ground under bamboo, or a count that tells a household's bamboo from a village thicket; the comparison of the regions' groves names bamboo as one of several lesser plants, after cedar.

What is recorded of a village's own thicket before modern times comes mostly from one bamboo-rich region beside a great city: the countryside round Kyoto.

  • A bamboo thicket kept as a resource was cut in moderation and renewed from its own shoots.
    A bamboo grove spreads its rhizome and multiplies by shoots that come straight up from it, and where people manage one as a resource they cut it moderately. Cutting and regrowth of this kind make it much like a coppice, a likeness this project draws, not one a page we read makes.
  • Today many thickets stand where the plain meets the worked hills.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on bamboo groves, which gives takeyabu as another name for them, says many are still seen on the outskirts, in the belt joining the flat land to the satoyama.
  • Before 1868, settlements near Kyoto were ringed by bamboo.
    An early-Edo screen of the countryside round Kyoto paints settlements surrounded by bamboo groves, which the historians of a city there take to be madake or hachiku.
  • In the Edo period, villages southwest of Kyoto paid a tax in bamboo and managed their own groves.
    The villages of the Otokuni district, southwest of Kyoto, owed bamboo as a tax from the start of the Edo period and managed their bamboo groves, some farmers keeping bamboo shops on the side.
  • In the Edo period, bamboo groves were not rare beside the shrines and temples on the hills round Kyoto.
    A reading of the period's pictures of the countryside finds that around 1658-1661, beside the shrines and temples on those hills, bamboo groves were not rare.
  • A sixth-century Chinese farm manual wants bamboo on high, dry ground.
    The Qimin Yaoshu, an agricultural text finished between 533 and 544, wants bamboo on high, level ground, near hills best, and says it dies in a low field once water reaches it.
  • No record we read says who held a village's thicket, or puts one at the edge of the rice paddies.
    None says either, of any period.
