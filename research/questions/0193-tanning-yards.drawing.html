How our maps site tanning yards

A town or city on our maps draws a tanning yard only where it has both an outcaste quarter, whose caste held the leather trade, and running water: the Japanese method soaks its hides in a river, so a place without water draws none, whatever its size. The yard stands on the water outside the walls, below every intake the settlement draws from, on the side of town where its quarter is, and clear of every house but the tanners' own.

  • A town or city draws a tanning yard only where it has both an outcaste quarter and running water.
    Tanning is a water trade, and the leather workers were named for the riverbed they lived and worked on. The yard is drawn in towns as well as cities because its hides come from a whole district's fallen cattle and horses, not from a city's butchers.
  • How many pits does a yard run?
    About 4 in a town and 12 in a provincial city. A county of about 6,800 inhabitants sheds, by this project's GUESS, a couple of dozen carcasses a year, so a town's yard is a small seasonal works. A city adds its lord's stable - most domains in this setting keep several hundred horses - and the armor, saddles, drums and bellows of about 300 samurai, also a GUESS.
  • A city's yard is drawn about 1.7 times as long and as wide as a town's.
    The pits set the size, not the population: a city runs about three times a town's pits, which is about 1.7 times each side, not the five times a city's inhabitants would give.
  • A medieval English tannery ran three times the pits of our largest yard.
    The largest tannery excavated at Northampton had 36 or 37 pits.
  • The yard stands on the water, its ground within about 20 ft of a bank.
    A yard set back on dry ground could not work. The 20 ft is this project's own figure.
  • The yard's ground stops at the bank, and only its staking frames reach into the water.
    The staking frames hold the hides in the current. A yard whose ground reads as a platform over the water is always drawn wrong.
  • The yard stands outside the walls.
    The tanners may live inside the walls; the work may not.
  • The yard never overlaps a field, a dry plot or a flower field, though it may abut one.
    A yard drawn on a field would claim ground at once cropped and ruined for cropping.
  • The yard's long side runs along its bank, within 15 degrees.
    Its long axis runs with the watercourse, not with the map, because the pit rank and the staking frames share one edge of water: a yard left square to the map on a slanted bank puts one corner in the water and strands the far end of the rank inland. A ditch counts as a bank.
  • Where a yard fronts more than one course, it is aligned to the reach it works.
    Not to a short tail beside it.
  • Why may a yard sit up to 15 degrees off its bank?
    It is a calibration against the drawn yards: a correct yard sits a few degrees off whichever stretch of bank it is measured against - the three yards drawn on the project's test maps at 2.1, 3.8 and 7.2 degrees - while the failure it separates, a yard square to the map on a diagonal bank, measured 22.9.

What a yard lets into its water decides where along the water it may stand.

  • A tanning yard stands downstream of everything the settlement draws from.
    The yard's own watercourse must end off the map, or in a drain that does - never in a pond, a field, an irrigation ditch or the moat - and no sluice, weir or channel head on that course may lie below the yard. Which lies further upstream is measured along the course itself.
  • Why must a tanning yard stand below every intake?
    For the salt, in this project's reading. Himeji's white-tawed leather (shironameshi), the Japanese method, is salted before the soak and after it, and rice is moderately sensitive to salt. The rot of the soak is the weaker reason - farmers spread night soil on purpose - so the rule rests on the salt, the oil film and the stench.

Where no stream runs near the quarter, the yard works a field drain, and a drain changes how it works.

  • A yard with no stream near its quarter may take a field drain as its water.
    An outcaste quarter could hold no share of the water upstream, in this project's reading, only the tail below every intake, and a drain is the one course on a map where every intake is upstream by construction, so it meets the rule above of itself. Hoshizora, one of the project's test maps, with no stream near its quarter, takes the drain of its northeast fields.
  • A drain-fed yard is drawn only where its drain runs off the map or into a marsh or pond.
    Lower fields commonly took up the drainage of the fields above, by this project's GUESS, so a drain-fed yard is drawn only where its drain leaves the map or ends in a marsh or a drainage tameike, never at another field's head. Hoshizora's drain leaves the east edge of its map.
  • A drain-fed yard is drawn with a sluice gate instead of staking frames.
    A drain has no current to stake hides in, so the yard ponds its own soak behind a gated cut and soaks its hides in standing pits, as tanners who used standing vats did, rather than in the current the Japanese method uses. Its leather is the poorer for it, in this project's reading.
  • Is a drain-fed yard worked all year?
    No, by this project's GUESS: it is a summer works. A field drain runs, as we GUESS, through the irrigation season and is near-dry from harvest to transplanting, while draft animals died most, as we also GUESS, in late winter and early spring. Salted hides keep until they can be worked - so its pits stand empty half the year, and its winter goes to salting hides down and to the clean leather craft in town.

The tanners live in their quarter, and the yard keeps its distance from everyone else.

  • A tanning yard keeps 120 ft from every dwelling, wall to wall, except the outcaste quarter's.
    The distance keeps the stench off the houses of the other castes. Distance from a nuisance is a privilege of the clean castes, and the quarter's own houses are exempt by design: counting them inverts the institution, and with them counted Hoshizora has no site for its yard at all. The 120 ft is this project's own figure.
  • No hut is drawn at the pits.
    No page we read says the tanners slept at the works, and an outcaste quarter was a community under its own headman, not scattered outbuildings.
  • Nothing ties a yard to its quarter by distance.
    The quarter follows the caste's duties in town and the yard follows the water: a walled city keeps its outcaste quarter inside, while the noxious part of the trade goes out to the water. So a long daily walk between them is the normal arrangement. On the project's test maps it runs from 90 ft at Hirameki to 2,587 ft at Tango, whose quarter is inside its walls at the northwest and its yard outside the southeast rim; Nagahara's yard stands about 1,390 ft from its quarter, and Hoshizora's crematory is across town from its quarter. All are drawn correctly, so a rule in feet, which would have condemned them, was declined. Being made to walk is one of the indignities the caste bears, even in a setting kinder to its underclasses than the societies it draws on.

What does tie the two together is the direction each lies in from the middle of town.

  • A tanning yard stands on its quarter's side of the settlement, within 90 degrees of the quarter's bearing from the middle of the laborers' dwellings.
    Within one town the quarter, its works and its execution ground went out the same way, in this project's reading, so the daily carcass haul need not cross the rest of the town. Whether towns kept designated carcass routes off their streets, no page we read says.
  • Why may a yard lie up to 90 degrees off its quarter's bearing?
    It is the same half-of-the-compass test the execution ground takes, borrowed rather than freshly invented. Measured from the settlement core, the middle of the whole settlement, the test maps' correctly drawn yards lie off their quarter's bearing by 0.6 degrees at Hirameki, 17.2 at Minami, 28.5 at Hoshizora and 56.0 at Nagahara. The rule measures from the middle of the laborers' dwellings instead, and from there Nagahara, the loosest, sits inside the line at 83.7 degrees, while the two wrong drawings the rule was written to catch sit outside it. Tango (127 degrees) and Ubame (100) lie outside it too, for the reasons in the next bullet.
  • Tango and Ubame are exempt from the direction rule.
    Tango because the Emperor lies to the southeast, and that claim of status takes its quarter to the opposite side from its works. Ubame because its land falls to the southwest while its only water fit for tanning is the valley stream to the east, so the fall of the ground and the water disagree. Both are recorded as exemptions rather than met by loosening the rule.
  • The cremation ground is left out of the direction rule.
    Monks officiate at a cremation in this setting, with outcaste assistants (where the crematory stands), so by this project's convention the ground follows the temple, not the outcaste side. On the test maps it stands near its monastery at Hoshizora (130 ft) and Ubame (360 ft) but not at Tango (2,606 ft), so the ground is excluded from the rule without a rule that it follows the monastery. Hoshizora's stands almost exactly opposite its quarter, and that map is right.
  • A town with no houses outside its outcaste quarter takes no direction test.
    There the middle of town falls on the quarter itself, and the quarter has no bearing from it.
