How our maps draw the formal entrance and a guest's arrival (genkan)

A building plan stages a guest's arrival as the record shows it: the gate, then open ground a palanquin can cross, then the step of the formal entrance. A guest's door opens onto that ground, never straight from the street into a room or against the side of a building. The household's own kitchen door is drawn the other way round, into the work space.

  • A plan's formal entrance takes one of two forms the record shows, and a knob chooses between them.
    In the first, the guest comes to a genkan with a shikidai, the form of a senior retainer's house. In the second there is no genkan with a shikidai: the guest's way runs through a middle gate in a wall and along a walled garden path to the veranda of the zashiki, and the household's everyday door is the kitchen entrance. That form is attested at a middle-rank house only.
  • Where a government office, such as an intendant's jin'ya, shares a compound with its official's residence, the genkan is drawn on the office.
    The way to the residence runs on through the office, as the visitor's route does at the Takayama jin'ya. Where the residence stands alone, the genkan is drawn on the residence.
  • The divider gate drawn behind the office hall is a guess.
    No page we read places a middle gate relative to an office hall. The one middle gate whose place is given leads from inside a retainer's street gate through the garden path to the veranda of the principal room, so the gate a plan draws in the dividing wall behind the office hall is a GUESS.
  • A guest's door opens onto open ground, never onto the street or against a building's side.
    A guest of rank stepped from the palanquin straight onto the genkan's step, and the entrance faced open ground inside the gate, so the ground before a guest's door is ground a palanquin can cross. This is historically accurate.
  • The ground before the entrance is drawn as an open court, and a garden in its place is a guess.
    No page we read on the genkan and the shikidai puts a garden between the gate and a genkan, and both drawings found show open ground just inside the gate. One of them, a plate from a Japanese garden book, runs a stone path on through a second gateway to the entrance, but its house is not named, its date is loose and it is not an official's court, so a plan that draws a guest garden in place of the court draws a GUESS. The stone lantern a plan sets in the court before the entrance is part of the same GUESS, a garden's ornament drawn where both drawings show open ground.
  • On the Hayakawa plan, the guests' river door opens into the guest garden.
    It opens there and not into the narrow strip beside the guest house's side. A garden there, in place of an open court, is a GUESS, as it is before any genkan: no page we read on the genkan and the shikidai puts a garden between the gate and a genkan.
  • A pond on the way from the gate to the entrance, or a forecourt fenced off from the garden, is a guess wherever a plan draws one.
    No page we read describes either.
  • The kitchen door is drawn opening into work space.
    The kitchen door, the katteguchi, is the doorway leading from outside into the kitchen. That it opens into the work space, the reverse of a guest's door, is this project's reading.
  • A guest's roji carries no well.
    An encyclopedia names the stepping stones and the water basin as the framework of a tea garden, and no page we read puts a draw-well on the roji a guest walks from a house's middle gate to its veranda. Where a page says what a house's working well stood beside, it is the kitchen or the bath: at a Matsue samurai house a well survives east of the main house, a place its surveyor thinks plausible beside one or the other, and even a tea master's house, the Urasenke's in Kyoto, lists its well with the kitchen. At an official's compound a well could stand inside the gate, before the office: at the Iwama jin'ya, past the gate range, the shikidai of the formal entrance was in front and a well on the right. So our plans draw a house's working well on its service side, and an official compound's on its service side or to one side of its front court, never on a guest's path. Those placements are historically accurate; that no well stood on a guest's roji rests on the record's silence. Other wells the record found, at the front, at the side and indoors, are at Samurai residences and their rooms.
