How our maps draw the main gate and its gatekeepers (nagaya-mon)

A building plan draws its compound's main gate in one of the forms the record shows, and its gatekeepers in one of the two places they sat. Where two forms are both attested, each plan takes one at random, a knob. The gate of a Japanese magistrate's office is drawn from about 6 to 12 ft wide; a yamen's is a roofed building of three bays, drawn 18 to 24 ft wide by our guess.

  • A Japanese magistrate's office's main gate is a one-bay gate or a gate range (nagaya-mon), a knob chosen for each plan.
    Both forms survive among buildings registered as cultural properties: the one-bay gate in three examples and the nagaya-mon in one.
  • How wide is each form drawn?
    A one-bay gate is drawn about 6 to 8.5 ft wide, the frontage the three registered examples measure, taken as the span between its pillar centers, as a bay is counted; its passage is that frontage less one pillar, about 1 ft, a GUESS: no page we read measures a one-bay gate's pillar. Its posts are drawn outside the passage, so the drawn gate is wider than its frontage: the hand-drawn plans draw them about 2 ft square, and the generated plans 2 ft along the wall and lengthened to about 4.3 ft across it, so that they stand past the wall's ink on both faces - a map drawing convention. A side gate's posts are drawn as dark blocks about 2 to 2.7 ft along the wall and about 4 ft across it, standing past the wall's ink on both faces - drawn large so they read at the plan's scale, also a map drawing convention: no page we read measures a side gate's post. A nagaya-mon's opening is drawn about 12 ft, the two ken the registered one opens in.
  • A samurai residence's gate on the town and city maps, 9 to 12 ft wide, falls between the one-bay gate and the nagaya-mon.
    That width is set at How our maps draw compound walls (neribei and tsuijibei).
  • A yamen's main gate is drawn as a roofed building of three bays.
    That is its form in the record: a building with a roof, not an opening in the wall, three bays wide at the county seats we read of.
  • A yamen's gate is drawn 18 to 24 ft wide, a guess.
    The width is a GUESS: no page we read gives such a gate's width.
  • A grievance drum stands at every seat's main gate, under the gate's roof, and is not drawn.
    Both county yamen we read of kept a drum at the main gate for the people to beat with a grievance: Pingyao's under the gate's porch from 1368, Neixiang's in the front half of the gate's east end bay. Our plans follow those two county yamen at every seat. The drum stands under the gate's roof and is not drawn as a mark of its own, a map drawing convention. Those two pages name a drum and neither a petition window nor a bell, and our plans draw neither.
  • Where the gatekeepers sit follows the gate's form, the knob chosen for each plan.
    A gate range holds their guardroom inside it, as the Kaibara jin'ya's does, and a one-bay gate has a gatekeepers' house of its own beside it, a building apart, as the Takayama jin'ya lists its gatekeepers' house apart from its gate; that page gives neither its gate's bays nor where the house stood. No plan draws a gatehouse beside a gate range: no source we found shows one.
  • The store of arms is drawn in one of three places, one chosen for each plan from its seed.
    The record shows a magistrate's arms kept in three places: in a room of the gate range on one side of the passage, as Kaibara's gate range keeps its gun store; in an end bay of the office hall, as Pingyao's rebuilt great hall kept its armory; or in a store of its own, a building apart, as the weapons store at Nirayama was, about 18 by 12 ft (5.4 by 3.7 m). That store, Hamamashike's weapons store (named among its storehouses) and Jiexiu's armory (listed inside its side gates, and not said to be a building or a room) are set out at The magistrate's manor in a town. A plan whose main gate is drawn as a gate range is offered all three places; a plan with a one-bay gate has no range to hold it, so it takes the office hall's end bay or a store of its own. Each plan takes one of the places it is offered at random, a knob. No page we read places a store of its own: the Nirayama and Hamamashike stores are unplaced, and Jiexiu's account lists its armory inside the side gates, before the middle gate, but its words leave open whether it stood there or west of a pavilion, and whether it was a building or a room. So a plan draws a store of its own wherever its courts have room. It is drawn about 11 to 18 ft by 11 to 24 ft (about 120 to 430 sq ft), a range about Nirayama's, also a GUESS. Ubame's plan took the gate range: its armory is the range's west room, about 13 by 12 ft, sized near one of Kaibara's eight-mat rooms, a GUESS: no page we read gives the size of a gun store or armory room. No plan in the pool draws a store of its own yet.
  • The gatehouse and the guardroom in the gate range are both drawn about 2 ken (~12 ft) deep.
    That depth is measured for both forms: the Kaibara jin'ya's gate range and the separate guardroom set against the gate of the temple Kita-in.
  • How long is a gatehouse of its own drawn?
    About 40 ft, a GUESS. The Takayama gatehouse, the one jin'ya gatehouse measured, has about 400 sq ft of floor (36.98 square meters); drawn 2 ken (~12 ft) deep, that would be a building about 33 ft long, so a 40 ft gatehouse, of about 480 sq ft, is about a fifth larger than the one measured. No page we read gives the Takayama gatehouse's length or depth. The one separate guardroom measured side by side, set against a temple gate, is about 18 ft long.
