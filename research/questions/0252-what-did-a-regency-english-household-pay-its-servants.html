What did a Regency English household pay its servants?

Rokugan pays its servants in rice and coin by its own customs, and no figure here sets one of them. This is a comparative question: what one English household manual of 1825 says servants were paid, a yardstick for players who know the shillings of Austen and Dickens.

In 1825 two former servants, Samuel and Sarah Adams, published in London a guide to the duties of every post in a gentleman's house, and gave the wage of nearly every post. A wage was a sum a year, in pounds or in guineas of twenty-one shillings, and many posts came with clothes, perquisites or a cottage besides. The best paid were the house steward, at £100 to £250 and more, and a governess, at up to £120; a royal duke's French cook was understood to earn £500. The least paid were a young maid in a small household, at 5 to 10 guineas, and a nursery maid, at 6 to 10.

What each post was paid

The book's account of each post ends with its wage. "l." is its abbreviation for pounds.

  • The upper women servants earned 18 to 50 guineas, and a governess more.
    The housekeeper, 25 to 50 guineas a year by the size of the family and her business. The lady's maid, 18 to 25 guineas, with tea and washing. The head nurse, £18 to £25, with perquisites at christenings. A governess, £25 to £120 a year by her qualifications and her pupils.
  • House maids, nurses and the maid of all work earned 6 to 16 guineas.
    The upper house maid, 12 to 16 guineas a year; the under house maids, £10 to £12; the under nurse, 10 to 12 guineas; the nursery maid, 6 to 10; the still-room maid, 8 to 12; and the servant of all work, 8 to 12 guineas. Its list of the servants kept at each income puts a professed maid of all work at 12 to 14 guineas.
  • The kitchen, laundry and dairy maids earned £8 to £15.
    The kitchen-maid, or under cook, 12 to 14 guineas a year; the scullion, 8 to 12 guineas; the laundry maid, £8 to £15; the dairy maid, £8 to £12, with perquisites of a penny for each pound of butter, a penny-halfpenny for each chicken or fowl killed, twopence each for ducks, geese and turkeys, and threepence a score for eggs.
  • The book gives no wage for the cook herself.
    Its section on the cook states none. The 12 to 14 guineas a year in the next section are the kitchen-maid's. A man cook, it says, is paid "frequently twice or thrice" what the most experienced English woman cook is. The Duke of York was understood to pay his French cook, Ude, £500 a year.
  • The house steward and the butler headed the men.
    The house steward, a salary of £100 to £250 and more. A butler, £50 to £80 a year in a large family and £30 to £50 in a smaller one; as valet he had his master's cast-off clothes, and as butler the ends of the wax candles, the second-hand cards, the tradesmen's compliments when their bills were paid, or Christmas boxes, and wine for his own use, finding his own clothes and washing. A valet, £30 to £60, more in some places, and his master's cast-off clothes.
  • Footmen and the other indoor men earned 16 to 30 guineas, a boy £8 to £12.
    The footman, 20 to 30 guineas, with two suits of livery and two undress suits; the under butler, 16 to 25 guineas; the lady's footman, £18 to 25 guineas, with two liveries and a working dress; the under footman, 16 to 20 guineas, with liveries; the hall porter, £24 to £30 a year. The boy of the steward's room, £8 to £12.
  • The stable men earned from £8 to about £50, most with their clothes.
    The head coachman, 25 to 36 guineas a year, generally with two suits of livery, a box coat every two or three years, two hats, two pairs of boots and one or two stable dresses. The under, or lady's, coachman, £20 to 24 guineas, with two suits of livery, a box coat at times, hat and boots, and one or two stable dresses. The groom, £22 to £25, generally with two livery suits and two stable dresses a year. A man hired as groom and valet, or groom and footman, about £50 a year living out, with the cast-off clothes. The postillion, 16 to 20 guineas. The stable boy, living in, £8 to £12 and clothes as agreed.
  • Servants hired by the week were paid 10s. 6d. to 21 shillings a week.
    Stable helpers were hired by the week at 16 to 21 shillings, living out, with no liveries. Under gardeners were paid 16 to 20 or 21 shillings a week, and vegetables. A nurse for the sick in London was paid 10s. 6d. to 15s. a week.
  • A head gardener earned £50 to £100, with a cottage.
    His salary came generally with a cottage, and vegetables and fuel allowed. A small family, or a garden of an acre or less, kept one gardener at a guinea a week, living out.

Board wages and allowances

  • When the family was away, servants were paid board wages.
    In general 10 shillings a week for women and 12 for men. All the servants of a large house might be reckoned at 10 shillings a head a week for their board.
  • Beer came on top.
    The men were allowed a pot of ale a day and the women a pint, besides table beer.
