How did cotton reach China and spread across it?

Rokugan's tax rules set cotton beside common iron as an ordinary, low-rate good, and the setting's list of regional cash crops does not name it. The findings below give what the historical record holds on how the crop came to China and moved across it; who wore it, and when, is in When did cotton become ordinary clothing in China?

Cotton cloth was known in China long before the plant was grown there, as tribute from distant peoples. Growing began on the frontiers, at Turfan, in Yunnan and on the southeast coast, and the earliest statement found that it was common in the lower Yangzi was written by 1285.

First record of the plant and the cloth

  • Does the Han history's wording mean cotton?
    It does not say so. Of the Ailao of the southwest it lists wool rugs, a cloth it calls bodie, and fine cloth it calls langan. The next sentence has one Ailao cloth spun from 梧桐木華 (wutong mu hua, "wutong tree flower"). Of Hainan under the Han emperor Wu it says only 廣幅布 (guang fu bu, "broad-width cloth"). A paper by Harriet Zurndorfer, marked by its author not for citation without permission, takes the Ailao passage as good-quality cotton, without saying which word it means, and says official documents recorded cotton cloth in what is now Yunnan around 200 BCE. Pelliot says that with the word po-tieh, for cotton, the Han history draws on a later source.
  • Was langan cloth ramie?
    Qu Dajun says so: in the speech of the Liao tribes it was the word for ramie.
  • Which is the oldest cotton found?
    Two modern accounts differ. Zurndorfer's paper reports cotton from Tarim Basin burials of the first millennium BCE. The historian Chen Guodong reports Han-period cotton among the fragments Aurel Stein took at Dunhuang, and calls cloth dug up at Minfeng in Xinjiang in 1959 the earliest known in China. He states no date for it himself, though a study in his footnote has Eastern Han batik from Niya in its title.

Cloth arriving as tribute and trade

  • Under the Han and Tang dynasties cotton came as tribute.
    Qiu Jun says distant peoples offered it, that China did not have the seed, that commoners did not wear it and that officials did not levy it. The Liang dynasty's history lists jibei, the name later writers give cotton cloth, among the tribute of Poli, in the southern seas, in 522, and elsewhere calls jibei a tree whose flowers, like goose down, were spun into cloth. The Liang emperor Wu's black cotton curtain of the first half of the 500s shows cotton at court between those dynasties, as part of a chronicler's picture of his austerity, though neither that chronicle nor Pelliot says it was tribute.
  • By the Southern Song dynasty it also came as trade.
    Chen Guodong has Indian cotton cloth appearing now and then as tribute from the Eastern Han to the Southern Song, and imported on some scale by the Southern Song at the latest. A revenue ministry list of 1141 names jibei cloth, and he says the Central Plains had not yet taken up the industry at the start of the Southern Song.
  • Tang writers placed the plant outside China.
    In 736 a commentator wrote that cotton cloth was woven from a plant which did not exist in China, and Pelliot found no Tang text on cotton growing in China proper.

The frontiers: first growing, region by region

  • The northwest: Turfan in the first half of the 500s.
    The Liang dynasty's history says of Gaochang, at Turfan, that a herb there bore a fruit like a cocoon with a floss inside, called baidie seed, that the inhabitants mostly gathered and wove it, and that the soft white cloth was used in trade. Pelliot, reading that history, has cotton grown around Turfan in the first half of the 500s, and tribute of its cloth required from that region in the statutes of 713-741, the only region for which the New Tang history names it. He found no sign of it passing from there into Gansu, Shanxi or Shaanxi at that date.
  • The southwest: Yunnan under the Tang.
    Pelliot has cultivation then limited to Yunnan and Tongking, or at least unrecorded elsewhere, and thinks it had probably already begun on Hainan.
  • Hainan and the south coast by 1178.
    Zhou Qufei records it in three south-coast prefectures (Lei, Hua and Lian) and among the Li, the native inhabitants of Hainan, used in place of silk and ramie. Pelliot adds that most of Hainan's cotton then was grown by the Li, and that by 1225 the island was the chief source shipped to Fujian.
  • What Zhao Rukuo recorded in the 1220s.
    He describes a plant like a small mulberry whose floss was stripped of its seeds with iron rods and spun straight from the hand. At Jiyang in the south of Hainan the women wove it and reared no silkworms, and of the island's goods areca and jibei were the plentiful ones in which merchants of Quanzhou dealt.
  • Did Zhou Qufei mean that cotton was plentiful, or that the rich wore it?
    The two characters 富有 (fuyou, "abundant; the rich have") can be read either way. This project reads them as "plentiful", as they plainly mean elsewhere in the same chapter; Pelliot translates "some of the rich".
  • Fujian and Guangdong by the late 1000s.
    A work of 1085 says the local peasants there competed to plant it, and Pelliot takes this and a work of about 1125 as proving it extensive by the end of the 11th century. Liu Pingsheng and Ge Jinfang add a case they date to 1078, though the entry they cite falls under 1084, when a prefect of Guangzhou lost his post because his son had made soldiers weave mumian, a word they take as cotton.

The Yangzi valley and the north

  • Did cotton reach the Yangzi by the 1100s?
    That is a modern inference, not a record. Pelliot reasons backward from a commentator's note of 1285 and from the wars of the 1270s, and concludes that it went back "at least to the first half of the 12th century"; elsewhere he says it was grown in Jiangnan "from the 12th cent." Yet he calls an order of 1289, which set up offices to collect cotton in the Yangzi provinces and Fujian, "the first mention of cotton cultivation in central China".
  • What the Song poems are made to show.
    Liu Pingsheng and Ge Jinfang, against what they call the view of most scholars that it flourished only from the Yuan, put its passage from the far south to Zhejiang at about the reign of Xiaozong (1162-1189). They rest this on poems, Lin Guangchao's on passing Tonglu in those years and Fang Kui's on Yangzhou, and they name Huangyan as well. Fang Kui lived at the turn of the Song and Yuan, so his lines do not reach the 1100s. The reading is theirs, and it is a modern one. They also report a cotton blanket from a Southern Song tomb at Lanxi in Zhejiang, as evidence of weaving skill rather than of where it was grown.
  • By 1285 it was common in Jiangnan.
    The Yuan commentator Hu Sanxing, a man of the region, wrote that there was much of it there, and described how it was sown, ginned and spun. Liu and Ge quote the same note as showing growing and processing already mature throughout the Southern Song, which is further than its date of 1285 carries.
  • Was it the late Song or the Yuan that spread it?
    A farming manual with a preface of 1273 is the crux, and the two accounts disagree over whose it was. Zurndorfer's paper calls it issued by the Song state in its waning days; Pelliot says it was prepared by order of Qubilai. A later edition credits one chapter to the Yuan's Office of Agriculture, and that chapter says cotton, a product of the Western Regions, had lately been planted in Shaanxi and throve there. Zurndorfer has growing reach the lower Yangzi, Sichuan and Shaanxi by about 1270-1300. Pelliot reads Wang Zhen's introduction in his work of 1313 as showing it had spread to the lower Yangzi and Sichuan before 1276.
  • Wang Zhen, in 1313, has it recent in the east and northwest.
    He calls it lately much sown in the lower Yangzi and in Shaanxi, the first time, Pelliot notes, that northwestern China appears as a cotton region.
  • When did Huang Daopo teach Songjiang to process it?
    The sources give different dates, and one calls the story a legend. Tao Zongyi, late in the Yuan, says only that an old woman of that name came from Yazhou "early in the dynasty" and that Wunijing, some fifty li east of Songjiang, had already sought its seed from Fujian and Guangdong. Zurndorfer's paper gives her a birth year of 1245 with a question mark and calls the story a "well-known legend". Pelliot stresses that much cotton was already grown in Jiangnan by 1285, before her date in later tradition. A newspaper feature of 2024 dates her return to 1295-1297, and quotes a Qing gazetteer of Songjiang putting the seed's arrival, not her own, in the Song.
