What did a night at an inn cost?

Rokugan's travelers lodge at inns along its roads, and the setting judges a price by how many days of a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give what Edo-period Japan's travelers paid for a night at the two kinds of inn, and what a horse was charged, each set beside the nearest day wage found.

Two kinds of inn lodged travelers in Edo-period Japan. A hatago served its guests meals; at a kichin-yado, the older and cheaper kind, travelers carried their own food and paid only for the firewood to cook it. On the Tokaido a night at a hatago cost about 120 to 200 mon of copper and a night at a kichin-yado 50 mon or less, and by the mid-19th century about 200 mon was the going rate at a hatago. No price was found for a horse's feed or stabling, or for a night at an inn in Qing China.

A hatago, the inn with meals

  • What did a night cost on the Tokaido?
    200 mon at the better inns and 120 at the lesser; on the Nakasendo the better inns charged about 150. The historian who gives these figures does not date them. By the mid-19th century the going rate on the Tokaido, for travelers from around Edo on their way to the Ise shrines, seems to have been about 200 mon a night, with lunch on the road another 70 to 80.
  • How many meals did the price take in?
    None of the pages read says. A hatago is described only as an inn that served meals.
  • And in a town?
    Only one town's price was found. At Tsu, a castle town on the Ise road, a traveler of 1790 found the hatago he wanted crowded and was directed to another inn, the Kami-ya, at 130 mon each.
  • And in a big city?
    No price was found for a night at an inn in Edo, Kyoto or Osaka.
  • How much dearer than a kichin-yado was it?
    In the Tenpo era (1830-1844), more than five times as dear.

A kichin-yado, the firewood-fee inn

  • What did the shogunate set as the charge?
    The firewood fee was fixed by law early in the period: 3 mon a person in 1611; 6 mon in 1658 and again in 1663; 16 mon for a master and 6 for a servant in 1665. In 1686 a shogunal intendant in Musashi set 12 mon for a master and 6 for a servant as the firewood fee his clerks paid, on top of their rice, when they stayed in a village on their rounds.
  • What did it cost by the 19th century?
    About 50 mon. A traveler of 1823 paid a firewood fee of 50 mon for a night at Tsu, and on the Tokaido a kichin-yado charged 50 mon or less; in the northeast, some charged 16.
  • And in Edo?
    In 1843 the kichin-yado of Edo, in Shitaya Yamazaki-cho and elsewhere, charged no firewood fee. A lodger paid a "roof fee" of 24 mon a day, and another 16 to 24 mon to borrow bedding.

A horse for the night

  • What was a horse charged?
    Only the regulated firewood fee: 6 mon a horse in 1611, twice a person's 3, because a horse used more hot water; 8 mon a horse in 1663, against 6 for a person.
  • What did its feed or stabling cost?
    No price was found.

The day's wage beside these prices

  • What did an unskilled laborer earn where these prices were paid?
    No unskilled day wage was found for the Tokaido, Tsu or Edo in the same years, and none in copper mon. The nearest is the Kyoto day laborer's wage in the Mitsui house's accounts, in grams of silver: 2.55 g in 1791, a year after the Tsu inn of 1790; 4.17 g in 1823, the year of the Tsu firewood fee; 2.80 g in 1843, the year of the Edo roof fee; and 3.00 g in 1850, for the mid-century hatago. Edo's laborers were paid at about the same level as Kyoto's (What did an unskilled laborer earn?).
  • Why are the two not set in one unit?
    The prices are in copper mon and the wages in silver, and this record does not yet give the rate between the two coins, so neither is converted.

China

  • What did a night at a Qing inn cost?
    No price was found, for a traveler or for a draft animal's fodder.
