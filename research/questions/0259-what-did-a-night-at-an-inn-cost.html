What did a night at an inn cost?

Rokugan's travelers lodge at inns along its roads, and the setting judges a price by how many days of a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give what Edo-period Japan's travelers paid for a night at the two kinds of inn, and what a horse was charged, with the nearest day wages found to set beside them.

Two kinds of inn lodged travelers in Edo-period Japan. A hatago served its guests meals; at a kichin-yado, the older and cheaper kind, travelers carried their own food and paid only for the firewood to cook it. On the Tokaido a night at a hatago cost about 120 to 200 mon of copper and a night at a kichin-yado 50 mon or less, and in the mid-19th century about 200 mon seems to have been the going rate at a hatago. In the cities, by a record of the customs of the 1830s to 1853, a first-class inn of Kyoto or Osaka charged 3 monme 5 bu of silver a night with two meals, the lesser inns about 200 to 250 mon, and an Edo inn about 248 mon with two meals. No price was found for a horse's feed or stabling, or for a night at an inn in Qing China.

A hatago, the inn with meals

  • What did a night cost on the Tokaido?
    200 mon at the better inns and 120 at the lesser; on the Nakasendo the better inns charged about 150. The historian who gives these figures does not date them. By the mid-19th century the going rate on the Tokaido, for travelers from around Edo on their way to the Ise shrines, seems to have been about 200 mon a night, with lunch on the road another 70 to 80. The Morisada manko gives the same: before the Ansei era (1854-1860), 200 mon at a first-class inn on the Tokaido and 148 on the Nakasendo, close to the historian's undated 150.
  • Did the price rise?
    Steeply, at the end of the period. As all goods grew dearer from the Ansei and Man'en eras (1854-1861), the inns of every province raised their charges, and by the Keio era (1865-1868) a night cost 700 to 800 mon; the three cities, it says, followed suit.
  • How many meals did the price take in?
    Two, morning and evening, at the first-class inns of Kyoto, where the midday meal was charged apart; two at the inns of Edo. Kyoto's merchants' inns took in the midday meal too.
  • And in a town?
    Only one town's price was found. At Tsu, a town on the road to Ise, travelers of 1790 found the hatago they wanted crowded and were directed to another inn, the Kami-ya, at 130 mon each.
  • And in Kyoto?
    The first-class inns, mostly on Sanjo street, charged generally 3 monme 5 bu of silver a night, and at a guest's wish, rarely, 5 monme of silver or 2 shu of gold. There were inns of about 200 or 250 mon at Sanjo and below it, which the record reads as Sanjo street and the streets south of it; and the merchants' inns scattered through the city, whose guests came on business, not for pleasure, charged 200 or 250 mon with the midday meal, and coarse dishes.
  • And in Osaka?
    A first-class inn usually 3 monme 5 bu, and a low-grade inn about 200 mon.
  • And in Edo?
    About 248 mon with two meals. Edo's inns lodged those with business at the government offices, and were called lawsuit inns; there were no handsome inns as in Kyoto and Osaka, the meals were coarse, and a dear lodging was not to be had even for the asking.
  • How much dearer than a kichin-yado was it?
    In the Tenpo era (1830-1844), five times as dear or more.

A kichin-yado, the firewood-fee inn

  • What was the charge early in the period?
    The shogunate's law of 1611 set the firewood fee at 3 mon a person. It was 6 mon in 1658; an order of 1663 set 6 mon, firewood included, against a horse's 8; and in 1665 it was 16 mon for a master and 6 for a servant. In 1686 a shogunal intendant in Musashi set 12 mon for a master and 6 for a servant as the firewood fee his clerks paid, on top of their rice, when they stayed in a village on their rounds.
  • What did it cost later?
    About 50 mon. A traveler of 1823 paid a firewood fee of 50 mon for a night at Tsu, and the historian's undated figures put a kichin-yado on the Tokaido at 50 mon or less, and some in the northeast at 16.
  • And in Edo?
    In 1843 the kichin-yado of Edo, in Shitaya Yamazaki-cho and elsewhere, charged no firewood fee. A lodger paid a "roof fee" of 24 mon a day, and another 16 to 24 mon to borrow bedding.
  • What was the cheapest lodging Morisada names?
    The gure-yado of Osaka's Nagamachi, which Morisada likens to the kichin-yado of other provinces, though different: rows of rooms about 6 by 9 shaku behind the keeper's house, each with old tatami and one earthen pot, that lodged beggars for 36 mon a night and lent no bedding. Kyoto had them near the Great Buddha, he heard, and Edo none.

A horse for the night

  • What was a horse charged?
    The one charge found is the regulated firewood fee: 6 mon a horse in 1611, twice a person's 3, because a horse used more hot water; 8 mon a horse in 1663, against 6 for a person.
  • What did its feed or stabling cost?
    No price was found.

The day's wage beside these prices

  • What did a laborer and a craftsman earn in the cities in 1837-1853?
    The Morisada manko gives the day's hire of both. In Edo a carpenter got about 5 or 5.5 monme of silver a day, and a tobi, a laborer of the building sites, 300 mon. In Osaka a carpenter's hire was fixed at 4 monme 3 bu of silver a day, or 3 monme 1 bu when the employer fed him; and the helpers of Kyoto and Osaka's building sites, who fed themselves, got 280 mon. No Kyoto carpenter's wage is given; the nearest is Osaka's.
  • How many days' work was a night at an inn?
    By this project's arithmetic: an Edo inn's 248 mon was about five-sixths of a tobi's day; a lesser Kyoto inn's 200 to 250 mon, about seven- to nine-tenths of a helper's day, and a low-grade Osaka inn's 200 about seven-tenths; and the beggars' 36 mon about an eighth; a first-class inn's 3 monme 5 bu about four-fifths of an Osaka carpenter's day.
  • And on the highways?
    No wage was found for the Tokaido or Nakasendo themselves. The nearest are Morisada's Edo pair above, of the same years: set against the tobi's 300 mon, the Tokaido's 200 mon before 1854 was two-thirds of a day and the Nakasendo's 148 about half, this project's arithmetic. For the Keio prices of 700 to 800 mon no wage of 1865-1868 was found in Edo or on the roads; the nearest unskilled wage is the Kyoto day laborer's in the Mitsui house's accounts, 4.11 to 4.26 g of silver in 1865-1867, and the nearest craftsman's Morisada's Edo carpenter, given for his writing years, 12 to 31 years earlier.
  • And beside the earlier prices?
    No unskilled day wage was found for Tsu in 1790 or 1823, and none in copper mon for those years. The nearest is the Kyoto day laborer's wage in the Mitsui house's accounts, in grams of silver: 2.55 g in 1791, a year after the Tsu inn of 1790, and 4.17 g in 1823, the year of the Tsu firewood fee. From 1818, Edo's laborers were paid at about the same level as Kyoto's (What did an unskilled laborer earn?). The Edo roof fee of 1843 and the mid-century hatago fall in Morisada's years: the roof fee's 24 mon was about a twelfth of a tobi's day.
  • Why are the Kyoto laborer's grams not set beside the mon?
    The ratios above set prices in mon beside wages in mon, and prices in silver beside wages in silver, so they need no rate between the coins.
