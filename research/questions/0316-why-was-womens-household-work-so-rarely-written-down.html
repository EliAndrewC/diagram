Why was women's household work so rarely written down?

Rokugan's money rules price a koku by what it buys and by the paid working days it stands for, and the work a household did for itself has few figures to set beside them. The findings below give why that is: what historians of Japan and China say about why so little of the household work women did was written down at the time, and which records they read to recover it anyway.

Most of what a household did for itself was never written down, and that is the reason the record's figures for it are spotty: no source read counts the days a city or farm household spent on its own work, while paid days and prices were recorded. The silence is a fact about the past in its own right. A historian of Edo-period Japan (1603-1868) puts its main cause in the sources themselves: writing served those who ruled, and the men who governed and kept the village's papers wrote the great majority of what survives, so women are hard to see in it. Historians recover women's work from diaries, registers and rules written for other purposes; for China, the readings found are narrower.

Japan: why the papers show so few women

  • Writing served rule, and the rulers were men.
    A historian introducing a 2020 book on Edo-period women's history puts the biggest cause in the sources: writing spread in part because rulers needed it to govern, so what survives is mostly about rule and politics, and the samurai who ruled were all men.
  • The village's papers were written by its male officials.
    In the farm household too the male head's labor was central; the village officials who dealt with the lord's business were all men, and the enormous body of documents villages left in the period was written by them. In such a society, he writes, men held the public roles and left the records, and women become hard to see.
  • Women's chances to earn cash grew.
    The same historian notes that women's chances to earn cash grew over the period, by the jobs called "wage work" (washing, odd jobs, petty trade) or by weaving. What a city household paid others for is in What did a city household buy that a farm household made?
  • What survives is administrative and economic, under the household heads' names.
    A university library's 2007 survey of books on Edo-period working women says the same: what was written down and survives is mainly administrative and economic records, in which the names of male household heads appear in great numbers, and historians have painstakingly picked mentions of women out of them.
  • Women were shut out of managing the farm.
    As the same survey reports Nagano Hiroko's study, when the small farm household formed in the Edo period, women's labor came to be excluded from most of the work of managing it.
  • Some historians look for why women could not appear in public life.
    The survey reports Oguchi Yujiro arguing that a history of the period without women is overcome not only by finding new sources but by finding the social causes that kept women off the public stage.

Japan: the records historians read instead

  • Village headmen's diaries.
    The survey names papers by Kono Jun'ichiro and Oguchi Yujiro that bring to light the work of wives and daughters inside the house from village headmen's diaries, beside Nagashima Junko on women in farm labor and Katakura Hisako on women in service in the cities.
  • Temple population registers.
    Historical demography, Hayami Akira's study of one village from its temple population registers among others, traced how people moved at marriage, childbirth and going into service.
  • Rules written for women, read against their purpose.
    The survey reports Yokota Fuyuhiko's reading of the Onna daigaku, a conduct book for women: the industrial and urban society of the period could not have run without women's labor, and the talk of chastity arose to silence women's own claims. A rule for farmers that tells the wife to work at the loom is in Is there an old saying that rising early makes a family rich?
  • Official commendations.
    The 2020 book has a chapter on rewards for virtuous women and the state, through the official compilation of commendations, the Kogiroku.
  • Another society's historians say the same of Europe.
    The survey reports that the multi-volume History of Women edited by Georges Duby and Michelle Perrot, on Europe, starts from women's not remaining in public and political records while images of women and talk about them abound.

China: narrower readings

  • Cloth was counted while it was a tax.
    As Ann Waltner's review reports Francesca Bray's Technology and Gender (1997), textiles tied the women's quarters to the state through taxes collected in grain and cloth until the Single Whip Reform of the late Ming; when the state stopped asking for bolts of cloth, the meaning of women's work shifted, cloth-making was gradually taken over by men, and women's economic activity, Bray argues, was masked, not lessened.
  • The best estimates of women's work rest on few documents.
    Kenneth Pomeranz, reviewing two of Li Bozhong's books on farming and handicrafts in Jiangnan (the Yangzi delta), calls Li's estimates of farm output and inputs generally the best we have, and his claim that men's and women's labor productivity both rose well enough based to put the burden of proof on those who deny it, but finds the estimates' documentary base fairly narrow; and Li's evidence that households came to buy goods rather than make them is weaker in the countryside than in the cities.
  • Gazetteer lives of women were written to shape virtue.
    A study of the biographies of exemplary women in the editions of one county's Ming and Qing gazetteers (Yueqing) finds that their differences often reveal the local gentry's shaping of women's virtue, which widened from chastity alone under the Ming to take in the virtuous mother in the mid-Qing and, late in the Qing, the talented woman.
