What did a famous good cost far from where it was made?

In Rokugan a famous good sells far from home: Fox white charcoal commands a premium throughout the Empire, enough that counterfeits of it are sold in Kakita and Etsuko lands. The findings below give what the historical record holds for it: a Chinese tea priced from its hills to Canton and London, a Japanese sake priced in the one city it was made for, and a Japanese charcoal sold across the sea.

Congou tea of good or average grade cost about 12 taels a picul in the Wuyi hills, and carrying it to Canton added 3.92, about a third of its cost, over six weeks to two months by river and porter; it reached the Canton merchants at about 20 taels, the East India Company at about 27 and London's sales at 60. Nada's new brewing licenses of 1832 were for Edo alone, and its sake sold there for about 15 to 20 ryo the twenty casks, but no price at the brewery was found. Tosa's charcoal sold in Kyushu in 1836 for 670 mon a bale, against 490 for the charcoal of Hyuga. China: Wuyi tea, from the hills to Canton

  • What did it cost in the hills?
    About 12 taels a picul (about 60 kg, ~133 lb) for Congou of good or average grade, as Samuel Ball (ball-1848-tea-cultivation), the East India Company's inspector of teas in China, resident there from 1804 to 1826, reckons it; a Mr. Pigou had put it at 11 about fifty years before he wrote. In the district, the teas the English called Congou sold for 7 to 13 taels a picul, those the Chinese themselves called Congou for 22 to 32, and the dearest, Paochong and Souchong, for 42 to 92, it was said.
  • How did it get to Canton?
    Down the Min river in Fujian, then on porters' backs eight days over the mountain passes into Jiangxi, down its rivers with many transfers between boats, over the Meiling pass by porter in a day, and on to Canton by ship: six weeks to two months in all, for 3 taels 9 mace 2 candareens, 3.92 taels, a picul, about a third of the tea's cost where it grew. The one mountain crossing into Jiangxi cost 1.25 taels, which Ball calls more than a third of the whole carriage, for less than a seventh of the distance; by his own figures it is nearly a third.
  • What did it cost at Canton, and in London?
    The tea at 12 taels, its chests and packing at 1.32, the carriage at 3.92 and the duties and charges at Canton at 3.0 brought it to the Canton merchants at about 20.2 taels a picul in the Company's years; the Company paid them about 27, and it sold at the Company's sales in England for three shillings a pound, 60 taels a picul. So the Canton cost was about 1.7 times the cost in the hills, the price paid by the Company about 2.25 times and London's 5 times, this project's arithmetic.
  • Was there a shorter road?
    By water to Fuzhou, about 270 miles (~435 km) from the tea country with no break in the waterway, in four or five days, for no more than 0.43 tael a picul, Ball writes, about a ninth of the cost to Canton, this project's arithmetic.
  • What did a laborer and a craftsman earn?
    In south China, Ball writes, a common carpenter or bricklayer got his food and about seven pence a day; the Canton merchants' porters three meals, lodging and 15 shillings a month; and the packers of Bohea tea three meals and five pence, he does not say for what time. At his reckoning of the tael at 6 shillings 8 pence, the carpenter's day was about 0.09 tael and the porter's month about 2.25 taels, some 0.075 a day over 30 days; so a picul's carriage to Canton came to about 45 carpenter-days or 52 porter-days, and the tea itself in the hills to about 137 carpenter-days, this project's arithmetic.

Japan: Nada sake, made for Edo

  • Where was it sold?
    In Edo. When the Nada brewers got new licenses in 1832 they had to pledge not to encroach on the Osaka market, so their licenses were for sake sent to Edo only, the Kobe City Archives (kobe-nada-shuzogyo) write.
  • How did it get there?
    By sea: a large ship carried 1,000 koku of sake at a time and took 10 to 14 days to Edo. What the freight cost is in What did carrying goods cost by water, against by land?; its standard, which has no year and rose toward the end of the period, set against the normal Edo price below at the law's 60 monme of silver to the ryo, was about a fifteenth to a twentieth of the price, this project's arithmetic, though the market rate of the two coins moved away from the law's.
  • What did it sell for in Edo?
    Normally about 15 to 20 ryo of gold for 10 horse-loads, 20 casks of 4 to (40 sho, about 72 liters) each; in the glut of the Bunka and Bunsei years (1804 to 1830), when Edo took in about a million casks a year, it fell to 4 to 6 ryo. One brewery's settlements with its Edo wholesaler in 1858 ran from 17.5 to 23 ryo for its brand Tancho and from 13.5 to 18.5 for Jukai, for 20 casks by this project's reading of the table, whose column is headed as for 2 casks but whose sums work only for 20; a spoiled lot fetched 6.5 ryo, and one with casks thrown overboard at sea 10.5.
  • And at the brewery?
    No price at the brewery was found for any year. The one figure is a founding story of the first sake shipped by sea, which the account places before the Itami brewers' shipments of about 1658 to 1661: a shipowner of Hyogo was shown settlements of about 6 ryo at the source against 55 to 60 ryo on arrival in Edo, for an amount the story does not give.
  • What did Edo's workmen earn?
    An Edo carpenter about 5 or 5.5 monme of silver a day and a laborer 300 mon of copper, in the 1830s to 1850s (What did a skilled craftsman earn against a laborer?). At Edo's printed rates of 1842, 6,665 to 6,995 mon to the ryo (What was copper coin worth in silver?), a cask at the normal price of 0.75 to 1 ryo was about 17 to 23 laborer-days, and about 125 to 175 mon a sho at wholesale, this project's arithmetic across these years.

Japan: Tosa charcoal across the sea

  • How far did it go?
    From the 1830s to the early 1850s Tosa shipped some 600,000 bales of charcoal a year to Kyoto and Osaka, to Edo and to all of Kyushu, the city history of Sukumo (sukumo-charcoal) writes, after a history of Japanese charcoal.
  • What did it sell for?
    670 mon a bale in Chikuzen, in northern Kyushu, by a record of 1836, against 490 mon for the charcoal of Hyuga, 180 mon more. Neither the grade nor the bale's weight is given, nor a price in Tosa, and no price of Tosa's white charcoal in Edo was found.
  • Against a wage?
    No wage of 1836 was found for Chikuzen. The nearest are Edo's, in the 1830s to 1850s: a laborer at 300 mon of copper and a carpenter at 5 or 5.5 monme of silver a day (What did a skilled craftsman earn against a laborer?). Against them a bale was about 2.2 laborer-days, and, at Osaka's market rate of about 105 to 110 mon to the monme in those years (What was copper coin worth in silver?), about 1.1 to 1.3 carpenter-days, this project's arithmetic across places.
