How our maps draw firewood sheds (kigoya)

On our maps about four farmsteads in ten keep their firewood in a wood shed of their own, a building 24 by 12 ft standing a short step off the house; the firewood of the other farms is not drawn. The share and the size are a calibration against the one count we have, of the houses of the village of Kakimochi at the end of the Edo period.

  • How many farmsteads have a wood shed?
    Between 35 and 45 in every 100, the share chosen at random for each hamlet from its seed. The range is set around the Kakimochi count, where 6 households of 16 had a wood shed and one household had two.
  • Which farms are given the wood sheds?
    The larger houses, first. The town history that gives the Kakimochi count also finds that the houses with the most outbuildings had the largest main houses. Giving the wood shed to the larger houses first is this project's reading of that.
  • Every shed is drawn 24 by 12 ft.
    That is 4 by 2 ken, the commonest size in the Kakimochi count, three of its seven sheds. The count's smaller sheds, down to about 9 by 9 ft, are not drawn.
  • Our maps stand the shed behind or beside the house, never in the front yard.
    It stands a ken (about 6 ft) beyond the clear strip kept round every wall of the house, so its edge is about 9.5 ft from the wall. This is a GUESS: no page we read says where on the plot a wood shed stood. The ken is measured from the strip, not from the wall itself, because at a ken from the wall a farmstead in the largest hamlets would have no way through to the lanes.
  • The maps draw no open stack and no stack along the windbreak.
    The open stack under the eaves is found only on a present-day page, and the stack along a farm's windbreak grove only in records of the twentieth century and today, with no date for the custom. The maps draw what is recorded before modern times, so the firewood is drawn in its shed.
