What did a book cost to buy, to borrow and to have copied?

The setting's notes count scribes among the commoner trades, but give no price for a book, for borrowing one or for having one copied. The findings below give what the historical record holds instead: what popular books cost in Edo-period Japan, what a lending library charged and what one copy by hand cost, with late Ming and Qing China beside them.

The cheapest Japanese books found are the popular picture booklets of the late 1700s, the kibyoshi, sold in volumes of five leaves at a few mon each; later, longer storybooks and the long illustrated novels cost their publishers' trade buyers from a couple of hundred mon to 15 monme of silver a set. Most townspeople borrowed rather than bought, from lending libraries that charged a few mon to a monme a loan. No price was found for a volume of a classic, a primer or a reference work in Japan, and no copyist's rate. Buying a book in Japan

  • A kibyoshi was a booklet of five leaves, two or three to a story.
    The kibyoshi, the yellow-covered picture booklets of about 1772 to 1818, were printed on a sheet of paper folded in two, five leaves (ten pages) to a volume, and a story usually ran to two or three volumes.
  • What did a kibyoshi cost?
    Two figures were found, and they do not agree. A magazine's feature gives 6 mon a volume; a bookseller's page drawing on a historian's book on the publisher Tsutaya Juzaburo gives 8 mon a volume, about 24 mon for a story in three. Neither says in which years, and this record does not choose between them. At five leaves a volume, either is between 1.2 and 1.6 mon a leaf, by this project's arithmetic.
  • What did a longer storybook cost the trade in the 1830s?
    The gokan, the longer illustrated storybook that followed the kibyoshi, was sold wholesale in the Tenpo era (1830-1844) at 176 mon for one of 40 leaves, a literary historian finds from the novelist Kyokutei Bakin's letters. That is about 4.4 mon a leaf at wholesale, by this project's arithmetic; what a reader paid at the counter was not found.
  • What did a long illustrated novel cost?
    The yomihon, the long illustrated novels of Bakin and his rivals, cost several monme of silver a set even at the trade price. For the second part of Bakin's novel Matsura Sayohime sekikonroku, 166 leaves with 21 of pictures, Bakin had heard the trade price would be about 12 monme, and its publisher put it out at a net trade price of 15. The same historian finds that a set went for 2 shu (7.5 monme), while new editions of the time were 1 bu (15 monme) or more; these are prices between publishers and booksellers, not what a reader paid.
  • What did a volume of a classic, a primer or a reference work cost?
    No price was found for a volume of the Confucian classics, of a school primer or of a household dictionary in Japan. A booksellers' catalog of titles first printed in 1696, online only as page images, was not read for this question.

Borrowing a book in Japan

  • Most readers borrowed from a lending library.
    Books were dear, so townspeople usually read them borrowed, and in the lifetime of the publisher Tsutaya Juzaburo (1750-1797), himself a lending librarian at first, the fee was 6 to 30 mon a volume, an article citing a literary historian's book on the book trade says, against 16 mon for a bowl of soba noodles.
  • What did a loan cost by the size of the work?
    A literary historian's study of the lending libraries, as another article quotes it, gives a reading fee of 2 fun of silver for a work in one volume, 3 to 4 fun for a work in four or five, and about 1 monme for a work of ten volumes or more, a fun being a tenth of a monme; the quotation gives no years. The same article says most readers kept a book from a month to half a year.

Having a book copied in Japan

  • What did a copyist charge?
    No copyist's rate by the leaf, by the volume or by the day was found for Japan, and no count of the leaves one wrote in a day.
  • What did one fair copy cost in 1846?
    The one figure found lumps the copyist with the illustrator. In 1846 the scholar Kimura Mokuro had a fair copy made, with pictures by the print artist Utagawa Kunisada, of the tenth part of Bakin's 40-leaf novel Shinpen Kinpeibai, and wrote that the copyist's and the illustrator's fees and the rest cost him ten-odd ryo of gold in all. The total took in Bakin's own fee of 8 ryo, and by the historian's reckoning the whole came to no more than 15, so the copyist, the illustrator and the rest together had something between 2 and 7 ryo, by this project's arithmetic; the copyist's share cannot be told apart.

Books in Ming and Qing China

  • Most late Ming books with a marked price sold for a tael or less.
    Of 27 Ming printed books found with their prices marked on them, the dearest was a Bencao (a book of materia medica) in ten volumes at 4.9 taels of silver and the cheapest the household encyclopedia Wanbao quanshu, of more than 30 juan, at 1 qian (a tenth of a tael); 16 of the 27 were a tael or less, a historian of the book trade reports.
  • What did an ordinary volume cost?
    The same historian concludes that in the late Ming and early Qing, unless a book ran to many volumes, carried many fine pictures or was printed on dear paper, it was probably not priced above a tael, and many single volumes sold for only 2 or 3 qian.
  • What did a cheap book cost under the Wanli emperor?
    Books for a wide readership sold for only 6 fen (six hundredths of a tael) a juan in the Wanli reign (1573-1620), one juan of a set and not the whole work, the cheapest of all printed by the publishers of Fujian; the 78 book prices another historian collected average 2 taels, a quarter of them under 1 tael. This is a Chinese translation of his book, in an excerpt.
  • What did a book copied by hand cost?
    In the early Qing catalog of the book collector Mao Yi, none of 17 single-volume hand copies of Ming books cost over 0.4 tael; the historian concludes that a single-volume hand copy cost more than a printed one, but not over 0.4 tael. What its copyist was paid was not found.
  • How much did an imperial copyist write in a day?
    The copyists of the Siku quanshu, the Qianlong emperor's great manuscript library, whose first four sets were finished in 1784, were held to 1,000 characters a man a day and 1.8 million in five years. They were paid no wage and bore their own costs; their reward was an official post, ranked by the characters they had copied, two million for the first class and 1.65 million for the second.
