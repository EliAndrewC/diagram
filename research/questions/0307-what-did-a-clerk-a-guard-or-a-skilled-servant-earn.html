What did a clerk, a guard or a skilled servant earn?

A clerk in one of Japan's great merchant houses lived in, fed and clothed by the house, and was paid a yearly wage in silver that rose with his rank: in the Mitsui house's Kyoto shop, by an estimate for the first half of the 1800s, from 160 monme in his first year to 3,240 as a manager in his sixth, though most of a first-year clerk's was held back by the house rather than paid out. A commoner clerk in a shogunal intendant's office had 20 ryo and rations for five people a year from 1725. A manservant who mostly cooked rice was paid about 3 ryo a year in Edo. In Qing China an official's private secretary was paid from 40 or 50 to about a thousand taels a year, where a teacher of boys had no more than a few tens. A Chinese escort agency kept its escort-masters on, with board and lodging and 4 or 5 taels a month by one undated account; no hired guard's pay was found in Japan. A Qing scribe was paid by the document; a Japanese ward's clerk held a standing post and was paid a wage by the ward.

Clerks in a merchant house

  • How did a clerk's pay rise with his rank?
    In the Mitsui house's Kyoto shop, a clerk in his first year was paid 160 monme of silver a year; an ordinary clerk of middle standing 180 to 190; the senior ranks of ordinary clerks 200, 210, 270 and 330; a group head 650 in his second year, 900 in his third and 1,800 in his sixth; and a manager 1,800 in his first year, 2,100 in his third, 2,700 in his fifth and 3,240 in his sixth. The page that gives the ladder calls it its source's estimate for the first half of the 1800s, taken from a historian's study of the shop by way of a labor newsletter, so it is a third-hand figure with no single year.
  • Was the wage paid out?
    Not all of a first-year clerk's, by the same page's account: a small sum of cash was handed over twice a year, at Bon and at New Year, and the rest was set aside by the house, in name for his retirement and the like, or vanished into its working capital.
  • What came in kind?
    Board, clothing and lodging. An apprentice, from about ten, had nothing else. At 17 or 18 he came of age, took a new name and became a clerk; his master gave him a crested cotton robe and a jacket, and a clerk was usually given a wage on top of his food and clothing.
  • What did a long-serving clerk take with him?
    At the Osaka money-changers Konoike, a clerk who had served from boyhood was granted 500 monme to 1 kan of silver at 22 or 23 and every two or three years after, held by the house at 1% a month, and 200 monme a year more into a second fund without interest; both were handed to him as capital when he set up on his own. These are deferred rewards, not a yearly wage.

Clerks in an intendant's office

  • What did an intendant's clerk earn?
    From 1725, 20 ryo and rations for five people a year, and 30 ryo and rations for five as the chief clerk who ran the office. These clerks of the shogun's intendants were farmers and townsmen skilled in local administration, hired through connections and promoted from scribe, and paid out of the intendant's office costs; how each was treated varied from man to man.
  • How much rice were five rations?
    About 9 koku of brown rice a year: a ration was 5 go a day, 1 koku 8 to (1.8 koku) a year, and five of them are 9 koku by this project's arithmetic.

Secretaries in Qing China

  • What was a magistrate's secretary paid?
    Wang Huizu, who served as one for nearly thirty-four years until 1785, wrote that a teacher of boys' yearly fee was no more than a few tens of taels and a secretary's several times, or several tens of times, as much. Only the secretaries for law and for revenue were paid well; the rest got about 100 taels, some only 40 or 50, and the great posts paid a thousand.
  • What reached his family?
    A fee of 100 taels brought no more than 60 or 70 home, he says, barely enough for a household of eight.

Scribes and writers hired by the job

  • In Qing China, a plaint was written for a fee by the sheet.
    In Nanbu county, in Sichuan, a rule of 1850 allowed only 100 cash a sheet for the writing, and it held until 1897. In Nanhui, in the Daoguang years (1821-1850), the official scribe Gu Zhi charged 100 cash a sheet to fair-copy a plaint, but a silver dollar or 400 to 500 cash a sheet to compose one. In Huizhou, in the Jiaqing years (1796-1820), an official scribe charged 0.5 tael for each plaint he wrote.
  • Could a scribe live on it?
    Barely. The rates the Qing offices set for their scribes were usually 100 to 300 cash a plaint, and a scribe living on them alone would usually live no better than a hired laborer.
  • Who else wrote for a fee?
    Many humbler literate men wrote plaints for others now and then and charged little; part-time writers such as diviners were the lowest-paid of all.
  • A Japanese town ward kept its clerk on.
    From 1721 the wards of Edo had a clerk (kakiyaku) who kept the ward's accounts and did its duties at the watch house, paid a wage by the ward and shop-watch money besides.
  • A petition to the offices was written by the case.
    The litigants' inns (kujiyado) where country people stayed for a lawsuit taught their clients the procedure and wrote the documents to be filed with the offices, and illegal suit-brokers wrote documents and acted for litigants for a fee. How much either charged was not found.
  • Could a scribe be hired in Japan for a day?
    No scribe or copyist hired by the day, and no day's pay for one, was found.

Armed guards on the road

  • How was a Chinese escort agency paid?
    Once a year. Beijing's escort agencies, which carried silver under escort where no bank could send it, were paid their fee each first month for the whole year before, a sum that often came to tens or hundreds of thousands of taels; silver went in packets of 500 taels, dozens or hundreds of them a trip, and the agency made good a loss if it could.
  • What did an agency charge?
    One agency, the Tongxinggong, charged 50 taels for every 10,000 taels of silver it escorted, and more by the distance, and clients contracted for an escort by the year, the season, the month or for a short term, by a 2013 popular-history article that gives no year for either.
  • What was an agency's escort-master paid?
    Board and lodging, 4 or 5 taels of silver a month, and a share of the profits at the year's end, by the same article. He was the agency's man, kept on; it was the agency that a client hired by the trip or the term.
  • What did an escort-master do between trips?
    Guarding houses and compounds was an agency's other main business besides escorting, and when the agencies closed, as the Tongxinggong did in 1914, their escort-masters became full-time house guards.
  • Did a Japanese courier house hire guards for its cash?
    None was found. The courier houses' convoy-leader couriers (saryo bikyaku) carried cash and local goods themselves, and drew robbers: a headman's diary records 900 ryo stolen from the Kyoya house at Kanuma in 1856, and 500 ryo in silver cut from a Shimaya load in Edo in 1860.
  • Were couriers hired by the run?
    Men with other trades were. The same headman, of a village in Kozuke, sent a tinker as courier for 64 mon in 1852, and in 1862 a rice-cake maker who between times worked as a courier was hired by the Eirakuya of the post town for a run to collect 1 ryo.
  • And the village's own courier?
    Kept on all year: the village paid its courier 12 kan of cash a year and 800 mon more, about 2 ryo, in 1850 and 1853.
  • What was a guard paid in Japan?
    No pay of a hired guard of goods or cash on the road was found.

Cooks and maids

  • What did a manservant who cooked earn?
    The Morisada manko, a record of the 1830s to the 1850s, as a blog quotes it, gives a manservant in Kyoto and Osaka 80 or 90 monme of silver a half-year, and in Edo about 3 ryo a year; such menservants mostly cooked the rice, and in Edo the head of the kitchen servants was called the kitchen's head clerk. The Kyoto and Osaka half-year is 160 to 180 monme a year by this project's arithmetic.
  • And a samurai house's servant?
    A samurai house that took on a lowly manservant in 1828 agreed to 3 ryo 2 bu for the year, of which 1 ryo 2 bu was advanced at hiring and the rest due at the year's end, by his guarantors' contract as the same blog quotes it from a historian's study of Edo. A retainer's yearly pay is in Servants in a samurai household.
  • What did a lord's cook earn?
    No stipend or wage of a lord's cook was found.
  • What were the shogun's women servants paid?
    A rice stipend, rations and a cash allowance, by rank. Under the thirteenth shogun, Iesada, a middle-rank lady-in-waiting had 12 koku, rations for four and 40 ryo; a scribe 8 koku, three rations and 25 ryo; a woman of the wardrobe 8 koku, three rations and 20 ryo; a junior attendant 5 koku, two rations and 15 ryo; and the lowest maid 4 koku, one ration and 2 ryo. These are the shogun's own women's quarters.
  • What came to them in kind?
    The cash allowance was, in effect, for clothes and cosmetics; firewood, charcoal and oil were given to all; money for miso and salt was given by rank; and their rations were reckoned at a man's 5 go of rice a day, not the 3 go a samurai house gave its women.
  • And a lady's maid in a lord's or a retainer's house?
    No wage was found for a lady's maid in a daimyo's or a retainer's household.
  • And a samurai house in a provincial castle town?
    No readable source was found: a search for the yearly pay of the servants and young steward of a retainer's house in Kanazawa found none.
