What did a clerk, a guard or a skilled servant earn?

A clerk in one of Japan's great merchant houses lived in, fed and clothed by the house, and was paid a yearly wage in silver that rose with his rank: in the Mitsui house's Kyoto shop, by an estimate for the first half of the 1800s, from 160 monme in his first year to 3,240 as a manager in his sixth, though most of a first-year clerk's was held back by the house rather than paid out. A commoner clerk in a shogunal intendant's office had 20 ryo and rations for five people a year from 1725. A manservant who mostly cooked rice was paid about 3 ryo a year in Edo. In Qing China an official's private secretary was paid from 40 or 50 to about a thousand taels a year, many times a village schoolteacher. No hired guard's pay was found, in Japan or in China.

Clerks in a merchant house

  • How did a clerk's pay rise with his rank?
    In the Mitsui house's Kyoto shop, its great draper, a clerk in his first year was paid 160 monme of silver a year; an ordinary clerk of middle standing 180 to 190; the senior ranks of ordinary clerks 200, 210, 270 and 330; a group head 650 in his second year, 900 in his third and 1,800 in his sixth; and a manager 1,800 in his first year, 2,100 in his third, 2,700 in his fifth and 3,240 in his sixth. The page that gives the ladder calls it its source's estimate for the first half of the 1800s, taken from a historian's study of the shop by way of a labor newsletter, so it is a third-hand figure with no single year.
  • Was the wage paid out?
    Not all of a first-year clerk's, by the same page's account: a small sum of cash was handed over twice a year, at Bon and at New Year, and the rest was set aside by the house, in name for his retirement and the like, or vanished into its working capital.
  • What came in kind?
    Board, clothing and lodging. An apprentice, from about ten, had nothing else. At 17 or 18 he came of age, took a new name and became a clerk; his master gave him a crested cotton robe and a jacket, and a clerk was usually given a wage on top of his food and clothing.
  • What did a long-serving clerk take with him?
    At the Osaka money-changers Konoike, a clerk who had served from boyhood was granted 500 monme to 1 kan of silver at 22 or 23 and every two or three years after, held by the house at 1% a month, and 200 monme a year more into a second fund without interest; both were handed to him as capital when he set up on his own. These are deferred rewards, not a yearly wage.
  • What did a scribe hired by the day earn?
    No day's pay for a hired scribe or copyist was found.

Clerks in an intendant's office

  • What did an intendant's clerk earn?
    From 1725, 20 ryo and rations for five people a year, and 30 ryo and rations for five as the chief clerk who ran the office. These clerks of the shogun's intendants were farmers and townsmen skilled in local administration, hired through connections and promoted from scribe, and paid out of the intendant's office costs; how each was treated varied from man to man.
  • How much rice were five rations?
    About 9 koku of brown rice a year: a ration was 5 go a day, 1 koku 8 to a year, and five of them are 9 koku by this project's arithmetic.

Secretaries in Qing China

  • What was a magistrate's secretary paid?
    Wang Huizu, who served as one for nearly thirty-four years until 1785, wrote that a village schoolteacher's yearly fee was no more than a few tens of taels and a secretary's several times, or several tens of times, as much. Only the secretaries for law and for revenue were paid well; the rest got about 100 taels, some only 40 or 50, and the great posts paid a thousand.
  • What reached his family?
    A fee of 100 taels brought no more than 60 or 70 home, he says, barely enough for a household of eight.

Armed guards on the road

  • How was a Chinese escort agency paid?
    Once a year. Beijing's escort agencies, which carried silver under armed guard where no bank could send it, were paid their fee each first month for the whole year before, a sum that often came to tens or hundreds of thousands of taels; silver went in packets of 500 taels, dozens or hundreds of them a trip, and the agency made good a loss if it could. The article that says so gives no rate per trip or per tael carried, and no year.
  • What was a guard paid?
    No wage of an agency's guards, and no pay of a hired guard of goods or cash in Japan, was found.

Cooks and maids

  • What did a manservant who cooked earn?
    The Morisada manko, a record of the 1830s to the 1850s, as a blog quotes it, gives a manservant in Kyoto and Osaka 80 or 90 monme of silver a half-year, and in Edo about 3 ryo a year; such menservants mostly cooked the rice, and in Edo the head of the kitchen servants was called the kitchen's head clerk. The Kyoto and Osaka half-year is 160 to 180 monme a year by this project's arithmetic.
  • And a samurai house's servant?
    A samurai house that took on a lowly manservant in 1828 agreed to 3 ryo 2 bu for the year, of which 1 ryo 2 bu was advanced at hiring and the rest due at the year's end, by his guarantors' contract as the same blog quotes it from a historian's study of Edo. A retainer's yearly pay is in Servants in a samurai household.
  • What did a lord's cook earn?
    No stipend or wage of a lord's cook was found.
  • What were the shogun's women servants paid?
    A rice stipend, rations and a cash allowance, by rank. Under the thirteenth shogun, Iesada (1853-1858), a senior lady-in-waiting had 12 koku, rations for four and 40 ryo; a scribe 8 koku, three rations and 25 ryo; a woman of the wardrobe 8 koku, three rations and 20 ryo; a junior attendant 5 koku, two rations and 15 ryo; and the lowest maid 4 koku, one ration and 2 ryo. These are the shogun's own women's quarters, the richest household in the realm.
  • What came to them in kind?
    The cash allowance was, in effect, for clothes and cosmetics; firewood, charcoal and oil were given to all; money for miso and salt was given by rank; and their rations were reckoned at a man's 5 go of rice a day, not the 3 go a samurai house gave its women.
  • And a lady's maid in a lord's or a retainer's house?
    No wage was found for a lady's maid in a daimyo's or a retainer's household.
  • What did a Kanazawa samurai house pay its servants and steward?
    No readable source was found for the yearly pay of a Kanazawa retainer's servants or of a young steward.
