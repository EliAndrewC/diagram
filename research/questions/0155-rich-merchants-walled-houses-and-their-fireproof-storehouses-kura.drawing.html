How our maps draw rich merchants' walled houses and their fireproof storehouses (kura)

A provincial city on our maps draws one to three walled merchant compounds. The wall is a right the daimyo grants to a particular family, not something money buys, so the city's other very rich merchants live in large unwalled houses. Our maps set each compound as a walled court in the core of a block, with the merchant's house inside it, on dry ground of the merchant's own: never in water, across a street or round the town's fire tower.

The great merchant houses that survive stand differently: at the street front, with a wall round the sides and back and three to five fireproof storehouses (kura) behind. The rule at the end of this section is written from them; our maps do not draw it yet.

  • Only a family the daimyo has granted the right has a walled compound.
    A city of about 3,000 in this setting holds a dozen or so very rich merchant families, and most of them could pay for a wall and a gate. A gated compound is a right the daimyo grants a particular family, of a piece with the other privileges a merchant may be granted: an audience with the daimyo at the New Year's celebration, a surname carried down the generations without being samurai. So the wall marks legal standing rather than money, and a very rich merchant without the grant keeps a large unwalled house like anyone else's, only bigger.
  • The grant is modeled on the historical gatehouse, which went with rank and office.
    The gatehouse with rooms either side was laid down by a warrior house's rank, and allowed to commoners only where they held office, as village officials or town elders. Its commoner form had no guard post, and its side rooms held men, attendants, sheds and stables, as a merchant compound's gate range would.
  • That money cannot buy the right to a wall is a deviation.
    A commoner's right to a surname and a sword was also a lord's grant, and from the middle of the Edo period the domains granted the surname for money contributions. It stayed the lord's favor, which the setting keeps; that no amount of money buys it is this setting's own departure from the later record. No page we read attaches the money to a wall or a gate.
  • A city draws one, two or three compounds, chosen at random.
    The count is a knob set by the map's seed: one 30% of the time, two 40%, three 30%. The shares are this project's choice; no source gives how many families in a city held the grant. A count left to chance keeps two cities honestly different from each other.
  • A town's compounds are placed by hand.
    Nothing about them is left to chance.
  • A compound stands in the core of a block - a deviation.
    It is set well inside the rampart, clear of the moat and off the temple neighborhood, rather than jammed against the rampart. The surviving great merchant houses are shop-houses at the street edge with their storehouses behind and a wall round the rest of the lot, so the court set back in a block is the map's placement, not the record's.
  • A compound's court keeps clear of everything else.
    The whole court, not just the house inside it, is kept clear when the map places it, so it overlaps no temple, government office, house or other court.
  • How large is a compound's court?
    About 230 by 170 ft, with a light wall about 2 ft thick and a cart gate about 10 ft wide. The court's size is a GUESS; the attested sites run from about 98 by 171 ft to a frontage of about 200 ft.

Where the wall may stand follows from what the ground around a compound is for. Water and fire towers are placed on the map before the compounds, in towns and cities both, so a compound's wall is fitted round them.

  • A compound's wall never stands in water.
    The whole perimeter, the gate gap included, stands clear of canal, dock basin, moat, river and pond, though it may come up to the quay. The reasoning is this project's own: a wall footed in water is undermined, and a canal or dock edge is working waterfront - the boats, the porters and the towpath that make the merchant rich.
  • A compound's wall never encloses a fire tower.
    The fire watch was the town's: every town kept a watch post manned around the clock. That its tower therefore stands on public ground, reached by a public way, never built into a private wall or shut inside a private court, is this project's reasoning, not the source's.
  • A compound's wall may line a street but never stand in it.
    A street, an alley or a road is the public way, so the wall keeps off its width and may run alongside it.
  • A compound's gate opens onto open ground.
    It never opens into the face of the building next door: a wall that abuts a temple turns its gate to the free side.
  • A compound's wall stays on the merchant's own lot, short of the drain at the back of the block.
    A great merchant's house in Kyoto has a high wall round its whole estate, and a castle town merchant's lot runs back to the block's shared drain, which is also the ward's boundary; that the wall stands on the merchant's own lot, inside that line, is this project's reading.

The great merchant houses that survive, the Sugimoto house in Kyoto and the Hasegawa house in the castle town of Matsusaka, stand on the street, not in a court. The rule below is written from them rather than from the court above; our maps do not draw it yet.

The rule our maps are to follow for a rich merchant's house and its storehouses:

  • The house fronts a commercial street.
    Its main house, about 100 ft along the street and 50 ft deep as the Hasegawa house's is, stands at the street edge with the shop at the front, and the lot runs back about 170 ft to the back of the block. There is no gatehouse and no court set back from the street.
  • How wide is a rich merchant's street frontage?
    From about 70 to 200 ft, chosen at random for each estate anywhere in that range ; both ends are accurate, from the two surviving houses. The 70 ft is a Kyoto guide's figure for the Sugimoto house, which a second page gives as about 98 ft; the 200 ft is the Hasegawa house's.
  • A wall runs round the sides and back of the lot.
    As the Sugimoto house in Kyoto has.
  • How many storehouses stand behind the house?
    Three or four, at the back of the lot in a row or a hook: the Sugimoto house has three in a hook, the Hasegawa house four side by side.
  • Some estates have one more storehouse at the street front, beside the entrance.
    The Hasegawa house has one there and the Sugimoto house none, so whether an estate has one is a knob, chosen at random for each estate.
  • Each storehouse is 14 to 20 ft square, the size of the Kyoto house's three.
    That is accurate: the Sugimoto house's three storehouses run from 14 to 20 ft.
  • The storehouses stand close together.
    The spacing is a GUESS, since no source gives one.
  • Who on a street has storehouses?
    A walled merchant estate carries three to five, a large unwalled merchant house one or two, and an ordinary shop-house none. This is a GUESS: an ordinary provincial street most likely held fewer storehouses than Osaka did in 1724, the one premodern town count we have, and no page we read says how storehouses were shared along one.
  • How many storehouses does a city hold?
    Roughly four for each walled estate plus one or two for each large merchant house, and never more than about one to every ten houses of the city, or every 35 to 55 households. That cap is a calibration to Osaka's counts of 1724, the one premodern town figure, on which the two accounts give the same share of storehouses to houses and differ for households. Kitakata's some 4,000 storehouses are not used, being a count of today, after storehouses spread to ordinary houses following the fire of 1880, more than a decade after the Edo period ended in 1868.
