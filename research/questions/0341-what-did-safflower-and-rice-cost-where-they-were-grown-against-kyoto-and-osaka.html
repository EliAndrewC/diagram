What did safflower and rice cost where they were grown, against Kyoto and Osaka?

In Rokugan a tariff of typically about 15% of the declared sales value is charged at the gate of the city where goods are sold, and the setting's price ledger reckons a good sold far from where it was made at its price there plus freight, a 15% tariff for each border and a merchant's margin; the Sparrow's tea, the setting says, can always be sold to raise the money for the food they cannot grow. The findings below give what the historical record of Edo-period Japan (1603 to 1868) holds for two goods whose price is known at both ends of their road: safflower, a dyestuff, and rice.

Safflower, the red dyestuff of the Yamagata basin, sold in Kyoto for about a fifth to a third more than it had cost its shipper in most of the seven years for which one house's papers give both sums, and in one of them for less than it cost. Where that cost was a landed one it already held the freight, which was small, about 2% of it; the Kyoto wholesalers took 18% of the sale in 1738 and 1% in 1822, and what was left between the landed cost and the sale after their deductions was the shipper's profit or loss, this project's reading. Rice in Fukui stood a tenth to a fifth below Osaka's from the 1780s to the 1830s. Raw silk and iron are in What did raw silk and iron cost where they were made, against the city that used them?, and sugar and tea in What did sugar and tea cost where they were made, against Osaka and Edo?

Safflower: Yamagata against Kyoto

  • What did Yamagata's safflower cost its shipper, against its sale in Kyoto?
    The Aida house, outside Yamagata, sent safflower to the safflower wholesalers of Kyoto, about 545 km (~339 mi) away in a straight line. Its papers, as Moriya Madoka (moriya-benibana-aidake-1) tabulates them, give the lot's cost and its sale for seven years. In 1737 the lot was 615 ryo landed and sold for 820; in 1738, 1,620 and 1,940; in 1742, 855 and 1,083; in 1743, 981 and 1,020; in 1747, 2,034 ryo at purchase and a sale of a little over 2,441 ryo 3 bu; in 1759, 3,216 and a sale of a little over 2,963 ryo 1 bu, a loss. The sale was thus 1.33, 1.20, 1.27, 1.04, 1.20 and 0.92 times the cost, this project's arithmetic.
  • The cost is not always the price in Yamagata.
    The table keeps the documents' own words: a landed cost in 1737, a purchase cost in 1747, and plain "price" in the other years; the article describes the table's cost as the total, with the purchase in the producing district and the charges of shipping in it. A seventh entry, six loads of 1763 entered at 250 ryo and sold for a little over 265 ryo 1 bu, is 1.06 times if the first sum is its cost, this project's reading.
  • How did the safflower travel?
    By river and sea, then on to Otsu. The house's lot of 1736, twenty-six loads, went down the Mogami river from the landing at Oishida, by way of Tsuruga to Otsu in Omi, a man sent by the house riding with one shipment of it to watch the cargo at sea and coming home by land, as the second part of Moriya's article (moriya-benibana-aidake-2) tells it. A graduation thesis on the route (yamagata-benibana-yuso-keiro-sotsuron) reckons that this way, by Tsuruga and Lake Biwa, took about half the days of the other, round by the western sea route to Osaka, for which it allows about two months from the port of Sakata: its own inference from a rice ship's log, not a record of a safflower cargo. No source read gives the days a lot of safflower was on the way.
  • What did a "landed" cost include?
    The purchase and every charge of shipping. The shippers entered for each load a landed cost: the total laid out before the sale, shown by the load and ordinarily as of its arrival in Kyoto, though the Aida house, which sold from Otsu, reckoned it there. Iwata Kotaro (iwata-2003-shohin-ryutsu-chakune) defines the Kyoto-landed cost the same way, as the net cost with the freight to Kyoto in it. So where a year's cost is a landed one, the gap between it and the sale holds no freight: it is the wholesalers' deductions and the shipper's profit or loss, this project's reading.
  • What did one load cost landed, and sell for, in 1737?
    Most marks of that year's lot - the parts of a lot a house shipped each under a name of its own, entered and sold at its own price - were entered at 20 to 21 ryo of the old gold coin a load landed at Otsu, and the two dearest at 35 and 37 ryo 2 bu; the loads sold on credit went at 2 kan 750 monme to 4 kan 300 monme of silver a load, and one mark at 50 ryo of the new gold. The two columns are in different money, the old gold coin against silver and the new gold of 1736, so no ratio is drawn load by load; on the whole lot the house itself reckoned 615 ryo landed against 820 sold, both in old gold.
  • What did the freight come to?
    Little. The freight sent with the waybills of 1737 was 1 ryo 3 bu to 2 ryo 3 bu of old gold for each group of four or five loads, about half a ryo a load and some 2 to 3% of a load's landed cost, this project's arithmetic; the article does not say whether that was the whole cost of carriage. By a waybill of 1763 the freight from Oishida to Kyoto was 3 bu and 503 mon a load, the thesis reckons, about 2% of what the Aida house entered for a load that year, this project's arithmetic.
  • What did the Kyoto wholesalers take?
    In 1738, 18% of the sale. That year's thirty loads, all but two marks and the waste flowers, reckoned apart, sold for 101 kan 925 monme of silver, from which the wholesalers took a discount of 15% and a commission of 3%, 18 kan 344.7 monme, leaving the shipper 83 kan 580.3 monme. The shippers complained to the Kyoto magistrate that the commission had been 30 monme of the old silver a load and had lately been raised to 3% of a load's price. The safflower invoices show a commission of 1 or 1.5%, the thesis says, and the invoices of 1822 below carry 1%.
  • Do the house's own totals for 1738 fit that reading?
    In kind, but not to the ryo. The memorandum closes with the shipper's whole take in gold, 1,747 ryo 1 bu and 7.9 monme: the silver left on all the marks after the deductions, turned into gold at 56.6 monme to the ryo, with two loads sold for gold. The 1,620 ryo of the table above was the cost of the whole lot sent up, thirty-two loads and three small bales of waste flowers, two loads of which were lost in a wreck; the lot ended in profit, the article says, though the house bore heavy losses on it, a wholesaler's failure among them. So the take was about 127 ryo over the cost, 1.08 times it, where the table's sale of 1,940 ryo is 1.20 times. The deductions the memorandum shows come to about 330 ryo at its rate, more than the 193 ryo between the take and that sale, and its sales before any deduction to about 2,077 ryo: the table's 1,940 is neither figure, all of it this project's arithmetic, and the article does not say how the two were reckoned, though its table enters the 320 ryo between them as new gold. For 1738, then, the gap between cost and sale did hold the wholesalers' deductions and the shipper's remainder, but the three totals do not close.
  • What did another house's loads cost landed, against their sale, in 1822?
    The Horigome house's lots of 1822 that had a sale, as Iwata tabulates them from its account books, were entered at 38.5 to 47 ryo a load landed in Kyoto, and those sold that year were invoiced at 39.5 to 53 ryo, the commission of 1% still to come off: 0.99 to 1.24 times the landed cost, this project's arithmetic. On those lots the house cleared 10.3% of its cost by the table's own reckoning.
  • An asking price is not a sale.
    For five bales under its mark Tobikiri, landed at 44 ryo 2 bu a load, the house's agent asked 50 ryo or more. They did not sell until 1823, and then at 32 ryo, a loss of 28.8% of their cost; with them the year's profit comes to 7.0%.
  • What is that in silver by weight?
    A load was by rule four bales of sixteen bags of 500 monme each, 32 kan or 120 kg (~265 lb), though bales of seventeen and eighteen bags were also sent, the thesis says, and Iwata's table reckons every price to a load of sixty-four bags. At that load, and the silver in the coin of each date (How much silver was a monme, a ryo and a bu, coinage by coinage?), the credit sales of 1737, if their silver was the new coin of 1736, which the table does not say, were about 40 to 62 g of silver for a kilogram of safflower in Kyoto, most of them 40 to 51 g; in 1822 a kilogram was about 28 to 43 g landed and 28 to 48 g as invoiced, the range coming mostly from the two silver coinages then passing, this project's arithmetic. The landed cost of 1737 is in old gold, for which no rate in silver is in the record; by the house's own totals it was three quarters of the sale.

Rice: Fukui against Osaka

  • How did rice in Fukui stand against Osaka?
    From the 1780s to the 1830s Fukui's market price of rice was generally 10 to 20% below Osaka's, by Kusano Masahiro's comparison of the two cities' year-end prices in silver (kusano-1996-kinsei-shijo-keizai): Osaka at about 1.1 to 1.25 times Fukui, this project's arithmetic, across about 165 km (~103 mi) in a straight line. At Katsuyama rice in 1781 was provisionally about 82% of Osaka's price.
  • From the 1840s Fukui's rice looks dearer than Osaka's, but only in silver.
    Fukui's price then stood some 40 to 80% above Osaka's in silver; reckoned in gold at each city's own rate it stayed below, and Kusano puts the difference down to Fukui's prices being in the domain's depreciating paper money.
