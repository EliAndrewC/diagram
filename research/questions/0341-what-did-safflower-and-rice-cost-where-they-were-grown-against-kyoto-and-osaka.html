What did safflower and rice cost where they were grown, against Kyoto and Osaka?

In Rokugan a tariff of typically about 15% of the declared sales value is charged at the gate of the city where goods are sold, and the setting's price ledger reckons a good sold far from where it was made at its price there plus freight, a 15% tariff for each border and a merchant's margin; the Sparrow's tea, the setting says, can always be sold to raise the money for the food they cannot grow. The findings below give what the historical record of Edo-period Japan (1603 to 1868) holds for two goods whose price is known at both ends of their road: safflower, a dyestuff, and rice.

Safflower, the red dyestuff of the Yamagata basin, sold in Kyoto for about a fifth to a third more than it had cost its shipper in most of the years one house recorded, and in one year for less than it cost. Rice in Fukui stood a tenth to a fifth below Osaka's from the 1780s to the 1830s. Raw silk and iron are in What did raw silk and iron cost where they were made, against the city that used them?, and sugar and tea in What did sugar and tea cost where they were made, against Osaka and Edo?

Safflower: Yamagata against Kyoto

  • What did Yamagata's safflower cost its shipper, against its sale in Kyoto?
    The Aida house, outside Yamagata, sent safflower to the safflower wholesalers of Kyoto, about 545 km (~339 mi) away in a straight line. Its papers, as Moriya Madoka (moriya-benibana-aidake-1) tabulates them, give the lot's cost and its sale for seven years. In 1737 the lot was 615 ryo landed and sold for 820; in 1738, 1,620 and 1,940; in 1742, 855 and 1,083; in 1743, 981 and 1,020; in 1747, 2,034 ryo at purchase and a sale of a little over 2,441 ryo 3 bu; in 1759, 3,216 and a sale of a little over 2,963 ryo 1 bu, a loss. The sale was thus 1.33, 1.20, 1.27, 1.04, 1.20 and 0.92 times the cost, this project's arithmetic.
  • The cost is not always the price in Yamagata.
    The table keeps the documents' own words: a landed cost in 1737, a purchase cost in 1747, and plain "price" in the other years, which the article says is either the purchase in the producing district or the total with the charges of shipping. A seventh entry, six loads of 1763 entered at 250 ryo and sold for a little over 265 ryo 1 bu, is 1.06 times if the first sum is its cost, this project's reading. The article does not give the weight of a load or the route, so no price by weight follows.

Rice: Fukui against Osaka

  • How did rice in Fukui stand against Osaka?
    From the 1780s to the 1830s Fukui's market price of rice was generally 10 to 20% below Osaka's, by Kusano Masahiro's comparison of the two cities' year-end prices in silver (kusano-1996-kinsei-shijo-keizai): Osaka at about 1.1 to 1.25 times Fukui, this project's arithmetic, across about 165 km (~103 mi) in a straight line. At Katsuyama rice in 1781 was provisionally about 82% of Osaka's price.
  • From the 1840s Fukui's rice looks dearer than Osaka's, but only in silver.
    Fukui's price then stood some 40 to 80% above Osaka's in silver; reckoned in gold at each city's own rate it stayed below, and Kusano puts the difference down to Fukui's prices being in the domain's depreciating paper money.
