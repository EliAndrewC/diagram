How our maps draw mulberry and other crops on pond dikes (sangji, guoji)

On our maps every dike of a dike-pond hamlet is planted, and all of them with one crop, chosen at random for each hamlet: mulberry, fruit or tea, the three found on pond dikes before modern times. Each is drawn in a form of its own, so a reader can tell them apart at a glance: mulberry as rows of round bushes, tea as clipped hedges, fruit as a single line of trees. Sugarcane, banana and vegetable dikes are not drawn.

  • One crop to a hamlet is this project's choice.
    Qu Dajun, who described Guangdong in 1678, names the fruit trees, tea and mulberry of the villages' dikes together, and a modern magazine has mulberry and fruit trees on the same dikes; only the gazetteer office's modern succession of dike types gives a place one type at a time (Mulberry and other crops on pond dikes (sangji, guoji)). The maps draw one crop to a hamlet so that each hamlet reads as one kind of dike-pond village.
  • How many hamlets plant each crop?
    Half the hamlets plant mulberry, a third fruit and a sixth tea, a GUESS: mulberry is weighted as the commonest because it became the dominant dike of the Qing, fruit next as the oldest, and tea least, since it is named only in a list (Mulberry and other crops on pond dikes (sangji, guoji)).
  • Kuwabata, the first of our dike-pond hamlets, plants mulberry.
    Its crop is set rather than chosen at random, so the first hamlet shows the mulberry dike, the commonest of the Qing, whose leaves fed silkworms (Mulberry and other crops on pond dikes (sangji, guoji)).
  • No sugarcane, banana or vegetable dikes are drawn.
    They are found only in modern accounts: the cane dike in one modern listing that dates it to no period, the banana and vegetable dikes in nothing earlier than the modern surveys, and this project draws nothing found only in modern times (Mulberry and other crops on pond dikes (sangji, guoji)).

Each crop is drawn in its own form. How the mulberry rows and crowns are laid out on each bank is at How our maps draw dike-ponds (sangji yutang).

  • Mulberry is drawn at the late-Qing spacing, about one bush to 24 sq ft.
    How thickly mulberry stood depended on how low it was cut, so there is no single right density; the maps take one figure from the range and say which. It is the late-Qing Yangtze delta's mid-trunk planting, about 300 trees a mu, the only figure dated before modern times, and it also falls inside Japan's modern mid-cut range (Mulberry and other crops on pond dikes (sangji, guoji)).
  • The Pearl River delta's far denser root-cut mulberry is not drawn.
    That planting, about a bush to the square foot, is given with no date, so nothing places it before modern times. It would be some 25 to 32 times as dense as the drawn rows (Mulberry and other crops on pond dikes (sangji, guoji)).
  • Each mulberry bush is drawn 4.4 to 7 ft across.
    The width is a GUESS: no page we read gives a bush's crown width.
  • Tea is drawn as two clipped hedges along the bank.
    Each hedge is about 3 ft wide and broken every 7.5 ft or so, so it reads as bushes set end to end rather than as a solid band. The hedge's width, its breaks and the two rows are a GUESS from the tea bush's habit: Qu Dajun names tea on the dikes but says nothing of how it stood (Mulberry and other crops on pond dikes (sangji, guoji)).
  • How far apart are the fruit trees in their single line along the middle of the bank?
    About 18 ft, a GUESS: the one figure found is for a field turned to orchard, about one lychee to 300 sq ft, which is about 17 ft between trees on a square, by this project's arithmetic, and Qu Dajun gives none along a dike (Mulberry and other crops on pond dikes (sangji, guoji)).
  • Each fruit tree's crown is drawn 8 to 10 ft across.
    The width is a GUESS of this project's: no page we read gives the crown of a lychee, longan or orange tree on a dike.
