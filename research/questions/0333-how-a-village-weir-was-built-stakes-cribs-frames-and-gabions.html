How a village weir was built: stakes, cribs, frames and gabions

The first weirs (seki) were built of whatever wood and stone lay near, too fragile to stand anywhere but on small streams. They were fences of stakes woven with brushwood or bamboo, or frames of timber packed with stone. Whether a village's intake needed a weir at all, and where on the bank it stood, is at Where the ditch leaves the brook: the intake and its weir (toshuko and seki).

A weir on small water was built of what lay to hand.

  • The first weirs could stand only on small streams.
    Held back with nearby wood and stone, they were fragile, so they were built only on mountain streams with small flow or on small tributaries.
  • Weirs on small mountain streams are more than two thousand years old.
    The prototype of the weir taking a small mountain stream into paddy is placed in the Yayoi period, so weirs have been built for over two thousand years. In the mountains, small streams are dammed and their water led off through small canals, usually for short distances and on gentle slopes.
  • The oldest weirs dug up in Japan are rows of stakes.
    The oldest is stakes and sheet piles set in a channel at the Itazuke site, from the end of the Jomon period or the start of the Yayoi, and the Kodera weir of the Kofun period is stakes and crosspieces in three tiers, 4.2 m (~14 ft) wide. At Kodera the stakes were driven at a slant, logs laid along them and more stakes driven to hold the logs, some joints tied with wisteria vine and the gaps packed with clay and gravel.
  • The woven stake fence across a river was known by the eighth century.
    The shigarami is a row of stakes driven into a river with brushwood or bamboo twined onto them from both sides, to hold back the flow. The word's earliest written examples are a poem of the eighth-century Man'yoshu, which imagines one laid across the Asuka River to dam it, and the Heike monogatari of the early thirteenth century, where one is heaped up as a work of war. A history of Japanese irrigation reads the fence of another Man'yoshu poem as stakes with wood or bamboo laid across, the same build as a weir excavated at the Kodera site - a reading that is the history's own.
  • Was an irrigation weir of the early Edo period woven onto stakes?
    The Kawaharazono weir at Kanoya, in Kagoshima, built about 1638 to take farm water, is said by local tradition to have been at first either a stone weir or plants woven onto a foundation of wooden stakes. Its brushwood is still laid anew every March, though the weir has stood on a concrete foundation since 1950.
  • Were stake weirs woven with reed before Japan began to modernize in 1868?
    A weir of driven stakes with reed (kaya) woven between them - the grass weir - is still used today at Chiyoda, in Saga, where an agreement with the users downstream rules out a permanent weir. A Japanese encyclopedia, after an older work it names, divides the Edo-period weirs, almost all built for farm water, into grass weirs and washing weirs, without saying what either was made of. The Kofun-period weir at the Kodera site held its clay packing down with woven grass, though not woven between its stakes. What the premodern record weaves into the stakes is brushwood and bamboo; a weave of reed we found only in the present-day Saga weir.
  • Before modern materials, weirs were built of timber frames, gabions and brushwood.
    They were used singly, or combined and covered with earth and gravel. The Tatai weir on the Takahashi River, built in 1183, was of timber frames packed with stone, an oblique weir about 230 m (~755 ft) long closing the whole river; the upper frame at its deep channel was taken out after each irrigation season and set in anew each year.
  • Until the late sixteenth century most intake works were small cribs of timber packed with stone.
    A Japanese engineering lecture on head works says intake works up to the end of the Sengoku period, in the late sixteenth century, were mostly small, of timber frames packed with stone.
  • Many old weirs ran obliquely upstream from the intake mouth.
    An oblique weir, "extended long in the diagonally upstream direction from the intake mouth", dams the shallow riffle rather than the deep pool, and stands off the fastest water in a flood, so it is hard to destroy.
  • Some flood-dividing weirs of the Edo period were timber frames weighted with stone.
    Diversion weirs of the Edo period were made of timber frames, of stone, of kaya-haguchi, a bank-protection work named for its reed, and the like, a frame being timber joined with mortise and tenon into a square, set in the river and fixed by stones thrown in from above. These were built to do their work in flood, not to take the ordinary water as an intake weir does, but the build is the crib of timber and stone the old village intakes were made of.
  • A gabion is a long basket of stones about 40 to 60 cm (~16-24 in) across.
    It is "a cylindrical basket woven of bamboo, wire or the like, packed with cobbles or rubble", mostly about 40-60 cm (~16-24 in) in diameter and 4-10 m (~13-33 ft) long. The word is in the Japanese-Portuguese dictionary of 1603-04.
  • Were gabions laid across small streams?
    The sources give them for bank protection, for groynes and as weights on river frames, and the only gabions we found damming water were on large rivers.
  • How thick was a village weir?
    No source we read gives the thickness or cross-section of a village weir; the only dimensions found are of river works, frames many meters long. The forms fix a range. A woven stake fence is as thick as its one row of stakes, and a single course of gabions, if one was ever laid on small water (a guess), one basket, about 1.3 to 2 ft. A crib is as thick as its frame, which at a village's scale is not recorded.
