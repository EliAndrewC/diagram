The office hall and its clerks (goyakusho)

The office hall was the working building of an intendant's office (jin'ya), the office where its public business was carried on. At the Takayama Jin'ya, which governed Hida province for the shogunate, the office's main rooms, among them the examination room where hearings were held, were rebuilt in 1816, on this project's reading as one block. Its floor came to about 8,100 sq ft, while a small branch office's whole central building was about 2,850 sq ft. A county magistrate's office hall, the kind this setting's counties keep, falls between the two, on this project's reading.

In Japan the clerks called tedai were chosen from the peasants and townsmen versed in rural administration, and at Takayama the officials hired mostly from the district, the jiyakunin, worked in a room partitioned from that of the officials sent by the shogunate. A Chinese county office worked through six chambers, each with an authorized staff of two or three men. This setting's clerks are three or four heimen scribes who live in town.

  • The Takayama intendant's office, which governed a whole province, had a floor of about 8,100 sq ft.
    Its office block, the goyakusho, measures 754.75 m² (~8,124 sq ft). It dates from 1816, in the Edo period, the year the office's entrance hall, examination room, working office and great hall were rebuilt; this project reads them as that one block. The hearing room inside it is at The hearing court (shirasu).
  • A small branch office, at Joge, had a central building of about 80 tsubo, some 2,850 sq ft.
    The Joge branch office was an outlying office set up away from the intendant's own, to which three officials came at the end of the shogunate. A plan drawn in 1869, soon after the shogunate fell, shows its buildings roughly as they stood at its end.
  • How big was a county magistrate's office hall?
    A county magistrate governs one county of this setting. On this project's reading its office hall lay between the two measured offices: larger than a three-man branch office and far smaller than an office governing a whole province. Neither source, nor any other page we read, gives an intendant's or a county office's hall a length or depth - only a floor area. The one hall measured both ways is of another kind: the council hall (評定所, hyojosho) the Sayama domain built at its jin'ya in 1775, 2 ken across the beams and 10.5 ken long, about 12 by 63 ft - a domain's seat, not an intendant's office.
  • Japan's tedai were chosen from among peasants and townsmen.
    The tedai were chosen from the peasants and townsmen versed in rural administration, with the shogunate's finance office's formal permission. Most were the children of village and town officials, hired first as scribes and promoted to tedai and then to chief tedai.
  • At the Takayama intendant's office, the clerks worked in rooms of the office hall.
    Beside the room where the officials sent from the shogunate worked, and partitioned from it, was the goyoba, the workroom of the officials hired mostly from the district. A room used only for writing, the kakiyaku-beya, was where the documents sent to the shogunate were drawn up, and they were bound in the room beside it.
  • At Takayama, what was bound in the binding room went to a ledger storehouse beside it.
    The jin'ya's own guide calls the binding room, the chotojiba, a workroom used only for drawing up and binding official documents and ledgers: the tax ledgers, the papers the villages and towns sent in, and copies of what went up to the shogunate. What was made there was kept in the ledger storehouse beside it, of which only remains are left. The guide gives neither room a size.
  • Who worked in the writing room?
    Takayama's guide says officials it calls shoyaku drew up documents there for the shogunate and the records of examinations, and that they were taken on as apprentices in the office's work, mostly officials' sons, such as a tedai's. A Japanese encyclopedia reads the same word as kakiyaku: most tedai were the children of village and town officials, hired first as kakiyaku before they rose to tedai. At the rebuilt Iijima Jin'ya, the museum says the office's apprentices kept night duty in the writing room while they trained, and a visitor's blog says that the intendant hired locals who could read and write - very likely the same junior clerks, seen from another side.
  • How many worked in the writing room?
    No page we read says.
  • Did a small office have a writing room or a binding room?
    No period source we read gives a small office either room. The 1869 drawing of the branch office at Joge marks its rooms with mat counts and names none of them a writing or binding room, though that is the silence of the drawing as the pamphlet reproduces it, not proof the rooms were missing. A writing room appears at a small office only in the Iijima Jin'ya, rebuilt in 1993 in its appearance at the end of the shogunate, where it is the first room in from the step and the entrance and stands beside the court room. That is a modern reconstruction, and the museum's page gives the room no size. A binding room appears nowhere but at Takayama.
  • At Edo's town magistracy, two of its sections kept their duty rooms among the rooms of its court.
    The duty rooms of two of its sections, the shacho-sen'yokata and the reikurikata, stood in the same quarter of the compound as its court, among the rooms given to suits and examinations.
  • At Edo's town magistracies the household had its own ways in, and corridors ran past the office rooms.
    At the North Town Magistracy, as a plan of 1841 shows it, the formal entrance for official business lay east of the office room and the inner entrance for the household side to its north; a back gate served the magistrate's family, retainers and servants; and an entrance for the private side, the oku, stood between the rooms where the household's staff worked and the family's own quarters. The plans of the North Town Magistracy of 1841 and the South of 1842, read here from their images under the page's modern labels, show corridors running from the entrance and the inner entrance past the office room, and inner corridors (irikawa) beside the South's formal rooms; the labels do not show whether a corridor ran on into the residence.
  • Where did the magistrate's office stand against the residence? At Edo, the office room stood at their join; at Takayama, the intendant worked inside the residence.
    At the North Town Magistracy, as a modern blogger reads its plan of 1841, the compound divided into the office on the east and the residence on the west, with the office room (yobeya), which might be called the magistrate's secretariat, at the center between them. On the plan's image, under the blogger's labels, one corridor lies between the office room and the household's inner entrance; on the South's plan of 1842 only an unlabeled hatched strip lies between the inner entrance and the office room, and two duty rooms and the scribes' office lie on its other side, toward the court. At the Takayama Jin'ya the intendant ordinarily worked not in the office hall, where the retainers brought from Edo worked, but in the residence behind it, coming out to the hall when needed; the guide calls the living room (ima) there the private space where the intendant did official business day to day, and the residence had a doorway of its own, the naka-no-kuchi. Where that residence stood on the site, and that no page we read says which way the office faces, are at Magistrates' compounds (jin'ya and yamen).
  • Did a jin'ya's hall have corridors?
    Some did, though no page we read says where they led. The Kaibara domain's front hall, rebuilt in 1820, keeps its entrance, its formal step and its formal rooms with the inner corridors that surround them; the Sayama domain's council hall of 1775 had corridors among its buildings; and at Takayama the envoys' room faces the great hall, rebuilt in 1816, across a corridor. Houses below the great houses hardly had corridors at all (Samurai residences and their rooms (buke yashiki)).
  • An intendant's staff, the tetsuki and tedai, lived in huts and row houses inside the office's compound.
    An intendant's office took in, beside the office itself, the huts and row houses that were the official houses of its tetsuki and tedai.
  • Did a Japanese intendant's clerks ever have a building of their own to work in?
    No page we read gives them one.

A county office in China organized its clerks differently, and in practice kept far more of them.

  • A Chinese county's clerks worked in six chambers on either side of the office's second courtyard.
    The chambers, one for each of the court's six ministries, were set on the east and west sides of the county office's second courtyard.
  • How many clerks did a Chinese county office keep?
    Its authorized staff was two or three men to each chamber. In Ming and Qing times the chambers often ran over that number, even to more than a hundred men.
  • In China's capital, a few families filled most of an office's clerkships, and the clerks grew rich.
    The clerks of the silver treasury were mostly of one surname, and the tax clerks of the Chongwen Gate, one of the capital's gates, of a few, entrenched there. While the court's officials were often too poor to support themselves, the clerks amassed vast fortunes.
  • What did permanent clerks mean for a county office?
    This project's reading of the entrenched clerks of the Chinese capital is that, as permanent locals, clerks were an office's memory under one magistrate after another, and its natural pressure point for tax-farming.

A county of this setting is much smaller than a Chinese county, and its clerks are few.

  • In this setting, clerks are heimen scribes who live in town.
    Scribes are heimen artisans by caste, hired locally from the county town; they live in town and come in each day to the magistrate's compound, where the office hall stands.
  • How many clerks would a county office in this setting keep?
    Three or four, each paid about 4-5 koku a year: that is this setting's own figure, the county budget's line for its heimen clerks. That is well under the two or three men to each of a Chinese county's six chambers, and a county of this setting, of about 6,800 inhabitants, is far smaller than a Chinese county, of about 100,000-200,000 on a figure of this project's own, which no page we read gives.
