Iron refining forges (chao)

Chinese ironworks turned the cast iron of their blast furnaces into wrought iron by fining - 炒 (chao, "stir-frying"): broken cast iron was burned down in a small hearth under a forced blast and stirred until its carbon burned out. In Japan the same work was done at a refining forge, the okaji, from the seventeenth century on.

Smelting and refining could share one site or stand apart. A charcoal furnace laid a heavy load on the forest around it, and in Guangdong the furnaces stood out in the forested mountains while the iron came down by river to a town, to be refined or cast.

  • A Chinese fining hearth was a small, well-insulated hollow in the ground under a blast of air.
    Wood, charcoal and broken pieces of cast iron were charged into it and lit, and when the iron was semi-molten, with the consistency of bean-curd dregs, it was stirred about with an iron rod under the blast.
  • A seventeenth-century Chinese treatise describes a different way: iron run straight from the furnace into an open basin.
    Song Yingxing's Tiangong Kaiwu leads the molten iron into a rectangular hearth - 塘 (tang, "pool, basin") - built a few chi from the furnace outlet and a few cun lower, against a short wall. Several men stand in a row on the wall stirring with willow poles, which burn down two or three cun at each fining, while one spreads a powder over the iron.
  • What was the powder spread on the iron?
    Song Yingxing calls it wuchaoni, "filthy wet loam", dried and sieved as fine as flour, and the label in the treatise's illustration reads "wet loam and ashes". The historian of Chinese ironmaking who translates the treatise thinks it may have been something like the stuff of a nitre bed, a heap of dung, earth, urine and wood ashes from which saltpeter was made.
  • Could iron be fined in an open basin?
    The historian of Chinese ironmaking who translates Song Yingxing's treatise doubts it. The basin is open, unfueled and unblown, and cast iron flowing into it would set solid before much of its carbon was gone; he calls it a very different process from fining, and a very difficult one to explain.
  • How old is fining in China?
    It may go back to the Han dynasty, some two thousand years ago. A historian of Chinese ironmaking says the process seems to have been used that early, and sets out the evidence in another of his books. No page we read describes an excavated Han fining site.

Where the fining was done depended on the fuel.

  • In Guangdong, the large ironworks smelted in the mountains and sent their iron down to a town to be refined or cast.
    The large firms of the province ran ironworks in its forested mountains and shipped most of their iron by river to the industrial town of Foshan, near Guangzhou, where some of it was made into wrought iron and the rest went to the foundries.
  • Blast furnaces that burned charcoal were kept apart from each other, for their fuel.
    Two charcoal blast furnaces would not normally be put close together, because that would double the load on the forest for little or no gain.
  • Why did the furnaces stand out in the forest?
    Charcoal is made from several times as much wood: a modern industrial kiln burns about four and a half parts wood to one of charcoal. In this project's reading, that is why the kiln went to the wood, and a furnace that burned charcoal went to the kilns.

Japan's ironworks came to two stages as well, by another road, when the tatara furnaces turned to making pig iron.

  • Japan's refining forge, the okaji, came in the seventeenth century.
    The tatara's chief product changed from a lump of iron and slag to molten pig iron (zuku), and that led to the invention of a two-stage refining process, the okaji, to make it into wrought iron. Pig-making by the tatara and refining by the okaji made up Japan's indirect way of making wrought iron.
  • The tatara's lump was crushed and sorted.
    By the 1750s the lump of iron from the furnace, the kera, was being broken with a crushing device and sorted finely by quality and size.
  • The refining hearth burned the pig iron's carbon down under the bellows.
    The pig iron was heated again to a semi-molten state, and its carbon burned off in the air the bellows sent in.
  • The refined iron was called waritetsu, and later hochotetsu for its shape.
    At the second hearth it was heated and decarburized once more and forged to drive out its impurities, and the wrought iron that came of it was called 割鉄 (waritetsu, "split iron"); from the Meiji period on it was also called 包丁鉄 (hochotetsu, "kitchen-knife iron") for its shape.
  • How much of Japan's iron came from the Chugoku Mountains?
    Most of the iron for its guns, at least: the iron used by Kunitomo and the other gun-making towns that flourished from the Sengoku through the Edo period came almost all from the Chugoku Mountains. No page we read gives the region's share of all Japan's iron.
