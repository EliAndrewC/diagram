What did a shrine's metal furnishings cost?

Rokugan's prices are counted in koku and its smaller coins, and the setting's money rules judge a price by how many days of a craftsman's or a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give what the historical record holds for the metal furnishings of a shrine in Edo-period Japan and, where Japan is silent, in Qing China, each price set beside the nearest day wages the record gives.

Of the six things asked, a price was found for one: a great mirror for a town shrine cost about 17 ryo in the 1830s, with the cost of bringing it from Kyoto. No price was found for the other five: a shrine's hanging gong, hanging metal lanterns, a small gilt-bronze reliquary, an offering chest with its lock, or a lacquered chest for remains. The nearest are a village shrine's payments for its door fittings and ironwork in 1840, the price of raw copper in 1774, and, in China, the price of coarse copper ware by weight.

The furnishings asked

  • What did a shrine's mirror cost?
    About 17 ryo for one great mirror, ordered from Kyoto, with the cost of bringing it to Takaoka counted in: so the townsman who set out to give three mirrors to the town's Sekino shrine wrote to the merchant Fukuokaya Seiemon in 1836. Gifts had nearly paid for that one, and he asked the town's great houses to pay for the other two. The mirrors that hang in the shrine are about 73 cm (~29 in) across and 34 kg (~75 lb) in weight, of an alloy of copper, tin and lead, and each is inscribed with a date of 1833, while the letter says the first was ordered on a visit to Kyoto two years before 1836; the record leaves the two dates as they stand. No retail price of a hand mirror was found.
  • What did a hanging gong cost?
    No price was found.
  • What did hanging metal lanterns cost?
    No price was found.
  • What did a small gilt-bronze reliquary cost?
    No price was found.
  • What did an offering chest and its lock cost?
    No price was found, for the chest or the lock.
  • What did a lacquered chest for remains cost?
    No price was found.

The nearest prices in Japan

  • What did a village shrine pay for metalwork?
    At the Hachiman shrine of Shibasaki, in the Tama district of Musashi, in 1840, the door fittings ordered from a fittings maker of Yamaguchi were settled with a last payment of 3 bu, and 1 ryo 1 bu was handed to one Kamekichi to order the worship hall's ironwork at Okawa-dori. These are building hardware, not the furnishings asked, and the whole price of the door fittings is not given.
  • What did raw copper cost?
    In 1774 the shogunate's copper office in Osaka bought copper at 139.48 to 156.52 monme of silver a hundred kin, by its mine. Copper for export to China was fixed at 115 monme a hundred kin, and copper sold at home, in a sentence the encyclopedia does not date, held at about twice the China export price. A kin is 600 g (~1.3 lb) by today's legal definition; the Edo-period units were somewhat lighter.
  • What was the metal in a mirror worth?
    About 80 to 90 monme, by this project's arithmetic: a mirror of 34 kg holds about 57 kin of metal, which at the copper office's buying prices of 1774 comes to 79-89 monme. It is a floor, not a price: the mirror is of copper, tin and lead, cast sixty years later, the copper is priced at what the office paid the mines, while what a caster paid for copper is not given, and the casting and polishing are left out. Set beside the mirror's price of about 17 ryo, some 1,020 to 1,050 monme of silver, the metal at these prices is under a tenth of it, by this project's arithmetic.

The nearest price in China

  • What did copper ware cost by weight?
    0.27 to 0.28 tael a jin for even the coarse heavy kind, by a memorial of the early Qianlong years, when a jin of copper coins was worth only 0.17 tael; the memorial names no place. It is ware sold by weight, not one of the objects asked.

Day wages beside them

  • Beside the Takaoka mirror, 1834-1836?
    No Takaoka wage of the 1830s was found. The nearest of the place are 106 to 108 years older: in 1728 the account for building an airing house on the grounds of the lodging house there, with repairs to its storehouse and gates, paid its carpenters 1.8 monme of silver a man and its day laborers 0.71 monme, each man read here as a man's day of work. The nearest in date are the Kumehara carpenter's 2 monme a day and 1 monme allowance of 1830, in Musashi, some 4 to 6 years earlier, and the Kyoto casual laborer's 0.92 monme of 1802-1804, some 30 to 34 years earlier. The letter counts in gold, and these wages in silver: at the Osaka market's mean of the 1830s, 61.8 monme of silver to the ryo (What was copper coin worth in silver?), or at 60, 17 ryo is about 1,020 to 1,050 monme. That is some 570 to 580 days of the Takaoka carpenter of 1728 and 1,440 to 1,480 of its laborer, or 340 to 350 days of the Kumehara carpenter with his allowance and 1,110 to 1,140 of the Kyoto laborer, by this project's arithmetic, with the cost of carriage left in.
  • Beside the Shibasaki payments, 1840?
    No wage of the place and year was found. The nearest craftsman's wage read is a carpenter's at Kumehara in 1830, in the same province, ten years earlier: 2 monme of silver a day and 1 monme more that this project reads as his food allowance. The nearest unskilled wage read is a Kyoto casual laborer's spring rate of 1802-1804, about 0.92 monme a day. At 60 monme to the ryo, a rate this project borrows, the last 3 bu for the door fittings was 15 days of the carpenter or about 49 of the laborer, and the ironwork's 1 ryo 1 bu 25 or about 82.
  • Beside the copper of 1774?
    No Osaka wage of 1774 was found. The nearest unskilled wage read is Kyoto's of 1745-1754, about 0.91 monme a day, some twenty-five years earlier; at it a kin of copper at the office's buying price cost 1.5 to 1.7 days, and the mirror's metal floor about 87 to 98 days. The nearest craftsman's wage read is Osaka's carpenters' of 1802-1804, some thirty years later, at 4.7 times Kyoto's unskilled rate of about 0.92 monme, so about 4.3 monme a day: about a third of a day a kin, and 18 to 21 days for the mirror's metal.
  • Beside the Qing copper ware?
    The nearest Qing wage read is the public-construction wage norm of Jiangsu, set in 1769, some twenty-five to thirty-five years later: 0.040 tael a day unskilled and 0.051 skilled, averages over the province's districts of regulated wages that the regulations' introductory memorial calls near market prices at low market activity. At them a jin of coarse copper ware was 6.75 to 7 unskilled or about 5.3 to 5.5 skilled days.
