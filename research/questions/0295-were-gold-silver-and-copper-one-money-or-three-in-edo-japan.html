Were gold, silver and copper one money or three in Edo Japan?

Rokugan's coins are one money in three metals: a gold koku is always worth 10 silver bu, 100 copper zeni or 1,000 sen, so a price is the same whichever coin pays it. The findings below give what the historical record holds instead for Edo-period Japan, where gold, silver and copper were three monies, each with its own rate, its own users and, at times, its own prices.

Edo-period Japan had three coinages: gold counted in ryo, silver weighed in monme and copper coins counted in mon. The shogunate set a legal rate between them three times, but the market set the rate people traded at, and the law was revised to follow it. Within gold, the ryo's quarters and sixteenths were fixed by count, and so, from 1765, were the counted silver coins. Large sums were reckoned in gold in the east and in silver in the west, and copper paid for everyday purchases everywhere, a division that grew from custom, not law.

The rate the law set and the rate of the market

  • The shogunate set a legal rate in 1609, 1700 and 1842.
    A ryo of gold was fixed at 50 monme of silver and 4 kanmon (4,000 mon) of copper in 1609, at 60 monme and 4 kanmon in 1700, and at 60 monme and 6,500 mon in 1842. The rates of 1700 and 1842 and the market series of Osaka and Edo are in What was copper coin worth in silver?
  • The legal rate held for payments to the shogunate; trade went at the day's rate.
    Dues paid to the shogunate kept the legal rate, while money changers dealt at each day's market rates, set in Edo, Osaka and elsewhere. The rate at which the leading Osaka changers exchanged the three metals became the standard everywhere; they set a daily rate for copper in monme of silver a kanmon and sent it to their clients.
  • How did the shogunate hold the market to its rate?
    With orders to the changers, and by buying and selling copper. In 1700 it forbade changers to deal at 58 monme of silver or fewer, or at 3,900 mon of copper or fewer, to the ryo. In 1736, when changers hoarded the good old silver, Ooka Tadasuke summoned them and told them to stop manipulating the rate, but silver stayed dear for some time. When copper fell, the shogunate bought copper in 1796, ordered its rate raised in 1800 and banned copper from other provinces in 1813; when copper was dear in 1829, it ordered 5,000 ryo of copper sold off, down to about 6,500 mon a ryo.
  • What did the order of 1842 threaten?
    It ordered Edo to trade at 6,500 mon to the ryo and banned copper coin from Sendai, Ishinomaki and other provinces, on pain of seizure and strict punishment. Town officials who neglected it were to be held to account too, and the order was posted at the guardhouses and the changers' shopfronts. No record of anyone prosecuted under these orders was found.
  • How far did the market stray from the law?
    Silver ran at about 50 monme a ryo after the Genroku recoinage of 1695, against the 60 of 1700, until the Hoei silver recoinages of the 1700s-1710s ended silver's dearness. Copper ran at about 4,800 mon a ryo in 1694, 3,700 in 1700 and 2,800 in 1737, against the legal 4,000. It held near 4,000 in the 1750s and 1760s, broke into the 5,000s in 1769 and into the 6,000s in 1778. Around the Restoration of 1868 a ryo fetched about 10,000 mon, against the 6,500 of 1842.
  • A ryo was 4 bu or 16 shu, by count.
    Gold coins of a quarter, an eighth and a sixteenth of a ryo passed at those fractions.
  • From 1765, silver coins were counted too.
    A ryo was 12 five-monme pieces, 8 two-shu pieces or 4 ichibu-gin. The nanryo two-shu piece, first cast in 1772, was meant to fix the rate between gold and silver coin and in effect unify the currency. The ichibu-gin, first cast in 1837 and equal to the gold ichibu, was the finished form of counted silver, and from it on counted silver coins were the main current of circulation.   • Weighed silver left circulation, but its unit stayed.
    Once the counted silver came out, weighed silver priced in monme hardly circulated, yet Osaka's changers and merchants kept dealing in monme of account until early Meiji. As chogin was recoined into nanryo, silver rose to 50 monme a ryo by 1786.
  • The changers fought the nanryo.
    Changers who lived on the floating rate between koban and chogin demanded a premium of 25% to change nanryo into either; in Kyoto and Osaka, 25 ryo in every 100 paid in gold went in nanryo. The shogunate answered with dealing allowances, 4 fun of silver a ryo given to a buyer of nanryo and 8 fun taken from a seller, and with loans of nanryo free of interest and security for 3 years, up to 10,000 ryo in Edo and 40,000 in Osaka.

Which metal paid for what

  • Gold in the east, silver in the west.
    An Edo-period writer noted that Kyoto and Osaka quoted rice at so many monme of silver a koku, and Edo at so much rice to a ryo of gold. Ise, Mino and Kaga are usually given as the border, with exceptions.
  • Gold and silver for large sums, copper for retail.
    Daimyo and upper samurai used gold for large transactions, merchants used silver, and commodity prices were usually quoted in silver. Ordinary retail was mainly in copper, which commoners handled, rarely holding even small silver; copper passed in both regions.
  • Laborers were paid in copper.
    In 1791 an Akita copper mine changed over 1,039 kanme of silver into copper at 92 mon, nearly 90,000 kanmon for diggers' pay and tools; a digger's day pay came to some tens of mon, so small coin was indispensable. An Akita merchant house in 1862 held over 90% of its money in gold, which its historian reads to mean that above a certain size gold was used even for a sum quoted in copper.   • The land tax was assessed in koku and paid in rice and money.
    The shares to be paid in rice and in gold or silver were fixed in advance, and where rice was hard to ship the whole was paid in money. In the Kanto's upland fields the tax was reckoned at a ryo to 1 kanmon of the old Eiraku coin and paid in gold.
  • Samurai stipends were rice, turned into coin by agents.
    The fudasashi collected a retainer's stipend rice for 1 bu of gold per 100 bales and sold it for another 2 bu, cashing all but the rice to be eaten at the day's price.
  • Domains paid outsiders in shogunal coin and themselves in notes and copper.
    Sales of tax rice, shogunal business and the costs of residence in Edo were settled in shogunal coin, trade inside the domain in domain notes and copper. In western domains prices were quoted in silver but, silver being scarce, paid in copper at a fixed ratio.
  • Big clients used the main changers; townspeople used copper shops.
    Only daimyo, big merchants and others making large transactions used the main changers; townspeople went to the copper changers.
  • What did changing cost?
    The fees on record are few. In 1859 the shogunate recalled copper one-mon coins at 1,548 iron and Tenpo mon a kanmon, 48 of them to the changers as their fee, about 3.2% on the 1,500 paid out. The nanryo allowances of 4 and 8 fun a ryo come to about 0.7% and 1.3% at 60 monme. The fudasashi's 3 bu per 100 bales is above. In Miyazu domain from the late 1820s, a silver monme was always 1.13 copper monme, which Kano reads as a fixed fee for exchange into shogunal coin.
  • Was the usual fee 1-2%?
    An encyclopedia says changers took about 1-2%, more for changing into small coin, but for some small coins changing up into gold cost more than changing down, such as nanryo and ichibu-gin into koban; it cites no source for the figure.

Did a price depend on the coin?

  • In 1842 Edo ordered copper prices cut, and left silver prices alone.
    The day after the 6,500 rate, Edo's prices were to be lowered in proportion, but goods sold in silver monme need not be surveyed.
  • How much were copper prices to fall, and did they?
    Oishi finds that the 6,500 rate raised copper by about 7-8%, so copper retail prices would have fallen about as much if the price returns were carried out; whether they were, he says, is very doubtful. Earlier that year the officials overseeing prices were warned that lowering only the price while the goods got worse was no use.
  • In Tsugaru in 1837-1838 goods had a coin price and a note price.
    A bale of rice was 40 monme in coin and 60 in notes two months after the notes came out; the next new year a small bale of charcoal was 7 fun in coin and 2 monme 5 fun in notes, notes at 28% of face.
  • Copper coins of one face passed at different values, 1854-1868.
    From the Ansei era the shogunate's orders to pass coins at face failed, and in 1865 it allowed Edo's changers to pay premiums: against the iron one-mon, copper one-mon coins 4 to 6 mon and brass four-mon coins 12. In 1867 it left all coins to the market.
  • The Tenpo hundred-mon coin passed at about 80.
    It fell to a sixtieth of a ryo in the Ansei era and a hundredth in the Man'en era.
  • After a recoinage, the finer old coin passed only at a premium.
    The good old coin was hoarded until a premium was allowed on it; in the Kyoho era coins of different fineness each passed at their silver content.
  • Before 1600, bad coin passed at a discount.
    At one time 4 bad coins were agreed to make one mon, and in 1569-1570 Oda Nobunaga graded ten kinds of coin into three classes with a rate for each. Bad coin then passed with a premium added, and copper came to be used only for small sums.
  • Was paying in gold worth a premium or a discount?
    No source read says so.
  • Did copper shop prices follow the rate by themselves?
    An encyclopedia says that once the four-mon coin came out prices moved to multiples of 4, and dango went from 5 for 5 mon to 4 for 4, "it is said", with no source cited. No series showing how fast copper prices followed a move in the rate, outside the order of 1842, was found.

Who won and lost when the rates moved

  • Copper was the money of the poorest commoners.
    Oishi writes that a fall in copper was no simple fall in a currency, but bore hardest on the commoners at the bottom of society. The orders of 1796 and 1813 name the hardship to the lower orders and to small merchants who traded in copper.
  • The iron coins of the 1760s cheapened copper by about a third.
    With iron one-mon coins cast in quantity from 1765, copper fell to about 6,000 mon a ryo by 1778. Oishi ties this fall to the hardship, risings and urban riots of the Tanuma years. A wage fixed in mon while a ryo went from about 4,000 to 6,000 mon lost a third of its worth in gold. No real wage in copper for these years was found.
  • In the 1830s Edo craftsmen's copper wages held while rice fell.
    In 1842 the officials overseeing prices were told that craftsmen's wages were reported not to have come down although rice had fallen since 1836, and to lower them.
  • Who took the gain of a recoinage?
    In the Genbun recoinage of 1736 the holders of old coin did, being paid 165 new ryo for 100 old; the money supply rose 40% and prices rose fast. In the Bunsei and Tenpo recoinages, exchanged at par, the shogunate took an estimated 5.5 and 5 million ryo, and in 1863 recoinage gains were 68% of its revenue.
  • A shrinking coinage hurt those paid in rice.
    When the Shotoku recoinage of 1714 cut the money supply, rice fell, and so did the income of shogunal and domain finances resting on rice taxes.
  • In the 1860s real wages fell further in the silver west than the gold east.
    After gold flowed out of Japan, prices in silver rose faster than in gold. Day laborers hired in a spot market, its author reasons, could expect none of the extra payments that softened rising prices for retained workers, so the gap between skilled and unskilled earnings presumably widened.
  • Did a tax burden rise or fall with the rates?
    No measure of it was found.
  • What did people do?
    They hoarded good coin, as the changers did in 1736; passed coins of one face at different values; and, in the changers' case, resisted the nanryo. No petition from wage earners about the rate was found.

Against the setting's money rules

  • Rokugan's fixed coinage is a deliberate deviation.
    Rokugan fixes a koku at 10 bu, a bu at 10 zeni and a zeni at 10 sen. In Japan only the steps within gold, and the silver stamped in gold units, were fixed; the rates between the metals floated.
  • Rokugan's coin use by size of sum is accurate; Japan also split by region.
    The setting's money rules give daily life to copper, a household's planned sums to silver and treasuries to gold, as Japan used copper for retail and gold and silver for large sums. Japan's east reckoned in gold and its west in silver, which Rokugan does not.
  • Should a sum in copper be converted through silver?
    The setting's money rules convert each coin into silver at its era's rate. In the one case measured, Edo in 1842, copper prices had to be ordered down after a move in the rate, and Oishi doubts they fell, so a sum in mon may be better measured against copper prices.
