How our maps draw the houses and shops at a temple's gate (monzenmachi)

On a town or city map, the approach to a temple's gate is lined with a single row of ordinary houses and shops, some twenty to forty to each temple complex and about 18 ft deep, drawn on the pattern of an ordinary Edo temple's gate. They are a neighborhood's homes and trades, counted in the city's own population. Inside the gate the forecourt is left open, and because a temple issued its own amulets, our maps put no shop of religious goods outside its wall.

  • How many houses ring a provincial city's temples?
    About 40 to 80 around the two temple complexes of a provincial city, which hold 30 to 60 resident monks between them: roughly one or two lay households for each monk, for a city whose worshippers come from its own region. The twenty to forty houses each complex gets compare with the twenty-two at one average Edo temple. The ratio to the monks is this project's own figure, a GUESS calibrated on the drawn cities: no page read sets a gate town's households against its temple's monks.
  • A pilgrimage city draws ten or more gate households for each monk.
    Where a temple draws pilgrims from far beyond its own city, our maps give it ten lay households or more for each monk, against the one or two of a provincial city's temples. This is the gate town at its fullest. The figure is a GUESS.
  • A temple's gate houses stand in a single shallow row about 18 ft (3 ken) deep, facing the temple's approach.
    That is the depth of the gate houses at the average Edo temple.
  • The gate houses are counted in the city's own population, not added to it.
    They are drawn inside the caste bands the city already has.
  • The gate houses hold a neighborhood's everyday trades.
    So it was at the gate of an ordinary Edo temple.
  • The row is drawn the same whether its houses stand on the temple's land or not.
    Who owned the ground does not show on a map.
  • No teahouse or eating house has a symbol of its own.
    At this scale a teahouse and an eatery are both the ordinary shop, so the map tells the story by where a shop stands - a shop beside the temple reads as a teahouse, as on Sensoji's approach - and never by its icon. This is a map drawing convention.
  • No shop selling amulets or other religious goods is drawn outside a temple compound.
    A temple issued its own amulets, and what a visitor paid was taken as a donation to it, so our maps draw no amulet seller at the gate. This project extends the same rule to other religious goods and keeps everything sacred on the map inside the compound.
  • No factory-made charm, or charm issued by no shrine, appears on our maps.
    Both are a modern trade, so neither such a charm nor its seller has a place in this setting.
  • A temple's retained carpenters are drawn in workshops that are also dwellings, not storefronts.
    Eiheiji, the temple Dogen founded, kept carpenters who, by the temple's tradition, lived in a village near its gate and handled its repairs for generations. Our maps give every temple's carpenters the same place, and drawing them as workshop-houses is this project's GUESS.
  • The forecourt inside a temple's gate is drawn as open ground with no market stalls.
    At a Chinese temple fair (miaohui), the temple's own ground became the market for the day, but a map shows one moment, so our maps leave the forecourt clear and draw no fair.
