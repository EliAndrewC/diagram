How often did players perform?

The licensed kabuki theaters of Edo, Kyoto and Osaka played by daylight, a whole day's program from about dawn to dusk, and could not play at night until gas lamps came in 1878. Their year began in the 11th month and was a calendar of a handful of named programs, each kept on as long as it drew, with the 6th and 7th months a rest; a new program every month came only in the 1910s. A play on shrine or temple ground was licensed for a hundred fair-weather days at a time. In the villages a play was a festival's, one to a few days, a few times a year (What did a play or a show cost to see?). No source read shows a country troupe playing a weekly market day.

The city theaters

  • The city theaters played by daylight only.
    A modern exhibition on Edo kabuki gives the playing day as dawn to dusk, about 6 a.m. to 5 p.m., an audience spending the whole day eating sweets and box lunches. On the opening day of the year's first program the theater beat its first drum at 4 a.m. Night playing became possible only when Tokyo's Shintomi-za was built with gas lighting, in 1878.
  • How long did a program last?
    All day: about eleven hours, from dawn to dusk, by that exhibition's hours (this project's arithmetic). No period source giving a program's length in hours was found.
  • The theatrical year began in the 11th month.
    An actor's contract ran for a year, from the 11th month to the 10th, and the year's first program, the 顔見世 (kaomise, "face-showing"), showed off the new company. The kaomise was usually short, about ten days, and the program after it carried more weight.
  • A program ran as long as it drew.
    In Edo the New Year play was kept as long as possible, its weak scenes cut and new parts added for the 3rd-month and 5th-month programs; to run on to the 28th of the 5th month counted a great success, but often a new play opened on the 3rd of the 3rd month and was changed again from the 5th of the 5th. In Osaka and Kyoto a third new play came about the 3rd month.
  • The theaters rested in the 6th and 7th months.
    As a rule there was no playing in those two months; later the theaters put on cheap summer plays built around junior actors, and in 1804-1830 the leading actors joined some. The 9th month's autumn program was the year's last, the "farewell" program.
  • How many programs a year?
    About five or six, by the calendar: the kaomise in the 11th month, the New Year play, the 3rd-month and 5th-month programs, a summer play in some years, and the autumn farewell in the 9th (this project's count from that calendar). A theater staged a new program every month only from the Taisho era (1912-1926).
  • Fire could shut a theater.
    When fire or the like closed their theater, the city actors went on provincial tours and played in shows bought in by provincial towns.
  • Did mourning close the theaters?
    Yes, on a shogun's death: every domain was ordered to stop all music and dancing, the 鳴物停止 (narimono choji, "stopping of the sounding instruments"). For how many days was not found.
  • What of the puppet theaters?
    No calendar of the puppet theaters' year was found.

Shrine-ground and other small theaters

  • A shrine-ground play was licensed for a hundred fair-weather days.
    Hence its name, the "hundred-day play"; as enforcement slackened some theaters renewed the hundred days again and again, until their playing differed little from a permanent house's. Such plays stood in Edo, Kyoto and Osaka, and at provincial shrines and temples too. What they were is at Theater stages on shrine ground (miyaji shibai).
  • A small theater applied for its license each time.
    Where the great theaters were permanent houses allowed to play without break, a small theater was in principle a temporary booth, its days were limited, and it had to apply each time; by their customs the plays of provincial cities and festivals counted as small theater too.
  • Did rain stop a play?
    Yes, by one account. An encyclopedia's article on the theater, quoted on the same dictionary page, says no performance could be given in rain, and on fair days the tower drum was beaten to announce a show; the passage as quoted does not say which theaters it means.
  • The great cities had many small theaters at once.
    Kyoto counted fourteen in 1688-1704, and Edo more than twenty in the early 1800s.
  • How many licenses did a provincial town see in a year?
    No count was found. One shrine town, Kotohira, put up a temporary playhouse about three times a year before 1835 (Roofed playhouses (shibai goya)).

Country troupes

What a village play was, and what a bought-in troupe was paid, is in What did a play or a show cost to see? and What did an entertainer or a troupe earn?; what they show of how often:

  • A country engagement was a festival's few days.
    A village play ran a day or three days; one market town's troupe of 1728 was hired for nine fair-weather days and gave fifteen shows; a Shima village hired a puppet company for three or four days, twice a year (both pages above).
  • How often did a town buy in a troupe?
    Rarely, at the one town counted: Hamanoichi's fifteen kabuki runs in 1690-1737 come to about one every three years (this project's arithmetic from the figures on the first page above).
  • How many engagements did a troupe fill in a year?
    No count was found, and no troupe was found playing a weekly market day or making a weekly round of villages.

Qing China

China's theaters kept to daylight too, and its country troupes, like Japan's, played festivals.

  • Beijing's theaters played afternoons.
    In 1796-1820 they mostly opened after noon and let out by the you hour (5 to 7 p.m.); a private party's performance began in the morning and also ended by the you hour; there were no night plays.
  • Beijing's troupes rotated among the theaters every four days.
    Before 1900 a theater was like an inn and a troupe like its guest: each troupe played at each theater for four days, round and round.
  • Shanghai's theaters slowed at year's end.
    In the 12th month, with travelers gone home and residents busy with the new year, business fell off sharply. Its new-style stages priced day shows and night shows differently.
  • Temple fairs, and their plays, clustered in spring.
    Of the North China temple fairs a study counts from Qing gazetteers, 317, 72.2%, fell in the 3rd to 5th months. A City God fair lasted one to fifteen days; a fair of the war god Guandi one to ten days, some a month, with plays very common. At one Guandi temple outside Beijing the temple opened for three days from the 11th of the 5th month, and players gave plays there every year. What a village festival troupe cost for three days is in What did a play or a show cost to see?.
  • Imperial mourning stopped plays.
    In 1689 the playwright Hong Sheng was impeached for staging his play privately during the national mourning for an empress. In 1881 a prince's son was denounced for setting up plays barely a hundred days after an imperial funeral, which this project reads as a closure of about a hundred days in Beijing.
