How our maps size a city's wall to its population

A walled city on our maps is sized from its population, never the other way round. The ground its households, its government, its temples and its streets need is added up first, the wall is drawn to enclose exactly that, and the finished map is held to it. A city then draws one house for every household its population promises, and almost all of them stand inside the wall.

  • A wall guessed first leaves ground that nothing asked for.
    Nagahara's first draft looked finished while about a sixth of its interior - five pockets, some of them seven acres apiece, about 24 acres in all - was open ground with no use. A density check averaged over the whole city treats such ground as still to be filled rather than as a fault, so the emptiness never registered. So the order is inverted: the ground the city needs is computed, the wall is derived from it, and the drawn map is held to that promise.
  • How much ground does a household take on the map?
    About 0.14 acre for a row-house household and 0.51 acre for a detached samurai house, each gross - the roof plus its share of eaves, alley and margin, which is why the two stand about 3.6 to 1 where the drawn houses are closer in size. Tango's well-filled quarters run 0.09 to 0.17 acre a household, and its samurai ward 0.60 acre. Both are calibrations against the drawn cities, not figures from a source; together they reproduce the wall of the finished Tango to within a fifth of a percent, which is the argument for trusting them elsewhere.
  • The ground is priced at the size the maps draw a house, not its real size.
    Small houses are drawn larger than life so they can be read, and budgeting true real-world ground would make the wall too small for the map drawn inside it.

The city's government and temples are budgeted by what they are, not by how many people the city holds.

  • The civic ground is a floor, not a share of the population.
    A seat of 2,000 inhabitants still carries its yamen, its six ministries, its temples, its theater and its gate furniture (the barbican and the toll barrier at each gate), so the civic ground is itemized rather than scaled. At Tango's measured compounds it comes to about 13 acres, near 9% of the interior.
  • How much of the interior does the civic ground take?
    About a tenth is treated as ordinary, and anything from 5 to 15% as in band. The band is a calibration against the cities this project has drawn, not a historical share, because no readable source gives a share of a walled city's interior for its civic buildings.
  • A city declares its temples rather than taking a fixed count.
    A city of the Fox clan runs eight small precincts where an ordinary clan runs two great ones. Minami declares eight precincts of about 0.70 acre each, with an itemized addition for its Inari ground, which puts its civic program at about 15.5% of the interior - above the band the other drawn cities sit in, and the honest mark of a city with four times the usual temples rather than a number to adjust.

Streets and open ground are taken off the top, at what the map actually draws.

  • Streets, lanes and alleys take 7% of the interior.
    That is the share at the widths the maps draw, measured at 6.8% and 7.0% on two of the drawn cities. It is a deliberate deviation from the tenth to a fifth of the walled ground this record estimates for a county seat's streets (at How densely a city is built, and what counts as empty ground): the budget has to price what the map draws, and deep blocks with alley warrens sit at the sparse end of that range.
  • Open ground is budgeted only where it is declared and drawn.
    An agricultural district inside the wall takes a further 15% of the interior, Tango's measured reserve, and a drill ground or a garden is entered by name. A city that wants the roomier character of the historical walled cities, much of whose ground lay under crops and water, takes it openly; open ground is never left over between buildings as unassigned space.
  • The wall's outline is computed, never picked by hand.
    The wall is the twenty-sided outline the map draws, enclosing exactly the required interior. Its area is a few percent under that of the smooth ellipse through the same radii, so sizing it as an ellipse would make every wall too small.

The rule the map follows: a walled city's wall is computed from its program before anything is drawn - the households' ground, the civic floor, the one water feature inside the wall (a pond, or in a river city the cargo canal and its dock basin) and the declared extras, divided by the share that streets and declared open ground take out - and the drawn wall is the twenty-sided outline that encloses that area. The drawn interior then stays within +8% and -5% of the budget.

  • Why 8% over and 5% under?
    More than 8% over is the empty-ground fault, and the answer is a shorter wall or a declared line that draws the extra ground; more than 5% under means the program does not fit, and the answer is a longer wall or a smaller program. Under is the tighter bound because an undersized wall breaks the packing at once. Both tolerances are calibrated against two drawn cities rather than derived from a source: the finished Tango encloses about a quarter of a percent under its budget, and Nagahara's first draft about 21% over.

A declared population is a promise about what the map contains.

  • A city draws exactly one house for every household its population promises.
    A household is five inhabitants, and there is no allowance either way. A band of even 7% is how a city ends up quietly smaller than its own figure: Minami was drawn at 486 dwellings against a target of 520 and read as finished.
  • When the houses do not fit, the wall grows.
    The wall grows from the budget, and the declared figure is never trimmed to fit the layout. An undersized wall is already a wall fault; having no tolerance closes the escape of shrinking the population instead. Where the budget genuinely under-counts, the budget is what gets corrected, and a layout that runs out of room before every house is placed is a wall too small, not a population question.

The rule the map follows: a city draws exactly one dwelling for every five inhabitants of its declared population, with no tolerance in either direction, and counts only the dwellings inside the wall. The count includes every dwelling the figure covers - the adept-monk households beside each temple precinct and, where a city has an agricultural district, its in-wall farmhouses.

  • The houses are added one caste at a time, smallest first.
    The filling pass asks each caste for precisely its shortfall, smallest houses first because those are what still fit once the quarters are full, and stops the moment that caste reaches its figure.
  • No caste may overshoot its own allowance on the way.
    Each caste's count may run no more than 30% past its own figure while the city fills: filling smallest first once pushed Tango's servants to 159 against a ceiling of 156.
  • No commoner household stands outside a provincial city's wall.
    Laborers, servants, burakumin and merchants all live inside. Only samurai country estates, farmhouses, the wharf shops and the gate-market shops stand outside, so a city can never meet its figure by spilling past its wall.
  • How many of a provincial city's samurai households live inside its wall?
    About two thirds. The rest live on country estates outside it. The split is a GUESS: no page we read gives one, and the history, at The samurai quarter: where a city's samurai live, and how many (bukechi), leans toward more of them inside.

A domain capital is the exception.

  • About 2.8% of a capital's households live outside its wall, at its wharves.
    That is 60 of 2,160 row-house households, some 300 inhabitants. They are the wharf hamlet only - the brokers', boatmen's and porters' households that must live at the water they work - and the towpath shore. This is this project's choice for a fortified seat, and it fits Japan, whose enclosed castle towns were walled to bring the town inside.
  • The gate markets outside a capital's gates house no one.
    Their shop rows, inns, flophouses and caravan yards serve travelers and are commerce only.
  • Why not the larger share of the Chinese commercial cities?
    A share of 30% was priced and declined. The cities whose life spread far outside their walls, Kaifeng with its large population there and Suzhou with its shop streets, were commercial cities of the Song dynasty and the Ming, and that their commerce spread past ramparts that no longer mattered militarily is this project's reading. The capital the maps draw, Shiro Daika, is a castle seat built for siege on the Crab clan's border; the commercial cities' share would have put about 1,800 inhabitants outside a wall that exists to keep them inside.
  • Putting 57% of a capital's households outside its wall was an accident, never a decision.
    It was only the overflow of a wall too small for its own population, and it was declined.
  • How tightly does a capital pack its row houses?
    About 0.23 acre gross a household: tighter than the capital's first draw, at 0.28 acre, though still looser than provincial Tango's 0.14. The capital keeps its wall and fits its 2,100 in-wall row-house households by building closer, not by enlarging it. That a fortified capital trades openness for the rampart's protection, packing tighter than its first draw, is this project's reading, a GUESS: no East Asian page we read says a walled city packed its inhabitants tighter, and a study of the walled cities of late imperial China finds that in many cities suburbs grew outside the gates before the ground inside the wall was completely built up.
  • The capital's samurai outside the wall are a separate line of the budget.
    About 47 samurai households, 15% of the capital's samurai, are seated on country estates along the approaches. They do not contradict the wharf-hamlet share, which governs the row-house commoner households - laborers, servants and merchants; the samurai outside are landed retainers whose estates are on the approaches by design.
