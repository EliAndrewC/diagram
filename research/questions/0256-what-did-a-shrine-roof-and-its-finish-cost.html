What did a shrine roof and its finish cost?

Rokugan's prices are counted in koku and its smaller coins, and the setting's money rules judge a price by how many days of a craftsman's or a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give what the historical record holds for roofing a small shrine and finishing its wood in Edo-period Japan, each cost set beside the nearest day wages the record holds.

The one cost found for roofing a small shrine is for copper: in 1749 the copper re-roofing of a one-bay sanctuary in the Tama district of Musashi was, its historian infers, the heart of a repair costing about 43 ryo in all, more than half of it materials. No price was found for a roof of cypress bark or of wooden shingle, for the roofers' days a tsubo of roof took, for persimmon tannin, or for the roof area of a small sanctuary.

A copper roof

  • What did the copper re-roofing of a small sanctuary cost?
    About 43 ryo 1 bu (43.25 ryo) for the whole repair of the Kasuga shrine's sanctuary at Nogami in 1749, which, the author infers, centered on re-roofing in copper plate and replacing the bargeboards and gable pendants. It is not a price for the roof alone.
  • Where did the money go?
    Materials took 53.1%, the copper plate and copper nails bought from Edo a large part of it; the craftsmen's wages, carpenters included, 14.6%; rice and the like 12.3%; the ridge-raising, the enshrinement and sundries 20.0%. In money that is about 23 ryo of materials and 6.3 ryo of wages.
  • How big was the sanctuary?
    Large for a one-bay sanctuary: its bay was 7.8 shaku (about 2.4 m), nearly twice the bay, a little over 4.2 shaku, of another small sanctuary in the same district.
  • What were its craftsmen paid?
    The same job's accounts pay its carpenters and sawyers 1 to 1.67 monme of silver a day, 187 man-days in all, and its roofers from Edo 3 monme a day, 24 man-days, the freight apart. At those rates the wages come to between about 4.3 and 6.4 ryo, in step with the 14.6% share of about 6.3 ryo. No unskilled wage of the district was found; the nearest is Kyoto's in 1745-1754, about 0.91 monme a day.
  • What was the materials bill in days of work?
    About 830 days of the job's best-paid carpenter, or about 1,500 days of a Kyoto laborer's pay, at 60 monme to the ryo.
  • What did copper roof tiles cost by the piece?
    About 2.7 monme each, in the one price found: rebuilding the Hagi domain's three Edo mansions after the great fire of 1772, the domain bought in Osaka 11,000 copper tiles of 1 by 1.2 shaku for just under 30 kan of silver, a bill of about 500 ryo at the historian's own 60 monme to the ryo. The historian's own Osaka figures do not quite agree: its total of about 107.5 kan, less 45.4 for timber and 35.1 for clay tiles, leaves under 27 kan for the copper tiles and the rest bought there, so a tile may have cost nearer 2.4 monme. No wage of Osaka or Edo in 1772-1773 was found. The nearest craftsman's wage read is the 3 monme a day paid the Edo roofers at the Tama job of 1749, twenty-three years earlier, so a tile cost about a roofer's day; the nearest unskilled wage is Kyoto's 0.91 monme of 1745-1754, about three days' pay a tile. How many tiles a tsubo of roof took is not given.

An Edo carpenter's wage

  • What was an Edo carpenter paid early in the 1800s?
    5.4 monme of silver a day, meals included, in a book's reckoning of an Edo carpenter's household budget, set at the prices of 1804-1830, reported at third hand by a blog.
  • And in the 1850s?
    A price table gives a flat 0.125 ryo (7.5 monme) a day, meals included, from 1853 to 1860; another wage table gives the going rate of 1854 as 4 monme for a top hand and 3.75 for an ordinary one, with 1.2 monme for meals.
  • What did a disaster do to it?
    In the year after the great earthquake of 1855, the same table's rates rose to 45 monme for a top hand and 37.5 for an ordinary one, with 1.5 monme for meals.
  • What was an unskilled laborer paid in Edo?
    No figure was found. A wage study says only that the Edo laborers' wages in the Mitsui company's accounts from 1818 were broadly level with Kyoto's (What did an unskilled laborer earn?).

Cypress bark, shingle and persimmon tannin

  • What did a cypress-bark roof cost?
    No price, per tsubo or per job, was found. The Hagi rebuilding of 1772-1773 counted nails for such roofs: among the 2,006,800 nails it shipped from the domain were 80,000 cypress-bark nails about 10.6 cm (~4.2 in) long and 3,000 about 9 cm (~3.6 in) long, but no price for the bark is given.
  • What did a shingle roof cost?
    No price was found.
  • How many roofer-days did a tsubo of roof take?
    No count was found, for bark, shingle or copper.
  • What did persimmon tannin cost?
    No price from the time was found.
  • How big was a small sanctuary's roof?
    No roof area was found for a one-bay sanctuary of about 4 shaku.
