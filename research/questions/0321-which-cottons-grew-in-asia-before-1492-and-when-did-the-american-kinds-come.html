Which cottons grew in Asia before 1492, and when did the American kinds come?

Rokugan's tax rules set cotton beside common iron as an ordinary, low-rate good, and the setting's list of regional cash crops does not name it. Cotton is easily taken for one of the crops that crossed the Atlantic after Columbus. The findings below give what the historical record holds on which cottons Asia grew and when the American ones reached it.

Cotton in Asia did not come from the Americas. Two Old World species were grown there long before 1492, when the American cottons had yet to arrive. India was cultivating cotton by about 2500 BCE, and the plant reached the lower Yangzi by the 12th century, Korea in the 1360s and the fields of Japan around 1500.

The American species came late. British trials in India began in the late 1700s and mostly failed, and the species is dated to 1865 in China and 1904 in Korea. No source read records one being grown in Japan. Japan's farmers did take up cotton in the century and a half between Columbus and the shogunate's closing of the country, but it was an Asian cotton, and the seed came from nearby Asia, by the accounts read.

The Asian cottons before 1492

  • Cotton was already grown across much of Asia when the American species arrived.
    A review of the archaeological finds by Dorian Fuller and colleagues says the American cottons entered an Old World where cotton production was already widespread in Africa and parts of Asia.
  • Two species belong to the Old World.
    Gossypium herbaceum is regarded as African in origin and G. arboreum as probably from the Indus region of South Asia.
  • India has the earliest cotton in Asia.
    Finds at Mehrgarh in Baluchistan show cotton in use by 5000-4300 BCE, wild or cultivated being unknown. Seeds appear regularly only from about 2500 BCE, in the Harappan period, when Fuller infers that cultivation was well established. By the late centuries BCE it was widespread across India. The early South Asian seeds are presumed to be G. arboreum.
  • China received both species, by two routes.
    Harriet Zurndorfer has G. herbaceum coming overland from Central Asia to Gansu and Shaanxi, and the South Asian cottons by sea from India to Burma, Vietnam, Hainan and the far south. How it then spread is in how cotton reached China and spread across it.
  • Annual cottons came late.
    Fuller dates annual varieties in China from the 13th century, on written evidence, and has annual forms established in parts of India only around the 18th.
  • What species did Korea grow?
    Cotton seed is recorded as brought from Yuan China in 1363 by the official Mun Ik-jeom. In-Sung Kim Han calls his variety a hybrid of G. herbaceum and G. arboreum in his text, and in a note says it was not G. herbaceum but a form of G. arboreum, against most Korean historians. A study of colonial Korea calls the native varieties G. arboreum.
  • Japan's cotton was G. arboreum.
    Japan's agriculture ministry says this diploid species was grown nationwide from the 16th to the 18th centuries. The ministry also says Japan had no cotton in antiquity; the earlier seed that seems not to have lasted is in when cotton became ordinary clothing in Japan. The economic historian Kawakatsu Heita names the variety that took root as sinense.
  • Older books call these plants by other names.
    Robert Fortune, in the 1840s, calls the Chinese cotton plant G. herbaceum. The 1911 Encyclopaedia Britannica says the Indian races are often referred to that species, and that the botanist Watt dissents. Han reports that a classification of 1947, by Hutchinson and colleagues, put Chinese Asiatic cotton under G. arboreum. The same plant can therefore appear under either name.

When the American cottons came

The American species are G. hirsutum, called upland cotton, and G. barbadense. Each country below met them at a different date.

  • India's trials began in 1788 and mostly failed.
    Between 1788 and 1850 the East India Company tried New Orleans, Upland, Georgia, Sea Island and other seed, with little permanent success. At Dharwar, in the Bombay Presidency, an experiment of 1829-36 was given up as a failure.
  • Dharwar was a local exception.
    An American planter went there in April 1843, and New Orleans seed, under 3,000 acres fifteen years before Marshman wrote, was reported at more than 178,000 acres in the year before he wrote. All cotton in the district in 1861-62 came to 379,000 acres, which makes the American seed about 47% of it, on this project's arithmetic, if the two figures belong to one season.
  • How much of India's cotton was American?
    The one all-India figure found comes from a single modern web essay by Avik Ray, which puts American cotton's entry in the late 18th century and its acreage at 2-3% from the 19th century to the mid-20th.
  • In China, American cotton is dated from 1865.
    One article's abstract gives that year, then three stages: trials under the late Qing, improvement under the Republic, and spread after 1949. No share of the crop was found for any year.
  • In Korea, upland cotton begins with a trial of 1904.
    A news report on a Mokpo city history says a trial led by the Japanese consul succeeded on Gohado island in 1904 and that the seed spread from there. Under colonial rule the cotton acreage rose from about 60,000 hectares (~148,000 acres) in 1910 to over 340,000 (~840,000 acres) in 1942. Upland was 93% of the crop in the six southern provinces by 1918, 70% of all Korean cotton by 1936, and the whole of the southern crop by 1935.
  • Was American cotton ever grown in Japan?
    No source read gives a date for an American species being planted there. What is recorded is the decline of the native G. arboreum from the 19th century and raw cotton imports: about 800,000 bales a year around 1900, chiefly from British India, China and the United States, with India regularly supplying over half.

The years between Columbus and Japan's closing

  • Japan's restrictions came about 150 years after Columbus.
    One encyclopedia places the edicts in 1633-39, a dictionary dates the policy from 1639, and another encyclopedia has it established in 1641. That is 141 to 149 years after 1492, on this project's arithmetic.
  • The founding of the Tokugawa shogunate came closer to a hundred years, but it closed nothing.
    The shogunate opened in 1603, 111 years after 1492. The closing came three decades later.
  • The closing left cotton's routes open.
    Trade continued with China, the Netherlands and, through Tsushima, Korea. Korea and China were where Japan's imported cotton came from, as the Japan question sets out.
  • Did those years have any bearing on Japanese cotton?
    Only in that they are when Japan began to grow it. Nothing read links that to the American crops. The plant was the Asian G. arboreum, and the proposed sources of its seed are Korea, Ryukyu and Ming China.
  • Japan had cotton in those years.
    Raw cotton appears in Echigo about 1494. Musashi had local cotton markets in the 1570s, and cotton is reported in Yamato in 1591.
