Were silver and copper one money or two in Ming and Qing China?

Rokugan's coins are one money in three metals: a gold koku is always worth 10 silver bu, 100 copper zeni or 1,000 sen, so a price is the same whichever coin pays it. The findings below give what the historical record holds instead for Ming and Qing China, where silver by weight and copper cash by count were two monies, each with its own rate, its own users and, at times, its own prices.

China paid in silver, weighed in taels, and in copper coins, counted in cash; the Ming also issued paper money. The state set a legal rate between silver and cash, a thousand cash to the tael under the Qing, but the market rate wandered from it for most of the dynasty and had doubled by the 1830s. Silver paid taxes and large sums, copper the daily purchases of most of the population, so when the rate moved, whoever earned in one metal and owed in the other gained or lost. The rate the law set and the rate of the market

  • The Qing fixed a thousand cash to the tael by 1653.
    In 1645 the court set 7 new coins to a hundredth of a tael, 700 to the tael, and soon, the people suffering from dear cash, 10 coins, 1,000 to the tael. A court conference in 1653 made a thousand cash to a tael the one rule for circulation, banned private mints and made law the old rule of taking tax seven parts in silver to three in cash.
  • The law could not hold the market.
    Some years after 1684, with cash still dear, the court forbade anyone to give fewer than 1,000 cash for a tael, but the rate never levelled: in the last years of Kangxi (to 1722) a tael bought 880 to 770 cash, and the state released cash from its grain-sale stores to bring cash down. In 1737 it ordered official brokers for the cash trade in the capital's two counties; before 1748, with a tael buying only 800 cash in the capital, it released cash from a Board of Works treasury. An edict of 1837 against the outflow did not stop it: silver at last drained away and a tael often bought 2,000 cash; a plan to raise cash's value against silver was blocked.
  • Old coin had its own legal rate.
    In 1702, while new and old coin passed together, a thousand new cash were set at a tael and a thousand of the old, lighter cash at 0.7 tael.
  • In the 1850s the state priced its own paper at 2,000 cash a tael.
    From 1853, to pay for war and river works, it issued silver notes in taels and cash notes in standard cash, and big coins of 10 to 1,000 cash. A one-tael note was set at 2,000 cash and 2,000 in cash notes at a tael; notes could pay up to half of a tax and the big coins up to three-tenths. Obstructing them was punished as defying an edict, and forging notes by death.
  • The notes and the big coins failed within a few years.
    The capital's official shops came to pay out notes at seven-tenths in cash, the notes' value fell further, and they stopped passing. The 1,000- and 500-cash coins were dropped first, then the 100s and 50s; the ten-cash coin lasted longest, but passed for 2 standard cash and not at all outside the capital's gates. By 1861, by a later account, the notes passed at 3% of their face; a 2016 article reports from a history of modern Chinese money that by mid-1861 the cash notes had fallen to 2.6-5.2%.
  • The Ming tied paper, copper, silver and gold by law in 1375.
    A string of paper money was a thousand cash or a tael of silver, and 4 strings a tael of gold. Trading in gold or silver was forbidden, though they could be changed for paper; two years later, sums of 100 cash or less were to be paid in coin alone, and commercial tax was taken three-tenths in cash and seven-tenths in paper. The law thus counted a tael of gold worth 4 of silver.
  • Ming paper fell far below its face.
    In the 1390s some in the south reckoned a string at 160 cash, 16% of its face; under Yongle (1403-1424) its value was a tenth of early Hongwu's; early in Xuande a shi of rice cost 50 strings; and by the Chenghua years (1465-1487) a string was not worth one cash, while tax reckoned in paper was still collected at 0.025 tael a string. The 2016 article cites the Ming court's annals for a 2,000-cash note changing for 500 cash in Jiangxi and Fujian in 1389.
  • The Ming banned silver, and then gave up.
    The ban on trading in gold and silver was renewed in 1397; in 1404 offenders were spared death but their households sent to a frontier garrison; under Xuande paying 0.1 tael of silver in a trade cost a fine of 1,000 strings. When Yingzong came to the throne in 1435, rice, silver and cash were taken in place of paper and the silver ban relaxed; in 1448 obstructing paper still cost 10,000 strings and frontier exile for the whole household, but in the Tianshun years (1457-1464) the ban was at last relaxed. Which metal paid for what

  • Silver for large sums, copper for small.
    After Yingzong came to the throne in 1435, court and people as a rule used silver, and cash only for small sums. In the Qing, a recent study writes, copper served mainly local retail, and silver taxes, official salaries, large-scale commerce and trade between regions.
  • The tax was set in silver.
    In 1436 over 4 million shi of the grain tax of the Southern Metropolitan Region and six southern provinces was commuted at 0.25 tael a shi; the rule was later applied across the realm, and four shi to a tael became the permanent rule for grain not handed to the army's canal transport. The Single Whip, applied everywhere from 1581, merged a county's land tax and labor service into one levy in silver by the mu.
  • But most farmers paid it in copper.
    About 1846 an official wrote that the state took in and paid out silver, so cash was all but useless to it; that seven or eight in ten farmers paid their tax in cash, which the county changed into silver to remit, often at a loss; that salt sold for cash; that labor and materials at the river works were paid in cash; and that soldiers paid in silver had to change it into cash to use it.
  • Salt merchants paid the state in silver and sold for copper.
    A salt censor wrote in 1728 that merchants paid the salt tax in silver while the people bought salt with cash. His rate of 2,000 cash a tael may count two cash as one: see What was a tael, a string of cash and a Chinese dollar worth in silver?
  • Even silver came in several taels.
    In the early 1900s every due to the state but customs, dues in kind and dues in cash was collected in the treasury tael of 575.8 grains of fine silver; the treasury paid out by a tael 0.2% lighter, provincial treasuries differed by up to 1%, and the tael of the provinces that paid tribute grain weighed 565.65 grains. From 1859 the Hunan Army was paid by its own scale, 103.6 of its taels to 100 of the treasury's, and Hubei, short of silver, reckoned its revenue in cash.
  • What did changing cost?
    Banks and treasuries in the early 1900s took a quarter to a half of one percent, not as a commission, but by weighing silver with one set of weights to receive and another to pay. Strings were short too: a nominal 100 cash was 97 or 98 real coins in some Hubei towns, and in the north a price of 100 cash was paid with 50 coins. Did a price depend on the coin?

  • Prices in copper held while the rate moved.
    The salt censor of 1728 wrote that under Kangxi a tael bought 1,400 or 1,500 cash and salt was 16 cash a jin; now a tael bought 2,000 and salt cost the same, some even 13 or 14 cash, so the cash no longer covered the silver owed. A rise of 0.001 tael a jin was approved in 1732. A memorial of about 1852 says that in the southeast a shi of rice sold for about 3,000 cash, little changed from of old. In rural Beijing a laborer's copper wage barely moved while the cash per tael more than doubled: see How steady were prices in Edo Japan and Qing China? The recent study likewise finds prices and wages steady in copper while the rate rose.
  • One official saw it the other way.
    The official of 1846 wrote that cash grew cheaper and prices dearer by the day, so that a soldier drawing a cash got only half a cash's worth.
  • Under Xianfeng the price depended on what you paid with.
    Anyone who brought notes to market in the capital found the seller raising the price or hiding the goods, and the official and merchants' shops changed notes only into big coins that were hard to use; merchants discounted and picked over the mint's coin at will, and traders who feared being paid in them stayed away. In 1857 a popular rising against the iron coins cost them their currency in a day, and by about 1905 the capital's ten-cash coin was rated close to its metal.
  • The state wanted goods priced in silver.
    Under Qianlong, before 1757, an edict said that goods were properly priced in silver, not cash, yet officials and people found cash handier, and profiteers gained by moving the rate; public works and wholesale purchases were to be paid in silver. Light coin passed at a legal discount, as in 1702 above.
  • Was paying in gold worth a premium or a discount?
    The Ming law of 1375 set gold at 4 strings a tael and forbade trading in it, and the ban of 1397, still enforced in 1404, covered gold and silver alike. For the Qing, no price, premium or discount for paying in gold was found.

Who won and lost when the rates moved

  • Farmers' tax doubled in rice.
    A memorial on the people's hardships says that when a tael bought 1,000 cash, a shi of rice at 3,000 cash brought 3 taels; at 2,000 it brought 1.5. Selling 3 dou of rice once more than paid a mu's tax; now 6 dou did not, so the court kept its usual levy while the people silently paid double, and the levies on house plots and graves doubled too. The page gives neither the memorial's title nor its date; reading it as Zeng Guofan's memorial of early 1852 is this project's.
  • Counties set their own conversion.
    About 1905, with a market rate of 1,200 cash to the tael, a tax set in taels and collected in cash was levied at 2,000, 3,000, 4,000 cash or more, and a tax set in cash and collected in silver at 800 or less. In one Henan county a tax in taels was turned into cash at 2,600 and back at 1,105, adding 135%, and conversions at 5,000-6,000 cash, more than five times the legal tax, were common; a magistrate who asked too much faced a riot.
  • Local officials lost when they had to remit silver.
    The official of 1846 wrote that when silver was plentiful, officials profited by collecting in cash, and now that cash was cheap they lost by changing it; he proposed paying troops in cash at no more than 1,700 and no fewer than 1,200 a tael, with shops forbidden to push the rate down on payday.
  • The state lost revenue.
    Jiangsu delivered its full tribute grain until 1835; from 1836 it reported dearth and was granted remissions year after year, silver's dearness harming officials, people and state alike.
  • Soldiers paid in silver could gain.
    Hu Linyi paid Hubei's troops half in silver and half in cash, 10 taels as 5 taels and 10,000 cash, while the market gave 1,500 cash a tael; he held that changing cash into silver only enriched merchants and that the margin should quietly go to his officers and men. The 10,000 cash in place of 5 taels is 2,000 a tael.
  • And wage earners?
    If wages and prices held in copper, as above, a wage earner paid in cash who owed nothing in silver lost little; no measured real wage through the crisis was found.
  • What did people do?
    The memorial of about 1852 names four cases in Hunan, Hubei and Jiangxi of tax resistance grown into major unrest, from the doubled silver price, over-collection and runners' abuses; Zhejiang's officials collected the next half-year's or year's tax in advance. The recent study estimates by a model that the silver shock cut China's welfare by 1.16%, most of it through taxes fixed in silver.

Against the setting's money rules

  • Rokugan's fixed coinage is a deliberate deviation.
    Rokugan fixes a koku at 10 bu, a bu at 10 zeni and a zeni at 10 sen. China's thousand cash to the tael was a legal rate the market left for most of the Qing, as Japan's three metals are in Were gold, silver and copper one money or three in Edo Japan?
  • Rokugan's coin use by size of sum is accurate.
    This project's money rules give daily life to copper and treasuries to the precious metals, as China paid taxes and large sums in silver and daily purchases in copper.
  • Should a sum in copper be converted through silver?
    This project's money rules convert each coin into silver at its era's rate. In Qing China salt in 1728, rice about 1852 and a laborer's wage held in copper while the rate doubled, with one official of 1846 dissenting, so a sum in cash may be better measured against copper prices.
