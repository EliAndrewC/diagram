Which goods' prices did foreign trade change, in Edo after the ports opened in 1859 and in Beijing in 1900-1919?

Rokugan's money rules value a koku by pricing a laborer's year of goods - the basket - in several historical eras, and they leave out of that average any good whose price foreign trade visibly changed. The findings below say, good by good, whether any source ties a price in Edo in 1860-1867 or in Beijing in 1900-1919 to foreign trade, and why soybeans in Tokyo in the 1880s were so cheap beside rice.

Japan's ports opened to foreign trade in 1859, as How steady were prices in Edo Japan and Qing China? tells, and the shogunate recoined its gold and silver in 1859-60, as Were gold, silver and copper one money or three in Edo Japan? tells; every price rose. A good counts as changed by trade here only where a source ties its price to trade, never because it rose in the same years; a price a source ties to another cause, such as the money supply or a harvest, is said so. Every ratio below that a source does not print is this project's arithmetic.

Edo 1860-1867, good by good

  • Where the prices come from: Osaka and Kyoto, not Edo.
    The one good-by-good price series read for these years, Shinbo Hiroshi's, is built from Osaka wholesale and Kyoto retail prices, with salt, silk, cedar, iron and fertilizer added from a collection of Osaka prices; no series of Edo's own prices for these years was read.
  • Why rising faster or slower than other prices proves nothing.
    Shinbo finds that prices, rising since about 1820, rose at an accelerating rate from 1859, and that this course broadly follows the amount of money in circulation; he also finds that the opening brought a kind of "price revolution", in relative prices as well as the level, and that silk and cotton goods' prices were largely set by international factors after 1859, silk jumping at once in 1859 and cotton rising steeply only from 1863. Yet raw silk, the chief export, averaged 2.95 times as much in 1859-1867 as in 1830-1858, less than the 3.39 of his index of all prices, because its rise came in one step. So a good's ratio over the period, high or low, does not by itself show a trade effect, and the ratios below are context, not tests.
  • Rice: NOT ATTESTED as changed by trade.
    The treaties let Japan keep its ban on exporting rice, and in 1870 Japan's imports peaked on high imports of rice, after the poor harvests of that year. In Shinbo's series rice fell 3.1% in 1859 itself, rose 21.8% in 1860, and rose most late, 195.8% in 1866; over the period it rose 4.00 times against the index's 3.39. No source read ties its price to trade.
  • Lamp oil and grains: ATTESTED ONLY FOR 1859-60.
    In 1860 the shogunate ordered five goods - grains (雑穀), rapeseed oil (水油), wax, dry goods and raw silk - shipped to Edo before any went to Yokohama, because as they grew rapidly into trade goods after the opening, less of them reached Edo and the hold of Edo's wholesalers on their trade loosened, which set off a surge in prices centered on export goods; from 1861 the exports of the four goods other than raw silk fell sharply. 水油 was pressed rapeseed oil, a lamp oil. The account of the order gives no price; in Shinbo's Osaka series seed oil (種油) rose 2.93 times over the period and white oil (白油), which was cottonseed oil, 2.94, both less than the index. In 1868-1875 vegetable oil was among the goods Japan imported.
  • Cooking oil: as lamp oil, ATTESTED ONLY FOR 1859-60.
    The basket's edible oil is the same rapeseed oil as its lamp oil, as What did a Kyoto laborer's year of goods cost? shows, so it takes lamp oil's evidence: the 1860 order's 水油 is that oil. No source read prices cooking oil apart from it.
  • Soybeans: SILENT on a tie to trade.
    No source read says whether the 1860 order's grains took in soybeans (大豆). In Shinbo's series soybeans rose 3.43 times, about as much as the index; a study of Japan's trade found the prices of rice and legumes had risen from 1851-53 to 1869, measured against goods that were not traded, and said this "may reflect poor weather conditions".
  • Salt, firewood and wheat: NOT ATTESTED as changed by trade.
    Over the period salt rose 2.78 times, firewood 3.22 and wheat 3.40, against the index's 3.39; no source read names a trade cause for any of them.
  • Barley: SILENT.
    Shinbo left barley out because its data are fragmentary, and no other price series of barley in these years was read.
  • Wax, which is not in the basket, for comparison: an export whose price rose by 1869.
    Measured against goods that were not traded, the prices of exports had risen from 1851-53 to 1869, the silk industry's and minor ones such as vegetable wax alike, some almost doubling.

Beijing 1900-1919: rice and millet

  • Rice and millet: SILENT on foreign trade.
    The yearly prices of Beijing's grain in these years come from Meng and Gamble's study, transcribed in a spreadsheet: rice from 1913, millet at retail from 1900. Rice's dearest year in 1913-1919 was 1.12 times its cheapest, and millet's dearest year in 1900-1919, 1912, was 1.63 times its cheapest, 1901. No source read names foreign trade as a cause of either price. The one cause of a rise a source names is domestic and after these years: in July 1920, as armies converged south of the city, its railroad to Tianjin and Hankou was cut off, grain became scarce and the price of food rose rapidly.

Why Tokyo's soybeans of the 1880s were cheap beside rice

  • The low ratio holds in every observed year from 1879 to 1895, and 1882 is its highest.
    In Kumon's yearly series a kilogram of soybeans cost 0.46 to 0.86 times a kilogram of rice in 1879-1895: 0.79 in 1879, 0.72 and 0.71 in 1880-81, 0.86 in 1882, 0.82 in 1883, 0.57-0.58 in 1884-86, 0.63-0.71 in 1887-89, 0.46 in 1890 and 0.52-0.70 in 1891-95. Bassino and Ma's figure for Tokyo, soybeans at 0.65 times rice, is their mean of 1882-1886, so it is neither the dear rice of 1880-81 nor a quirk of 1882.
  • It begins where the series changes its source.
    From 1879 Kumon takes rice and soybeans from the Long-Term Economic Statistics, whose prices of 1882-1886 Bassino and Ma describe as retail prices in Tokyo; before that his soybeans come from the Mitsui Bunko's printed price tables (to 1867 with Kusano's study of regional markets as well), and in 1872-1878 they are estimated, not recorded. The drop may therefore come from the change of series, or of the grade of soybean priced, rather than from the market; that is a GUESS of this project's, which no source read makes.
  • The ratio is lowest in 1884-1886, inside Bassino and Ma's years.
    Soybeans fell further than rice in those years, to 0.57-0.58 of its price, which pulls the five-year mean down.
  • Imports from Manchuria: SILENT.
    Beans were among Japan's imports in 1868-1875, but no source read says when soybeans from Manchuria began to reach Japan, or that they lowered Tokyo's price.
