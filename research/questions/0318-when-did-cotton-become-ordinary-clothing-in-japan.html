When did cotton become ordinary clothing in Japan?

Rokugan's tax rules set cotton beside common iron as an ordinary, low-rate good, and the setting's list of regional cash crops does not name it. The findings below give what the historical record holds for Japan.

Cotton reached Japan late and in stages. Cotton was being shipped in by the Nara period, as a few surviving pieces show, and a seed landed in 799 seems not to have lasted. From around 1400 the cloth came as an import, for the upper class and for soldiers' dress. Farmers in Japan were growing it by about 1500, and it became a commercial crop, first in the east and then around Osaka.

Before cotton, commoners wore ramie and other bast fibers. The sources place cotton as their usual clothing anywhere from the 1500s to the later Edo period, and part of that spread is region: the Hirosaki domain, in the cold north, could not grow its own.

In what century did a cotton kimono become widespread?

No source read states a century for the whole country. The reading below is this project's guess.

  • Around the Kinai and in the Tokai region, was it the 1600s?
    Probably, among farmers and townspeople. A shogunal order of 1628 already names cotton as peasants' clothing, though two sources naming no region differ: one has its popular use coming only with the transplanting of the 1500s, and a Matsue city history has its dominance in the later Edo period.
  • Across all Japan's commoners, was it the 1700s?
    Probably, with low confidence. One entry has cotton padding spreading from the middle of the Edo period; the city history's later date argues against it.
  • In the cold north, was it the end of the 1700s or later?
    In Hirosaki it was around then that nearly all but the lower-stratum peasants could get it, at least as old clothes.
  • Among low-ranking samurai, was it the 1660s?
    Only as a rule, and only in one domain: Hirosaki ordered retainers below 100 koku into cotton in 1661.

First record of the plant and the cloth

  • The earliest cloth a reference article names is a few imported Nara-period fragments.
    Two or three cotton fragments kept in the Shosoin, the imperial treasure house at Nara, show it was shipped in then.
  • The first record of the plant is a seed brought ashore in Mikawa in 799.
    One reference entry has a castaway land in Mikawa province with cotton seed in 799; another, citing the chronicle Nihon Koki, gives only the Enryaku years, 782-806. An economic historian, Kawakatsu Heita, calls him a Malay from Sumatra. The chronicle was not read.
  • How long did that cotton last?
    One source says it seems to have died out soon, with no material on cotton through the Heian period. Kawakatsu says a record of 892 described how and where it was grown, and that it had died out by the Kamakura period. A third, not naming the 799 seed, says the ancient court had cotton seed from abroad planted in various places and that this had declined by about the 1500s.

Cloth arriving mainly as an import

  • When did imported cloth begin to matter?
    One source has cotton an important import in the Song and Yuan trade from the Kamakura period on. Another finds only a few foreign pieces then and has it coming in earnest in the Muromachi period. A city history has it arriving in the 14th or 15th century, as a costly import, and a draft by Masayuki Tanimoto, marked by its author not to be quoted or cited without permission, says around 1400.
  • Cotton came first from Korea.
    Under King Sejong (1418-50) cotton became the largest item of Korea's trade with Japan, lasting to Seongjong's reign (1469-94).
  • How much came?
    The officially recorded consignments of Korean pieces include 1,539 in 1418, 27,208 in 1475, 37,421 in 1476, 85,000 in 1525 and 60,000 in 1542, and the largest are 500,000 in 1486 and 100,000 in 1488. Kawakatsu's text calls 500,000 an annual level, which his table shows for one year only.
  • When did Chinese cloth overtake Korean?
    Two reference articles have it displacing Korean cloth from the Tenbun years, 1532-55, one of them gradually. Kawakatsu says the 1570s.
  • When did imports stop mattering?
    By 1651 Tsushima was asking Korea for rice instead of cotton, since Japan's own industry met the need, and by 1774 Korean cloth had virtually ceased to be used.

First cultivation on any scale

  • Cotton was grown in Japan by about 1500.
    A reference article places the start in the late 1400s and early 1500s. Yajima Iwao, summarizing the historian Nagahara Keiji, has it spread almost nationwide, except places such as the cold Tohoku, from the end of the 1400s to the start of the 1500s, and elsewhere in the same paper within the 16th-century wars.
  • The earliest places named are Echigo and Izumo.
    About 1494 raw cotton appears in the Uesugi lands of Echigo. A Matsue city history reads a work of 1682 as showing Izumo households weaving cotton for themselves by the late 1400s at the latest.
  • What does the record of Mikawa cotton in 1510 show?
    The name appears at Nara in 1510. A local historian says the Nara chronicle has it collected as tax, and that this is held to be the oldest domestic cotton cloth in the documents; Kawakatsu says some records suggest it was sold in Nara from around the 1510s. The chronicle was not read.
  • When did the seed that took root arrive?
    Kawakatsu reports three theories, the 1490s to 1510s, 1588, and 1592-95, and suggests several arrivals. The local historian has it from Ming China in the late 1400s.

Cultivation as a regular commercial crop

  • It was sold commercially in the east first, in the 1500s.
    Mikawa's merchants carried cotton to the Kinai and sold it there. In the 1570s Musashi had local cotton markets, and about then cotton cloth was collected as tax at Kofu. By late Sengoku it grew from Kanto along the Pacific and Inland Sea coasts.
  • From the late 1500s the center moved west, to the Kinai.
    Cotton is reported in Yamato in 1591 and at Osaka in a record of 1623. Yajima, summarizing Nagahara, has the stages of the work divided, especially in Settsu, Kawachi and Izumi, by about the Kan'ei period at the latest, and the Tokai outside Mikawa, Owari and Ise, and the Kanto, losing weight.
  • How much did Kawachi make?
    Its real growth is placed in the Genroku period, 1688-1704. One dictionary reports 3 million tan a year about 1830; an encyclopedia reports over 2 million for 1830-44, not saying whether a year's output.
  • New regions rose in the 1700s.
    In Hoki on the Japan Sea, growing spread from 1764-81 and weaving from the 1780s. Yajima, summarizing Nagahara, has the San'in and the Inland Sea coast rising from the 1700s as the Kinai leveled off.

Cotton as commoners' usual clothing

  • When was cotton the usual clothing of commoners?
    The sources give four answers, none with a count behind it.
    • The 1500s: Tanimoto has its use becoming popular once it was transplanted in that century.
    • The Edo period, no date within it: one article has it wholly overcome hemp, and another entry has it used widely even among commoners.
    • The middle of the Edo period, for cotton padding.
    • The later Edo period: a Matsue city history.
  • What does the shogunate's order of 1628 show?
    It sets ordinary peasants' clothing as hemp and cotton, and Nagahara, through Yajima, takes that to mean cotton was already readily obtainable. A booklet adds that headmen and peasant wives could go as far as pongee. The order is a ceiling, and shows what was allowed, not who wore what.
  • Traces of cotton in use come from about 1600 to the middle of the 1600s.
    William Adams wrote in the Keicho years (1596-1615) that Japan had so much cotton that imported calico found no demand. A farm book, a volume of a chronicle of a late-Sengoku lord of Iyo, which one historian says depicts about the mid-1600s, has a farm wife weaving cotton in winter for New Year clothes.
  • Who wore Kawachi's cloth?
    An undated entry on the province says Osaka shop apprentices were issued it and that the cotton clothes of Kyoto and Osaka were nearly all of it.
  • Was early cotton a winter cloth?
    Ono Koji, as Kawakatsu quotes him, held so: hemp served for summer, and the first homemade cottons were thick.

What cotton replaced, and how fast

  • Cotton replaced hemp and ramie, not silk.
    Ramie had been the mainstay of medieval clothing, and a museum's note says bast fiber was the usual clothing until cotton came at the end of the Middle Ages. Silk stayed with the ruling class and the wealthy. Ramie's labor against cotton's is in how long cloth took to make.
  • Hemp and ramie did not vanish.
    Ramie went on being grown where cotton did not suit the climate. Akita around 1810 still produced ramie and hemp.
  • How fast did the change go, and what of kudzu?
    No source read gives a rate, or any share for kudzu or other bast fibers.

The cold north against the warm regions

  • The north bought its cotton.
    Hirosaki, too cold to grow it, depended on what came from elsewhere, and Akita around 1810 had no cotton growing or market weaving. Ginned cotton was reaching Kanto and Tohoku already in the 1600s, and a guild selling raw cotton north formed in 1658.
  • Were Tsugaru's peasants forbidden cotton?
    The sources disagree. Aomori Prefecture's page says flatly that in the Edo period they were not permitted to wear cotton clothing. The Hirosaki domain's orders, as a historian lists them, allow cheap cotton on festival days in 1703, cotton for the wealthy and old and for others' underclothes and sashes in 1724, homewoven cotton with hemp the rule in 1777, and both from 1790.
  • What did Tsugaru's peasants wear?
    From diary cases of 1757-60 the historian concludes that the ordinary dress was short hemp jackets and leggings all year, with padded clothes or cotton underclothes in the cold. A traveler in 1788 heard of no padded clothes kept by peasants below the middling rank, who wore up to three layers of quilted hemp, and reported those of middling rank and up buying old clothes from Osaka.
  • A mountain village further south bought it too.
    At Kashimo in Mino, cotton did not suit the climate, so villagers bought it from merchants.

By class

  • At first cotton was for the upper class and for soldiers.
    It began as a luxury of the ruling class, such as warriors, and in the Sengoku wars took on a strong character as war material.
  • What were low-ranking samurai told to wear?
    A Hirosaki order of 1661 allowed retainers of 100 koku and up silk, pongee and cotton, and those below cotton only. In 1711 those of 300 koku and under were to wear cotton, and in 1807 all retainers. In later Morioka, low retainers and below were allowed only hemp-type cloth and cotton. Nothing read speaks to them before 1661.
  • Northern townspeople were to wear cotton.
    Hirosaki's rule says so in principle, and Morioka's says so. Hirosaki's main buyers were retainers and townspeople, with peasants seeming to join the market from about the end of the 18th century.
  • Edo's commoners wore it secondhand.
    An undated popular account says their everyday clothes were cotton from old-clothes dealers.

Shares of households or clothing

  • What share of households wore cotton?
    No source read gives one, for any region or century.
  • How did Akita clothe itself around 1810?
    Tanimoto's table for 1808-10 gives yearly supply for a population of 450,000. On this project's arithmetic it was about 31% new cotton cloth, 32% ginned cotton, 21% used cloth of no stated fiber and 15% hemp or ramie, about 1.2 tan a head.
  • How much cotton reached Hirosaki?
    Aomori port landed 52,460 tan in the year from the 10th month of 1850, against a domain population of 221,496 in 1853, enough, its historian notes, for one tan a head for retainers and Hirosaki's townspeople. Over the whole domain that is about 0.24 tan a head, on this project's arithmetic, from one port.

Prices are in what cloth cost and what hemp cloth cost, and household weaving in a farm household's unpaid work.
