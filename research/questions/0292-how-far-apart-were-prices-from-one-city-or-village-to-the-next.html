How far apart were prices from one city or village to the next?

Rokugan's money rules give each era's prices as one place's prices, and a price from elsewhere borrows a nearby place's row. The findings below give what the historical record holds instead: how far the same good's price stood apart in Edo-period Japan and Qing China, from one city to the next and between town and country, at the same time.

Rice prices in the great cities of Edo Japan moved together and stood close: Kyoto and Osaka within a few percent, Edo a little above. Farther out the gap widened, most of all in the cold northeast, where the domains' rice stood about a third below Osaka's. Other goods stood farther apart than rice, by a third or more for firewood. In Qing China prices moved together within a few hundred kilometers and hardly at all across a thousand, while the cost of a laborer's basic goods in silver stood within about a tenth from Beijing to Canton in the 1700s.

Japan: how closely prices moved together

  • Kyoto and Osaka moved in parallel.
    Where both an Osaka wholesale price and a Kyoto retail price of the same good are known, the two moved nearly in parallel, and the gap between wholesale and retail did not change much over time.
  • A nearby market followed Osaka within a day or two.
    Otsu, about 50 km from Osaka, took in Osaka's rice price within two days in the early 1800s and within a day by the mid-1800s. A letter took about six days from Edo to Osaka, about 500 km, and a day from Osaka to Otsu.
  • The west moved together; the east less.
    A study of rice in 13 markets in 1720-1857 found Japan's rice markets relatively well integrated, western Japan above all; in the east, distance from Osaka and the cold that struck rice in the northeast held integration back. It did not stop the famines of 1732-1733, 1783-1786 and 1833-1838.

Japan: how far apart the three great cities stood

The Mitsui merchant house's books price the same goods, in the same coin, in Kyoto, Edo and Osaka from 1818 to 1858. Each figure below is the average gap to Kyoto over those years, by this project's arithmetic.

  • Rice: within about 1% in Osaka and 3% in Edo.
    White rice averaged 1% above Kyoto in Osaka, swinging about it by 4% from year to year, and 3% above in Edo, swinging by 10%.
  • Fuel: Edo's firewood a third below Kyoto's, Osaka's 43% above.
    Lamp oil was 11% above in Edo and 6% above in Osaka, and candles within about 6% either way.
  • Processed foods stood far apart.
    Miso was 29% below Kyoto in Edo and 20% below in Osaka; vinegar 86% and 53% above; sake 35% above in Edo and 7% in Osaka; soy sauce 13% and 19% above.
  • Labor: Edo's day wage stood 10% below Kyoto's.
    Over the same years the house paid its Edo day laborers about a tenth less than its Kyoto ones.
  • Salt is left out.
    Kyoto's salt series falls to a third of its level between 1762 and 1791 and stays there, which reads as a change of unit, so no Kyoto salt gap is given.

Japan: rice in fourteen places

A table of the price of a koku of rice year by year in fourteen places, 1620-1867, gives the castle towns and the countryside beside the great markets. Most of its places are domain prices, which its notes say probably damped the year-to-year swings. The figures below compare each place with Osaka in its own coin, silver in the west and gold in the east, by this project's arithmetic.

  • In the silver-using west, the dearest place was about a quarter or a third above the cheapest in a typical year.
    Across the eight western places, the highest price stood 1.31 times the lowest in the median year of 1701-1800, and 1.25 times in 1801-1858.
  • Market towns stood close to Osaka.
    Otsu was 6% below Osaka in 1739-1800 and 3% below in 1801-1858, its yearly changes almost the same as Osaka's. Rural Banshu was within 5%, and the merchant books of Hiroshima 3-4% above.
  • Western castle towns stood a little below.
    The domain prices of Fukuchiyama, Bocho, Saga and Kumamoto were 3% to 13% below Osaka, Saga the lowest.
  • In the east, Edo and Nagoya stood above Osaka and the northeast a third below.
    Edo's price was 6-8% above Osaka's, Nagoya's 13-18% above, while Shinshu, Aizu and Dewa were 21% to 34% below, and moved least with Osaka; the dearest eastern place was 1.7 to 1.9 times the cheapest in the median year.

Japan: town against country

  • Wages: a village near Kyoto paid about what the city did.
    In 1802-1804 a farm worker's spring rate in Kami-Kawarabayashi, a village a little over 40 km from Kyoto, was 1.0 monme a day, against Kyoto's 0.92 for casual labor. The city's wage indices swung more than the village's.
  • A good's price in a village and in a city, set side by side by one source: SILENT.
    No source read sets a village's price of a good beside a city's in the same year. The record's nearest are a mountain village's salt of the 1760s and 1770s (What did salt cost?), which no Kyoto salt price can be set against (above), and rural Banshu's rice, which stood within 5% of Osaka's.

China: together nearby, apart at a distance

The Qing price reports were kept in many local silver and copper monies, so studies of them measure how closely prices moved together rather than how far their levels stood apart.

  • Rice prices moved together within a few hundred kilometers, and hardly at all beyond a thousand.
    In 1770-1794 the rice prices of Lower Yangzi prefectures less than 150 km apart had an average correlation of 0.83, and 0.81 at 150-300 km; along the Yangzi, 0.66 at 450-600 km. Between provincial capitals it fell from 0.59 at 150-300 km to 0.08 beyond 1,050 km. In England it was 0.87 under 150 km.
  • The Lower Yangzi and Canton were poorly integrated.
    The rice prices of Suzhou and Guangzhou were weakly correlated in the 1700s.
  • Levels: Beijing, Suzhou and Canton stood within about a tenth in silver.
    The cost of a bare-bones year of a laborer's goods showed very little difference between Beijing and Suzhou in the 1700s, nor Canton. Canton's stood 10% below Suzhou's on average in 1730-1799 and 1% below in 1800-1839; Beijing's, a reconstruction from other cities' prices, 8% below in the 1700s.
  • Level spreads good by good: SILENT.
    No source read gives the same good's price in two Qing cities in the same year as a typical gap.

What drove the gaps

  • Distance and carriage.
    What carriage cost by water and by land, how far goods traveled before carriage priced them out, and what tea and famous goods cost far from where they were made are their own questions: What did carrying goods cost by water, against by land?, How far did goods travel before carriage priced them out?, How did tea travel? and What did a famous good cost far from where it was made?
  • The state's monopoly, for salt.
    How far China's official salt rose on the way from the works is in What did salt cost?; the monopolies of Japan's domains are in How did a good's price change from one dynasty or era to the next?
  • Climate, in Japan's northeast.
    Cold years struck rice there hardest, and the region's markets were less tied to Osaka.

Against the setting's money rules

  • Borrowing a nearby city's price is close for rice, looser for other goods and for remote places.
    Among the western markets, a year's rice price in one place stood within about a quarter of another's, and Kyoto's, Osaka's and Edo's within a few percent on average; but firewood, miso and vinegar stood a third or more apart between the great cities, and the northeast's rice a third below Osaka's. That spread is the honest size of treating an era's prices as one place's.
