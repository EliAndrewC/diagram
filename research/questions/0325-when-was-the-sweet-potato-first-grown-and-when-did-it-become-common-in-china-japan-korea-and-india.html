When was the sweet potato first grown, and when did it become common, in China, Japan, Korea and India?

Rokugan's notes mention no potato of any kind, though they list the yam (imo) among the season words of autumn. The findings below give what the historical record holds for the sweet potato in China, Japan, Korea and India.

The sweet potato is an American plant, of a different botanical family from the shu-yu tubers (薯蓣) China has had since antiquity. It reached southern China in the later 1500s, and Japan by way of the Ryukyu islands early in the 1600s; Korean sources disagree on whether it came by 1633 or in 1763, and no source read dates its arrival in India. Where it became common it did so slowly, on this project's arithmetic from the dates below: about 130 to 160 years to a daily food of the poor in Fujian and Guangdong, about 80 years to wide planting around Satsuma and Nagasaki, and more than 130 years to planting across Korea. Tobacco, which spread far faster, is in when tobacco was first grown, dry-field crops in general in dry fields and their crops, and other commercial crops in cash crops on rice land.

China

  • Which plant do the Chinese words mean?
    The agricultural writer Xu Guangqi separates fan-shu (番薯), brought recently from overseas, from shan-shu (山薯), a native plant that climbs, by how each grows, looks and tastes, and says that every Chinese book's shu (薯) means shan-shu. The historian Pan Honggang warns that the new plant was then often called gan-shu (甘薯) or shu-yu (薯芋) too, so that the names were mixed. The dates below are for the sweet potato only as far as each source's word and context allow.
  • When is the sweet potato first recorded in China?
    Three dates are given, and no text of any of those years was read:
    • 1563: a magazine summary by Jacqueline Newman has the historian Ho Ping-ti documenting it that year as the "red tuber". Ho's own article was not read, so the identification rests on Newman.
    • 1582: a modern encyclopedia of Chinese farming says fan-shu reached Xiaojie in Dongguan in Wanli 10.
    • 1593: the Chinese Wikipedia article has Chen Zhenlong of Changle bringing it from the Philippines to Fujian.
    Newman also has Ho placing it in China, from the Philippines, before 1594, when it helped to stave off starvation. Hobson-Jobson, the glossary of British India, reports a far earlier claim, that Chinese books of the 3rd or 4th century describe the plant as kan-chu, against Xu's statement that China's books mean shan-shu.
  • The route is disputed.
    The Guangdong writer Qu Dajun says Luzon. Berthold Laufer, in his history of tobacco, also has it obtained on Luzon. Ho, in Newman's summary, points two ways: the Philippines in one sentence, and in another unsure of India or Manila but certain that it came first into Yunnan. Pan lists the Philippines, Annam and the Ryukyus, and Chinese Wikipedia lists three paths at once, by India and Burma to Yunnan, Vietnam to Guangdong and the Philippines to Fujian.
  • Where was it first grown in China?
    Xu names Fujian and Guangdong, where, by the local report he passes on, the plant was divided and planted after a man smuggled its vine past a ban overseas, wound into a well rope. Dongguan is the one planting place the modern sources name. Chinese Wikipedia names Changle only as Chen's home county, and adds that the governor of Fujian, Jin Xuezeng, promoted the plant against famine.
  • By 1639 Fujian and Guangdong were relying on it against famine.
    Xu says the people of the two provinces depended on it to relieve hunger, and that the gain was great. He also says they ate shu in place of grain from the tenth month to the fourth, until the wheat ripened, though his word there is only shu. His treatise was published in 1639. Pan says that between the late Ming and the Kangxi reign it became fairly common in some southern provinces. In the early Qing, the encyclopedia says, it was the coarse grain second only to rice in the Pearl River delta. Qu describes Guangdong's tubers, fan-shu among them, as eaten in a "shu-rice" that supplemented grain, and fan-shu diced, steamed and dried for storing as "shu-grain".
  • By the 1720s it was a daily food of the poor in Fujian and Guangdong.
    Pan quotes two reports to the throne. In 1725 the governor of Fujian wrote that the poor of the Quanzhou coast suffered when the winter's crop was poor and rice was dear. In 1728 the governor-general of Guangdong and Guangxi wrote that the commoners of Chaozhou grew it in place of rice, that in that season's great harvest it sold at 1 copper cash a jin there and 7 for 10 jin around Huanggang and Jieshi, and that a day's food for one person cost at most 1 or 2 cash. Pan concludes that before the Qianlong reign it grew mainly in those two provinces and had become the daily food of the lower classes. 1725 is 132 years after the 1593 date, 143 after 1582 and 162 after 1563.
  • For most of the Qing it was a small share of China's fields.
    The historian Li Xinsheng reports that for most of the Qing it stayed under 1% of the cultivated area, and that the value of the American crops showed itself only after the mid-1800s. Pan describes it spreading further north from the Qianlong reign, with the governor of Anhui requiring it in 1747. Locally it could be a leading crop: at Fuzhou in the 1860s, the missionary Justus Doolittle names it with rice and wheat as the most common crops, and says that great quantities were dried as "potato-rice", as food for the poor when rice could not be had.

Japan

  • Which plant do the Japanese words mean?
    The Japanese Wikipedia article gives kansho (甘藷), kara-imo (唐芋), Ryukyu-imo (琉球薯) and bansho (蕃薯) as names of Ipomoea batatas. It also reports that the scholar Kaibara Ekiken, in 1708, treated bansho and kansho (甘薯) as two plants, which this project takes to mean that a date under either name is not certainly a date for the other.
  • When did the sweet potato reach Japan?
    The article says it came from China to the Ryukyus early in the 1600s and on to Satsuma, and doubts a claimed date of 1597 for Miyako island. It allows that the plant may also have reached Kyushu ports and Sakai directly, earlier still. It reports a later Satsuma book placing it at the port of Bonotsu between 1596 and 1623, brought from Luzon in trade with the Portuguese. It reports William Adams bringing tubers from the Ryukyus to Hirado in 1615.
  • The first dated planting on record is at Hirado in 1615.
    The English merchant Richard Cocks wrote in his diary that he took a garden that day and planted it with "Pottatos brought from the Liquea, a thing not yet planted in Japan". Hobson-Jobson reads his "potato" as the sweet potato. The Japanese article places his field at Hirado, says he called it "Ryukyu-imo", and of the early routes in general that most did not take; it does not say how his planting ended. It says the plant is held to have first settled on the main islands in Satsuma.
  • A writer of the 1690s has it widely grown around Satsuma and Nagasaki, and unknown elsewhere.
    The article reports the agricultural writer Miyazaki Yasusada saying this in 1697, a year its era name does not match, and it says he may never have seen the plant. Other dates there are 1698 for a basket sent to Tanegashima's lord by the Ryukyu king, and 1705 or 1709 for a seaman's tubers in Satsuma. From 1615 that is 82 years to 1697.
  • It left Kyushu in the 1710s, and famine proved its worth in the 1730s.
    The article says a monk carried it from Satsuma to Omishima, in Iyo, in 1711 (its era name would make that 1713), and that in the famine of 1732 no one around Omishima starved. The scholar Aoki Konyo, on the shogun's order, had it sent from Satsuma to Edo for trial planting, and growing spread in eastern Japan from there. The article gives 1735 for this in one place, and 1734 in another, with success confirmed in 1735.
  • Edo's commoners knew it by 1773.
    The article infers this from a 1773 joke book with a story about a potato seller, which shows a trade in it and that it was familiar to commoners. That is 158 years after Hirado and 38 after 1735. No source read counts how much was grown or eaten.

Korea

  • Which plant do the Korean words mean?
    Gamjeo (감저, 甘藷), jojeo (조저, 趙藷) and namgamjeo (남감저, 南甘藷) are all goguma (고구마), Ipomoea batatas, in the Encyclopedia of Korean Culture. Gamjeo is not to be confused with gamja (감자), the word another article of the encyclopedia uses to gloss the northern tuber bukjeo (북저). That plant has its own page, when the white potato was first grown.
  • When is it first recorded in Korea?
    The encyclopedia says the royal annals record, in 1663, a castaway who saw it eaten abroad and reported that growing it would prevent hunger.
  • When was it first planted in Korea? The encyclopedia's own articles disagree.
    Both sides:
    • 1763: the article on the plant reports the Unseok yugo, that the envoy Jo Eom obtained seed at Tsushima that year and had it tried at Dongnae and on Jeju, and it says that planting began only in the later 1700s.
    • About 1633 or earlier: the article on the scholar Seo Gyeong-chang reports his Jongjeobang of 1813, that the plant is estimated to have arrived in the reigns of Seonjo and Gwanghaegun, and that the Border Defense Council worked to spread it in 1633. The article says this tells against 1763.
    Neither of the older texts was read, and no source read reconciles them.
  • Who first made it grow?
    The encyclopedia says the magistrate of Dongnae, Kang Pil-li, had some success there and wrote a book on it, Korea's first on the plant, after Yi Gwang-ryeo's own attempts failed. It gives no year for his crop.
  • It was grown across Korea only from the 1900s.
    The encyclopedia says so, after trials had carried it from the south as far as Seoul. The government had Seo's book printed and sent to the counties in 1814. The 1900s are at least 137 years from 1763. It was valued as a famine crop, and no source read says how much of the poor's food it made.

India

  • When did the sweet potato reach India?
    No source read gives a date. A writer named Achaya, as a blog quotes him, thinks the "potatoes" named at Ajmer in 1615 and in gardens in 1675 were perhaps sweet potatoes, "known much earlier in India", which the passages the blog reproduces do not date. The blogger adds that most accounts take the 1675 plant to be the white potato. Hobson-Jobson says that one word, alu, could mean the common potato or the sweet potato.
  • When was it first grown in India?
    No source read says.
  • When Hobson-Jobson was compiled it was grown in most parts of India.
    Hobson-Jobson says so, and that in the Bengal Presidency it was commonly called shakar-kand. No source read says when it became common or who ate it.
