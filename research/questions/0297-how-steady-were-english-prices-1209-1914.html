How steady were English prices, 1209-1914?

Rokugan's prices are reckoned in koku by its own money rules, and no figure here sets a koku's value or a Rokugani price. This is a comparative question: how far English prices moved from 1209 to 1914, a yardstick for players who know the shillings of Austen and Dickens, set beside the Edo, Qing and present-day figures elsewhere in the record.

The Bank of England publishes a consumer price index for England, and later Britain, running from 1209 to 2016; Gregory Clark compiled the yearly prices of single goods and day wages in England from 1209 to 1914. Read together they show the same split as the Edo and Qing series: prices that drifted little over a century, but swung hard from one year to the next. The figures below are this project's arithmetic, measured as in How steady were prices in Edo Japan and Qing China, from one day to the next century?: the swing is the standard deviation of the change from one year to the next (roughly, two changes in three fall within that percentage, up or down), and the drift is the average change a year over the whole span.

The drift: almost none for three centuries, then one rise that stayed

  • Before 1500: a fraction of a percent a year.
    The price level drifted by +0.20% a year over 1210-1499; it ended the 1300s 1.01 times where it began them, and the 1400s 0.90 times, about +0.01% and -0.10% a year.
  • The 1500s: the one long inflation that was never undone.
    Prices ended the 1500s 3.99 times where they began them, about 1.4% a year, and drifted by +1.22% a year over 1500-1650. The 1600s rose 1.41 times and the 1700s 2.18 times, but the 1800s fell back to 0.67 times their start, so the later rise did not last as the 1500s' did.
  • After 1650: a drift within half a percent of zero.
    The price level drifted by -0.37% a year over 1651-1750, +0.41% over 1751-1913 and -0.09% over 1821-1913.
  • What the index is made of.
    The Bank names its column's sources from 1661 on: the Schumpeter-Gilboy index to 1750, then the series of Crafts and Mills and of Feinstein, then the official statistics of the ONS from 1914. Before 1661 the column is Clark's English cost of living index, rescaled: in every year from 1209 to 1660 it stands at the same fraction, 0.01259, of Clark's figure, by this project's check.

The swing: about a tenth a year for the price level, a quarter for grain

  • The price level: about 10% a year before 1650, falling to 4% by the 1800s.
    From one year to the next the price level moved by 11.4% in 1210-1499 (over 290 pairs of years), 9.9% in 1500-1650 (151), 7.0% in 1651-1750 (100), 6.4% in 1751-1913 (163) and 4.3% in 1821-1913 (93). It rose or fell by more than 10% in 97 of the 290 years of 1210-1499, 42 of 151 in 1500-1650, 14 of 100 in 1651-1750, 17 of 163 in 1751-1913 and 4 of 93 in 1821-1913.
  • Over ten years: a wider swing still, around almost no trend.
    Over the 280 ten-year spans of 1210-1499, the price level moved by an average of +1.6%, with a standard deviation of 17.8%.
  • Single goods: grain swung most, wool cloth and wages least.
    Clark's series give each good's price and each day wage year by year. In the table, each figure is the swing from one year to the next, with the number of pairs of years it rests on; the last column is the largest rise and fall from one year to the next over the whole series. Salt swung as hard as grain before 1500; the farm wage swung twice as far as the building laborer's in 1500-1650, 12.5% against 6.2%; and charcoal and wool cloth steadied most after 1650, to 4.8% and 2.6% in 1651-1750.

Beside Edo, the Qing and today

  • Like Edo and the Qing: a wide swing around little drift.
    English wheat moved by 17-27% from one year to the next, where Mitsui's rice in Kyoto, Edo and Osaka moved by 15-17% before 1859; England's price level moved by 6-7% a year in 1651-1913, where the cost of a bare-bones year of goods moved by 9.6% a year in Suzhou and 8.1% in Canton; and English firewood moved by 9-22% a year (9.0-21.5%), where Mitsui's firewood, the steadiest of its goods, moved by 3-7%. Mitsui's rice in Kyoto and Osaka drifted by less than 1% a decade before 1859, and England's price level by a fraction of a percent a year before 1500 and after 1650.
  • Today: a drift of several percent a year, and a smaller swing.
    In 1984-2016 the British price level drifted by +2.72% a year and moved by 1.6% from one year to the next, never by more than 10%; in 1972-2016 it drifted by +5.00% a year and moved by 4.6%, by more than 10% in 7 of 45 years. Over the 1900s it rose 64.29 times, about 4.25% a year. The US price level moved by 2.8% over twelve months in 1972-2024 and 1.3% in 1984-2019, and drifted by 3.9% a year from 1971, in How steady are prices today, under paper money?

What the files leave open

  • Clark's figures are his as of 2006.
    The file's own caveat says they may be revised.
  • A year with no price is left out.
    Some years carry no price for a good: 1210 has none for any good in the table. A change is measured only between two years that both have a price, so the pairs counted fall short of the years in a period: 262 for wheat in the 290 years of 1210-1499, 123 for charcoal.
  • Some prices repeat from one year to the next.
    Over the whole series, charcoal's price is unchanged in 46 of its 475 pairs of years and wool cloth's in 45 of 538, against 1 of 677 for wheat and none of 621 for the farm wage. The file does not say whether a repeated price is one that held or a gap its compiler filled; that the prices repeat is this project's observation, and each repeat counts as a change of nothing, which lowers the swing measured.
  • The index is Britain's; only its years before 1661 are England's alone.
    The Bank gives its column's coverage as Great Britain and the United Kingdom, where the Clark column, from which its early years are rescaled, covers England.
