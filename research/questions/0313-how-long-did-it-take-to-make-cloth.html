Cotton

  • How long did a tan of cotton take in Japan?
    Four days from ginned cotton - two spinning, one on "preparation" and one weaving - in Niikawa, in the mid-1800s, by a table in a draft by the economic historian Masayuki Tanimoto, marked not to be quoted or cited without his permission; the table names no source.
  • How much did a household weave in a year?
    In Niikawa a household of slightly under five wove 12.8 tan a year for its own use, 51.2 days of work, and 39.4 tan for sale, 157.6 days: one woman for about seven months. The merchants paid for the cloth in ginned cotton, not money, and the cotton paid in, the author suggests, may have matched what the household needed to clothe itself.
  • How long did a bolt take in China?
    Seven days with hand-spun yarn, in the eighteenth-century Yangzi delta: four spinning, one weaving and two fluffing the cotton, sizing the yarn and the other steps, as the historian Philip Huang puts it and Kenneth Pomeranz accepts. A bolt was 3.63 square yards. In a draft he marked not to be cited, Pomeranz counts 3 days a bolt with machine-spun yarn after 1900, 7 with hand-spun, and in one note only 6; spinning took half or four-sevenths of the time from ginned cotton to cloth, by the wheel used, and almost four hours of spinning were needed for every hour of weaving.
  • How fast could a weaver work?
    One person could weave one duan of cloth a day, and some two, around Shanghai under the Qing, by the historian Zheng Tianting's account, which cites no count for it. The farm book of 1658 has a common farm wife make two bolts of cloth a day, or spin eight liang of cotton yarn.
  • When did a woman spin?
    At night, in a poet's verses on the Shanghai country, which the historian quotes: "the women suffer most in farm households," sweating at noon hoeing the cotton field and spinning yarn by night, hungry, among the mosquitoes; the poet's own note says the farm women had no midday rest in high summer and spun again at night to trade the yarn for rice. Silk, ramie and hemp

  • How much silk could one person reel in a day?
    Thirty liang of silk with a full day's strength, and of headband silk, reeled from fewer cocoons, only twenty, by a Chinese handbook of crafts of 1637, the Tiangong kaiwu. No time was found for weaving a tan or a bolt of silk, in Japan or China, beyond the Huzhou farm manual of the last years of the Ming, printed with the farm book of 1658, whose two women weave 120 bolts a year.
  • How slow was ramie?
    Splitting the ramie fiber took a full day for only 3 to 5 zhu by weight, by the same handbook, though a skilled woman spinning ramie on a treadle wheel did the work of three. That is about 5 to 8 grams of fiber a day, by this project's arithmetic.
  • How much longer was ramie than cotton in Japan?
    Nearly ten times the labor for a tan, from raw ramie, spinning and weaving included, by the historian Nagahara Keiji's estimate as Tanimoto reports it; another historian writes that ramie is said to have taken ten times the labor of cotton from growing to clothing. No count of the days for a tan of hemp, ramie or silk in Japan was found.

Made or bought?

  • Where did Akita's cloth come from?
    In the Akita region, which grew no cotton, in 1808-1810, a year's cloth for 450,000 people was 174,397 tan of cotton cloth brought in, ginned cotton for about 180,000 tan, 117,101 pieces of used cloth, and hemp and ramie grown at home for about 84,000 tan. Farm households ran the whole making of hemp and ramie cloth, from growing to weaving, for their own use, and the women spun and wove the bought cotton.
