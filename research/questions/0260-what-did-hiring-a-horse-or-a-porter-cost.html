What did hiring a horse or a porter cost?

Rokugan's travelers and merchants hire horses and porters on its roads, and the setting judges a price by how many days of a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give the fares Edo-period Japan's post stations charged, and one hire in Qing China, each set beside the nearest day wage found.

On Japan's main highways a post station's horses carried goods only to the next station, where the shipper paid the fare and changed horses. Travelers on the shogun's warrant or a senior councilor's certificate rode free, and those on the shogunate's business paid the low official fare the shogunate fixed, and everyone else, or anyone who came when the official horses were used up, paid a negotiated fare that seems to have run about twice the official one. No price was found in Japan for hiring a horse with its driver, or a cart, by the day.

The official fares by the stage

Three loads were priced: a pack horse (honma) with a full horse-load, at first 30 kan (about 113 kg, ~250 lb) and later 40; a light horse (karajiri) carrying a rider with 5 kan of hand baggage; and a porter carrying 5 kan. The fares below are per stage, between two stations; the per-ri figures beside them are this project's arithmetic, at 36 cho to a ri (one ri is about 3.9 km, ~2.4 miles).

  • What were the fares in 1665?
    From Hamamatsu on the Tokaido, after an increase the stations had asked for that year, a pack horse cost 83 mon and a light horse 54 to the next station, Maisaka, and 151 and 104 to Mitsuke; at the stage's distance in the 1820s, 2 ri and a half and 10 cho, that is about 30 mon a ri for a pack horse and 19 for a light horse. The 20 percent increase of the 10th month of that year was withdrawn before the year was out. At Takamiya, on the Nakasendo, the station's statement of the same year gives three fares for the 1.5 ri to Toriimoto, 40, 27 and 20 mon, which by their proportions this project reads as a pack horse, a light horse and a porter: about 27, 18 and 13 mon a ri.
  • How did the three loads compare?
    By the shogunate's rules for the Nakasendo of 1665, a porter carried up to 5 kan (about 19 kg, ~41 lb); one load, which the order as quoted does not name, went at the light horse's fare up to 5 kan and at the pack horse's above it; and at night a light horse was charged as a pack horse.
  • What was the base fare from 1711?
    The shogunate fixed a base fare in 1711, the fare of 1690 raised by 30 percent in 1707 and then kept; later increases were surcharges added to it. From Maisaka to Hamamatsu, 2 ri and a half and 10 cho, the base fare for a pack horse was 125 mon, about 45 mon a ri.
  • And in the 1820s?
    With the surcharges of 1819 and 1824, together 50 percent, the same pack horse cost 186 mon, about 67 mon a ri.
  • And at the negotiated fare?
    About twice the official fare, the historian says, hedging it ("seems").

The return

  • Did the hirer pay for the return?
    Not on the official relay. A station's horse carried only to the next station, where the shipper paid and took a fresh horse, so, this project reads, each fare paid for one way only. The way back was the station's loss: in 1853 Shinagawa station gave as a chief reason for asking a 50 percent surcharge that its men and horses that went into Edo had no earnings on the way back.
  • And on a hire by the day?
    No record was found.

The day's wage beside these fares

  • What did an unskilled laborer earn where these fares were paid?
    No unskilled day wage was found for the Tokaido or the Nakasendo, and none in copper mon. The nearest found, all in silver, are a laborer's 0.8 to 1.2 monme a man in Osaka in 1628, 37 years before the fares of 1665 (What did a torii, stonework and earthwork cost?); and the Kyoto day laborer's wage in the Mitsui house's accounts, 2.57 g of silver in 1741, 30 years after the base fare of 1711, and 3.02 to 3.97 g in the years of the 1820s surcharge, 1824 to 1829.
  • Why are the two not set in one unit?
    The fares are in copper mon and the wages in silver, and this record does not yet give the rate between the two coins, so neither is converted.

China

  • What did hiring a mule cost?
    The one price found is a private hire. In August 1863 a traveler setting out from Peking hired eight pack mules at 4 taels each, and three mule litters at 8 taels each, for the four days and about 400 li to Kalgan, after the dealers had first said there were no mules and then offered them at what he called slightly exorbitant prices. A pack mule cost about 1 tael a day of the journey. He does not say whether the price took in the mules' return.
  • What did a laborer earn beside it?
    The nearest wage found is Beijing's, about 0.09 taels a day at its level of 1700, so a pack mule's day was about 11 days of a laborer's pay. A rural Beijing series of 1865, wages from a fuel store's account books at 0.026 taels a day, is nearer in time, but the study calls its level very low, since the workers were also given food, and uses it only for its trend.
  • What were the official rates?
    Not fares a traveler paid, but what Zhili, the capital's province, could claim back from the Ministry of Works for hauling to its public building, under the province's regulation whose memorial is dated 1769. Its first chapter allows, for a cart over 100 li, 1.0 tael of silver for a big cart of 2,500 jin and 0.7 for a small cart of 1,200 at Liangxiang; 0.8 and 0.6 at Yongqing, for 2,000 and 1,200 jin; 1.2 and 0.9 at Xianghe, for 1,500 and 700 jin; and 0.8 and 0.5 at Tongzhou, for 1,000 and 300 jin.
  • What did a laborer earn beside them?
    The same chapter sets the day wages of the same four districts, in the same silver: a craftsman 0.12 to 0.15 tael and a laborer 0.06 to 0.09. So a big cart's 100 li came to about 9 to 17 days of a laborer's pay and 5 to 9 of a craftsman's, district by district, and a small cart's to about 6 to 12 of a laborer's, this project's arithmetic.
  • What did a porter or a boat cost at the official rate?
    The rates found are for Hunan province, under a memorial presented in the 12th month of the Qianlong emperor's 33rd year, early 1769. In Changsha prefecture, at its counties of Changsha and Shanhua, a porter was allowed 0.003 tael of silver a li for 80 jin, and a boat 0.002 tael every 10 li for 100 jin. A note under the two says they are the rules already in force, matching the rates the counties' register sent, and that later building works were to enter the weight of the materials and the li of road in their accounts. Like Zhili's, these are what official building could claim back, not fares a traveler paid.
  • What did a laborer earn beside them?
    The same page sets a craftsman's day at 0.04 tael and a laborer's at 0.03, again the rules in force checked against the counties' register; the laborer's rate is written per man, which the database of Zhili's edition reads as a man's workday. So a porter's 100 li came to 0.3 tael, 10 days of a laborer's pay and 7.5 of a craftsman's, and a boat's 100 li for 100 jin to 0.02 tael, two-thirds of a laborer's day, this project's arithmetic.
  • And in Zhili?
    Zhili's regulation prices a porter, a pack animal and a boat too, in the districts of Tianjin, but they are not given here. The database that transcribes it labels these rates per li for some carriers and per 100 li for others, even in one county, and since the Hunan original writes its unit row by row, per li for the porter and per 10 li for the boat, one unit cannot be assumed for a whole column. No public scan of the Zhili original was found to read it.
  • And a pack animal?
    Only for timber, among the rates whose unit can be read. In Gansu province, under its regulation of the same year, a donkey carried 120 jin (about 72 kg, ~160 lb) of timber at 0.001 tael a li at Qinzhou and three of its counties, where a laborer got 0.04 tael a day (and at Qinzhou a craftsman 0.05, in What did carrying stone cost, by cart and by porter?); 100 li came to 0.1 tael, 2.5 days of a laborer's pay, this project's arithmetic. Hunan's Changsha rows are the porter and the boat only, and Zhili's are left out for the reason above.
