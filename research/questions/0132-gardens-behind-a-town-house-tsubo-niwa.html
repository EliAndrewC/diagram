Gardens behind a town house (tsubo-niwa)

A merchant's town house in Kyoto kept a garden inside its own plan: the tsubo-niwa, a small court open to the sky, one or two tsubo in size. Houses built close together were short of daylight and air, so a piece of the house was left open between it and its neighbor. Many Kyoto town houses had a planted garden at the back as well. In China a courtyard house planted its courtyard with fruit and flowering trees.

  • How big was a tsubo-niwa?
    It is described as a small garden of about one to two tsubo, seen mainly in Kyoto's town houses and hermitages. A tsubo is one ken square, about 6 ft on a side, so the court was about 6 by 6 to 6 by 12 ft.
  • The tsubo-niwa let daylight and air into houses built close together.
    Town houses standing close together had trouble with daylight and ventilation, so a small garden was made between the house and its neighbor, as though a part of the house had been cut away.
  • The tsubo-niwa arose with the town house, at the start of the early modern period.
    It appeared in the first part of the early modern period, when town-house building developed.
  • In a Kyoto town house the tsubo-niwa lay toward the back, with only the storehouse behind it.
    The house's long plan ran from the shop room on the street through the middle room, the kitchen and the back room to the washroom, privy and bath, and then the tsubo-niwa and the storehouse. The storehouse was fireproofed.
  • Many Kyoto town houses also had a back garden, the zashiki garden.
    The account of Kyoto's town houses says many had this back garden, and that its planting was called senzai.
  • A Kyoto town house with a separate shop block had a court on either side of the block joining shop and house.
    In the omoteya-zukuri form, the open entrance court and a tsubo-niwa lay on either side of the entrance block joining the shop block to the main house.
  • How common was the tsubo-niwa outside Kyoto?
    The one page that places it says it was seen mainly in Kyoto's town houses and hermitages. No page we read says how common it was in a provincial market town: the pages describe Kyoto's town houses.

A town house that was not a merchant's kept other ground behind it, or none.

  • A town household that also farmed often kept a vegetable garden at the back of its lot.
    The back of a farming household's lot was often its vegetable garden, as How a town is zoned: shops on the street, housing behind tells.
  • Did a back-tenement unit have a garden?
    By this record's reading of its plan, no: a unit was a single room with its earth-floored kitchen, its lane, well and privy shared, as Laborers' housing: back-lot tenements (uradana) and the master laborer's house tells.

In China a town house could be a courtyard house (siheyuan), its rooms built around an open courtyard of their own.

  • A Chinese courtyard house planted its courtyard with fruit and flowering trees.
    They were planted for enjoyment, commonly jujube and persimmon, with peony, herbaceous peony, magnolia, lilac, crabapple, wisteria and pomegranate among the flowering kinds.
