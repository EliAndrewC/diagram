What work did a farm household do that was never paid?

Rokugan's money rules price a koku by what it buys and by the paid days of work it stands for, and neither sees the work a farm household did for itself. The findings below give what the historical record holds on that work - who did it, in what season, and how long it took - in Japan, with China beside it.

No source read splits a farm household's year into hours for each of its members. The one count of a farm's year task by task is a farm book dated, by one prefectural history, to between 1629 and 1654, and it leaves out night work and almost all the women's work at home. That gap is the record's, not the households': the farm book gives the women's days as one lump and counts no night work, a survey of Chinese farms in the 1920s counted only work on crops and animals, and a historian of Edo village women notes that history written from politics tends to overlook the value of women's labor. How many days a farm worker worked in a year, and a village's rest days, are in How many days a year did a worker work?; who lived in a household is in Households.

A Japanese farm's year

  • How many days did a farm need?
    The Seiryoki, a chronicle of a warlord who held his castle at Mima in Kita-Uwa county, whose farming books that history dates to between 1629 and 1654, counts 293 men's days a year for one cho of paddy, at an average man's pace, and over 200 women's days besides; night work is not counted. It adds 66 days for barley on part of the paddy and 90 for a quarter-cho of dry fields, and 362 days of other work: fuel 120, the care of oxen and horses 120, cutting grass for fertilizer 40, silkworms 20, house repairs 20, dredging the ditches 20, cutting thatch grass for mats and rain capes and mending and forging tools 12, and making tea 10 - in all 811 days for a holding of 1 cho 2 tan 5 se. The 811 is the sum of the counted items alone, so the women's 200-odd days are not in it. The holding is that of a samurai who farmed his own land, larger than most farms, which the same history puts mostly under one cho.
  • What was a farm wife's round?
    The same book says a peasant's wife must work like an actor playing every part: she cooks the morning and evening meals, weaves the cloth for the unlined summer robes twice a year, threshes the barley, plants the rice, strips the grain from the rice stalks in autumn, readies the tax goods, weaves cotton in winter for the New Year clothes and finds the money for the levies; few people grasp this, it adds, and samurai least of all. The historian who quotes it adds childbirth, child care and the nursing of aged parents, so that, she judges, the wife must have had no time to rest.
  • Which work was a man's and which a woman's?
    Broadly, by the farm books and account books, men did work that took strength in bursts, such as plowing and puddling the paddies, and women work that took deft hands and less strength. Women's sowing, earthing up and threshing were valued cheaply, and a maidservant's cooking, cleaning and washing more cheaply still.
  • What did one household's accounts show?
    The Imai, wealthy farmers of Shinano, worked their land in 1821 with the head's mother, wife and sister, two servants hired by the year and eight day laborers. Only women spread yard refuse, hay and cut grass in the 4th month, and made rice dumplings and sorted millet in the 5th. The best-paid man earned 3 ryo 1 bu a year and an ordinary man 2 ryo 2 bu; a woman 1 ryo and her clothing for the seasons; a day laborer 50 mon if a man, 16 to 33 mon if a woman.
  • Did a woman rest on a rest day?
    Not in one great house. The house rules of the Muramatsu, landholders of Noto with about 20 servants, written in the late 1700s and early 1800s, call the women away from any work at all three meals to cook for and serve the men, and on rest days have all the women wash and wipe the household's goods; even on a half-day off they were to help morning and evening.
  • What did a day look like?
    In one village near Kanazawa, as its local history recalls the late 1800s and early 1900s, the men went out to work before breakfast while the women cooked, kept house and minded the children; after the midday meal the men napped, from seedbed time to harvest, while the young wife cleared up, made supper, did the washing and nursed the baby. In autumn there was no nap and night work (yonabe) was added, and some households did night work all year, the whole family making straw goods by the hearth even in the slack season. Many farm households there wove, under the domain and still in the middle of the Meiji era (1868-1912), the women mostly in the slack winter.
  • How many farm people had a side trade?
    About a quarter of the men and women farming in Yamanashi in 1879, and in four silk-raising villages there 13 to 22 percent of the women of farm families had a side trade at home such as reeling silk or weaving, the poorer the family the likelier; such households moved their labor between farming and side work by the season, from day to day and within the day, as an economic historian, quoting another, sums up the evidence in a draft he marked not to be cited. Side trades as paid work are in Shops and trades in towns and villages.

A Chinese farm's year

  • What did hired men do when it rained?
    On a managed farm of Huzhou about 1640, worked by hired hands, the farm manual reckons seven fine days to three of rain, each with its own work: on rainy days the men dredged mud from the ponds, split firewood, twisted rope, husked and pounded rice, tied grain bins, wove screens for the silkworms and mended dikes, and a rainy day at home earned no wine. A good worker twisted 7 to 8 jin of rope a day, an average one 5; a woman's ration of rice was half a man's.
  • "Men plow, women weave."
    So the Huzhou manual calls the farm household's basic work, in its section on silkworms: two women weave 120 bolts of silk a year, and a household must feed its weaving woman whether she weaves or not. In the farm book of 1658 that follows it, a common man works ten mu, and his wife raises ten baskets of silkworms, or makes two bolts of cloth a day, or spins eight liang of cotton yarn. In the rainy silkworm month, the men toil outside and the women fret within, day and night.
  • How much of the year went on the fields?
    About a quarter, by John Lossing Buck's survey of 1921-1925: a farm's median of 190 ten-hour days of work on crops and animals, done by about two workers, left the equivalent of several idle months each, which, he grants, other work, rain and holidays partly filled. In Jiangnan in the mid-Qing, a farm of ten mu probably took no more than 200 days a year, by Li Bozhong's estimate as Kenneth Pomeranz cites it.
  • Did women work in the fields?
    It depended on the region. Women did a little over a fifth of the farm work Buck counted, 31.6 percent in east central China and 11.8 in the north, a gap Buck thought probably came from foot binding being commoner there. In a silk village of the Yangzi delta in 1936, Fei Xiaotong found women entirely free of farm work: while home reeling prospered they had reeled silk as the men prepared the soil, but by his visit the factories had taken the reeling and left the women at leisure. In a Yunnan village in 1938, by a later reviewer's account of the study, women did more of the work on both staple crops than men, about a third of households' men had left farming, and a woman's day of farm work usually paid half a man's.
  • Who did the work at home?
    The women, in Fei's village: girls learned silk work, sewing and other housework from their mothers, and the women went to town less than the men because of their housework and the care of the children. Jiangnan women probably worked almost as many days for income as their husbands, Pomeranz judges, and in North China gathering fuel was usually a job for women and children.
  • What did a farm do in winter?
    Little that Buck counted: he calls winter work a problem not easily solved, and his example is side work for sale, women and children making winter shoes of cow's hair.

The household's tasks, and their time

Few of the household's tasks have a time in any source read; each below names who did it where a source says, and its time or that none was found.

  • Cloth.
    Women's work everywhere a source says. Spinning and weaving a tan of cotton took 4 days, from ginned cotton; growing the fiber, dyeing, sewing, unpicking and washing robes (araihari) and mending have no time. The stages are in How long did it take to make cloth?
  • Food.
    Cooking was the women's, morning and evening, and they were called from any work to serve the men's meals. Threshing and readying the grain was women's work in Japan; on the Huzhou farm hired men husked and pounded rice on rainy days, and one person could husk at most one shi a day in Jiangnan in 1834, though husking was generally done at a mill. No time was found for cooking, pickling, or making miso and soy sauce.
  • Fuel and water.
    Fuel took 120 days a year on the Seiryoki's farm; on the Huzhou farm men split firewood on rainy days. No time was found for making charcoal or hauling water.
  • Straw.
    Cutting thatch grass for mats and rain capes came with tool mending to 12 days a year on the Seiryoki's farm; rope is the Huzhou farm's rainy-day work above, and in some households of the Kanazawa village the whole family made straw goods at night. The Huzhou farm bought its rain capes and bamboo hats. No time was found for a pair of sandals, a mat or a rain cape.
  • The house, the tools and the animals.
    House repairs took 20 days a year, ditch dredging 20, the oxen and horses 120 and the silkworms 20, on the Seiryoki's farm.
  • Care.
    Children, childbirth and aged parents were the women's in Japan, and the children were the women's in Fei's village; no time was found for the care of children, the sick or the old.

Was making clothes the largest claim on one person's time?

Only the cloth can be tested: growing the fiber, dyeing and sewing have no time in any source read, so the days below for cloth are the least that clothes took, not the whole.

  • In the cotton age in Japan, no.
    A household of about five in Niikawa in the mid-1800s wove 12.8 tan of cotton cloth a year for its own use, about 51 days of spinning and weaving from ginned cotton, against the over 200 women's days the Seiryoki counts for a cho of paddy - two sources two centuries apart. Cotton, two historians argue, freed women's time for the fields. A household in a weaving district that also wove for sale kept one woman at it about 7 months of the year, but most of that was a trade paid in cotton, not work for the household.
  • In the hemp and ramie age, perhaps.
    Ramie cloth took nearly ten times the labor of cotton by Nagahara Keiji's estimate, as Tanimoto and Nagashima report it, and most of women's labor went into it. At ten times, the same 12.8 tan would take about 512 days, more than one woman's year - this project's arithmetic, multiplying one source's days by another's estimate.
  • In China, only cloth for sale is counted.
    A Jiangnan woman spinning and weaving for about 200 days a year was at the practical maximum, as Li and Pomeranz each reckon it, about level with a ten-mu farm's field year above; but that is cloth for sale, and no source read times a Chinese household's own clothes. Fei's village bought most of its cloth by 1936.
  • Was fieldwork larger in total?
    In Japan, yes for the cotton age: the 811 counted days and the women's 200-odd far exceed the cloth's 51. In Jiangnan, where women were mostly out of the fields, wives probably worked almost as many days for income as their husbands, by Pomeranz's estimate.
