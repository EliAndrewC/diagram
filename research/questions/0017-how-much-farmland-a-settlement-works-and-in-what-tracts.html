How much farmland a settlement works, and in what tracts

A village's paddy had to feed the village and pay its tax. In Edo-period Japan, reckoned from the rice a person ate, what a tan of paddy was assessed to yield and the lord's share of the crop, with millet, barley and other coarse grains making up the rest of the farmers' food, it comes to a little under a tan of paddy, about a quarter of an acre, for each inhabitant.

A Japanese farm family in the early 1900s worked about 2.5 acres, seldom in one piece. Its paddy was not watered farm by farm: a well served one farmer or a few, and a river's canals were run by villages. The ground one irrigation work waters is a tract. The small pond-fed systems of one Japanese ward water about 10 ha (~25 acres) each.

  • One koku was reckoned the rice an adult ate in a year.
    One koku is taken to be the rice one adult eats in a year, though a grown man's standard stipend was in practice about 1.8 koku of brown rice a year.
  • A tan of middling paddy was assessed to yield 1.3 koku.
    The land surveys of early modern Japan assessed a tan of upper paddy at 1.5 koku, middle paddy at 1.3 and lower paddy at 1.1. The figure was a statutory estimate of the yield, not a measured harvest.
  • The lord took 40 percent of the crop early in the Edo period, and is said to have taken half later.
    The rate is given as four parts to the lord and six to the farmer in the early Edo period, and five and five from the Kyoho era (1716-36) onward; one of the two encyclopedias that say so calls the later rate uncertain.
  • Farmers ate much of their grain as millet, barley and other coarse grains.
    Outside Edo, where rice gathered from every part of the country, coarse grain tended to be the staple itself.
  • Coarse grain was left out of the tax and grown as the farmers' own food.
    It was treated as outside the tax levies, and grown for the farmers to eat and against famine.
  • How much of a farm household's everyday rice pot was rice?
    From two parts in five to four in five, in the few accounts found, and less where the land was dry. A farm village's rice was cooked with coarse grain, and around 1830 a village of Ina district in Shinano ate at midday in winter a mixed rice that was 40 percent rice. In one undated account a paddy district in the Katsushika delta cooked about four parts rice to one of barley, while the dry-field districts ate millet and barley with a little rice, and in the Kanto dry-field belt a rice that was 70 to 80 percent barley.
  • How much paddy did it take to feed one inhabitant?
    About 0.8 to 1.0 tan (~0.20-0.25 acre), by this project's reckoning. A tan of middling paddy was assessed at 1.3 koku. After a tax of about 45 percent, the midpoint of the early and later Edo rates of 40 and 50 percent and on no page, it leaves its farmer about 0.7 koku. Rice alone, at a koku a head with every inhabitant counted as an adult, would take about 1.4 tan. Coarse grain made up the rest: taken as roughly a third of what was eaten, it brings the paddy down to 0.8 to 1.0 tan, which leaves coarse grain 28 to 43 percent.
  • How much of a paddy village's diet was coarse grain?
    About a third, a GUESS of this project's. No source we read gives coarse grain's share of a whole diet, but the everyday rice pot brackets it. A paddy district of the Katsushika delta cooked one part barley to four of rice, and a village of Ina district in Shinano around 1830 ate a midday mixed rice that was three parts in five not rice. A third lies between the two, and well short of the dry-field districts, as it should for a village among its paddy.
  • A Japanese family's fields in the early 1900s were seldom in one piece.
    An eyewitness account of Japanese farming in the early 1900s found that the lands one family worked were seldom contiguous, might be widely scattered and were very often rented, and that the government had passed laws in force since 1900 to consolidate them.
  • How much land did a Japanese farm household work?
    About 2.5 acres, paddy and dry field together. An eyewitness account of Japanese farming in the early 1900s puts the average worked by one family nearer 2.5 acres. That is close to 1 cho (~2.45 acres), the holding this project takes for an Edo farm household, a GUESS: no page we read gives an Edo figure.
  • A 1987 study found well irrigation in Japan run by single farmers or small groups, and river canals by villages.
    A well serves a small area, so groundwater irrigation is almost always small and simply managed, while a river system's branch canals are run by villages and groups of villages, and its main canal and source by a municipality.
  • How much land did one small pond system water?
    About 10 ha (~25 acres), in the one case read. In Tsunai ward of Kunisaki, as a present-day study describes it, five pond systems water 50 ha (~124 acres) of farmland among 11 farmers; the ten each is this record's arithmetic, since the paper gives no area for a single system.
  • In the Kiso valley, mountains left farmland only on the narrow river flats.
    What a village needed was not always what its ground allowed. In the Kiso valley steep mountains take most of the land, and cultivated ground is found only on the narrow flats along the Kiso River.

How large one tract of paddy grew

A tract, the paddy watered from one pond, canal or reservoir, ran in the sources read from under a hectare to about 45 ha (~110 acres). Only one ran larger, and it is the largest found: a polder of about 60 ha (~148 acres) in a Yangtze delta village, and its farmers worked it as smaller drainage units. A settlement with more paddy than one tract could hold had several, and every larger work found was a weir or canal divided into channels, or shared among villages.

  • A village's canal in Shaanxi watered 10 to 700 mu (~0.6-43 ha).
    The county registers collected in the Shaanxi provincial gazetteer of 1735 name, for Qianyang county, one canal after another led off the river past a village: 50 mu at Qujiawan, 300 at Caobei, 700 at Duanjiawan, 10 below Fengtai hill. In Xixiang county, in the south, a pond storing rain watered 15. The hectares are this record's arithmetic at the mu of 1915, which was based on the Qing definitions.
  • The largest of one county's old ponds watered 730 mu (~45 ha).
    Chongyang county in Hubei remembered five ponds, and the county's modern gazetteer, which Yang follows, gives them as watering 210, 449, 531, 730 and 470 mu (~13-45 ha), the Wupo pond's 730, after its enlargement of 1866, the first among them. An older gazetteer of Xingguo, in the same province, says its villages' official ponds and weirs could each water a hundred mu or more (~6 ha and up).
  • Every paddy belonged to one pond.
    In the hills of southern Hubei each pond or weir had its own watered land, and each paddy belonged to one of them, so a deed of sale named the pond that watered the field.
  • A Musashi village's paddy hung on one small reservoir and the rain.
    Doshida lower ward's detailed return of 1850, the village's own written account of itself, says the paddy of somewhat over 7 cho (~7 ha, ~17 acres) drew its water from a reservoir of about 3 tan on the village's border, which ran dry in drought; the village was, it says, on the whole rain-fed. The same return registers the ward's own paddy at about 2.4 cho, and the whole village's return of 1831 registers about 7.4, so the 7 cho reads as the whole village's paddy.
  • Japan's early excavated paddy blocks cover 2 to 7.5 ha.
    At the Hattori site, of the earliest Yayoi period, plots of 10 to 180 square meters, about 400 by the site's estimate from the area, cover some 2 ha (~5 acres) along a central canal; the late Yayoi paddies at Toro cover about 7 ha, and the Yayoi paddies at Hyakkengawa about 4. Toro's 7.5 ha of third-century paddy were watered by one canal more than 370 m long.
  • The largest tract found was a polder of about 60 ha, worked as smaller units.
    Fei's village on the Yangtze delta held its land in eleven polders, each ground surrounded by water, its size set by the streams; the 1932 survey gives them from under 9 mow to 986 mow. At the village's 3,065 mow, which Fei gives as 461 acres, the largest is about 148 acres (~60 ha), by this record's arithmetic. Two of the polders were shared with other villages, and for those Fei could only estimate the village's part, so a shared polder may have been larger than its figure. Within a polder each strip watered from one pumping spot was the unit of irrigation, and a big polder had to be divided into smaller drainage units; one, in the largest polder, held 336 mow (~50 acres).
  • A settlement with more paddy than one tract worked several.
    Fei's village had its eleven polders. Chongyang's Huapi weir watered 7,940 mu (~490 ha) through a channel that split three ways, and the work of keeping it was shared out at one work unit for each 50 mu and 53 units to a channel.
  • Every larger work found was a canal divided among channels or villages.
    In Japan part of one branch canal might serve most or all of a village's land. The Wada canal in Shinano is recorded as watering 589 cho (~1,444 acres) in 1655 across three villages, Wada's own share 315 cho. Shaanxi's Wumen weir watered more than 40,000 mu (~2,500 ha) by an inscription on it, and was split into waterwheels, seven sluices and eight further channels, the channels watering some 28,700 mu.
