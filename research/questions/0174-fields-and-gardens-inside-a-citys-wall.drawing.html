How our maps draw farmland inside a city's wall

Farmland inside a city's wall was common in China, and lasted into the twentieth century. Our maps still draw none by default: they keep a city's interior for its houses, and a city that wants fields inside its wall declares a farming quarter. One provincial city does, because it has water sources inside its wall and a shape that made walling its farms out save almost no length of wall. A whole quarter of the ground inside that wall is fields and vegetable gardens, and the same quarter holds the city's burakumin neighborhood.

  • The farming quarter takes the ground the city's households do not need.
    It lets the city hold an average provincial-city population with more room per inhabitant than average inside its wall: the surplus is farmed, not built on.
  • Ground saved by building in rows goes to farmland, never to more houses.
    Rows of houses sharing party walls take up less ground than a looser scatter would. The ground they free goes to fields, because the share of a city's households each caste is given already fixes how many households the city holds.

Our maps edge a field outside a city with farmhouses, which is what makes its edge look farmed. Inside a wall the rule is different:

The rule the map follows: no farmhouse stands inside a city's wall. An in-wall field is edged by the city's own houses, laborers' and merchants' among them, facing it from the surrounding quarter, and its edge looks farmed because those houses line it (accurate for the city dwellers who farm; the setting's own rule for there being no farmer households). How densely they line it is a calibration against the drawn city.

The drawn city does not follow this rule yet: it still rings its in-wall fields with farmhouses, as a field outside a wall is ringed.

  • The drawn city rings its in-wall fields densely with farmhouses.
    The whole of such a field is in view, so its whole edge must look farmed: about sixteen farmhouses for every 3,000 ft of edge, against seven for a field outside the wall, in a tight inner row with one or two denser outer bands, rather than a sparse few on one side that leave long bare edges. Both densities are a calibration against the drawn cities and against the farmhouses round a village's fields, not taken from a source.
  • The drawn example's ring is denser still.
    It runs between twenty-four and thirty-seven farmhouses for every 3,000 ft of edge.
  • The farmhouses inside the wall are not counted in the city's population.
    The setting counts only a city's own households inside its wall.
  • The ring is a deviation from the setting and from the history.
    The setting puts no farmer household inside a city's wall (Who lives in a provincial city?), and in the history city dwellers grew their own vegetables on the idle plots inside a city.

Not all of the in-wall ground is rice paddy. Some of it is drawn as vegetable tracts: intensively worked garden ground, fed from wells and from night soil.

  • A vegetable tract is drawn with no surface channel.
    It is watered from wells, as the old farm manual's market garden against a city wall was, so a paddy's channel has nothing to show there. East of a city's Imperial spine a channel could not reach the tract in any case, since it would have to cross the road.
  • A vegetable tract has no farm households round it.
    It is worked by the households of the quarters around it, as Chinese city dwellers grew their own vegetables on the idle plots inside a city.
  • A vegetable tract's parcels are the smallest dry parcels on the map.
    Each is about 55 ft across and no more than 0.15 acre, against 90 to 100 ft for a strip of the grain fields beside a paddy. A vegetable tract is hand-worked ground, so its parcels are smaller than a grain field's, not larger.
  • A vegetable tract is cut across its width into columns of about 55 ft, an even number of them.
    Cut only once, a tract would be two columns of about 150 ft, the biggest dry parcels on the map - backwards for a garden.
  • A garden parcel is drawn as a block of beds, because one bed is too small to draw.
    The 55 ft is a calibration against the drawn cities: some five to thirteen of the hand-worked beds the history gives across. No page we read gives the width of a garden parcel of the size the map draws.

An in-wall field is watered from a pond, and the ground its water reaches spreads out downhill of the pond like a fan.

  • Where does the pond that feeds an in-wall field sit?
    At the high corner of the field's ground. Water was let into a field by gravity, so the pond stands where water enters a city from high ground, and the fan fills the ground downhill of it. That is our own reading of the gravity feed.
  • The maps draw a fan widening to the left or to the right, whichever its ground suits.
    A fan has a hand: its wide side falls to the left or to the right. No page we read speaks of it, so the maps draw either hand. The ground an in-wall field sits in is rarely even - a ring road inside the wall leaves a crescent that narrows toward one end - so the maps mirror the whole field where the crescent calls for it, rather than force it into one hand.
