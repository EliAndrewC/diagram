Lodgings for lords and officials on the road (honjin)

On the highways of the Edo period, a traveling lord had a lodging house of his own rank. Each post station kept a great lodging house, the honjin, for daimyo on their alternate attendance journeys to and from Edo and for other travelers of rank, usually one or two to a station. It was in principle a house of one story, with an imposing gate, a wide entrance, a raised audience room and a garden beside it. One of the largest that survive stands on a site of about 50,000 square feet.

  • The honjin lodged daimyo and other persons of rank.
    A Japanese dictionary calls it the great lodging house at a post station where daimyo on their alternate attendance in Edo, and besides them princely houses, court nobles, shogunal officials and high priests, rested and stayed.
  • How many honjin did a post station have?
    One or two on average, but some stations had none, and some had five or six side by side.
  • A honjin was in principle a house of one story, with matted rooms, board and earth floors, and a garden beside its raised room.
    A highway office of the Japanese government describes it as single-story in principle, with many matted rooms, board floors and earth floors, an imposing gate, a wide entrance, a raised room in the formal shoin style, and a garden beside that room.
  • How big was a honjin?
    The one whose size we found is the honjin of the post station of Kusatsu, among the largest whose site and buildings largely survive. Its site covers about 4,719 square meters (~50,795 sq ft), or 1,427 tsubo, and its floors about 1,547 square meters (~16,652 sq ft), or 468 tsubo.
  • The waki-honjin, the second-rank lodging, was furnished to the honjin's standard.
    It had a raised room like the honjin's, and when no daimyo or imperial envoy was using it, it took ordinary guests.
  • How big was a waki-honjin?
    Smaller than a honjin, its dictionary entry says; no page we read gives one's measured size.
  • No page we read says where in a post town's street the honjin stood.
    The pages on the honjin and the waki-honjin describe its guests, its count and its building, but none places it in its street.
  • The ordinary inn was a honjin with the gate, the formal entrance and the raised room left out.
    It is at Travelers' inns and cheap lodging houses (hatago, dian, kichin-yado).

China's post stations kept lodging for officials too.

  • The Chinese post station at Jimingyi kept an official lodging court, three courtyards deep.
    The court is a Ming building of brick and timber in the northwest of the station town. What the station town around it held is at Relay stations: the Imperial waystation, the relay office and their stables (umaya, toiyaba).
  • No page we read says whether a Chinese county seat kept a guest house for passing officials.
    The searches found Jimingyi's post-station lodging court, but nothing on lodging kept by a county's government compound.
  • In this setting, a busy trunk-road waystation occasionally hosts ceremonial Imperial travelers.
    The setting's notes say so of the waystations on busy trunk routes and at junctions. The waystation itself is at Relay stations: the Imperial waystation, the relay office and their stables (umaya, toiyaba).
