The magistrate's manor in a town: where it stands and which way it faces (jin'ya and yamen)

A town's magistrate worked and lived in one enclosed compound. In Japan the shogunate's intendant governed from a jin'ya, which was at once their residence, their office, their court and the store for the year's tax. The lord of a small domain governed from a jin'ya too, a seat ranking next to a castle. In China a county magistrate governed from the county yamen. What stood inside the walls, court by court, is at Magistrates' compounds (jin'ya and yamen).

The two stood differently in their towns. A Japanese seat had no one siting, and was often built beside a settlement that was already there, while the Chinese county yamen we found stood inside its town, in the middle, on the main street. The Qing county yamen whose plan we read, at Neixiang, was laid out to the Qing regulations for local offices and faces south, after the traditional ritual idea of sitting to the north and facing south.

  • An intendant's jin'ya kept the year's tax inside its own grounds.
    The intendant's jin'ya was where the annual tax was stored, and the intendancy at Takayama kept its numbered earth-walled storehouses inside its grounds; eight of them survive, numbered one to four and nine to twelve.
  • Jin'ya differed in what they held and in how strongly they were enclosed.
    A small domain's jin'ya could ring itself with earthworks or moats but no tall stone walls, and hold a residence, offices, retainers' houses, earth-walled storehouses and a drill ground. Jin'ya varied in how far retainers' quarters and townhouses gathered around them, in what they held inside, and in how defensible their perimeter was.
  • Did a jin'ya stand at the edge of its town?
    Often, it seems. Some seats grew a small town of their own around them, but the seats of outlying holdings and the like were often built beside a village or town already there. One surviving domain seat, at Kaibara in Tamba, stands at the highest point of its old town, on a gentle slope backed by the hill behind it, though barely above the rest of the town. That a seat beside its settlement stood at the town's edge rather than in its middle is this project's reading of these two.
  • No page we read says which way a jin'ya's gate faced.
    The pages on the jin'ya and on the Kaibara seat say where a seat stood and what it held, not which way its gate opened.

In the Chinese towns we found, the magistrate sat inside the town instead, on its main street, and the office faced south.

  • Did a Chinese county yamen stand in the middle of its town, on the main street?
    Both the yamen we found stood on a main street inside their towns. Neixiang's yamen stands on East Main Street at the center of its county town. The yamen of Huozhou department stands inside its city on the north side of its East Main Street.
  • A Qing county yamen faced south, after the ritual tradition.
    The yamen at Neixiang, a Qing county yamen, follows the Qing regulations for local offices in its overall layout, and that layout expresses the traditional idea of sitting to the north facing south, civil on the left and military on the right, the court in front and the sleeping quarters behind, the jail to the south. The page puts the facing under a traditional idea of ritual propriety, not of luck, so it is the formal orientation; it says the layout as a whole follows the regulations, not that they required the facing. It describes Chinese offices, not Japanese ones.
  • The great hall faced south over its court, with a broad terrace before it.
    Neixiang's yamen sits to the north facing south, and its great hall has a broad terrace before it. Pingyao's yamen sits to the north facing south too, and its great hall rises straight ahead past the ceremonial gate, with a terrace before it.
  • Pingyao kept its money, grain tax and arms at the two sides of the great hall, and its granary behind the offices.
    A modern account says that when the yamen was repaired on a large scale in 1619 its great hall was rebuilt at five bays, the east end bay the store for money and grain and the west end bay the armory. The oldest account, the imperial encyclopedia, the Gujin tushu jicheng (古今圖書集成), says only that the money-and-grain store stood east of the great hall and the armory west of it, words that fit a room at the hall's end or a building beside it. No source gives how deep the armory ran. Pingyao had no standing barracks under the Ming or the Qing, yet it kept the armory, which its war office guarded. Office buildings were built behind the offices of war, justice and works, and north of them the county granary.
  • Jiexiu kept its gunpowder and weapons in the great hall's end bays, and in a later account in an armory by its side gates.
    When its magistrate enlarged the great hall of the Jiexiu county office from three bays to five in 1591, the middle three held court and the east and west bays became storerooms for gunpowder, weapons and the like. In its account of the office after 1677, when a magistrate rebuilt at least part of it, the imperial encyclopedia lists an armory (武庫) on the east, inside the side gates, across from the rooms of the gatekeepers, runners and jailers on the west, with the jail and an archery strip (箭道); its words leave open whether the armory stood there or west of a pavilion, and do not say whether it was a building of its own or a room; the strip was a practice ground for archery the office kept inside its walls, and a magistrate built an archery hall for it, its size unsaid. At its street front, inside a memorial arch, stood barracks and three lock-ups for minor offenders; the entry says neither who the barracks housed nor whether the office of 1591 had any.
  • Across county offices, the armory's side varied.
    The same imperial encyclopedia, on the offices of Nanyang prefecture, gives Neixiang's office, rebuilt from 1666, a weapons room on the left of its ceremonial gate; Wuyang's, repaired from 1652, an arms store beside the money-and-grain store, to the right of its hall; Zhenping's a great hall of five bays whose east and west bays were stores, their contents unnamed; and the department seat of Dengzhou a regalia store on the west of its hall, with its archive and treasury on the east. At the Fenzhou prefecture office it was silver that was stored on the west. The encyclopedia's left and right are not glossed; for a seat facing south the left is the east, this project's reading. With Pingyao's armory on the west, and Jiexiu's in both end bays and later on the east, no fixed side appears. Modern pages lay out a yamen "civil on the left, military on the right", Pingyao's page its six offices by that rule, the war office on the west, and Pingyao's war office guarded its armory, but no page we read says the armory was set on a side because of that rule or because of the war office.
  • No page we read puts a seat's arms in its barracks, or says what doors an armory had.
    The accounts of Pingyao and Jiexiu put the arms in the great hall or in an armory listed by the side gates. Of the three Japanese weapons stores we read of, the gun store at Kaibara in Tamba is a room of the gate range. At Nirayama in Izu, in the house of the Egawa family, the Nirayama intendants, the weapons store (武器庫) is a building of its own from late in the Edo period, 5.4 by 3.7 m, gabled, roofed in pantiles and entered at its gable end under a pent roof; the page does not say where in the compound it stood. At Hamamashike, the Shonai domain's administrative seat in Ezo, with a garrison, whose main buildings were finished in 1860, a city leaflet names a weapons store (武器蔵) among the storehouses without placing it, and the seat's old plans put its powder store on the hill behind. None says what doors any of them had; the Nirayama entry says only that its store is entered at the gable end.
  • How deep was a county great hall?
    About 41 ft, at the one we found measured. The great hall of the Neixiang county yamen, as it stands today, is 27.22 m (~89 ft) across and 12.37 m (~41 ft) deep, in five bays, so a bay there averages about 18 ft wide by this project's arithmetic. Another account gives the same hall a floor of 230 m² (~2,476 sq ft), smaller than the about 337 m² (~3,624 sq ft) those figures make. An end bay used as a store could run that whole depth, this project's reading: no page we read gives the depth of an armory or store in a hall's end bay.
