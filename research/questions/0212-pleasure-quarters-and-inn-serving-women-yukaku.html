Pleasure quarters and inn serving women (yukaku)

Our maps draw no walled pleasure quarter: in Rokugan prostitution is illegal by Imperial decree, a law honored more in breach than observance, and the trade goes on in a city's geisha houses, outside the law. Nor do they give a post town's serving women a house of their own, for those women worked in the inns the maps already draw. The research below shows the licensed quarters the setting leaves out and the serving women of the inns.

A licensed pleasure quarter, a yukaku, was a district of Edo-period Japan that gathered the licensed brothels and was closed off behind walls or moats. There were few of them: the three great quarters of Osaka, Kyoto and Edo, and twenty-odd more across the country. Osaka's was laid out on new ground west of the castle town, and Edo's, the Yoshiwara, on reed ground remote from the town of the time, in a block some 720 ft on a side.

Most pleasure districts never stood behind a quarter's wall but grew up unplanned, and along the highways the inns of the post towns kept serving women, meshimori, who were unlicensed prostitutes half-tolerated as the post town's servants. The cities of Song-dynasty China had entertainment precincts of performance halls and shops, but no page we read describes a walled brothel quarter in a Chinese city.

  • By definition, a licensed quarter gathered the licensed brothels behind walls or moats.
    The Japanese Wikipedia article on the licensed quarters defines one as a district that gathered the licensed brothels and was enclosed all round by walls, moats and the like.
  • There were three great licensed quarters and twenty-odd more.
    Osaka's Shinmachi, Kyoto's Shimabara and Edo's Yoshiwara were called the three great quarters, and besides them there were licensed quarters in twenty-odd places across the country in the Edo period.
  • Most pleasure districts were never walled.
    Most grew up on their own, without a plan. A quarter set apart from the town and closed off by barriers or moats was a minority, a special case.
  • Even the three great quarters were closed off in three different ways.
    Shinmachi lay inside the town, with seven gates and a board fence on its east side only. Shimabara lay at the town's edge, with two gates and an earthen wall all round. The Yoshiwara had a single gate and was cut off from the town by its wall and the fields about it.
  • The Yoshiwara had one approach, one gate, a high wall and a ditch.
    Its one official approach was a road down from the embankment to its great gate, and past the gate lay the quarter, shut in by a high wall and a ditch, the ohaguro-dobu.
  • Osaka's quarter was laid out in 1627, early in the Edo period, on new ground west of the castle town.
    It gathered brothels that had been scattered through the town, and was called the west quarter because it lay west of the castle town.
  • Edo's quarter was pushed outward as the city grew.
    Its first site was a block near Nihonbashi on reed ground by the bay, remote for the Edo of the time. As the city spread until daimyo mansions stood beside it, the quarter was ordered in 1656 to move behind the Senso-ji temple, to the embankment at Nihonzutsumi.
  • How big was the Yoshiwara?
    Its first site was 2 cho, about 220 m (~722 ft), square. The same page gives the second site both as 3 cho square, about 1,070 ft on a side, and as a little over 20,000 tsubo, about 16 acres, a square some 840 ft on a side, and does not reconcile the two.
  • How big was Osaka's quarter?
    It held seven blocks and, in the Genroku era (1688-1704), more than 800 courtesans. The page does not give its area.

Outside the licensed quarters, the trade went on where travelers lodged and where crowds gathered.

  • Outside the quarters, prostitutes gathered at the post-town inns and in unlicensed places.
    In the Edo period the places outside the licensed quarters where prostitutes gathered were the post towns' meshimori inns and the okabasho, such as the towns before temple gates.
  • The serving women of the post towns were half-tolerated.
    Prostitution was otherwise allowed only inside the licensed quarters, but the meshimori were half-tolerated under the name of servants of the post town.
  • At some post towns, the inns kept the serving women as employees.
    At the three post towns under Mount Asama it was the custom for the inns to keep meshimori as employees to entertain their lodgers.
  • How many serving women did a post town keep?
    They were capped per post town: in 1772 a limit was set of 150 each at Senju and Itabashi, 500 at Shinagawa and 250 at Naito-Shinjuku. Under Mount Asama the women were so many that at Karuizawa and Oiwake the female population greatly exceeded the male. No page we read gives the number at an ordinary post town.

China's cities under the Song dynasty gathered their entertainers differently, in precincts of halls and shops.

  • Cities of the Song dynasty had entertainment precincts, the washe.
    They rose in the eleventh century, under the emperors Renzong and Shenzong. Within them railings or cloth curtains parted the performers from the audience, and among them were shops for clothes, toys, sundries, tea, wine and haircuts.
  • The precincts' performance halls later gave their name to brothels.
    The word goulan, which named the actors' halls of the Song dynasty, came to mean a brothel from the Ming dynasty on.
  • Did a Chinese city have a walled brothel quarter?
    No page we read describes one.
