What did arrows cost?

Rokugan's samurai and ashigaru go to war with bows, and the setting judges a price by how many days of a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give what the historical record holds instead: prices for war arrows and fine arrows in Ming and Qing China, set beside the nearest day wages found, and a silence in Japan.

No price for an arrow or an arrowhead was found in Japan, for the Sengoku or the Edo period. In China the record is the state's: the prices at which Ming counties were assessed for the arrows they owed, the cost of the arms a commander proposed to make, and the prices the Qing fixed for arrows by the kind of feather that fletched them. An ordinary war arrow ran from about 0.014 tael of silver late in the Ming to 0.05-0.08 in 1727, and a fine arrow up to 0.36.

Ming and Qing China

  • What was an ordinary war arrow reckoned at late in the Ming?
    About 0.0136 tael. Jintan county, in Zhenjiang prefecture on the lower Yangzi, owed the throne 9,000 arrows, booked in the old Ming fiscal book at 0.0135632 tael each, about 122 taels in all, with a further 70 taels of matting money charged on the same levy. When the Qing turned the levy into silver in 1646 it charged 0.1 tael an arrow, a tax rate rather than what an arrow cost to make.
  • And as part of a soldier's set?
    Under 0.033 tael an arrow. An estimate that Tan Lun put before the throne for the Jizhou and Changping commands north of Beijing priced 1,620 sets, each a bow, a string, thirty arrows and a quiver, at 1,620 taels for materials and labor together: a tael a set. A thirtieth of a tael is this record's arithmetic, and an upper bound, since the bow, the string and the quiver are in the same tael.
  • What did the Qing allow early on?
    0.04 tael an arrow. A ruling of 1650 set what the provinces could spend making their soldiers' arms: a bow 1 tael, an arrow 0.04, a saber 0.92, a musket 1.3.
  • What did an ordinary arrow cost against a fine one?
    In 1727 a ruling fixed war arrows fletched with the feathers of the sesame eagle at 8 taels the hundred, and arrows fletched with goose feathers at 5 taels the hundred; it priced the better arrows, here called fine arrows, by the five, by the bird and the grade: from 1.8 taels for five of the first grade fletched with blue-crane feathers down to 0.6 for five of the second grade fletched with bustard. A shop that raised the price was to be punished. That is 0.05 to 0.08 tael for an arrow sold by the hundred and 0.12 to 0.36 tael for a fine one, this record's arithmetic. The ruling does not name the city whose shops it bound; that it was the capital, where the Board of War sat, is a guess.
  • And in Beijing in 1736?
    The official price list the Ministry of Works and the Nine Chief Ministers settled in 1736 priced a bow at 1.2 taels, a large arrow for a quiver at 0.2, a quiver of 17 arrows at 0.6, and a birch arrow shaft at 0.006. The quiver of 17 comes to about 0.035 tael an arrow, this record's arithmetic.
  • How many arrows did a fletcher make in a day?
    No figure was found, in China or in Japan. The Qing Board of War kept ten arrowsmiths to make the state's arrows; the ruling gives them no quota.

The day's wage beside the Chinese prices

  • What did a laborer earn beside the Ming prices?
    No unskilled day wage was found for Jintan, Jizhou or the late Ming. The nearest wages are craftsmen's, in an estimator's costing of a matchlock musket in a Ming military compendium, which names no place or year: the forge's ordinary smiths 0.03 tael a day and its tongs-man 0.04, and a carpenter 0.06 for two days. At a craftsman's 0.03 tael, the Jintan arrow is about half a day's pay and Tan Lun's set about 33 days, this record's arithmetic.
  • And beside the Qing prices?
    No wage was found for 1650 or 1727. The nearest are in Beijing's official price list of 1736, nine years after the arrow prices of 1727 and the same list that priced the quiver of arrows: a laborer 75 copper cash a day, and carpenters, masons, bricklayers, tilers and painters 154 cash. The Qing farm laborer's day wage in silver is in What did an unskilled laborer earn?
  • Why are the prices and the 1736 wages not set in one unit?
    The arrows are priced in silver and the wages in copper cash, and this record does not give the rate between the two, so neither is converted.

Japan

  • What did an arrow or an arrowhead cost?
    No price was found, in the Sengoku or the Edo period. The one statement found is a historian's guess: that soldiers made their own bows and arrows, but that arrowheads, being metal and hard to make at home, were probably traded at about the value of a spear, which he puts at about 1 kanmon (1,000 mon) from a phrase of the time, "a spear of a hundred hiki".
  • What wage stands beside it?
    None. No Sengoku day wage, of a laborer or a craftsman, was found; the nearest in this record is Kyoto's laborer of 1745-1754, two centuries later (What did an unskilled laborer earn?).

Gathering arrows after a battle

  • Were spent arrows or their heads gathered up?
    No source found says so, in Japan or in China. The Qing Code charged a soldier for the arms he discarded or lost, but not for losses in battle; and each arrow had to bear its owner's name, on pain of eighty blows.
