Domain schools (hanko)

A domain school, the hanko, was the school a Japanese domain founded to educate the sons of its retainers. The first was founded at Okayama in 1669, early in the Edo period, and in their period of growth they numbered 255, in almost every domain. A domain school was a school of letters first, with the martial arts taught after letters: boys entered at seven or eight, learned to read and write, then studied the martial arts, and left between about fourteen and twenty.

A small school stood on under an acre, with a hall of letters and a hall for each martial art; the largest, Mito's, on some 26 acres, with a shrine to Confucius, a medical school, an observatory and riding and shooting grounds.

  • The domain schools we read kept their martial halls on their own grounds.
    Aizu's Nisshinkan, restored in 1987, has an archery hall and a martial-arts hall, and its boys studied etiquette, calligraphy and the martial arts in its elementary hall until they were fifteen. Mito's Kodokan set its hall of letters north of its main hall and its martial hall south of it, with an archery ground among its grounds. Hagi's Meirinkan ringed its Confucian hall with sword, spear and gunnery halls. The martial halls a city kept outside its schools are at Martial training is an urban institution.
  • Some domain schools began as a scholar's private Confucian hall.
    At Yanagawa and Yonezawa, a shrine to Confucius and a lecture hall built at the house of the domain's Confucian scholar were moved, enlarged and taken over by the domain as its school.
  • How much ground did a domain school take?
    From about a third of a hectare (~0.8 acres) to about 5 hectares (~12 acres), with a few great schools far larger. Matsushiro's school had 3,089 square meters (~33,000 sq ft, 934 tsubo) in all. Hagi's Meirinkan began on 940 tsubo (~0.8 acres) inside Hagi castle's third bailey, and in 1849 moved into the castle town onto 15,184 tsubo, about 5 ha (~12 acres), with a drill ground of 3,020 tsubo (~2.5 acres). Aizu's Nisshinkan stood on about 120 by 60 ken, about 220 by 110 m (~720 x 360 ft), or 2.4 ha (~6 acres).
  • Mito's Kodokan was the largest domain school in the country.
    It stood on about 32,000 tsubo, about 10.5 ha (~26 acres), ahead of Fukuyama's school at 23,704 tsubo (~19 acres), Kanazawa's at 18,256 (~15 acres) and Hagi's Meirinkan after its move at 15,184 (~12 acres). The Japanese Wikipedia article on the Kodokan agrees, at about 105,000 square meters (~26 acres), the largest of any domain school.
  • No page we read gives the ground of a typical domain school, or the size of its lecture hall.
    The schools whose grounds this section gives were not chosen by the size of their domains, so none of them can be called typical.
  • Matsushiro's school, the smallest whose ground we read, held a hall of letters and a hall for each martial art.
    Matsushiro's school had a hall of letters with east and west classrooms, a book store, separate halls for the sword, jujutsu, the spear and archery, an office and a guardhouse.
  • Hagi's Meirinkan keeps its martial hall: one long building, a sword hall at one end and a spear hall at the other.
    It is 37.8 by 10.8 m (~124 x 35 ft): a wooden-floored sword hall of 39 tatami mats in its north half and an earth-floored spear hall of 54 mats in its south half.
  • Did every domain school keep a shrine to Confucius?
    Some did and some did not. Hagi's Meirinkan was first laid out around its Confucian hall, the Taiseiden, and the school it moved to in 1849 kept a shrine to Confucius inside a stone fence, with its own gate. Mito set its Confucian shrine side by side with a Shinto one. Matsushiro's list of buildings names no shrine.
  • How big was a school's Confucian hall?
    The two measured were small, three bays square. The lord of Taku founded a school in 1699, set an image of Confucius in its lecture hall, and in 1708 finished a Confucian hall three bays by three, with a skirt roof (mokoshi) round it. The Confucian hall the Okayama domain built at Shizutani in 1684 is likewise three bays by three.
  • Two dozen structures of Okayama's school at Shizutani, opened for commoners, are protected today.
    Beside its lecture hall, a national treasure, 24 structures are listed as national important cultural properties, among them a study, a drinking room, a library, the Confucian hall, a shrine and a stone wall.
  • The great schools held much more than halls.
    Mito's ground also held a medical school, an observatory, a Shinto shrine, a riding ground, a drill ground, an archery ground and a gunnery ground. Aizu's Nisshinkan had a swimming place and an observatory, and Hagi's Meirinkan a pond where pupils swam and rode in the water.
  • A domain school stood inside its castle, beside it, or in the town.
    Mito's Kodokan was built inside the castle's third bailey, on ground that had held senior retainers' residences, and Hagi's Meirinkan first stood in the castle's third bailey. Aizu's Nisshinkan was built next to Wakamatsu castle, on its west. Hagi's Meirinkan after its move in 1849 stood in the castle town at Emukai, and Matsushiro's school on the ground of a converted official residence in the town's Kiyosu quarter.
  • The shogunate kept its own school, at Yushima in Edo.
    In 1799 its ground grew from 12,000 to over 16,000 tsubo (about 10 to 13 acres), and its Confucian hall was rebuilt at two and a half times its first size, modeled on Mito's Confucian shrine.

In the setting, the samurai families of a county town send their children to the provincial city or the domain capital for schooling. The canon names no Confucian temple.

Chinese cities, which had no domain schools, kept two kinds of school. Every prefecture and county had its official school with its Confucian temple, which is at Temples of the state cult: the City God and Confucius (chenghuang miao, wenmiao); beside those stood the academies.

  • Chinese academies stood apart from the official schools.
    The academies were a separate system from the official schools, founded privately, though sometimes under official direction.
  • How big was a provincial capital's academy?
    The one read is large: the Yuelu Academy at Changsha, one of the academies of the provincial capitals, covers 21,000 square meters (~226,000 sq ft, ~5 acres).
  • How was a provincial capital's academy laid out?
    The Yuelu Academy's page lays it out as a run of courtyards along one axis - gate, second gate, lecture hall, library and Confucian temple on it, dormitories and shrines to either side - though the same page elsewhere puts its Confucian temple to one side of the axis.
