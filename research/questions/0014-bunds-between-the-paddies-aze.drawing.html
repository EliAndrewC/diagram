How our maps draw bunds between the paddies (aze)

Our maps draw the bunds between paddies as one connected network of shared walls in a dark red-brown line: a dividing bund, which only parts two paddies, about 1.5 ft wide, between the one and two shaku the old records give (Bunds between the paddies (aze)), and a walking bund, wide enough to serve as a footpath, 2 to 5 ft, a GUESS, since no page we read gives a walking bund's width. The walls meet at T-junctions and crossings, bend and wander as a hand-piled bund does, and never step sideways; the path from a hamlet to its fields runs onto them. Some bunds carry a row of soybeans, drawn as dark green beads.

  • The dividing bund is drawn about 1.5 ft wide, at its real size.
    That is within the one to two shaku (about 1 to 2 ft) the records give: the Edo survey's reckoning of one shaku and the two-shaku bunds of a field replanned in 1869, early in the Meiji era. It sits inside the widths of the early Yayoi bunds dug up at Hattori as well. The line never thins below half a pixel, so on a city map, drawn at about 3 ft to the pixel, it still stands for about 1.5 ft.
  • The bund's height is not drawn.
    Nothing on a map depends on it. Where a map's explanation gives a height, it is a few inches, the height of the small early bunds dug up at Hattori; the large bunds there stood up to a foot. No height for a bund of the Edo period was found, and a foot as a set standard is a modern design figure (Bunds between the paddies).
  • How wide is a walking bund drawn?
    Between 2 and 5 ft, a GUESS: no page we read gives its width, so it is held between the two-shaku (about 2 ft) dividing bund and the one-ken (about 6 ft) farm road of a field replanned in 1869.

Two paddies side by side share one bund on our maps, never two.

  • No bund stands alone inside a field, and no bare strip is left between two paddies.
    No free-standing ring of bund is drawn inside a field, and no bare strip is left between two paddies: a strip of watered rice land is worth more, area for area, than a dry field's crops, and on this project's reading no farmer left it idle between two walls.
  • A plot that laps a little over its neighbor is left alone.
    A plot drawn over part of its neighbor paints out the bund it covers, so the pair still reads as one shared wall; only a plot lying nearly inside another is redrawn.
  • A strip of ground too thin to be a paddy of its own is joined to its neighbor.
    It goes to the paddy it shares the most bund with, so the two walls become one. A needle-thin basin could not, on this project's reading (a GUESS: no page we read says so), be leveled or walled at any reasonable cost, so it takes the same least width as the narrow toe of a valley fan.

A bund on our maps may bend, but it never hops sideways and carries on.

  • A bund runs on, bends, or meets another at a junction or a crossing.
    A basin may be a trapezoid and a wall may bend, but a wall that runs, steps a few feet sideways and carries on parallel to itself is not drawn. On this project's reading, a real boundary that stepped had a ditch, a path, a rock or an inherited holding at its corner, and at the scale our maps are drawn those things are drawn themselves, so a step with nothing at its corner is no boundary at all - a GUESS.
  • A long sideways offset makes an L-shaped plot, not a step.
    Past about 30 ft the offset is a limb of the plot, the honest odd shape a patchwork of old paddies has (Bunds between the paddies (aze)).
  • Our maps do not yet draw a plain ruled on the ancient jori grid, only the wavering patchwork of old paddies.
    A western plain whose fields descend from the jori division was ruled, so the record shows two forms, which a map should choose between for each settlement. Our maps do not yet make that choice: every map draws the patchwork, a known gap; see How our maps draw rice paddies and their plots (suiden).

No bund on our maps is ruled straight or square at its corners, and how the earth is drawn at a corner depends on whether the bund there is shared.

  • Where plots stand apart, their corners are rounded off and the bund between them widens.
    Where the plots are separate shapes with a real gap between them - a polder, a block of ponds - each plot's corners are rounded off and the bund, the space between, widens there. A polder's plots share no corner, so nothing is piled at a crossing there.
  • Where plots share a bund, earth is piled at the crossings.
    Where a field is divided by shared bunds, the basins must tile without a gap, so the dividing lines stay and earth is piled into the crossings: a junction becomes a lumpy node of bund, and the four basin corners read rounded because the earth has taken them.
  • A junction's pile of earth is drawn four to six feet across, a GUESS.
    No page we read gives a size. It is drawn at true size, never thinner than the map's line so it never vanishes, and never inflated past the node itself, since a dot sized only to be legible would be a fake fifteen-foot earthwork at every crossing.
  • The earth at a shared crossing is piled corner by corner.
    Each basin corner meeting at a crossing gets its own fillet of earth, with its own reach, and roughly a quarter are left bare, so a crossing can be piled heavily on one side and untouched on the other.
  • Some corners are drawn broad, some all but square.
    A corner's rounding is drawn from a wide spread, from all but square to a broad sweep, because on this project's reading corners really do differ that much: the one on the walked side wears to a broad sweep while the one behind a neighbor's bund keeps its angle for years - a GUESS. The arc itself is roughened, since a mathematically clean curve reads as stamped.
  • Each bund line wanders on its own.
    Rounding the corners alone makes a circle at each crossing, and a lattice warped as one piece, every line keeping its neighbors' shape, reads as stamped. Land split among many households did not look like that: each bund was laid by eye by a different household in a different decade (Parcels and bunds inside a polder (aze)). So each row and column line of a polder takes its own gentle bow, up to about a tenth of the grid's spacing (about 19 ft on a rice polder's 190 ft module, along a line several modules long), still meeting its neighbors at the shared crossings. How far a line bows is this project's setting, chosen so the wander shows on the map: no page we read says how far a bund's run bowed (Bunds between the paddies (aze)). The polder's planned lattice was its canals, set far wider apart than these lines, so it does not hold them straight (How our maps draw parcels and bunds inside a polder (aze)). The bow fades to nothing at both ends and is kept off the four boundary lines, so the block's edge, its dike and its ring canal do not move, and the ditches on the column lines meander with them.
  • In a valley fan, no two rows of paddies carry the same shape.
    Each point along a row's bund drifts downhill as well as along the row, by an amount that depends on where it sits along the row and on which row it is. The drift fades to nothing at a sector's first and last rows, which the water pins: the head row sits on the supply canal and takes water through cuts in its own bund, and the last row sits on the drain (Field drains (akusuiro)).

The rule the map follows: the field path runs from the hamlet to the paddy's outer bund and joins it, however short that leaves the path; it never ends in open ground short of the bund and never passes through a gap in it, since no page we read tells of either. Where no path is left to draw, the hamlet's nearest lane runs on to the bund. That the path joins the bund at its point nearest the hamlet is a GUESS. How a lane runs across the dry fields is at How our maps draw village lanes. How close a farmhouse stands to the bund is at How our maps draw farmhouses (minka).

The bund's color, and its showing at all in summer, are choices made so the map reads.

  • The bund is drawn in a dark, red-leaning mud brown.
    A pale tan would depict several feet of bare earth - a walking bund on every boundary - and reads as an orange lattice over the green. The dark brown was chosen from five candidate colors, each rendered on the same village, over a lighter chestnut that stays more plainly brown at hairline width: a line that dark blends toward black against the rice green at map view, and that weight was preferred.
  • The pale tan is kept for earthworks of real width.
    Perimeter dikes, the house pads on dike tops and the banks of dike ponds are filled in it, where the ground is bare earth several feet across (Polders: fields diked against the fluctuating water (weitian, waju); Dike-ponds: fish ponds ringed by mulberry dikes (sangji yutang)).
  • A terrace lip is drawn in the dark brown, but with a wider stroke.
    A terrace's near-vertical face has real width seen from above, where a flat bund does not.
  • The bund shows in high summer by a map drawing convention.
    The dark color is true of the season of re-plastering, before transplanting, when the ridge is fresh wet mud (Bunds between the paddies). By high summer, the season the paddies are drawn in, a real bund carries mown grass and a row of beans, and on this project's reading the green bund then all but disappears into the green field; no page we read says so. The network is drawn anyway, because it is what carries the plots' structure to the eye: the honest summer view, green on green, would erase the field's whole pattern of plots.

The soybeans on the bunds are drawn by a convention of their own.

  • Each bean plant is drawn as a round bead about 3 ft across, in a single row on the bund.
    The beans grew in a row along the bund, and a stretch of bund that carries them shows at least two beads, because a single bead does not read as a row of anything. The real plant is a knee-to-waist-high bush, 50 to 125 cm (~2-4 ft) tall; the bead says only where it stands, and since no page we read gives a plant's spread, the bead's width is not compared to one.
  • The beads are a deep pine green, darker than the plant.
    Real soybean leaves are medium green. An olive green sits between the rice green and the bund brown and vanishes at map zoom, so a darker green was chosen from three candidates rendered on one map. The other two were declined: a hunter green grayed out, and a forest green read as black against the near-black bund.
