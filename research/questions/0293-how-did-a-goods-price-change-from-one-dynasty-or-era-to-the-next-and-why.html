How did a good's price change from one dynasty or era to the next, and why?

Rokugan's money rules give one price for a good, and a range at most. The findings below give what the historical record holds instead: how far a good's price stood apart from one Chinese dynasty to the next and from one part of the Edo period to the next, measured where the sources allow in days of a laborer's wage, and what the sources say drove the change.

Salt's price swung widely across the dynasties, because it was the state's price. In the Song a jin of official salt cost a laborer more than a day's pay; under the Ming and the early Qing, in Beijing, a fifth of a day or less; and in the far markets of the Huai salt district in the 1830s, most of a day again. The sources tie the jumps to the monopoly: where it reached, what it charged merchants, and how far smuggled salt undercut it. Other goods moved with the price level: in Beijing, food and fuel about doubled in silver between 1615 and 1736 while cloth held, and in Edo-period Japan most goods held steady for a century and then tripled after the ports opened in 1859.

  • Measured in rice, Song salt fell to a fifth.
    A memorial of 1122 said that before 1068 rice was no more than 600-700 cash a shi and salt 60-70 a jin, the old saying being "a dou of rice for a jin of salt"; now rice was 2,500-3,000 a shi and salt still 60. A jin of salt went from about a dou of rice to about a fifth of one, by this project's arithmetic; the memorial does not say why rice rose.

What moved salt's price in China

  • The Song state bought cheap and sold dear, by distance.
    It bought sea salt from the makers at 4 to 6 cash a jin and set its selling price by the distance from the works, with a profit of up to tenfold. Its prices for sea salt ran from 47 cash a jin down to 8, in twenty-one grades.
  • Where the monopoly reached, the price doubled.
    In 1085 a censor reported that in the two years since Hebei's new salt law the price had doubled everywhere, and that poor households now reckoned salt like medicine.
  • Private salt undercut official salt by about a third.
    In 1086 several Sichuan prefectures sold the state's well salt at 120 cash a jin while merchants' and private small-well salt went for 70-80, and officials forced it on the people. In Fujian after 1181 the official price was high and the private cheap, and most people ate private salt.
  • Under the Ming, what merchants paid rose tenfold, and smuggling followed.
    In 1534 an official wrote that merchants now paid ten times what they had paid under the dynasty's founders while the salt makers got not a tenth of it, that official salt did not move while private salt came from every side, and that when official salt was cheap, smuggling stopped of itself.
  • The Qing held its price for decades and moved it by decree.
    The Changlu price was fixed in 1688 and raised by 0.001 tael a jin in 1732. In 1791 a prefecture of Jiangxi cut it by 2 cash a jin to the price across the border in Fujian, to beat the smugglers, and Changlu did the same in 1848, restoring the 2 cash in 1858.
  • In the 1830s official salt cost twice the private.
    Tao Shu, put in charge of the Huai salt district in 1830, wrote that in the counties of Jiangxi and Huguang official salt cost 60 to over 70 cash a jin and private salt no more than 30-odd.
  • Cutting the merchants' costs brought the price down.
    Under the old system a license of Huaibei salt cost over 10 taels by the time it reached its market, and official salt could not compete with private; Tao Shu's ticket salt of 1832-1833 cut it to a little over 5, and the smugglers turned to buying tickets. In Huainan in 1849 the cost fell by more than half, and salt prices in Hubei, Hunan and Jiangxi fell abruptly.

Salt in Japan: domain monopolies, and the Edo periods

  • Some Japanese domains held a salt monopoly.
    Kanazawa monopolized its salt from the Kan'ei years (1624-1644) almost without a break to the end of the shogunate, and banned salt from the Inland Sea; Sendai ran a salt monopoly too. Ako, a great salt-making domain on the Inland Sea, ran one from 1680 and again from 1809, until the producers' resistance ended it in 1821. No source read says what these monopolies did to the price.
  • In Edo, salt cost a fifth of a day's wage a sho in 1710-1716, a sixth in 1818-1829, a third in 1830-1858, and two-thirds after 1859.
    By the Mitsui house's Edo books, a koku of salt (100 sho) cost 23 days of the house's day wage in 1710-1716, 17 in 1818-1829, 31 in 1830-1858 and 68 in 1859-1871; in Osaka the price in silver doubled from 1818-1829 to 1830-1858. No source read says why salt doubled after 1830.

Other goods in China, dynasty by dynasty

  • In Beijing, food and fuel about doubled in silver from 1615 to 1736, while cloth held and pig iron fell.
    Between the Ming Ministry of Works' price list of 1615 and the Qing list of 1736, wheat flour went from 0.008 to 0.014 tael a jin, glutinous rice from 1 to 2.52 taels a shi and coal more than doubled, while coarse white cotton cloth stayed at 0.094 and 0.09 tael a zhang and pig iron fell from 0.006 to 0.004 tael a jin.
  • Wages rose more than flour.
    A Ming laborer's day bought 3.75 to 8.75 jin of flour, and a Qing laborer's 6.7 to 13.75; the list's historian calls this a preliminary judgment.
  • The driver named: silver bought less and less rice.
    An index of silver's purchasing power in rice runs 100 in the 15th century, 61.83 in the 16th, 41.80 in the 17th, 27.78 in the 18th and 17.37 in the 19th. One historian puts the 18th century's rise in rice at more than fourfold, from American silver and the growth of population.
  • Song rice quadrupled in half a century.
    From 600-700 cash a shi before 1068 to 2,500-3,000 in 1122.
  • In Songjiang at the fall of the Ming, cotton swung wildly and cloth less.
    Raw cotton went from 0.5-0.6 tael a hundred jin after 1644 to 9 taels in 1651; a bolt of cloth went from 0.15-0.16 tael to under 0.1 in 1644-1645 and 0.4-0.5 in 1654, then back to about 0.2.

Other goods across the Edo period

The Mitsui house's books give the same goods in Kyoto from 1741 and in Edo and Osaka from 1818, with a laborer's day wage in Kyoto and Edo, so each good can be measured in days of the same city's wage, by this project's arithmetic.

  • For a century most goods held steady in Kyoto.
    A koku of rice cost 86 days of the Kyoto wage in 1741-1762 and 91 in 1791-1817, ten kan of firewood 3.2 and 3.3 days, and a kin of tobacco 2.1 and 2.2; the day wage itself stood at 0.86 and 0.85 monme.
  • The recoinage of 1818-1829 raised the wage before prices.
    Kyoto's wage rose to 1.03 monme while rice stayed near 74, so a koku cost 72 days; the debasement and the famine of 1833-1838 made the next two decades extremely volatile.
  • From 1830 prices rose by about a third.
    In 1830-1858 a koku of rice cost 104 days in Kyoto and 122 in Edo, where the wage stood still at 0.91 monme; the Osaka price level averaged 36% above that of 1802-1829.
  • After the ports opened in 1859, prices tripled.
    In 1859-1871 a koku of rice cost 294 days in Kyoto and 239 in Edo, lamp oil 824 and 763 days, and ten kan of firewood in Kyoto 6.3 days, about twice its old figure. The price level of 1859-1867 averaged 239% above that of 1830-1858.

Against the setting's basket and day wage

  • Salt swung widest of the goods measured here; firewood and cloth least.
    Salt ran from a fifth of a day's wage a jin to most of a day between dynasties and places, with the monopoly the driver the sources name. Rice, flour and fuel moved with the price level: about double in silver in Beijing from 1615 to 1736, steady in Japan for a century, about a third higher after 1830 and three times higher after 1859. Cotton cloth held flat in Beijing across the change of dynasty, and firewood and tobacco were the steadiest Mitsui goods until 1859.
  • The day wage held while prices moved.
    Kyoto's day wage stood at 0.85-1.03 monme from 1741 to 1858, and Edo's at about 0.9 from 1818 to 1858, so a price set in days of a wage moves with the good, not the wage.
