The formal entrance and a guest's arrival (genkan)

A samurai house of standing had a formal entrance, the genkan, kept for the head of the household and honored guests; the rest of the household came and went by an inner entrance. In the Edo period the word meant an entrance with a shikidai, the low board step where guests were bowed in and seen off. A guest of rank came by palanquin, and the palanquin was brought right alongside the step, so that its rider went into the building without setting foot on the ground.

So a guest's arrival was staged: the gate, then open ground a palanquin could cross, then the step. Not every house had such an entrance. The houses of senior retainers did; a middle-rank house could do without one, and bring its guest in through a garden instead.

  • In the Edo period, "genkan" meant an entrance with a shikidai.
    In the shoin style of building, the genkan is the room or building set at a residence's formal entrance. In a warrior's house the projecting board floor where a palanquin was set down had been called the genkan, and the room of reception the shikidai; in the Edo period the whole came to be called the genkan, and the shikidai the lower board step where guests were bowed in and out.
  • The shikidai entrance was for receiving formal guests.
    It was a special entrance room for them.
  • How big was a shikidai?
    About 12 by 9 ft, in the one example found measured. The council hall that the small Sayama domain built at its jin'ya in 1775 had a genkan of 8 mats and a shikidai of 2 ken and 6 mats, with a closet. That the 2 ken (~12 ft) is the shikidai's width, so that its 6 mats make it about 1.5 ken (~9 ft) deep, is this project's reading and arithmetic. It is an official building's entrance, not a house's.
  • A guest of rank stepped from the palanquin straight into the building.
    The shikidai entrance had a step, and the palanquin was brought right alongside it, so that its rider stepped up into the building without getting down onto the ground.
  • The main genkan was for the head of the household and honored guests.
    The rest of the household and its senior staff used an inner entrance, the uchigenkan.
  • The kitchen door was the way in from outside to the kitchen.
    The kitchen door, the katteguchi, is the doorway leading from outside into the kitchen.
  • The houses of senior retainers had a shikidai.
    They had earthen walls, a gatehouse with rooms in it (a nagaya-mon) and a shikidai.
  • Where an intendant's office and residence shared one compound, did the genkan belong to the office?
    At the Takayama jin'ya, the only jin'ya in the country whose principal parts survive, a visitor enters by the genkan, and the route goes round the rooms of the office and continues on to the residence where the intendants lived. The page describes a visitor's route, not how the office and the residence were laid out against each other.
  • Did a guest's way from Takayama's office to the residence run indoors?
    The sources read leave it open, because no Edo-period plan of the jin'ya has been read. Today's visitors' route goes round the rooms of the office and continues on to the residence, but that is a modern route over the restored compound. The prefecture's table of the site's buildings lists a connecting corridor of 12.78 m² (about 138 sq ft) beside the office of 1816 and the residence restored in 1996, and does not say what it joins. The jin'ya's own illustration of the compound shows the office's roofs running straight back into the wings behind it, with no open ground between; the illustration carries no labels, and that those wings are the residence is read from their place. But it draws the compound as restored, and the residence was restored from a drawing of 1830 that has not been read.
  • A middle-rank house could have no genkan with a shikidai, and bring its guests in through a walled garden instead.
    The old Koseki house at Shiroishi, the house of a middle-rank retainer, has none. The gate, reached over an earthen bridge, leads along the earthen wall to the kitchen entrance, which the page calls the front entrance. A guest instead passes through a middle gate (nakamon) in the wall and along the roji, the walled garden path, straight to the veranda of the principal zashiki. The earthen wall encloses the roji in an L.
  • Where did a middle gate stand against an office hall?
    No page we read says, at an intendant's office or anywhere else. The Koseki house's middle gate is a gate in the wall of the roji, leading from inside the street gate straight to the veranda of the principal room, and the house is a middle-rank retainer's residence, not an office. The city of Takayama lists a middle gate among the surviving structures of its jin'ya, but does not say where it stands.
  • Was there open ground between the gate and the entrance?
    One drawing shows it: on a drawing made in 1869, just after the Edo period, of the jin'ya at Joge, the entrance with its step faces the open ground inside the main gate.
  • Was the ground before the entrance ever a garden?
    No page we read on the genkan and the shikidai puts a garden there. Two drawings show the ground just inside the gate, and both show it open. At Joge the entrance faces it directly. The other is a plate from a Japanese garden book that Edward Morse, who wrote on Japan's houses, reproduced in 1886. Its stone path crosses a courtyard and runs past a storehouse to a second gateway, then on to the genkan, the main entrance. Morse places the gardens of the plates he took from that book in Sakai "nearly two hundred years ago", and says the plates show stone lanterns and rocks. So the dating is loose, the house's owner is not named, and the plate shows a garden approach rather than the surface of an official's court. The other garden approach found is the middle-rank house's, where there was no genkan with a shikidai. Nor does any page we read describe a forecourt before the genkan of an intendant's office fenced off from its garden, or a pond set on the line from the gate to the entrance.
  • Was the ground before a formal entrance swept, raked, graveled or sanded?
    Graveled, at Edo, the shogun's capital. At its South Town Magistracy a stone path about 6 shaku (about 6 ft) wide ran from the main gate to the entrance, with black Nachi gravel laid outside the paving, according to an Edo history blog that cites a plan of the magistracy for its area but names no source for the paving. A former officer of the South Town Magistracy, born in 1853, recalled the same there: paving straight to the entrance and small black Nachi gravel spread over the ground. The houses granted to the town magistracies' officers had small gravel laid from the street gate to an entrance with a shikidai. All three are of Edo, told on enthusiasts' pages. No page we read says that the ground was swept, raked or sanded, or left as bare earth, before Japan began to modernize in 1868, or gives its surface outside Edo. The record treats a shrine precinct's swept surface as a guess too, and records the cleaning of graves and village shrines only as modern custom (Ground swept clear around shrines and graves). Morse saw one swept courtyard in Kyoto in the years before he wrote in 1885. Just inside the gateway of a house nearly three centuries old, "The yard was well swept, and quite free from weeds". That is one house, seen in Morse's own day; he does not say whether a formal entrance faced that yard.

In China, a surviving governor-general's office was entered the same way, through its gate into open ground.

  • Through the main gate of the Zhili governor-general's office lies an open front court.
    At that office, a yamen, the open front court inside the main gate is planted with dozens of stout junipers.
