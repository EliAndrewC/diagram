Tanning yards

In Edo-period Japan, before 1868, the hides a tanner worked came from the oxen and horses that died in the district around it. When an ox or a horse died, it went, without payment, to the outcaste kawata community that held the right to fallen animals over that district, and that community held the trade in leather, which armies could not do without. So a tanning yard worked the dead draft stock of a whole district.

Tanning was a water trade. The method of Himeji, a town known for its white leather, steeped salted hides in a river for several days, and the tanners lived, it seems, at the edge of the castle town, at a chokepoint on the roads or on the riverbank.

  • Hides came from the cattle and horses that died in a district, which went to the outcaste community by right.
    In the Edo period the right to take the carcasses of cattle and horses that had died was held, as a monopoly, by the outcaste community, and the district in which a holder collected them was called the kusaba or danna-ba. An owner of whatever status, under whatever lord, had to hand a dead ox or horse over to the right-holder without payment.
  • The outcaste community held a monopoly on leather, which armies needed.
    It worked the dead cattle and horses for its income and held the leather production that arms and armor depended on. The warlords of the Sengoku period, building their castle towns, placed the leather producers under special protection and control to keep a steady supply of the leather their armies needed.
  • In this setting, a domain's surplus horses go to its farms.
    Daimyo with more horses than they need give them to village headmen to share out among the farms as draft animals, and such horses are often slaughtered and eaten when the need for meat outweighs the farmers' means to feed them. The farms' draft stock is where a tanner's hides come from.
  • No page we read gives how many carcasses a district sent its tanners in a year.
    The article on the right to fallen cattle and horses names the district a holder collected from, but gives no count for any.
  • Draft animals, we guess, died most in late winter and early spring.
    That is when the fodder ran out and the animals were worn from the autumn hauling. It is a GUESS: no page we read gives the season.

The method set where a tannery could stand: by running water, and at the edge of town.

  • Himeji's white leather, shironameshi, was made by soaking hides in a river for several days.
    The white-tanned leather of Himeji is made by steeping salted hides in the river for several days while microorganisms loosen the hair roots, scraping the hair off with a blade, then adding salt and rapeseed oil, beating the hide dry and kneading it soft in the sun.
  • Japan's medieval leather workers were named for the riverbed.
    They slaughtered animals and worked hides, and were called kawaramono, "those of the riverbed", because they lived on the riverbeds and around them. The article on them gives two theories of why: that the riverbed was untaxed, and that leather work needs a great deal of water. In the medieval centuries the riverbed was their workplace, for executions as well as for slaughter and leather.
  • Hides were salted so that they would keep until they could be worked.
    Curing a hide with salt stops it rotting in the time between taking it and working it; wet-salted hides are pressed into packs for about 30 days.

Tanners outside Japan worked differently, and an English excavation gives the only count of a tannery's pits we found.

  • Other tanning traditions used urine, lime and dung.
    Tanners elsewhere took the hair off by soaking the skin in urine, painting it with lime or letting it rot, and softened it by pounding in dung, commonly a dog's or a pigeon's. The steps the Himeji account lists use none of these.
  • Some tanneries soaked their hides in standing vats instead of a river.
    At the Chouara tannery of Fez, in Morocco, hides are soaked in a series of liquids of urine, pigeon droppings, quicklime, salt and water, held in numerous stone vats.
  • Tanning was ranked a noxious trade and kept to the outskirts of town.
    It was counted an "odoriferous trade" and pushed to the edge of town among the poor, and the first step was to soak the skins in water.
  • The largest tannery excavated at Northampton, England, had 36 or 37 pits.
    The excavated tanneries there were of round and rectangular pits, and all were small; the largest, the Western Tannery, had 36 to 37.

What a tannery let into its water mattered to the rice fields below it.

  • Rice is moderately sensitive to salt.
    The FAO's table of crop salt tolerance ranks paddy rice moderately sensitive, with full yield up to 3.0 dS/m in the soil and 2.0 in the irrigation water.
  • What in a tannery's waste water would harm a rice field?
    In this project's reading, the salt. The Himeji method's waste is the soak's rot, salt and an oil film. Rice, sensitive to salt, answers, in this project's reading, to the water of the flooded field it stands in, so brine coming down an irrigation ditch is the wrong thing to send it. The rot is the weaker objection: farmers spread night soil on their fields on purpose.
  • Irrigation water was held by custom and shared out by the villages.
    Over the Edo period the management of water rights passed from the lord's judgment to self-government among villages, through irrigation associations upheld by customary practice, and disputes that in the medieval period ended in violence went to the courts. Rights to farm water drawn before the River Act of 1896 are recognized under modern law. How water was held in China we have not read.
  • Could an outcaste quarter hold a share of the water upstream?
    In this project's reading, no: a community of the lowest caste would not have been granted an intake at the head of a system its villages fought over in the courts, and the water it could hold is the water no one upstream wanted, the tail below every intake. The untaxed-riverbed theory of the kawaramono reads the same way.
  • Lower fields, we guess, took up the drainage of the fields above.
    A tannery at the tail of a system sent its waste on down the drain, so where the drain went mattered. In this project's reading, in Japan and China alike, a drain at one level was commonly a supply at the next. It is a GUESS: no page we read says so.
  • A field drain, we guess, ran only through the irrigation season.
    A tannery whose water was a field drain had water only while the drain ran. It ran, in this project's reading, heaviest when the fields were drained before harvest, and near-dry from harvest to transplanting. It is a GUESS: no page we read describes a drain's flow across the year.

Where the tanners lived was a matter of their caste as much as of their trade. For where a town's outcaste quarter stood and how large it was, see Burakumin quarters and caste zoning.

  • Where in a town did the tanners live?
    At its edge. The outcaste community often lived on ground unsuited to farming, at the edge of the village or beside a river; the leather workers the Sengoku warlords took under their control were housed, it seems, at the edge of the castle town, at a chokepoint on the roads, or on the riverbank. That a castle town's quarter stood at the town's edge as a village's did is this project's reading.
  • Most castle towns had no wall around the town at first.
    In the first castle towns only the lord's castle had a moat and wall, and the town had none; enclosing a whole town in a moat and earthworks, the sogamae, came later. So in Japan the question was which edge of town the tanners took, not which side of a wall: this project's reading.
  • Takada set its outcaste quarter at the town's northwest edge, on the Kaga road.
    When the castle town was laid out around 1614, those who bore its guard and cleaning duties were moved from Naoetsu to a block along the Kaga road at the town's northwest edge.
  • Edo's outcaste head lived at the city's northern edge.
    Danzaemon, the hereditary head of the outcastes, lived near the path and canal that ran from the Sumida riverbank along the city's northern edge to the Yoshiwara pleasure quarter.
  • Danzaemon's compound in Edo was an enclosed precinct, screened from view.
    The Asakusa Shinmachi block was screened by temples, shrines and fences so that no one could see in. It held his official and private residences, storehouses, a shrine, a court and a jail, and housing for three or four hundred family members of his officials. That it stood inside the city is this project's reading of its Asakusa address.
  • Was the outcaste quarter set toward one compass direction, such as the unlucky northeast?
    No page we read ties it to the northeast, the kimon, or to any other bearing. The edge differed from town to town: northwest at Takada, north at Edo.
  • Did a town's outcaste quarter, its tannery and its execution ground lie on the same side of town?
    In this project's reading of Edo and Takada, yes: they went out the same way, toward the edge where the road leaves and the river runs. In Edo the north was where the Kozukappara execution ground lay and where the pleasure quarter was moved, behind Senso-ji, though Edo had a second great execution ground to the south. No page states it as a rule.
  • Towns, we guess, kept the carcass haul off their main streets.
    A dead ox or horse was carried from the farm where it fell to the tannery, and handling it was death-pollution, kegare. A quarter and works on the same side of town would keep that haul out of the rest of it. It is a GUESS: no page we read mentions a designated carcass route, and one says only that the handling of fallen cattle and horses was strictly controlled.
  • Chinese towns, we guess, put their tanners in the suburbs outside the walls.
    In this project's reading, China had no hereditary outcaste caste tied to leather, though it had debased statuses of its own, and its tanners and butchers were ordinary craftsmen, with the noxious water trades in the suburbs outside the walls, the guanxiang, below the water gate. It is a GUESS: no page we read places them.

Only one part of the tanners' work needed the river.

  • Where did the outcaste caste do its other work?
    In town or at home, in this project's reading. It served as executioners, constables, watchmen and wardens of the hills and the water, made and sold straw and leather-soled sandals, and made and sold lamp wicks. That it worked drum heads and armor lacing too is a GUESS: the article that lists these trades names neither.
  • Did a tannery need its quarter close by?
    No, in this project's reading: the quarter stood where the caste's town duties were and the tannery where the river was, so the two could lie far apart and the tanners walked between them.
  • Each outcaste quarter was a community under its own headman.
    Each settlement had a head who guarded it and was paid by its residents, and in the Kanto the shogunate ruled the outcaste and hinin statuses through Danzaemon.
  • Did the tanners sleep at the works?
    No page we read says they did. The hides lay several days in the river, where no watchman could guard them anyway. In this project's reading a stolen hide was worth little: whoever took one had handled death-pollution, kegare, and could not sell it, since the outcaste community held the right to every carcass in its district and the trade in leather. That no lone outcaste house stood away from its quarter is this project's reading of the quarter's corporate form.
