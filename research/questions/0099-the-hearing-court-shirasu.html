The hearing court (shirasu)

A hearing court (shirasu) was the floor below a magistrate's hall where the plaintiffs and defendants of a case sat. The magistrate and the officials sat above, in the hall; the parties sat below, on straw mats spread on the floor. The court was roofed, or was an earth floor inside the building.

Intendants' offices kept hearing courts too. The one intendant's office that survives, the Takayama Jin'ya, keeps its court paved with cobbles and roofed, beside an examination room that was rebuilt in 1816 as one part of the office. How big the office hall itself was is at The office hall and its clerks (goyakusho).

  • A magistracy's hearing court was roofed, not open to the sky.
    It either had a roof built over it or was an earth floor inside the building, spread with gravel; the open-air court of white gravel is the scene of period dramas.
  • The earliest courts were bare earth, and the white gravel came later.
    In the oldest times the earth floor was used as it was; the floor of white gravel came later.
  • Intendants' offices and barrier checkpoints had hearing courts too.
    Hearing courts were set up at intendants' offices and at the barrier checkpoints (sekisho) on the roads, as well as at the magistracies.
  • Takayama's is the only one of more than fifty intendants' offices that survives.
    The city of Takayama's page on the jin'ya calls it the only one surviving of more than fifty across the country.
  • At Takayama, the court and the examination room are paved with cobbles and roofed.
    The same page names both the examination room (ginmisho) and the court as paved with cobbles and roofed.
  • Takayama used cobbles because its province had no white sand.
    Hida province has no sea, the jin'ya's guide explains, and not enough white sand could be had to spread on the court, so cobbles from the riverbed were spread in its place.
  • Takayama's court was built inside because of the snow.
    Hida is snow country, and a court with no roof would be buried in winter and unusable, so the court was made inside the building; so the jin'ya's guide explains it.
  • Takayama has two hearing courts.
    One received the villages' suits and petitions, as an office's service window would now; at the other, those who had committed crimes were examined and judged, as in a court of law.
  • At Takayama, most criminal cases went to the shogunate for its ruling.
    The office heard them, but for the majority the shogunate's ruling was sought.
  • Was Takayama's court part of the office hall, or a stage of its own?
    Part of the hall, by this project's reading. The office was rebuilt in 1816, in the Edo period, in such parts as its entrance hall, examination room, working office and great hall, which this project reads as one block. So the court lay against a room of the office hall, not a stage of its own.
  • Takayama's office block was built to display the office's rank.
    The great hall of the office is of a design that sets great weight on formality, and its entrance room keeps, as rebuilt in 1816, a great alcove marking a rank of 100,000 koku and a wave pattern that even daimyo refrained from using.

At Edo, the shogun's capital, the town magistracy's court was a stepped hall, and its plan records who sat on each level.

  • At Edo the court was a stepped hall: a tatami room on the top level, a gravel floor on the bottom.
    By a plan of the South Town Magistracy made in 1810, the top level was a tatami room, the kujiba, where the magistrate and the other officials sat, and the bottom level a gravel floor on which straw mats were spread for the plaintiffs and defendants.
  • At Edo, the magistrate's tatami room was divided into three rooms, with a board veranda at its front.
    The veranda was 3 shaku (about 3 ft) wide, with a stair of three steps, one ken wide, at its middle.
  • At Edo, the magistrate sat in the innermost room, the examiner and the clerk in the middle one, and a witnessing officer in the room nearest the court.
    The examining yoriki and the clerk, a doshin, sat in the middle room, and the witnessing doshin in the room nearest the court.
  • Doshin crouched at the left and right of the court itself.
    The same account puts crouching doshin in attendance at the court's two sides.
  • Did the examiners and scribes have a level of their own?
    At some magistracies. One dictionary puts the examiners and the scribes on an upper level below the magistrate's room; another says only some magistracies set that level apart.
  • No page we read gives the size of the clerks' place in the hall.
    The accounts of the court say who sat on each level, but not how much room any place took.

Where the parties sat was set by status.

  • Samurai, priests and monks did not kneel on the gravel, but sat on the hall's verandas.
    Samurai (not masterless ones), Shinto priests, monks and townsmen who were official purveyors sat on the verandas of the hall, which ran in two steps: samurai, priests and monks on the upper, the rest on the lower.
  • Peasants, townsmen and lesser ronin knelt below, on straw mats spread on the gravel floor.
    Two dictionary entries say so.
  • Did an accused samurai sit on the upper veranda or the board veranda?
    Accounts differ. The imidas encyclopedia also puts commoners on mats, but seats a samurai or monk accused of a crime on the board veranda, where the Wikipedia account puts samurai on the upper of the two verandas.
  • The accused sat roped at the center of the court, with the plaintiff and the town officials behind.
    A servant held the end of the accused's rope. Behind the accused sat the town officials, headmen and landlords on straw matting on the left, and the plaintiff on the right.
