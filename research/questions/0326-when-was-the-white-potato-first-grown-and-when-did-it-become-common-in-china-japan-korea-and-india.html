When was the white potato first grown, and when did it become common, in China, Japan, Korea and India?

Rokugan's notes mention no potato of any kind, though they list the yam (imo) among the season words of autumn. The findings below give what the historical record holds for the white potato in China, Japan, Korea and India.

The white potato, Solanum tuberosum, comes from South America. Most of its dates in Asia are modern statements about older texts not read here: Taiwan in 1650 for the earliest reported planting, a disputed date around 1600 for Japan, 1824 or 1825 for Korea, and about 1780 for the first secure date in India. Most often it was a food of mountains, of the poor or of famine years. Korea took about 22 years to reach four provinces, on this project's arithmetic, and the others took longer. The sweet potato, a different plant, is in when the sweet potato was first grown, tobacco in when tobacco was first grown, dry-field crops in dry fields and their crops, and commercial crops in cash crops on rice land.

China

  • Which plant do the Chinese words mean?
    The anthropologist Berthold Laufer's history of the potato was read only as a newspaper's excerpt from its Chinese translation, so any English wording of it here is a translation of a translation. The translation's word for the plant is ma-ling-shu (马铃薯). Laufer says the botanist Wu Qijun titled it yang-yu (阳芋), colloquially shan-yao-dan (山药蛋). A magazine's summary of the historian Ho Ping-ti adds ho-lan-shu, the "Holland potato". Tu-dou (土豆) is another name for it today.
  • When is it first recorded in China? The modern accounts disagree.
    None of the older texts below was read:
    • Late Ming, before 1644: the summary has Ho saying it came from Peru then, with no place or year, and itself wonders about another origin.
    • Late Ming, as tu-dou: Zhang Jian, in a magazine article, says the earliest record "may be" the tu-yu (土芋) or tu-dou of the Bencao gangmu, reads a Ming palace list's tu-dou as the potato, and quotes a gazetteer of about 1680 on tu-dou that "tastes like fan-shu", the sweet potato. Laufer says the Bencao gangmu does not mention the potato. The passage Zhang quotes calls the plant a vine humans cannot eat in one sentence and one that humans boil in lye water and eat in the next, and also names it huang-du (黄独), so this project treats these as dates for the word only.
    • 1650: Laufer says the earliest mention is by the Dutchman John Struys, who listed potatoes among the products of Taiwan.
    • 1700: Laufer takes as the earliest Chinese record the Songxi county gazetteer's ma-ling-shu in Fujian, a vegetable "leaning on trees", black and bitter-sweet. This project thinks that fits the white potato badly, so 1700 is the first date for the name, not a secure first record of the plant.
    Laufer concludes that an absolute date cannot be fixed.
  • Where did it come from?
    Laufer thinks the Chinese may have brought it to Taiwan from the Philippines, though he lacks details, and says the theory of a Dutch introduction rests only on the name "Holland tuber", while granting that the Dutch could have carried it to Taiwan. Zhang says there "may" have been two routes into China: the Dutch carried it from Japan to Taiwan in the mid-1600s, and a northern route had reached Hebei before the 1680s.
  • The first reported planting is on Taiwan in the 1650s.
    Laufer and Zhang both report Struys finding it grown there in 1650, and the summary of Ho says the 1650s.
  • By 1848 it was a field crop in Shanxi and a stored food of the poor.
    Laufer reports Wu, whose botanical description he calls the earliest and best in Chinese: it was grown in Guizhou and Yunnan, as a field crop in Shanxi, and on the Zhongnan mountains near Xi'an, and it could save the poor from famine. Laufer adds that poor mountain farmers in parts of Shaanxi were growing it in the 1700s. From 1650 to 1848 is 198 years.
  • For most of the Qing it was negligible across China as a whole.
    The historian Li Xinsheng's estimate is that the potato "can be ignored altogether" in the crop area of most of the Qing. Laufer agrees that its history in China is "not national history" but scattered local records, and quotes an unnamed writer on a food of the poor that the rich were ashamed to eat. The exceptions are mostly high, cold places. His study of the southwest says it is concentrated in the cold mountains, and reports it in Guizhou in the Daoguang reign as "a store for the poor". A local gazetteer in Shaanxi calls it the staple of the mountain population. The diplomat William Rockhill wrote in 1891 of northern Shanxi living on white potatoes and oats. At Fuzhou in the 1860s the missionary Justus Doolittle says two crops of "the Irish or foreign potato" could be raised on one piece of land, but he does not name it among the most common crops.

Japan

  • Which plant do the Japanese words mean?
    The Japanese Wikipedia article gives jagaimo (ジャガイモ), from jagatara-imo, and bareisho (馬鈴薯), a Chinese plant name, for Solanum tuberosum.
  • When did it reach Japan? The date is disputed.
    Three dates are given, all saying the Dutch brought it:
    • 1598: the article, saying that accounts differ.
    • The early 1600s, by Dutch ship from Jagatora in Java: the same article.
    • 1601, at Nagasaki: Zhang, citing "Western historical records" he does not name.
    Against the first date, the first Dutch ship to reach Japan left Rotterdam in June 1598 and came ashore in April 1600, so this project doubts that the Dutch brought it in 1598. Laufer says the theory of a Dutch introduction to Japan rests only on the name jagatara-imo. No record of those years was read.
  • The first dated planting named is in Hokkaido in 1706.
    The article says a man named Matsubei grew it at Setana that year, 105 to 108 years after the arrival dates above. It says the intendant of Hida brought in seed tubers from Shinano in 1748, and that the intendant of Kai, Nakai Seidayu, promoted it in the 1700s. The scholar Ono Ranzan recorded it being grown at Kurobera in Kai in 1801; his record was not read.
  • Under the Tokugawa it was a crop of mountains, the cold north and famine.
    The article says growing spread from the Edo period in mountain and cold districts poor for rice, and one local name, "help potato", comes from its use in famine. In the late 1700s, it also says without squaring this with 1706, it entered Hokkaido and the northeast under Russian influence as a crop against famine, and in the later Edo period the Ainu grew it too. No source read counts how much was grown.
  • It was not taken up in earnest before Meiji.
    The article says it was introduced in earnest only after the Meiji Restoration of 1868, for settling Hokkaido, with demand at first for Western cooking. That is 267 to 270 years after the arrival dates above.

Korea

  • Which plant do the Korean words mean?
    The Encyclopedia of Korean Culture gives gamja (감자) the scientific name Solanum tuberosum L. and the names in Chinese characters bukjeo (北藷), togamjeo (土甘藷), yangjeo (洋藷) and jijeo (地藷). Gamjeo (감저, 甘藷) alone is the sweet potato, in when the sweet potato was first grown. The dates below use bukjeo, or a modern gamja set apart from goguma, the sweet potato, so they concern the white potato as far as the encyclopedia's reading goes.
  • It is first recorded for 1824 or 1825, coming from the far north.
    The encyclopedia says the exact date is not known, and that the Oju yeonmun jangjeon sango has bukjeo, also called togamjeo, first coming in from Gwanbuk, the far northeast, in those years. Another article reports that the Gamjeo gyeongjang seol, a book of unknown author, records Sin Jong-min bringing in bukjeo in 1830.
  • A second introduction is reported in 1832, in the southwest.
    The encyclopedia reports the Wonjeobo of Kim Chang-han: a missionary aboard a British merchant ship, lying about a month off what is now North Jeolla, handed out seed tubers and taught how to grow them.
  • By 1847 it had reached four provinces.
    Another article says it spread very quickly, and that by 1847 it had reached Jeolla, Gyeonggi, Chungcheong and Gangwon, outdoing the sweet potato that had come 60 years before it. That is 22 or 23 years from the first date. The article names no text for 1847, and no source read counts the crop.
  • Who grew it, and who ate it?
    At Musan, the encyclopedia says, farmers grew it so profitably that they raised no other grain, leaving nothing to tax, so a ban followed. Around Yangju, Wonju and Cheorwon it reportedly spared people hunger in bad years. In the 1890s the traveler Isabella Bird Bishop, who does not say which kind she means here, found it grown more on the north branch of the Han River than the south, and "largely cultivated" around Tok Chhön, in the diet there, though she says the poor lived on millet.

India

  • Which plant do the Indian words and the English "potato" mean?
    Hobson-Jobson, the glossary of British India, says alu is in modern use the common potato and sometimes the sweet potato. A writer named Achaya, as a blog quotes him, says the English name began as the sweet potato's, so an early English "potato" may be either plant.
  • The first mentions, in 1615 and the 1670s, are disputed.
    Achaya places potatoes at a dinner at Ajmer in 1615 and says, in another passage, that Edward Terry mentioned them that year, and John Fryer named them in a garden. Hobson-Jobson quotes Fryer under 1673, with potatoes beside yams, which separates them from the yam but not the sweet potato; Achaya dates him 1675 in one place and 1678 in another. Both sides:
    • Sweet potatoes, probably: Achaya, who says the white potato had not yet reached India.
    • White potatoes: the blogger, who thinks most accounts take Fryer's plant so, and an industry directory, which reports a 2019 review placing it in the gardens of Surat and Karnataka by 1675, while calling the date and route of arrival uncertain.
  • The first secure date is about 1780.
    Achaya says the identity of a "basket of potatoes" given to Warren Hastings around then "is not in doubt", and cites a report of 1860 that by 1780 potatoes were in high repute as foods in Calcutta.
  • Who brought it is disputed.
    That report says the Dutch "are said to have been the first to introduce the culture of potatoes", from the Cape of Good Hope. The directory and a popular article say the Portuguese, the directory saying "probably".
  • The East India Company pushed it from 1797.
    The article says that when the Company arrived in the 1600s very few Indians grew or ate it, and that ledgers of 1797 show it giving out free seed in Bengal, and that a campaign began at Madras that year.
  • It was an established vegetable by the early 1800s, and never a staple.
    The directory says so of the late 1700s and early 1800s, in hills and plains. The article credits the Company's promotion with widespread cultivation, quotes an army wife's letter of 1814 saying Indians "are all fond of it", and adds that it never displaced rice. From about 1780 to that letter is 34 years. No source read counts how much was grown.
