Fire, and the fireproof storehouses (dozō)

The office hall at Takayama, the one office whose buildings are listed by construction, was a wooden building, and wooden halls burned: the Sado magistracy burned and was rebuilt five times. The answer to fire was the earth-walled storehouse (dozō), built to resist it. In 1720, in the Edo period, the shogun's government came to permit and then encourage earthen-storehouse building for the townhouses of Edo that had been forbidden it, and in a fire, valuables that could not be carried out were put into the storehouses.

An office kept its papers in storehouses of this kind. The one office whose records storehouse is measured, the intendant's office at Takayama that governed the province of Hida, kept its documents in a small earth-walled one of about 450 sq ft. Against fire in the wooden buildings themselves, the townspeople of Edo kept tubs of standing water at the front entrance and on the roof.

  • Takayama's office was built of timber, its storehouses of earth.
    The Takayama Jin'ya's facilities table groups its buildings by construction: the office, dated 1816, among the timber buildings, and the kitchen storehouse, the great storehouse (okura) and the book storehouse among those of earthen-storehouse construction. The office was a single-story hall roofed with wooden shingles.
  • How often did an office's wooden halls burn?
    Some many times, some never, in the two offices read. The Sado magistracy, built in 1603, burned and was rebuilt five times. Takayama's office, rebuilt several times, by good fortune never suffered a fire.
  • Edo came to encourage earth-walled building against fire.
    A town ordinance on fireproof building was issued in 1720, permitting and then encouraging tile roofs, earthen-storehouse construction and earth-plastered walls on the townhouses that had been forbidden them. Buildings came to be ordered to adopt such fire-resistant construction.
  • In a fire, Edo's townspeople put what they could not carry out into their storehouses.
    Valuables that could not be carried out went into the plastered storehouses or pit cellars.
  • Why did an office keep its grain and papers in storehouses rather than in rooms of the hall?
    Against fire, this project judges - a GUESS at the reason, since no page read says why.
  • How big was an office's records storehouse?
    Small, on the one measure found. The Takayama Jin'ya kept its documents in a book storehouse built in 1841, earth-walled, with a floor of 41.46 m² (~446 sq ft). Its kitchen storehouse, built the year before, has a larger floor, of 68.82 m² (~741 sq ft). The book storehouse survives. It was the records store of the office that governed the whole province of Hida, so this project judges that a county office's, governing less, was no larger - a GUESS. No page read gives a records storehouse's length and depth, or measures any other office's.

The record of fighting fire in the wooden buildings themselves is of the towns, above all Edo.

  • Edo's townspeople kept standing water at the entrance and on the roof.
    Rainwater tubs (tensuioke) and other fire water were kept ready, as a matter of daily habit, at the front entrance and on the roof, with soaked fire-beaters to put out falling sparks.
  • A town order in Edo required tubs of water kept ready against fire.
    Tubs filled with water were to be kept ready against fire, and the town block swept clean.
  • Did every building in Edo keep its own tub of fire water?
    Perhaps, as a custom rather than a rule, on this project's reading - a GUESS. The pages read show the daily habit and the order, not a rule for every building, and none puts stacks of hand buckets beside the tubs.
  • Edo organized its town firemen into companies, one to about twenty blocks.
    In 1720 the town fire brigades were reorganized by district, with forty-seven companies, the iroha-gumi, covering the city west of the Sumida River.

The Qing emperors' palace in Beijing, the Forbidden City, kept its fire water in great vats instead, and the record of where they stood is fuller.

  • The Qing statutes record 308 great water vats in the Forbidden City.
    The vats stood before its great halls and in its courtyards, 308 of them by the Qing statutes, of which 231 remain.
  • The vats had auspicious names.
    They were called "taiping vats" or "auspicious vats", and popularly, it is said, men-hai, "the sea before the gate", in the hope that a great sea before the gate would overcome fire with water.
  • Qing regulations set the Forbidden City's vats courtyard by courtyard, the finest beside the greatest halls.
    Qing regulations said which kind of vat stood in which courtyard and how many: gilt bronze beside the great halls and gates, plain bronze or iron in the courtyards of the rear palace.
  • The Forbidden City's vats stood wherever a hall was far from water.
    Because the palace's halls were mostly timber and caught fire easily, vats stood everywhere far from a water source, so a burning hall could be put out in time.
