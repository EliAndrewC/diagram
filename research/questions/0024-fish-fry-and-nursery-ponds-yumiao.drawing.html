How our maps draw fish fry and nursery ponds (yumiao)

On our maps most dike-pond hamlets keep no nursery ponds: they raise grown fish from fry bought in. About one hamlet in four is drawn instead as a fry village like those of Jiujiang, in the Pearl River delta, where most of the pond water went to rearing fry. In a fry village the smallest ponds are given to fry until they hold up to seven-tenths of its pond water, and its fry ponds are drawn in grayer, muddier water than the clear ponds where the fish are grown on.

  • Each hamlet is either a fry village or a hamlet that buys its fry.
    The Pearl River delta knew both: Jiujiang, whose ponds were mostly nursery water, and the villages elsewhere, whose ponds mostly raised grown fish. These are two forms of village, not one share of nursery ponds that every village kept, so each hamlet takes one form or the other, chosen at random.
  • How many hamlets are fry villages?
    One in four, a GUESS. Qu Dajun says fry ponds were found only in Jiujiang, so the fry village is made the rarer form; no source gives the odds, and one in four is arbitrary.
  • A fry village gives up to seven-tenths of its pond area to fry.
    Qu Dajun gives seven parts in ten of Jiujiang's pond water to fry; reading that as a share of the ponds' area is this project's reading. Ponds are added to the nursery for as long as their area stays within that share, so the nursery water comes to about seven-tenths and never more.
  • Which of a fry village's ponds hold the fry?
    The smallest, a GUESS. Which of Jiujiang's ponds held the fry is on no page we read; taking the smallest follows the graded ponds of the late-Ming Jiangxi method, where the fry start in the small pond and are carried on to a middle pond as they grow.
  • A fry pond is drawn in grayer, muddier water than a grow-out pond.
    In Jiujiang the fry water was turbid and the grown-fish water clear, so the color of a pond told what it held, and a fry village reads as one on the map at a glance. The exact shade is a map drawing convention.

Some forms of nursery the record holds are not drawn.

  • No small nursery pond is drawn beside a big one.
    The small pond about ten feet square set beside a big one is the method of the inland province of Jiangxi, away from the delta, as the Nongzheng quanshu, a farm compendium printed late in the Ming in 1639, gives it; the ponds our maps draw are the delta's.
  • A modern fish-farming manual's pond sizes and nursery share are not used.
    Its fry pond of one to three mu, and nursery ponds making up a quarter to three-tenths of a farm's ponds, are a design for a modern farm that rears its own fry, and this project draws nothing found only in modern times. A fry pond is whatever size its plot on the map is.
