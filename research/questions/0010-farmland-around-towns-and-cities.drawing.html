How our maps draw the farmland around a town or a city

Our maps draw the farmland around a town or a city with the same irrigation works as a village's: a sluice tapped on a real stream, a head race forking into supply canals, flooded paddies stepping downhill, a dry hem on the upslope margin, a drain, and farmhouses around each field. The settlement sits among those fields, and the open ground lies mainly toward the edges of the map, reading as pasture and commons, and as paddy country carrying on past the edge.

A town map shows a sample: the far countryside is taken to lie off the sheet, and the fields near the town need not cover every foot of it.

  • Fields crowd closest to the town, not scrub.
    The fields nearest the castle town of Kanazawa got the richest manure, so a few paddies in a sea of scrub up to the gate would draw that gradient upside down.
  • The maps leave their unworked land at the far margins, toward the edge of the map.
    That the fallow lay far out, where labor ran short, is a GUESS: the account of how manure was shared out around Kanazawa says nothing of fallow.
  • Near a town the maps draw only irrigated fields with waterworks of their own.
    Neither a patchwork of dry fields and kitchen plots nor gridded paddies with no water supply is drawn there. The ground nearest a town was the most heavily worked, and this project draws it with the same watered fields a village works, so that a town's farmland reads as a village's does.
  • On a town map at least one field runs off the edge.
    A field cut by the margin says there is more farmland beyond, and a town that shows only enclosed fields claims to show all of its countryside, which no town sheet can.
  • On a town map, a field running off the edge need not show its farmhouses or its water.
    Its houses and its water are off the map too. Around a city the rule is stricter: see its fields' farmhouses below.
  • Farmland takes at least an eighth of the ground near a walled city, and over a quarter near a town.
    Within about 800 ft of a walled city's rampart at least 12 percent of the ground is farmed, and around a town at least 28 percent. Both floors are calibrations against our own drawn maps, not figures from a source: a city with no fields around it measured about 7 percent, and a town with five fields about 31.
  • The maps aim at the look of a dense wet-rice plain near a city.
    Within the farmed ground itself, roughly 70 to 75 percent is paddy, the rest bunds, ditches, lanes and farmsteads, with about 1.5 to 2 farming households for each hectare (~0.6-0.8 per acre) of paddy. That is a GUESS from general reading: no page we read gives either figure.

Around a city the maps go further: the fields ring it, and each is a complete irrigation system of its own.

  • A city is ringed with farmland until the ground refuses it.
    Cities grew up to serve the countryside around them, and a single token field on one flank would draw that the wrong way round. So fields and farmhouses are added around a city until there is no more room, at least four fields across at least three sides; fewer means the ring was not attempted, not that the ground ran out.
  • Each field is a whole water chain, not a colored patch.
    • Its sluice sits on the water and follows the watercourse's own line.
    • Its head race bends rather than running ruler-straight, with a gate at each end.
    • Where it draws from a moat, the offtake is angled downstream with the moat's current.
    • It has a named source and a drain, and the drain is carried clear off the map rather than stopping in mid-air.
  • How large is a field around a city?
    Its paddy strips run about 78 ft across, its rows of paddies step down every 39 to 57 ft, its supply canals run 180 to 780 ft long, and its comb fan runs 270 to 570 ft downhill; the dry hem across its head is 140 to 265 ft deep. These are calibrations against the three drawn cities, chosen so that a field at a city's fringe stays the size of the field it replaces, not figures from a source; the hem's depth is set field by field, because a single figure for all fields is deeper than some ground allows.
  • A field is fed from the moat only if it would cover more than about eleven and a half acres.
    A smaller field is not worth a moat offtake. The threshold is a calibration against the three drawn cities, not a figure from a source.
  • On a city map the head of each field is planted with dry crops.
    The ground along the supply canals, and the triangle between the two branches where the head race forks, sits at or above its own canals, so their water cannot be brought onto it by gravity. It is the well-drained high ground that dry crops take, and barley, millet and buckwheat are drawn there beside the head race.
  • No more than a fifth of the ground along a city field's main channels is left bare.
    The ceiling is a calibration against the drawn maps, which run 13 to 16 percent bare with the heads planted and about 25 percent with them left bare, not a figure from a source.
  • A farmstead stands above its field's drain.
    In a wet lowland the settlements sat on the well-drained rise along a river, and the low, poorly drained ground behind it was made paddy. The maps carry that principle to a feature those accounts do not name, the strip just below a dug drain, and take it for the wettest ground in the valley. A farmstead's seat is measured against the drain's line rather than the field's center, since a seat can be above the middle of a field and still below the drain where it bends. It matters only where a city's drain runs across the map rather than straight off it.
  • A farmstead may stand hard against a strip of dry crop, but never on it.
    The dry crops at a field's head are ground to be farmed, not a seat for a house.
  • Every field outside a city's wall has farmhouses around it, even where it runs off the map.
    What is counted is the part a reader can see: along the field's boundary inside the map, about seven farmhouses for every 3,000 ft. Village fields run between four and nineteen by the same measure, about twelve on average, so seven is a floor rather than a target. Because most of a fringe field lies off the map, the ring is drawn densely enough that enough houses land on the visible edge, and those that fall off it are not drawn.
  • Our maps count the farmers who work a city's land in the surrounding villages, not in the city.
    The farmhouses around a city's fields are drawn but left out of its population. On a town map the same houses are counted in the town's population, because a town's figure takes in the farmers of the countryside around it.
