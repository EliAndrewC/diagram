Labels on maps (cartographic label placement)

Every name on these maps - a notice board, a well, a road, a temple, a city district - is a caption placed by the rules mapmakers already use. Placing names on maps is one of the fundamental tasks of cartography. A caption has two jobs, in this order: it must be easy to read, and it must be plainly the name of this feature and no other. The maps follow that standard rather than a rule of their own.

So a small feature's caption stands beside it, at the first free place in a ranked list of eight, a gap of half its letter height away. It lies on empty ground before anything else, and never on another caption or a building while any other choice exists. It turns with a feature that is itself turned, and breaks across lines only when that clears the ground. What a caption may lie across on a crowded town or city sheet is at What labels may cover, and how districts are named, on town and city maps. Which things get a caption at all is at The map sheet: its title, legend, frame and margins.

A feature large enough to hold its name - a building, a field - is named on itself. One too small for its words - a notice board, a well, a privy, a small shrine - is named beside it; mapmakers call such a feature a point.

  • A larger feature is named on itself.
    A building's name written across its roof on a plan, or a field's name across the field, is an area caption, set inside the area it names, over its middle when that is free.
  • Which side of its feature does a caption go?
    Authorities differ on the order, and these maps follow the one readers themselves chose. The only study we found that asked them - nearly 800 of them - found they significantly prefer a name directly above its point to the conventional upper right, and ranked the places in this order: directly above, directly below, to its right, upper right, lower right, to its left, upper left, lower left. Every caption tries them in that order, so that every caption on every sheet follows the same pattern.
  • The cartography textbooks try the four corners around a point first.
    Their order is upper right, upper left, lower right, lower left, straight right, straight left, above and slightly right, below and slightly left. Eight positions like these are a typical set of places to try for a point's name. The readers' study leaves out the two "slightly" places as a relic of older printing.
  • Several authorities rank the places straight above, below or to the side of a point higher than the textbooks do.
    A German reference dictionary of cartography counts the place to the right of a point as ideal, though without saying whether level with it or at its upper right. A series of schemes for labeling oil wells puts directly above higher than other authors do. A mapping library's own example tries only the four places beside the point, and no corner at all.
  • A cartography course advises lifting a caption above its point or dropping it below, not setting it level.
    Its first guideline is that a point's caption is not like an item in a bulleted list, but shifted up or down from the point.
  • The gap is measured from the edge of the feature as it is drawn.
    The mapping programs measure it from the boundary of the feature's symbol to the caption's nearest edge.
  • How far from its feature does a caption stand?
    No source found fixes the number. The cartography course says only that it depends on the map, and that consistency is what matters most, so the gap is the same for every caption of a size. These maps use half the height of the caption's lettering, which for a notice board's 8 ft lettering is 4 ft of air: close enough to read as one thing, far enough that the words do not touch the glyph. That figure is our calibration, not a finding.

When the first places are taken, the standard says what a caption may cover and how far it may move.

  • Only when no empty ground is left does a caption cover something, and then the least important thing it can.
    That is how the mapping programs place a label. On these maps another caption, the feature itself, a house or any other building, a well or a small fixture is never covered while any other choice exists.
  • A lane or a ditch may be crossed, but crossing one is better than crossing two.
    The mapping programs move a label to where it crosses one road rather than several.
  • Fields, scrub, groves and open ground are not in the way at all.
    These maps treat them as what the mapping programs call available space, so a name lies on the land the way a name lies on any map.
  • A place that would run off the edge of the picture is never taken.
    A clipped caption cannot be read.
  • A caption's place is scored by what it covers plus how far down the ranked places it had to go.
    Those are two of the three factors by which Yoeli's scheme judges a labeling.
  • When every place at the usual gap is taken, the caption tries the same places a step further out.
    Then another step, and the nearer free place always wins. It may go as far as twice the usual gap with nothing more, the kind of allowance a mapping program lets a mapmaker set for a label that cannot keep its preferred distance.
  • A caption more than twice the usual gap from its feature is tied to it by a thin leader line.
    It is the device mapmakers use for a caption displaced from the thing it names.
  • A caption the map calls for is never left off.
    On a sheet with no free ground at all, the caption still goes down, where it covers the least.
  • Captions are placed last, once the map is finished.
    Where a caption can go depends on everything else already being there.

The rule the map follows, for a caption's place: a feature large enough to hold its name is named on itself, over its middle; a smaller one is named beside it, trying in order directly above, directly below, to its right, upper right, lower right, to its left, upper left and lower left, half the caption's letter height from the feature's drawn edge. A place on empty ground wins; failing that, the place that covers the least and lies highest in the order; a place off the edge of the picture is never taken. When every place is taken the same places are tried a step further out, up to twice the usual gap, and beyond that the caption is tied to its feature by a thin leader line. A caption the map calls for is never left off.

A caption turns with the thing it names, so that the words read as part of it, the way a level caption reads as part of a level building.

  • A caption takes its feature's own angle, exactly.
    Where that angle would set the words upside down, the caption is turned a half turn so it reads the right way up. Nothing else changes its angle.
  • Mapping programs can turn a caption to its feature's own angle.
    Where a feature carries a direction of its own, its caption can be turned to that angle, and then the angle - not the usual places around the feature - decides where the caption goes. So on these maps the ranked places are read in the feature's own turned frame: "upper right" means upper right as the words read, and the gap is measured off the feature's turned edge, not off an upright box around it.
  • A road's name follows the road, above it rather than below, and never upside down.
    That is the cartography course's rule for a line's label. On these maps a road's caption takes its bearing from the stretch of roadway it is seated against, and a row of shopfronts takes the line of the street it fronts.
  • Everything with no direction of its own is labeled level.
    Horizontal lettering is the easiest to find and read. So a caption over an upright block of packed houses ("laborers' dwellings", "samurai houses", "barns"), a caption on a point fixture such as a boundary stone, and a city district's caption stay level. Tilt follows the feature; it is not decoration.
  • The ground under all three drawn cities' district captions is level.
    It was measured, so tilting those captions would line them up with nothing on the map.
  • A feature turned a quarter, a half or three quarters of a turn keeps a level caption.
    It has a level edge of its own, so a level caption already lines up with it.
  • A tilted caption near the edge can widen the picture.
    It takes up a taller block than the same words set level, so where the picture's edges are drawn to take in its captions, it can push one out: one city's sheet grew about 45 ft for its tanning yard's caption.

The rule the map follows, for a caption's angle: a caption naming a turned feature is drawn at that feature's own angle and seated off its turned edge; a caption naming a road or a row laid along one runs along that line at its bearing; a caption naming an area, an upright block or a point fixture is level. The text never reads upside down.

A caption breaks across lines only when that is what clears the ground beneath it.

  • A caption is drawn on one line if that clears the sheet.
    Otherwise it takes the first of two, then three, lines that does; and if nothing clears, it goes back to one line.
  • Whether a caption clears is tested on the block as it will be drawn.
    Its true outline, turned about its own center exactly as the finished text is, is tested against the true outlines of everything built and every caption already placed. Ground cover and land parcels, which never block a caption, do not count here either.
  • A caption is cut only between words, where its longest line comes out shortest.
    Among the ways of cutting a caption into a given number of lines, the one whose longest line is shortest wins, with ties going to the most even lengths.
  • A short word is never left alone on a line.
    A line holding a single short word - "of", "the" - is refused whenever there are more words than lines, so "Shrine of Benten" is cut as "Shrine of / Benten" or "Shrine / of Benten", and never leaves "of" stranded on a line of its own.
  • A wrapped caption keeps the single line's center and its tilt.
    The map records the block rather than the line, so what is checked is what is drawn. One hamlet's notice board is the worked example: "notice / board" at about 57 degrees, clear of the farmhouse the single line crossed.

The rule the map follows, for wrapping: a caption is drawn on as few lines as clear the ground beneath it, preferring one; words are never broken; a wrapped caption's lines are as even as they can be and never leave a two- or three-letter word alone on a line; and the wrapped block keeps the place and the angle the single line would have had.
