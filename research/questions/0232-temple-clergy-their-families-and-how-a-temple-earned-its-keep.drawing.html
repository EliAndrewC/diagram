How our maps house a temple's clergy

On a city or capital map a temple complex is drawn as a walled compound with an empty court. Its celibate monks, a few dozen with their initiates and servants, live inside, implied rather than drawn, and two or three families of its married clergy live in ordinary houses around it (five to nine round a Fox temple), as hereditary clergy historically lived round the temple or shrine they served. The setting's temples keep both kinds of clergy: most keep a few householders among many celibate monks, while the Fox clan's temples keep a small celibate council and hereditary families for everything else.

  • A city temple is drawn as a walled compound with an empty court.
    On a city or capital map, at 3 ft to the pixel, a major temple is its wall, its gate and a blank court, drawn like the governor's yamen: the inside of the compound is the subject of its own building plan, drawn when the players visit it. How big the compound and its halls are is at How our maps size and draw a city's temples (garan). The blank court is a map drawing convention.
  • Who lives inside a city temple's walls?
    Celibate monks, resident initiates and a few lay servants, some 25 to 45 of them in a provincial city's complex (about 15 to 30 monks, 5 to 10 resident initiates and a few servants), in halls implied inside the wall, as the monks of a Japanese Zen temple lived in common in its monks' hall. The count is this project's own arithmetic, a GUESS. They are counted on top of the dwellings the map draws rather than housed in its streets, and they are not counted in any lay caste's share of the city, because the setting's clergy are not a lay caste.
  • How many initiates does a temple keep?
    About twice its monk count ("many are called, few are chosen", as the setting's scripture says of the monastic life), of whom only a few sleep inside: roughly 5 to 10 at a provincial city, 10 to 20 at a domain capital.
  • Initiates who live outside the temple are drawn as ordinary houses of their own caste.
    They are laborers, carters, servants and shopkeepers who study while they work ordinary jobs, so they never get buildings of their own.
  • A temple's married monks keep homes in its neighborhood.
    In the setting an adept may marry and raise children, and its campaign notes put married monks' families in or next to the temple complex. So a share of each complex's monks are householders, whose families keep ordinary homes near the temple. Married clergy lived so historically: Japan's shrine priests married, their children usually inherited the office, and their houses gathered near the shrine they served.
  • Two or three clergy homes stand round each major temple, a guess of our own.
    Two or three round each major complex, a GUESS: no source we read counts the clergy homes round one temple.
  • Clergy homes are drawn exactly like a laborer's house, a deliberate deviation.
    They carry no label and no glyph of their own, so the eye cannot pick the temple families out of the town; only the map's own records mark which houses are theirs. A real priest family's house could be picked out: at Kamigamo in Kyoto the shrine priest families' houses were one-story, gabled and tiled, where the town houses beside them were raised for an upper room, and each stood inside an earthen wall with a gate at its entrance.
  • A retired abbot's hermitage or a daughter chapel may stand apart from any temple.
    At most two such features stand away from the temples on a map: a retired abbot's hermitage, or a small daughter chapel with two to four monks of its own, drawn because they are the exceptions to clergy homes staying near a temple. Old monks historically did retire away from their monastery, as the monks of Mount Hiei retired to residences at Sakamoto at its foot; the number is a GUESS of our own.

The Fox clan's temples keep a different rule of ordination, and it changes both where their clergy live and where the temples stand.

  • In a Fox temple only its council of three is celibate, so its clergy are householders.
    The Three Bonds who run a Fox temple are its only monks bound to celibacy; every other position is hereditary, and "the temple's revenue sources are effectively family businesses passed down along bloodlines". A Fox temple is thus a small celibate core that runs the house, with hereditary households for everything else.
  • A Fox precinct is ringed by five to nine clergy homes.
    Every position below its council of three is hereditary, so each family that holds one keeps a house by the precinct. The count of five to nine is a GUESS of our own. A Fox city's clergy housing therefore grows with its number of precincts.
  • The Fox temples lend money, as historical Buddhist monasteries did.
    In the setting's campaign notes the Fox clan's Seven Temples hold the use of the forest, and lend money with Fukurokujin and Ebisu, the setting's Fortunes of wisdom and mercy and of honest work, and make wedding loans with Benten, the Fortune of romantic love. In China, Buddhist monasteries were the largest institutions that lent money, and they generally lent it as pawnbrokers.
  • A Fox city's precincts stand where their trades are, not gathered into a temple quarter.
    Each precinct is an economic house, so the precincts spread across the city's quarters. The Japanese temple quarter along a town's rim was considered and not taken: it was a castle town's defensive arrangement - at Hirosaki the temples were gathered for the defense of the castle town and the control of its people - and the Fox temples are commercial houses first. How many precincts a Fox city draws is at How our maps place a city's temples and its temple quarter (teramachi).
  • A Fox precinct that stands alone needs no shrines beside it.
    Our maps set shrines beside the temples of a temple neighborhood, and that rule applies to each cluster of temples. A precinct sited by its trade is sometimes the only temple in its quarter, and it is exempt, as the Bishamon temple on our map of Tango, which stands in a converted estate, already is.

The rule the map follows: on a city or capital map (3 ft per pixel) a major temple is a walled compound of wall, gate and empty court. Two or three clergy homes stand within about 500 ft of each major complex, and none stands stranded away from every temple; a precinct whose clergy are hereditary householders carries five to nine instead, ringing its wall. Beyond those, at most two exception features stand apart - a retired abbot's hermitage, or a small daughter chapel with two to four monks of its own - drawn precisely because they are exceptions.
