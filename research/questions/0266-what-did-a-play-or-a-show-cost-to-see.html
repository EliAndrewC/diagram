What did a play or a show cost to see?

Rokugan's towns have playhouses and its villages hold festivals with performers, and the setting judges a price by how many days of a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give what the historical record holds instead: the prices of a seat at kabuki, noh and sumo in Japan and at a Beijing theater, what a provincial show earned, how troupes were made up, and the nearest day wages found.

An Edo theater sold its seats by class, cheapest in the open floor and dearest in the boxes along its sides, and priced a box by the bay. At sumo too a box was priced by the bay, and a party could share it. A Beijing theater charged its pit by the head and its gallery by the table. A village either played for itself or bought in a troupe; for the troupes a provincial town bought in, the record gives what a run took in, not what a seat cost.

Kabuki in Edo

  • What did a seat cost?
    In the list prices given for Edo's theaters from the Kyowa era (1801-1804), a place in the pit before the stage cost 132 mon of copper and one in the floor behind it 100 mon; at the back of the house, one act could be watched for 16 mon. The boxes, upper and lower, were of several classes, and a bay of them cost 25 to 35 monme of silver. An encyclopedia gives the same for 1803: 132 mon a head in the pit, and 25 to 35 monme for a box seating seven.
  • And a seat in a box, a head?
    About 3.6 to 5 monme, if seven shared it, this record's arithmetic. How many sat in a bay is given three ways: a historian's estimate of six for 1720, the encyclopedia's seven for 1803, and eight at sumo in late Edo.
  • Was the list price what was paid?
    Not always. Just after the recoinage of 1714 a bay of boxes cost 1,200 mon (16 monme) and a place in the pit 64 mon, half the list price. In 1737 the Nakamura theater's return of its takings for one play gives 2 bu of gold a bay of boxes, within the list price, and 164 mon a head in the pit and standing room, about 25% above it. The historian notes that no source of the Kyoho era (1716-1736) names a list price, and that the price moved with the times and with the house.
  • And later?
    A bay of boxes at Edo's kabuki ran about 40 to 60 monme in the closing years of the shogunate, as another historian reports it.

Noh

  • Could anyone pay to see noh?
    Only at a fund-raising performance, a large noh show open to the public for an entrance fee. A jokebook of about 1614-1624 tells of one taking 30 mon a head for admission, and a popular novel speaks of boxes at one gold piece (by the usual reckoning a large oban) each at a fund-raising noh.
  • Who bought the tickets?
    At the fund-raising noh that the head of the Kanze school was allowed once in a lifetime, the tickets in Edo were allotted by force to the townsmen of each ward, which assured the school's head a huge income. The last show of that kind, by the Hosho school's head in Edo in 1848, ran fifteen fair-weather days and issued 52,500 tickets. No price list by seat class was found, and no ordinary noh performance sold to the public.

Sumo and other shows

  • What did sumo cost?
    At a fund-raising sumo tournament at Kyoto's Okazaki shrine in 1700, a bay of boxes of two mats cost 35 monme and a place on the open ground 3 monme. At Osaka in 1702 a box is said to have cost 43 monme. At touring tournaments in the castle town of Aizu-Wakamatsu, admission is reported as 130 mon in 1787 and 135 mon in 1789. A bay of boxes at Edo's sumo is said to have held eight.
  • And a show at a temple or shrine fair?
    No price was found for the plays or the sideshows put on at temple and shrine fairs.
  • And a street performer?
    Street performers played at crossroads and in the busy quarters and asked the onlookers for coin, or sold goods. How much a crowd gave in Japan was not found.

In the countryside

  • Who played in a village?
    Most village plays were put on for a very short time, a day or three days, at the tutelary shrine's festival, a rain prayer or the Bon rites, played by the village's own young men. A village could also buy in a troupe of local players from the villages around, and touring troupes existed for that. City kabuki actors who had no year's contract with a theater toured the provinces and played in shows that a provincial town's merchants or officials bought in.
  • What did a bought-in troupe take?
    At Hamanoichi in Funai domain, the domain's diary records 15 kabuki runs between 1690 and 1737; for seven of them it gives the money, 839 to 6,530 monme a run, 2,709.6 on average. The troupes averaged 43 players, 54.5 monme each a run. These are the shows' takings, and the historian treats them as the troupe's fee only as a hypothesis, since whether they went to the players, and how the troupe shared them, is not known.
  • And a single performance?
    In 1728 a local troupe from Nakatsu was engaged for nine fair-weather days and played fifteen day-and-night shows, with ticket money of 2 kan 392 mon. Taking a run as about nine and a half days of one show a day, the historian puts the kabuki troupes' average take at 285.2 monme a performance, 5.7 monme a player. No price for a single seat was found.
  • Did villagers give the players gifts?
    No gift or collection for players at a village show was found for the Edo period.

Who was in a troupe?

  • Actors only?
    The kabuki troupes whose runs at Hamanoichi are recorded averaged 43 players, and the puppet theater's 26. In the cities the music was the theater's own: late in the Kyoho era (1716-1736) the corner before the actors' entrance at the side of the stage became where the music was played, and that offstage music has been played, since it arose, by musicians belonging to the theater.
  • Jugglers and acrobats?
    Until early in the early modern period, players of noh, kyogen, kabuki and acrobatics formed troupes and played at lords' banquets, at the fund-raising shows of temples and shrines, and at provincial mansions and festivals. No acrobat or juggler was found traveling inside a kabuki troupe.

Qing China

  • What did a Beijing theater charge?
    The pit by the head and the gallery by the table. The pit was filled mostly by the riff-raff of the market; gentlemen always went upstairs, where the seats beside the stage were called official seats, those on its left the most prized. Before 1900 the fixed price was 130 copper cash a seat; after the Boxer upheaval each theater set its own. The passage does not say which count of cash it means.
  • And a troupe for a village festival?
    A local troupe of Kaifeng, in Henan, playing village festivals for the gods, cost no more than ten-odd thousand cash for three days, and if fed it would stay ten days, even months.
  • And a street act?
    Street performers, monkey-men and sword-dancers among them, played to one gong and one drum. When the writer Yuan Zicai was a child in Hangzhou, a man who made toads perform on a shop counter asked 3 cash when he was done, and left. Conjurors could be called into a household's celebration to play; no fee is given.

The day's wage beside these prices

  • Beside the Edo prices of 1803?
    No unskilled wage was found for Edo in those years. The nearest is Kyoto's: its casual laborers' spring rate averaged 0.92 monme of silver a day in 1802-1804 (What did an unskilled laborer earn?). At that wage a box for seven cost 27 to 38 days' pay, and one seat in it about 4 to 5.4, this record's arithmetic. The nearest craftsman's wage is an Edo carpenter's 5.4 monme a day, meals included, in the Bunsei era (1818-1830), 15 to 27 years later. The pit's 132 mon is in copper and these wages in silver, and they are not converted.
  • Beside the sumo prices?
    No wage was found for Kyoto in 1700 or for Aizu-Wakamatsu. The nearest Kyoto laborer's wage is about 0.91 monme a day in 1745-1754, some 45 years after the Okazaki tournament (What did an unskilled laborer earn?); at it, a place on the ground cost about 3 days' pay, this record's arithmetic. Beside Aizu-Wakamatsu's 130 and 135 mon, the nearest is the Kyoto laborer's 0.92 monme of 1802-1804 above, some 15 years later and in another region; the copper is not converted.
  • Beside the Funai takings?
    No wage of either class was found for Funai. The nearest unskilled wage is Kyoto's of 1745-1754 above, 8 to 64 years from the Funai runs and in another region, and the nearest craftsmen's are the carpenters and sawyers of a shrine job at Nogami in 1749, 12 to 59 years from them, at 1 to 1.67 monme a day (What did a shrine roof and its finish cost?); both are in silver, as the takings are.
  • Beside the Qing prices?
    No account of the wages paid in Beijing itself was found beside the 130 cash charged before 1900. The nearest laborer's is rural Beijing's, from one fuel store's account books, in the store's own count of copper cash: between 372 and 448 cash a day in the years recorded from 1890 to 1900. The historians who compiled the series say the wage they derive from it in silver seems extraordinarily low, though the hands were also given food (What did an unskilled laborer earn?), and whether the store's count of cash is the theater's is not known. The nearest craftsman's is a painter's 40 cents a day, a wage Beijing's painters' guild recalled in 1918-1919 as paid 25 years before, about 1893-1894. In 1885 the city's jade workers struck, and failed, for 6 cash instead of 4 for each hole they made in the mouth-piece of a pipe, a piece rate, not a day's pay. How far any of these stands in years from the seat price is not known, since the source dates the price only as before 1900; the cents and the cash are not converted. No wage was found for Hangzhou or for Kaifeng; the nearest are Beijing's of 1736, a laborer 75 copper cash a day and carpenters, masons and other craftsmen 154, in another city, and at an unknown distance in years, since the passages do not date the Hangzhou act or the Kaifeng troupe.
