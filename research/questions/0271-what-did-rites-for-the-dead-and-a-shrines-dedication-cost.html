What did rites for the dead and a shrine's dedication cost?

Rokugan's prices are counted in koku and its smaller coins, and the setting's money rules judge a price by how many days of a craftsman's or a laborer's work it would take. The findings below give what the historical record holds for a shrine's dedication, its festivals and the rites of the dead in Edo-period Japan and, where Japan is silent, in Qing China, each figure set beside the nearest day wages the record gives.

No fee was found for a priest's enshrinement of a god, a rite to pacify an angry spirit, perpetual memorial, washing the bones of the dead, sending a portion of the remains to a distant mountain temple, or a calligrapher's writing of a shrine's plaque in Japan. The nearest found are a city ward's budget for its float at a Gion festival in 1838 and a Chinese calligrapher's price list of 1759. What was found for the dead and for a temple's showing of its hidden image is narrower than the question: how mounds and a memorial for the unclaimed famine dead were paid for in 1784-1785, a fee a head to see an image, and the takings and costs of the great traveling showings of the Zenkoji temple in Edo. No cost was found for the rites of a sacred rope, a sacred tree or a grove.

An enshrinement

  • What did enshrining the god in a new sanctuary cost?
    Only as a share of a repair: in the 1749 repair of a small sanctuary in the Tama district, the ridge-raising, the enshrinement and sundries together took 20.0% of about 43 ryo, with no share for the enshrinement alone (the roof page).
  • What were the priests paid for it?
    No fee was found. The one list of an enshrinement's needs found, for the great shrine of Kizuki in Izumo in 1549, before the Edo period, counts robes, silk and cloth for the priests, shrine maidens and attendants, and gives no money.

A festival

  • What did a festival to pacify an angry spirit cost?
    No cost was found. The nearest is a city ward's share of a great festival, not a priests' rite or a village's.
  • What did a ward's float cost at Hakata's Gion festival?
    In 1838 Gyo-machi, one ward of Hakata, reckoned the silver for building its float on each of its future turns of duty at 7,300 monme in all. About 800 monme of it was expected from the festival subsidy called o-watari money, and the other 6,500 monme was to be raised within the ward. The float was not the whole duty: on the paper's reckoning it was about 64.6% of the ward's whole cost of a turn.
  • What did the other wards give the ward on duty?
    A little over 200 monme, normally. The wards of the same group not on duty gave the ward on duty a contribution called Gion money, by their quotas of labor; from those quotas in 1691 the paper reckons it at 213 monme when Gyo-machi was on duty.

Rites for the dead

  • What did perpetual memorial cost?
    No fee was found.
  • What did washing the bones cost?
    No fee was found.
  • What did a shroud cost?
    No price was found in Japan. The nearest is Chinese and not a price: in Fuzhou, five or six years before the writer's book of 1865, a poor widow who came into fifteen dollars put them into gold earrings, to be sold at her death for her coffin and in part the other costs of her burial, as they were. In the same book carpenters and masons there were paid twenty to thirty cents a day and hired workers four to six dollars a month, boarding themselves, so the fifteen dollars were 50 to 75 days of a carpenter or about two and a half to four months of a hired worker.
  • What did sending a portion of the remains to a distant mountain temple cost?
    No fee was found, searched as Mount Koya (the rites page).

A mound for the famine dead

  • What did burying the unclaimed dead under a mound, with a stone and rites, cost?
    Only as one fund. After the famine of 1783-1784 in Sannohe, in the far north, the head of the Rinsenji temple's parishioners, after consulting its priest, bought up the skulls left by the roads at 24 mon each until they came to a thousand, and as sponsor held a great memorial rite, at which worshippers gave 60 kanmon, 60,000 mon. With those offerings, in 1785, four mounds of the "thousand men", also called "the thousand heads", were built and a memorial stone set up. A memorial of a million recitations on thirty cold nights was held over three years, until 1787; the prefecture's account does not say what paid for it. The four mounds are of heaped earth, the memorial and its base of stone. The account gives no builder's cost, no price for the stone and no fee for the rites.
  • And the skulls?
    A thousand at 24 mon each is 24,000 mon, by this project's arithmetic, paid by the sponsor apart from the offerings.

Showing a hidden image

  • What did it cost to see a temple's hidden image?
    100 mon at Tsu: a diary of 1790 gives the showing of the Kannonji temple's image at 100 mon, and one of 1834, naming its Amida image, 100 mon each, whoever you are; the diarist of 1790 paid 130 mon apiece for the night's inn in the same entry. No takings of a village temple's showing were found.
  • What did a great traveling showing take in and cost?
    The Zenkoji temple's showings of its image in Edo, by another historian's study of the temple as reported: in 1692, 10,210 ryo taken in against 914 spent, a profit of 9,296; at the last, in 1820, 3,062 taken in against 3,819 spent, a loss of 757.
  • And its temporary buildings?
    93 ryo. For the Zenkoji showing at the Denboin of Asakusa in 1803, four master carpenters bid to build the temporary buildings of the grounds, and the work went to Suzuki Chikugo at 93 ryo. That showing ended at a loss: the temple's officer wrote that the offerings came in very thinly and did not nearly cover the costs.

Ropes, sacred trees and groves

  • What did the rite for a rope strung between two trees as a gate cost, or a sacred rope round the precinct, naming a great tree as a god's dwelling, or hallowing a grove?
    No cost was found for any of them, for the rope, the priest's fee or the rite.

A calligrapher's plaque

  • What did a noted calligrapher take for writing a shrine's plaque?
    No fee was found in Japan. The nearest is the price list the painter and calligrapher Zheng Banqiao posted in 1759, whose carved stone is in the Yangzhou Museum: six taels for a large piece, four for a medium and two for a small, one tael for a hanging scroll or a couplet, and five qian, half a tael, for a fan or a small square sheet. It has no line for a plaque.

Day wages beside them

  • Beside the Sannohe mounds, 1784-1785?
    No wage of Sannohe was found. The nearest are Osaka's, far to the southwest: its building helpers' 236 mon a day in 1785, the same year, and a carpenter costed at 4 monme of silver, 420 mon by a historian's conversion, for a rebuilding after a fire of 1801, sixteen years or more later (What did a skilled craftsman earn against a laborer?). At them the 60,000 mon of offerings were about 250 helper-days or 140 carpenter-days, and the skulls' 24,000 mon about 100 or 57.
  • Beside the fee to see an image at Tsu?
    No wage of Tsu was found. At Osaka's helpers' 236 mon of 1785, five years before the first diary, 100 mon was under half a day's pay, and a quarter of the carpenter's 420 mon (the craftsman page).
  • Beside the Zenkoji buildings, 1803?
    The nearest laborer's wage in this record is a Kyoto casual laborer's of 1802-1804, about 0.92 monme a day, and Edo's laborers were paid about Kyoto's from 1818 (What did an unskilled laborer earn?). The nearest Edo craftsman's is a carpenter's 5.4 monme a day, meals included, at the prices of 1804-1830, reported at third hand (the roof page). At 60 monme to the ryo, a rate this project borrows, the buildings' 93 ryo was 5,580 monme, about 1,000 carpenter-days or 6,100 laborer-days.
  • Beside the Hakata float, 1838?
    No wage of Hakata was found. The nearest unskilled wage read is a Kyoto casual laborer's of 1802-1804, about 0.92 monme a day, thirty-five years earlier and far to the east; the nearest craftsman's are Osaka's carpenters' of the same years, 4.7 times that, about 4.3 monme, and the carpenter's wage of Edo-Tokyo in 1853-1860 by a price table, fifteen years later, 7.5 monme with meals, which this project reads as a day's pay and the paper reading it takes for an official or agreed rate rather than the market's. At them the float's 7,300 monme was about 7,900 laborer-days, about 1,700 days of the Osaka carpenter or about 970 of the Edo one, and the Gion money of about 200 monme about 220 laborer-days or 46 days of the Osaka carpenter.
  • Beside Zheng Banqiao's prices, 1759?
    The nearest Qing wage read is the public-construction wage norm of Jiangsu, set in 1769, ten years later: 0.040 tael a day unskilled and 0.051 skilled, averages over the province's districts of regulated wages that the regulations' introductory memorial calls near market prices at low market activity. At them a large piece's six taels was 150 unskilled or about 118 skilled days, a scroll's one tael 25 or about 20, and a fan's half tael about 12 or 10.
