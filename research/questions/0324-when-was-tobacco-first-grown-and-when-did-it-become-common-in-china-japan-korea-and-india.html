When was tobacco first grown, and when did it become common, in China, Japan, Korea and India?

Rokugan's notes list tobacco among the cash crops that tend to be specialties of the regions suited to them, and they mention tobacco merchants. The findings below give what the historical record holds for China, Japan, Korea and India.

Tobacco is an American plant, and no species of it is native to Asia. It reached all four countries at about the same time, around 1600: from Luzon into Fujian in China, with Portuguese traders to Japan and India, and from Japan into Korea. Writers of the time describe it as common within about ten years of its arrival in Japan and Korea, about fifteen in India and about forty in China, on this project's arithmetic from the dates below, though none of them counts smokers.

Dry-field crops in general are in dry fields and their crops, and other commercial crops in cash crops on rice land.

China

  • When is tobacco first recorded in China?
    The Ming physician Zhang Jiebin, in the English of Berthold Laufer, says the plant was unknown in antiquity and was grown "only recently", in the Wanli reign (1573-1620). Laufer himself has it first becoming known in Fujian at the start of the 1600s. The historian Timothy Brook finds tobacco in the Wanli-era price lists of Shen Bang, at 3 fen of silver a jin for domestic leaf and 1 qian for leaf from Luzon, and calls this the earliest record of tobacco use in China. The excerpt gives no year, so it cannot be set against Laufer's date. Neither Zhang's nor Shen Bang's Chinese text could be read.
  • Where was tobacco first grown in China?
    Zhang names Fujian and Guangdong, from where it spread north. Laufer has the seeds brought from Luzon to Fujian and carried on to Guangdong, Zhejiang and Jiangsu. A chronicle of Taiwan written in 1694, as Laufer reports it, says instead that it was first grown on Taiwan and taken to Fujian by men of Zhangzhou. No source read gives the year of the first planting.
  • Was tobacco common in China by about 1640?
    Two Ming-era writers say so, in Laufer's English. Zhang says everyone in the south-west, old and young, smoked day and night, and Laufer says the first users were soldiers. A writer Laufer leaves unnamed says that in the closing years of the Chongzhen reign (1628-43) hardly a boy three feet tall did not smoke. Neither gives a count, and the second names no place.
  • How long did tobacco take to become common in China?
    About forty years, on this project's arithmetic, from Laufer's start of the 1600s to about 1640. The figure would be longer if Shen Bang's prices prove to be earlier than 1600.
  • Edicts against tobacco did not stop it, though the sources differ on which one threatened death.
    Laufer gives an edict of 1638 threatening decapitation for selling it, and says that growing it never stopped. An encyclopedia of tobacco lists edicts of 1612, 1638 and 1641, and puts the threat of decapitation in 1641.
  • By the time of a British embassy described in 1797, tobacco was a field crop on the road from Beijing to Tartary.
    George Staunton saw great quantities planted in the low grounds, and girls of ten or younger with pipes in their mouths.

Japan

  • When did tobacco reach Japan?
    Laufer reports Japanese records in which Portuguese traders first brought the leaves toward the end of the 1500s. The Japanese Wikipedia article has a Franciscan giving seed to the lord of Hirado in 1601, a date marked there by a modern monument. The first dated mention by someone on the spot is the diary of Richard Cocks, the English merchant at Hirado, in 1615.
  • Tobacco is said to have been first planted at Nagasaki about 1605.
    Laufer and the Japanese Wikipedia article both give about 1605, the second naming Sakurababa, and neither's own source was read. Plants were certainly in the ground by 1615, when Cocks reports an order to pull up all that was planted. Laufer puts cultivation on an extensive scale in the later 1600s.
  • Tobacco was in common use around Hirado by 1615, within ten years of its arrival.
    Cocks wrote that men, women and children were "besotted" with it, and that it had not been in use ten years. Tobacco pipes were on sale at Kyoto in the same years.
  • Who smoked in Japan?
    Cocks names men, women and children, and Laufer concludes from him that all classes smoked within a decade. The Tobacco and Salt Museum says that by the end of the bans it was popular at all social levels, including the lower and middle classes. The encyclopedia dates the end of the bans to 1625, and says tobacco was part of daily life by 1640.

Korea

  • The first Korean account of tobacco that survives in the sources read dates from 1614.
    The scholar Yi Su-gwang, in his Jibong yuseol, says the plant had first come from Japan in recent years. A 2015 study by Jeong Kee-Taeg dates the work to 1614. The National Archives of Korea gives the first mention in the royal annals as a fire of 1623 at the Japanese quarter at Dongnae.
  • When did tobacco arrive in Korea?
    The sources do not agree:
    • Jeong reckons 1611-12, by subtracting a few years from Yi's 1614.
    • Jang Yu's collected works of 1635 say twenty years earlier, which Jeong reckons as 1615.
    • The royal annals of 1638 say 1616-17, on Jeong's reckoning of its years.
    • The Dutch captive Henry Hamel, in Laufer's summary of his account of 1668, says fifty or sixty years before, which is 1608-18 on this project's arithmetic.
    • The National Archives calls arrival during the Imjin War the usual view, and Jeong gives the published range as 1592-98 to 1618, with some authors deriving it from China and others from Japan.
  • When was tobacco first planted in Korea?
    Yi says in 1614 that many were planting it. Jo Gyeong-nam, writing in 1622, says the seed came from Japan four or five years before, which Jeong reckons as 1617-18, and that many southerners grew rich by planting it. Yi's planting is earlier than Jo's seed, and no source read reconciles them.
  • Was smoking common in Korea by the early 1620s?
    The annals say that from 1616-17 some used it without its being widespread, and that from 1621-22 no one did not. Jang Yu says in 1635 that everyone smoked, from high ministers to cart-drivers, woodcutters and herders. Hamel, held captive in Korea for thirteen years, found both sexes smoking, even children of four or five. That makes about ten years from Jeong's arrival date, as Jeong himself says, or about five from the annals' own, on this project's arithmetic.

India

  • Tobacco reached India about 1605.
    Laufer has the Portuguese bringing it to the Deccan first, and from there to the north. Asad Beg, an officer of the emperor Akbar, wrote in 1605 that he found some at Bijapur, not having seen the like in India before.
  • When was tobacco first grown in India?
    A university course module, following the historian Irfan Habib, says soon after 1600 on the western coast. Habib's own book was not read. The English chaplain Edward Terry, in India in 1616-19, wrote that they sowed it "in abundance".
  • How soon did tobacco become common in India?
    Within about fifteen years, if Terry's sowing "in abundance" is taken as the measure; that is 11 to 14 years from 1605, on this project's arithmetic. Laufer says smoking became general, with tobacco shipped from India to Red Sea ports in 1619, and the emperor Jahangir banned it in 1617 for its effect on "many persons". The module has it grown in almost all parts of the Mughal empire by 1650, about 45 years after arrival.
  • Which Indians smoked?
    The sources read speak of the court and of an unspecified "many persons". None says whether the rural population smoked before 1650.
