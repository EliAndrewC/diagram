How our maps draw a town's fodder ground and hay stores

A town's hayfield is drawn as open cut grass with no fence around it, because the record shows fodder cut on a fodder ground, usually a village's common land, and carried to the stable, and no page we read shows a hayfield fenced or a pasture penned. Hay kept in the field, where a town keeps any, is drawn as stacks standing in the open, the form a Chinese farm manual describes; the maps draw no hay barn and no bale.

  • The maps draw no barn in a town's hayfield.
    No page we read describes a hay barn standing in a field, so the maps put no building in the hayfield.
  • A large store of hay, apart from the hayfield, is drawn as stacks and thatched storehouses together inside a wall.
    That is the form of the one fodder depot read, in a Ming novel: stacks all round, thatched storehouses and halls, and an earth wall with a gate.
  • The maps put a town's stables near the town, not out in the hayfield.
    Fodder was cut and carried to the stable, and a farmhouse's outer stable - a stable standing by itself, apart from the house - had a shed built on, so the horses' buildings are drawn near the town and not in the grass.
  • The maps draw no hay bales.
    The bale is what a baling machine makes, and balers came from the mechanical cutters of the 1860s; before the nineteenth century, hay was kept in haystacks.
  • The maps draw no fence around the hayfield.
    No page we read says a fodder ground was marked off by anything built, so its edge follows the lie of the land - a stream, a slope, the foot of a hill.
  • Whether a town's hayfield carries haystacks is a knob, rolled per town.
    Both forms are attested: the Chinese record keeps hay in stacks in the open, while the Japanese record shows fodder cut and carried to the stable, with no page we read showing it stacked in the field. No page we read says which was commoner, so the share between them is arbitrary: a town either carries no stored hay or carries a few round haystacks standing in the open grass.
  • The number and size of the stacks are our guess.
    They are a GUESS, drawn to read as haystacks rather than as buildings. The one premodern measure read, in a sixth-century Chinese farm manual, is a stack a zhang high inside a fence five or six paces around or across, a sheep flock's winter store, so the maps do not use it to set their size. They also draw the stacks without the manual's ring fence, which was there for the sheep to feed through - a GUESS too.
  • A town's hayfield lies past its fields, on the side away from the road.
    It stands beyond the fields on the town's hill or river side, as a village's meadow does (Grass hills and fodder meadows (kusayama, magusaba)), a band of open grass running to the edge of the map.
  • The hayfield's width and the line of its inner edge are our guess.
    No page we read gives the size of the fodder ground of a town on a road, so both are a GUESS, set to read as a meadow beside the fields rather than as a measured size.
  • The caption says hayfield or fodder meadow, not grazing.
    The record found cutting and not grazing, so "grazing" in a caption would be a GUESS.

The rule the map follows: a town's hayfield is a band of open grass past its fields on the side away from the road, meeting the edge of the map, with no fenced or dashed outline, no rectangular bales, no barns, no pen and no grazing animals; its edge follows the lie of the land, and its width and inner edge are a GUESS. Per town, rolled from the seed, it either carries no stored hay or carries a few round haystacks standing in the open grass, a GUESS at their number and size, drawn to read as stacks rather than as buildings. Its caption reads hayfield or fodder meadow. A large store of hay apart from the hayfield is drawn as stacks and thatched storehouses inside an earth wall with a gate, and the horses' stables stand near the town, not in the grass.
